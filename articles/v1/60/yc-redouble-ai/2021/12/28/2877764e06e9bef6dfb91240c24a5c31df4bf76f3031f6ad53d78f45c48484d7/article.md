# Deconvolutions and what to do about artifacts

This post was first published on[NeuralCeption](https://www.neuralception.com/convs-deconvs-artifacts/) .


Figure 1: Example for checkerboard patterns on image generated by a network with deconvolutions.


## Introduction


Convolutional neural networks are an efficient way to extract features from images by convolving inputs with learned filters. While this operation typically reduces the spatial resolution of the input, the reverse operation can be used to increase the spatial resolution, thus performing upsampling tasks. This is called a deconvolution, transposed convolution, or fractionally strided convolution, depending on the parameters used.


Common applications of deconvolutions include semantic segmentation[Long et al. (2015)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4038) , super-resolution[Shi et al. (2016)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7780576) , and other image generating tasks such as real-time style transfer[Johnson et al. (2016)](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08155) .


However, when networks generate images from lower resolutions using deconvolutions, the resulting images often have checkerboard artifacts, see for example figure 1.[Odena et al.(2016)](https://distill.pub/2016/deconv-checkerboard/) provides an excellent discussion about the reasons for this.


This post reviews what deconvolutions are, why they tend to produce artifacts, and what to do about it based on Odeana et al. Then we dive deeper into these findings with experiments. To follow along, you can get the code[here](https://github.com/daved01/deconvolutions) .


## What deconvolutions are


Deconvolutions were first introduced by[Zeiler et al.(2010)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5539957) for unsupervised learning of features, and then later used to visualize the learned features of a convnet in[Zeiler et al. (2013)](http://arxiv.org/abs/1311.2901) .

## Why deconvolutions lead to artifacts


Now that we know what deconvolutions are, let's see why they can lead to checkerboard artifacts. Let's assume we convolve a kernel size of 3x33x3


3


x


3


with an input using a stride of 2x22x2


2


x


2


. Figure 4 shows how many times each cell is seen by the kernel.


Figure 4: Overlap leads to checkerboard patterns.


As you can see, some elements are visited up to four times, while others are just once. This is the reason for the checkerboard artifacts!


Figure 5 shows that these artifacts are a network-inherent issue and not related to training. Already after the first epoch you can spot the artifacts, and they remain largely unaffected by training. This was already pointed out for GANs by Odean et al. (2018).


Figure 5: Artifacts are model-inherent and not related to training. Left: Image after the first iteration. Right: After 70,000 iterations (10 epochs), the artifacts have not changed much.


## How to avoid checkerboard artifacts


Odean et al. (2018) describe three ways to limit the effects.


First, one can use a deconvolution with a large filter and a stride of 11


1


as the last layer. This seems to smooth out artifacts and is used by many models. In my own experiments this does not help sufficiently.


The second approach is to choose a kernel size that is divisible by the stride. For example, if a kernel of shape 4x44x4


4


x


4


is used, a stride of 2 reduces the artifacts, see figure 6. Like in the example of figure 4, the maximum number of times a cell is marked by the kernel is 4. However, these cells are all adjacent in the centre, largely reducing the checkerboard artifacts.


Figure 6: If the stride (here 2) divides the kernel size (here 4x4) the effect of artifacts due to overlap is reduced since there aren't any gaps between the most frequently marked cells. The numbers indicate how many times a cell is marked by the kernel.


To fully avoid artifacts, it is best to avoid the deconvolution and implement a padding/upsampling directly followed by a convolutional layer instead. As discussed above, this is equivalent to a deconvolution, albeit less computationally efficient due to the additional upsampling operation.


Figure 7 shows how the results of a model change. On the left you can see 3 images. In the centre the images show reconstructions with a model which has deconvolutions. On the right you can see the same model where the deconvolutions have been substituted with upsampling followed by a convolutional layer. The two models are the[transformation networks](https://github.com/daved01/real_time_style_transfer) trained with a pixel loss for 10 epochs on around 7,000 images from the kaggle art[dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/c/gan-getting-started/data) .


Figure 7: Images reconstructed by a model with deconvolutions (centre) and one with substituted deconvolutions (right). Originals are on the left.


## Conclusion


There are many names for deconvolutions, but they all lead to the same result which can also be implemented with a direct convolution and padding/upsampling of the input. To avoid checkerboard artifacts, which typically occur on images generated by models which use deconvolutions to increase the spatial resolution, it is best to avoid these deconvolutions entirely and use their slightly less efficient equivalents of convolutions on upsampled inputs.
