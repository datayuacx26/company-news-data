# 7 Signs Your Nonprofit’s Technology Is Creating More Work Than It Saves

Nonprofit technology is supposed to make work easier, but sometimes organizations end up with the opposite result. A new platform solves an immediate problem, another tool gets added for a different team, and staff develop a few workarounds to keep everything moving. A few years later, there may be technology supporting nearly every part of the organization, yet staff are still spending a surprising amount of time on administrative work.


This time of year is particularly useful for taking a closer look. You have enough of the year behind you to know which processes are creating friction, and there’s still time to make improvements before year-end fundraising, events, communications, and reporting put more pressure on your team.


That doesn’t necessarily mean any one platform is the problem. Donations are processed, emails go out, volunteers get scheduled, events happen, and reports get finished. The more useful questions are how much staff effort it takes to make all of those things happen and how confident you are in the information you’re working from.


When supporter and organizational data lives across multiple systems, staff may need to check which information is current before they can even begin using it. Which email address is right? Is that donor total current? Did this person also volunteer or attend an event? Which report should leadership trust? Without a reliable source of truth, verifying information becomes another task added to the team’s workload.


At Giveffect, we spend a lot of time talking with nonprofit teams about the technology they use every day. One thing we’ve learned is that the biggest frustrations aren’t always tied to a missing feature. Often, they’re found in the work happening between systems: moving information, checking records, managing handoffs, recreating processes, and making sure the right people know what happened somewhere else.


That’s a big part of why Giveffect was built as a connected nonprofit system. When fundraising, volunteer management, events, communications, payments, reporting, and supporter activity are connected, staff don’t have to spend as much time holding those pieces together themselves. They also have a more complete source of information to work from. The value isn’t simply having fewer tools. It’s having the different parts of your organization work together.


Before the busiest fundraising months of the year arrive, it’s worth finding out whether yours do.


**Quick self-assessment**


## Is Your Technology Actually Saving Your Team Time?


Read each statement below. Give yourself **one point** every time your answer is yes. Keep a mental tally, then check your score at the end.


1.


We regularly copy or move information from one system to another.


2.


We sometimes check multiple places before we’re confident information is current.


3.


Someone on our team has a spreadsheet or process that only they fully understand.


4.


Preparing a campaign, event, or report often means digging up work from the last one.


5.


Staff have to remember routine follow-up steps that could easily fall through the cracks.


6.


We have processes that involve more people or handoffs than they probably should.


7.


When donor, volunteer, event, or program activity increases, the administrative workload


increases right along with it.


8,


We’ve caught ourselves saying, “That’s just how we’ve always done it,” about a fairly


cumbersome process.


###


### How did you score?


**0–2 points**


Your technology and processes are probably doing a pretty good job of supporting your team. As you prepare for year-end, pay attention to whether that holds true when activity increases.


**3–5 points**


There are likely a few places where manual work has quietly become part of the routine. Now is a good time to look at those processes before year-end adds more volume.


**6–8 points**


Your team may be spending a meaningful amount of time compensating for systems and workflows that aren’t doing enough of the administrative work for you. Addressing even one or two of those areas now could make a noticeable difference during year-end.


The score isn’t meant to be scientific. It’s simply a way to notice the small tasks that are easy to accept individually but can add up to a surprising amount of staff time.


## 1. Your staff has become the connection between your systems


Think about what happens after someone makes a donation. Beyond processing the gift, what does your team have to do? Someone may need to update a record, add the donor to a list, notify another staff member, create a follow-up task, or make sure the donor receives the right communication.


Now consider what happens when gift volume increases during year-end. If each gift brings several manual steps with it, all of those steps increase too. A process that feels manageable when ten gifts come in can become a very different experience when there are 100.


The same thing happens outside fundraising. A volunteer registers, chooses a shift, completes a waiver, receives instructions, logs hours, and eventually needs follow-up. An event attendee registers, receives communications, attends, and may need to be included in future outreach. There are good reasons for staff to be involved in these experiences, particularly when a personal response or decision is needed. The question is whether staff need to manually move the process forward at every stage.


You can often hear this happening in everyday conversations: “Did you add them?” “Can you send me that list?” “Did anyone follow up?” “Don’t forget to update that in the other system.” None of those requests sounds particularly serious, but multiply them across several departments and hundreds or thousands of supporter interactions, and your staff can end up functioning as the integration between systems.


This is where the difference between having multiple pieces of nonprofit technology and having a connected system becomes important. When a supporter registers for an event, volunteers, makes a gift, or engages with a communication in Giveffect, those activities can be connected to the same relationship. Staff have more context without having to continually carry information from one system to another.


## 2. Simple tasks have accumulated a surprising number of steps


Administrative work often grows gradually, which makes it easy to miss. Consider something as routine as sending an email about an upcoming campaign or event. Someone needs the right audience, so they request a list. Another person pulls it. The list gets reviewed, someone realizes another group should be included, and an additional list is pulled. The final audience gets uploaded into the email platform. After the email goes out, engagement information may need to make its way back into another system.


No individual part of that process is especially difficult. That’s exactly why processes like this can survive for years without anyone stopping to question them. Five-minute and ten-minute tasks repeated every week across fundraising, volunteer management, events, communications, finance, and operations can consume a meaningful amount of staff capacity.


Year-end tends to expose this kind of friction because everything happens more frequently. There are more communications, more gifts, more segments, more follow-up, and eventually more reporting. A process with several unnecessary steps doesn’t become easier simply because your team has less time to complete it.


A useful way to spot these processes is to listen for the phrase **“and then.”** Ask someone to explain how a routine task gets done from beginning to end. If the explanation contains a long string of “and then I…” steps, take a closer look. Not every step should disappear, but you may find handoffs that exist simply because that’s how the process evolved.


## 3. Your staff is verifying data before they can use it


Sometimes the extra work isn’t entering information. It’s figuring out whether the information in front of you is the right information.


A staff member preparing donor outreach notices two different email addresses. Finance and development have slightly different totals. Someone asks for a supporter count, and the first question is, “Which system are we using?” Before anyone can act on the information, someone has
to investigate.


This is where disconnected data can become a staff-capacity problem. If different systems contain different pieces or versions of information, staff may develop their own ways of deciding what to trust. One platform becomes the place to check donations, another is considered more reliable for contact information, and a spreadsheet becomes the unofficial reference because someone keeps it updated manually.


The deeper issue of[nonprofit data silos](https://www.giveffect.com/nonprofit-resource-center/nonprofit-crm-data-silos/) deserves its own conversation. But from an operational standpoint, the important thing to notice is the labor they create. Every time someone has to compare records, confirm a number, or reconcile information before moving forward, that’s another step in the process.


It’s particularly worth addressing before year-end, when your team will be segmenting appeals, reviewing donor histories, tracking gifts, preparing stewardship outreach, and eventually reporting on campaign results. Staff should be able to spend that time using information, not determining whether they can trust it.


## 4. Your team keeps rebuilding work you’ve already done


Year-end fundraising shouldn’t feel like your organization is inventing year-end fundraising from scratch every November. Yet nonprofit teams often spend time tracking down last year’s emails, figuring out which segments were used, recreating campaign lists, looking for old plans, or trying to remember what follow-up happened and when.


This is a useful time to pull up last year’s year-end campaign and look beyond the results. What did your team have to recreate manually? Which parts were harder than they needed to be? What worked well but lives mostly in someone’s memory?


Ideally, recurring work should become easier over time. Your team learns what works, improves the process, and carries those improvements into the next campaign. When staff have to reconstruct the process each year, you’re losing some of that advantage. If that sounds familiar, we’ve also taken a closer look at[why nonprofit teams keep starting over with campaigns](https://www.giveffect.com/nonprofit-resource-center/nonprofit-campaigns-starting-over-crm-systems/) and what can make recurring work easier to repeat.


Thoughtful automation can help, and it doesn’t have to mean handing supporter relationships over to software. Some of the most useful automations are administrative: creating a task, updating a record, notifying a staff member, assigning a follow-up, or placing someone into the appropriate communication sequence. Technology handles the routine step so your team has more time for the part that benefits from a person.


## 5. Too much depends on someone remembering what happens next


A donor makes a particular type of gift, so someone knows they need to alert the development director. An event ends, and a staff member remembers to send the attendee list to someone else for follow-up. A volunteer reaches a certain milestone, and the volunteer manager makes a mental note to reach out.


These processes can work remarkably well, especially on small teams where people communicate frequently. The weakness appears when things get busy, someone is out of the office, or the volume of activity increases. All three are especially likely during year-end.


If an important next step depends on the right person noticing something at the right time and remembering what to do, the process has a built-in opportunity for something to fall through the cracks.


Technology can help without removing the human part of the process. A workflow might automatically create a task when a donor reaches a certain threshold, for example, while the actual outreach remains completely personal. There are plenty of opportunities to think strategically about[what nonprofits can automate after a gift](https://www.giveffect.com/nonprofit-resource-center/the-first-48-hours-after-a-donation/) while keeping the donor experience personal.


The goal isn’t to automate the relationship. It’s to make sure your team doesn’t miss the opportunity to have one.


## 6. Your technology works because certain people know how to make it work


Every nonprofit has people who know how things really get done. That’s valuable institutional knowledge, but it can also mask processes that are more fragile than they appear.


Think about your year-end fundraising campaign. If the person who normally manages it were unexpectedly out for two weeks in November, could someone else step in? Would they know which lists to use, which systems need to be updated, what happens automatically, and which steps have to be completed manually? Would they know about the spreadsheet that’s not mentioned in the official process but is actually essential?


The same issue appears in smaller ways. One person knows the trick for pulling a particular report. Someone else knows that three fields have to be updated before a workflow will run correctly. Another staff member has their own process for cleaning up registrations before importing them.


Those aren’t failures on the part of your staff. Usually, they’re evidence that your team has gotten very good at adapting to the technology available to them. The risk is that an important organizational process works because one person knows the workaround rather than because the process itself is easy to understand and repeat.


You want experienced staff to build deep knowledge about your donors, volunteers, programs, community, and mission. Their most valuable institutional knowledge shouldn’t be how to make three pieces of software cooperate.


## 7. Growth automatically creates more administrative work


Most nonprofits want more people engaging with their mission: more donors, more volunteers, more event participants, more program reach, and more community involvement. Year-end can give you a particularly clear view of whether your technology can support that growth.


If more donations automatically mean more manual acknowledgments, record updates, reconciliation, and follow-up tasks, fundraising success brings an operational burden alongside the additional revenue. If increased event or volunteer participation creates a similar increase in administrative work, there’s eventually a limit to how much activity your team can absorb without adding capacity.


Some work should increase as your organization grows. More donors create more relationships to steward, and that’s work you *want* your development team doing. More volunteers mean more people to engage and support. Technology isn’t going to make that disappear.


What it can do is prevent routine administrative work from growing at exactly the same rate as activity. If year-end giving increases by 30%, your team shouldn’t necessarily have 30% more data entry, list management, internal notifications, or routine follow-up to complete.


This is one of the reasons Giveffect takes a connected approach. Growth in fundraising, volunteer participation, events, or communications shouldn’t automatically create the same growth in administrative work. When those activities are connected, your team can spend more of its additional capacity responding to the people engaging with your mission rather than managing the technology behind those interactions.


## The issue isn’t necessarily how many tools you have


When technology starts creating extra work, it’s tempting to assume the organization simply has too many systems. Sometimes consolidation is the right answer, but the number of tools alone doesn’t tell you much.


A specialized platform may be exactly what a particular program needs. The better question is how well work and information move through the organization as a whole. Can a routine process continue without someone manually pushing it forward at every stage? Can your team repeat a successful campaign without rebuilding the process? Can another staff member understand what needs to happen? Can people trust the information they’re working from? Can your organization increase activity without administrative work increasing at the same rate?


Those are useful questions at any point in the year. Right now, they’re especially practical because there’s still time to address some of the answers before year-end puts your processes under more pressure.


## The real cost is staff capacity


Software costs are easy to identify because they appear directly in the budget. The cost of administrative work is much harder to see because it’s distributed across people’s days in small increments.


Maybe someone spends fifteen minutes cleaning up a list before every email send. Another staff member takes half an hour to recreate something from the previous campaign. Someone else regularly checks whether a task was completed, compares records before trusting a number, or enters the same information somewhere else. None of those activities feels significant enough on its own to trigger a technology conversation.


Over the course of a week, month, or year, though, they represent real staff capacity. During year-end, when your team is already balancing campaigns, stewardship, events, reporting, and everyday responsibilities, that capacity becomes particularly valuable.


This is why we encourage nonprofits to look beyond the individual price of each piece of
technology. Several lower-cost systems may look economical when they’re evaluated separately,
but that calculation changes if staff spend hours moving information between them, checking
data, managing handoffs, maintaining separate processes, and filling the gaps. Understanding[what your nonprofit technology is really costing you](https://www.giveffect.com/nonprofit-resource-center/nonprofit-technology-roi/) means accounting for staff capacity alongside subscription fees.


At Giveffect, we think the value of connected technology is partly found in the work your team no longer has to do. When fundraising, volunteer management, events, communications, payments, reporting, and supporter activity work together, you’re reducing the administrative work required to connect those parts of your organization yourself while giving your team a more reliable source of information to work from.


That doesn’t mean every manual task needs to disappear. Some work is worth a person’s time because it requires judgment, creativity, expertise, or a relationship. If an experienced development professional is spending an hour preparing and verifying information before they can begin donor outreach, the real question isn’t exactly what that hour costs. It’s what that person could have been doing instead.


That’s where the cost of inefficient technology starts to matter.


## Before year-end, look at the work


If some of these signs sound familiar, you don’t need to immediately start looking for replacement software. A better first step is to examine one or two processes that feel more cumbersome than they should.


Choose something you know your team will do frequently between now and December, such as processing and acknowledging a donation, preparing an appeal, segmenting supporters, following up with event attendees, onboarding seasonal volunteers, or producing campaign reports. Then map what *actually* happens from beginning to end, not what the process is supposed to look like.


Look at who gets involved, which systems they open, what they have to remember, where information gets copied or moved, when someone has to wait for another person, and where they stop to verify that the information they’re using is accurate.


### Ask four questions about every step:


**1.** Does a person need to do this?


**2.** Does it need to happen this way?


**3.** Could our technology handle more of it?


**4.** Do we trust the information we’re using to make this decision?


You don’t need to redesign your entire technology stack before GivingTuesday. Even removing one recurring handoff, cleaning up one unreliable process, or automating one routine task can give your team valuable time back when the pace picks up. ## Frequently Asked Questions About Nonprofit Technology and Year-End Readiness


### Why should nonprofits review their technology before year-end?


Year-end often brings higher volumes of donations, communications, supporter activity, and reporting. Manual processes that feel manageable during quieter periods can become much more time-consuming as that volume increases.


Reviewing workflows before year-end gives nonprofit teams an opportunity to identify unnecessary steps, confirm where reliable data lives, and address repetitive work before the busiest fundraising months arrive.


### How can nonprofit technology reduce staff workload?


Nonprofit technology can reduce workload by handling repetitive administrative tasks, connecting information across functions, making recurring processes easier to repeat, and reducing manual handoffs between staff and systems. This might include automatically creating follow-up tasks, updating records, sending routine confirmations, notifying staff, or
triggering the next step in a workflow.


The goal isn’t to automate the relationship. It’s to reduce the administrative work surrounding it so staff have more time for work that benefits from their expertise.


### What is a single source of truth for a nonprofit?


A single source of truth gives your team a reliable, shared place for the information it uses to understand supporters and make decisions. Instead of different departments maintaining competing versions of the same information, staff can work from connected, current data.


For nonprofits, this is particularly valuable during year-end when teams are segmenting supporters, personalizing outreach, tracking gifts, and evaluating campaign performance.


### What nonprofit tasks can be automated before year-end?


Good candidates for automation are usually frequent, predictable tasks that don’t require much human judgment. Examples include gift acknowledgments, internal notifications, task creation, record updates, event or volunteer reminders, recurring reports, and certain types of supporter segmentation and follow-up.


A useful place to start is to listen for someone on your team saying, “Every time this happens, I have to…” What comes next is often worth examining.


### How do connected nonprofit systems reduce manual work?


Connected systems reduce manual work by allowing information from fundraising, volunteer management, events, communications, payments, and other activities to work together rather than requiring staff to continually transfer, verify, or reconcile it.


For example, if someone volunteers, attends an event, and later makes a donation, a connected system can associate those activities with the same supporter relationship. Staff get useful context without having to manually reconstruct that history.


### Can automation make year-end fundraising feel impersonal?


It can if it’s used indiscriminately, but automation and personalization aren’t opposites. Technology can handle routine administrative steps while helping your team identify moments that deserve personal attention.


For example, a system might automatically alert a development officer when a longtime volunteer makes a significant first-time gift during a year-end campaign. The notification is automated. The development officer’s outreach is personal.


### How should a nonprofit evaluate whether its technology is working?


Look beyond the feature list and examine how much staff effort is required to complete everyday work and how much confidence staff have in the information they’re using. Pay attention to how many manual steps common processes require, how often people have to verify information across systems, whether processes depend heavily on one person’s knowledge, and how much additional administration is created as activity increases.


Technology is doing its job when it helps your team execute good processes consistently and gives people reliable information to act on without requiring staff to constantly hold everything together.


## Give your team more room for the work that matters at year-end


Year-end will always be busy. That’s not necessarily a problem. For many nonprofits, it’s an exciting time when supporters are paying attention, generosity increases, and teams have more opportunities to deepen relationships around the mission.


The question is how much of that busy season your staff spends on work that actually needs them.


When routine tasks can happen reliably and people can trust the information in front of them, staff have more capacity for donor conversations, thoughtful stewardship, volunteer engagement, storytelling, decision-making, and all of the other work that deserves a human touch.


So before the pace picks up, don’t look only at everything your team needs to accomplish between now and December. Look at **how the work will get done and how much effort it takes to trust the information behind it.**
