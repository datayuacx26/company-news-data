# Physical Security Trends in 2026: AI & What's Changing

The physical security trends gaining traction in 2026 share a common thread: closing the gap between what security infrastructure captures and what it actually understands.


Enterprises have invested heavily in cameras, access control systems, and trained staff. Yet operators still discover incidents after they happen, investigations still require hours of manual review, and teams still struggle to distinguish genuine threats from routine activity. The technology captures everything but interprets nothing.


This disconnect is driving the physical security trends that matter most this year. Security leaders aren't asking for more sensors or additional feeds; they're demanding intelligence that turns raw data into actionable insight before incidents escalate. Here's what's shaping their decisions.


## **Security AI Faces ROI Accountability**


[Forrester Research](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forrester-tech-security-2026-predictions/) forecasts that enterprises will defer a quarter of planned AI spending to 2027 as fewer than one-third of decision-makers can currently tie AI spending to financial growth. This creates tension between market momentum pushing toward AI-powered security and executive scrutiny demanding measurable return on investment.


For physical security leaders, this means CFOs will require documented operational improvements before approving deployments. Vague promises about transformation won't secure budget approvals. Security leaders must demonstrate quantifiable metrics: false alarm reduction percentages, hours recovered from manual verification, improvements in investigation time, and operator turnover rates.


Organizations that succeed demonstrate clear operational improvements rather than promising transformation through technology alone. Security operations that can't connect AI investments to specific cost savings or efficiency gains may find themselves waiting until 2027.


## **Security Infrastructure Splits Between Edge and Cloud**


Security leaders planning infrastructure upgrades face a critical architecture decision: where AI processing happens. According to the Security Industry Association, the market is standardizing around edge processing, with a growing majority of network camera and recorder revenue expected to come from devices with deep-learning analytics onboard by 2027. The decision isn't purely technical.


The shift toward edge processing reflects several factors. Bandwidth constraints make streaming thousands of cameras to central servers impractical, while processing on dedicated edge appliances eliminates raw footage backhaul. Pre-trained models for object detection are widely available and vendor-optimized, and purpose-built hardware for security environments removes the need for adapted processors from other industries.


This doesn't eliminate cloud processing. Edge keeps video data on-premises for regulated industries. Cloud enables centralized management across distributed sites. The trade-off is control versus connectivity requirements. For operations managing thousands of cameras across multiple facilities, this decision affects processing speed, network load, and data governance.


## **Behavioral Detection Outperforms Traditional Object Recognition**


Traditional object detection sees someone in a restricted area. It flags a person near a door. It identifies a vehicle in the parking lot. What it can't do is answer the question that actually matters: is this routine activity or a genuine threat?


Behavioral analysis separates advanced security incident detection from simple object recognition. Someone waiting for a colleague moves differently from someone casing a facility for intrusion. They exhibit different spatial patterns, posture, and trajectories. Reading this context distinguishes systems that reduce false alarms from systems that generate noise.


The most effective behavioral systems detect precursor patterns before situations escalate (like individuals loitering near restricted areas, escalating confrontations, unusual movement toward entry points, or people running from a specific location). These early warning signals create intervention windows that pure object detection cannot provide.


The challenge for security leaders planning 2026 infrastructure is straightforward. Traditional motion-based and object detection systems cannot read behavioral context. Physical access control systems log badge swipes but can't identify tailgating. Cameras record door entries but can't distinguish authorized dual entry from unauthorized following. Without systems that understand behavioral patterns, operations remain stuck manually verifying every alert.


## **AI Relieves Security Staffing Crisis**


Security teams struggle to maintain adequate staffing. Operators leave positions after months of clearing repetitive false alarms from access control systems and motion sensors. Operators want to do security work, not spend shifts validating cleaning crews opening doors or reviewing footage of authorized personnel moving through facilities.


This staffing challenge compounds operational costs. Organizations invest in camera infrastructure and access control systems but cannot staff monitoring operations at scale. A single analyst cannot effectively watch hundreds of camera feeds simultaneously. Teams attempt to divide monitoring responsibilities across shifts, but gaps in coverage persist because qualified operators are difficult to recruit and retain.


The traditional approach of adding more analysts to handle growing alert volumes creates unsustainable cost structures. Guards are expensive and high-churn. SOC operators are underutilized, forced to sift through noise instead of acting on real insight. Each additional hire represents salary, benefits, and training investments without addressing the underlying problem: systems generate overwhelming volumes of alerts that require manual verification.


Platforms that automatically verify routine activity against video context change this operational model. When systems distinguish between authorized personnel and genuine security events, analysts focus on threats requiring human judgment rather than administrative validation. This reduces the repetitive work driving turnover while improving threat detection capacity.


## **Regulatory Compliance Shapes Technology Decisions**


Physical security systems using facial recognition and biometric access control face increasingly complex privacy regulations.


The[Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA)](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=3004&ChapterID=57) imposes strict biometric privacy requirements, mandating written consent before collecting biometric identifiers and providing a private right of action that allows individuals to sue directly without proving actual harm. Penalties can reach $1,000 per negligent violation and $5,000 per intentional violation.


Demographic bias adds operational and legal risk.[NIST Face Recognition Vendor Test](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2019/12/nist-study-evaluates-effects-race-age-sex-face-recognition-software) documentation reveals higher false positive rates for women, elderly individuals, and people with darker skin tones. These disparities create compliance exposure under FTC unfair practices prohibitions and civil rights laws.


The[Federal Trade Commission issued warnings about biometric misuse in May 2023](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ftc-warns-about-misuses-biometric-information-harm-consumers) , requiring organizations to implement bias testing and substantiate accuracy claims. For security leaders planning 2026 deployments, compliance requirements now shape technology selection as much as operational capability.
