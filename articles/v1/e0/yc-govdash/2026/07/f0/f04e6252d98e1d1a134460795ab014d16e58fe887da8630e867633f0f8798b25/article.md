# Fixed Price Contract Guide: Everything You Need to Know in May 2026

Every[firm fixed price contract](https://www.govdash.com/glossary/firm-fixed-price-(ffp)-contract) you sign locks in one number before work starts, and that number holds no matter what the project actually costs you to deliver. The government gets budget certainty, and you keep whatever margin survives after execution. That trade works when scope is tight and requirements are stable, but it strains fast when change orders pile up, site conditions don't match surveys, or material prices move against you mid-performance.


**TLDR:**


- Fixed price contracts set one price before work begins; contractors absorb all cost overruns while keeping any savings.


- Four FAR contract types exist: FFP places all risk on contractors, FPIF adds performance incentives, FPEPA adjusts for inflation, and FPLOE caps hours instead of outcomes.


- Cost plus contracts reverse risk to the government and require full expense transparency, fitting R&D better than defined-scope work.


- Scope creep drives most disputes; written change order processes and contemporaneous documentation prevent costly claims later.

## What Is a Fixed Price Contract


A fixed price contract sets one agreed-upon price before work begins, and that price holds regardless of what the work actually costs to complete.


The contractor absorbs the financial risk. Cost overruns come out of their margin; savings stay in it. The buyer gets spending certainty from day one with no surprise invoices at the end.


That risk trade is the defining feature of this contract structure. Whether you are building infrastructure, delivering a software project, or bidding on a government solicitation, the same principle applies.


## How Fixed Price Contracts Work


The process starts with scope. Before a price can be fixed, both parties align on a detailed statement of work that defines deliverables, timelines, and quality standards. A vague scope is where disputes are born.


The contractor then builds an internal cost model and adds overhead and profit margin that reflects the uncertainty they are accepting. Once both parties sign, that number holds regardless of the actual execution costs.


During performance, the buyer has no visibility into the contractor's actual costs. Internal variances are the contractor's to manage privately. Whether true costs run higher or lower, the invoice stays the same.


Pricing in a fixed price bid typically includes a risk buffer. If the scope is well-defined, that premium stays reasonable. If it isn't, both parties tend to regret it.


## Types of Fixed Price Contracts


Not every fixed price contract works the same way. Under FAR, four distinct variations exist, each suited to different levels of cost uncertainty and administrative complexity.


Type Best For Risk Allocation


Firm Fixed Price (FFP) Well-defined scope with stable requirements Contractor holds all cost risk


Fixed Price Incentive Fee (FPIF) Known cost uncertainty with measurable performance targets Shared; contractor earns additional fee for hitting targets


Fixed Price with Economic Price Adjustment (FPEPA) Multi-year contracts exposed to labor or material cost volatility Limited price adjustments tied to specific indices or actual costs


Fixed Price Level of Effort (FPLOE) Research or exploration where deliverables can't be precisely defined Contractor commits to a set number of hours, not a specific outcome


[FFP](https://www.govdash.com/glossary/firm-fixed-price-(ffp)-contract) dominates federal contracting because it favors clear, stable work. FPIF adds an incentive layer without abandoning price discipline, which is useful when buyers want to reward cost control. FPEPA handles the reality of long-term contracts where market changes can erode contractor margins. FPLOE fits exploratory work where outputs are uncertain but labor investment is not.


## Fixed Price Contract vs Cost Plus Contract


Under a[cost plus contract](https://www.govdash.com/blog/cost-reimbursement-contract-guide) , the government reimburses a contractor's allowable actual costs and pays a separate fee on top. Cost risk moves entirely to the buyer. Contractors submit real expense invoices, and contracting officers review them for compliance under DCAA standards.


That reversal from fixed price is the core distinction. Fixed price rewards cost control since savings stay with the contractor.[Cost plus](https://www.govdash.com/glossary/cost-plus-fixed-fee-contract-cpff) removes that incentive but fits work where scope genuinely cannot be defined upfront, such as R&D or early-stage systems development.

Exploratory or high-uncertainty work typically points toward cost plus structures like CPFF, where the government accepts financial exposure in exchange for contractor flexibility.


## Fixed Price Contract vs Lump Sum Contract


A lump sum contract and a fixed price contract describe the same core arrangement: one price, agreed before work starts, covering the full project scope. The two terms exist because construction and federal procurement developed their vocabulary independently. Construction trades settled on "lump sum" decades ago; the FAR codified "firm fixed price" for federal use. Same mechanics, different industries, different words.


In construction, "lump sum" is the standard term. In federal contracting, the FAR uses "firm fixed price" for the same structure. Different words, same mechanics, different industries.


"Lump sum fixed price contract" is not a third distinct type. It simply reinforces that the agreed amount covers total project cost as a single figure, with no cost breakdowns required from the contractor after award.


## Fixed Price Contracts in Construction


Construction introduces risks that stress a fixed price structure from day one. Scope changes, surprise site conditions, and material price swings can all erode contractor margins faster than any cost model anticipated.


The financial stakes reflect this reality. According to the[2025 Arcadis Construction Disputes Report](https://media.arcadis.com/-/media/project/arcadiscom/com/expertise/global/contract-solutions/2025/2025-15th-annual-construction-disputes-report-final-19jun25.pdf) , the average value of a construction dispute in North America reached $60.1 million, up from $43.0 million the prior year.


Three risks drive most of those disputes:


- Change orders without formal pricing add-ons get absorbed as contractor costs, quietly cutting into margins with no contractual path to recovery.


- Unforeseen subsurface or site conditions invalidate the original scope assumptions, leaving contractors exposed when the ground doesn't match the survey.


- Multi-year projects face material price volatility that a fixed price simply won't absorb, particularly in periods of supply chain disruption.


Managing these risks starts with contract language. Strong change order clauses, differing site conditions provisions, and clearly defined scope boundaries are what separate a well-structured fixed price construction contract from one headed for litigation.


## Fixed Price Contracts in Government Contracting


Fixed price contracts are among the most widely used contract structures in federal procurement. Under the[Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR)](https://www.govdash.com/glossary/federal-acquisition-regulation-far) , they fall under FAR Part 16.2 and are favored when the government can define its requirements clearly enough for contractors to price work with reasonable confidence.


In government contracting, a fixed price contract places most financial risk on the contractor. If costs run over, the contractor absorbs the difference. This arrangement gives agencies cost predictability and makes contractors accountable for their own cost management.


Contracting officers are required under FAR 16.104 to consider several factors when selecting a contract type, including price competition, urgency, contractor risk, and the complexity of requirements. Fixed price is preferred when the government has mature requirements, can write a clear statement of work, and can reasonably assess whether delivered work meets the standard. For commodity purchases, standard commercial services, and well-defined IT deliverables, FFP is typically the default.


From a contractor's perspective, winning on a fixed price vehicle means pricing discipline starts at proposal time. Unrealistic bids that win on price but lose on execution create cost overruns the contractor must absorb, which is why internal cost modeling and historical pricing data matter as much as technical approach. Contracting officers can also request cost or pricing data under the Truth in Negotiations Act (TINA) when a contract exceeds the applicable threshold, adding a layer of pre-award scrutiny to the pricing behind the fixed number.


Post-award, fixed price contracts carry less administrative burden than cost-reimbursement vehicles. There is no requirement to submit incurred cost reports or undergo DCAA cost audits on individual contract performance. That simplicity benefits both the agency and the contractor, but it also means the government has limited recourse if a contractor underestimates costs and starts cutting corners to preserve margin.


## Advantages and Disadvantages of Fixed Price Contracts


Fixed price works when scope is stable and requirements are clear enough to price with confidence. Buyers get spending certainty, and contractors keep every dollar saved through efficient execution, which creates a real incentive to manage costs well. That accountability is the structure's core strength.


The arrangement strains under uncertainty. Shifting requirements or surprise conditions during performance push risk back toward the buyer through change orders, delays, or contractor default. What looked like price certainty on paper can become a dispute in practice.


The honest test is straightforward: can you define what you're buying precisely enough for a contractor to price it responsibly? If yes, fixed price holds. If not, you're moving risk around without actually managing it.


## Common Fixed Price Contract Disputes and How to Avoid Them


Scope creep sits at the center of most fixed price contract disputes.[Scope changes remain the leading cause](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/scope-changes-still-top-list-of-construction-dispute-causes/588695/) of construction disputes industry-wide. When project requirements shift after contract signing, contractors face a hard choice: absorb the added cost or fight for a contract modification. Neither outcome is painless.


A few practices reduce that risk considerably:


- Document scope boundaries in precise, unambiguous language before signing. Vague deliverable descriptions are where disputes are born.


- Build a formal change order process into the contract so any scope additions require written approval and repricing.


- Keep contemporaneous records throughout performance. If a dispute does arise, documented communications and decisions carry far more weight than memory.


On government contracts, disputes often stem from differing interpretations of FAR clauses, especially around what qualifies as a government-caused delay versus contractor underperformance. Understanding the distinctions between contract types, like[cost plus fixed fee contracts](https://www.govdash.com/blog/cost-plus-fixed-fee-contract-guide) , helps prevent such misunderstandings. Contractors should review the "Changes" and "Disputes" clauses carefully before award.

## FAQ


### Fixed price contract vs cost plus: which should I use for government bids?


Use fixed price when you can define scope precisely and price the work confidently; it gives agencies budget certainty and you keep the savings if you execute efficiently. Cost plus fits R&D or exploratory work where requirements are too uncertain to price upfront; the government pays actual costs plus a fee, which transfers financial risk away from you but adds DCAA oversight.


### What are the main risks in fixed price construction contracts?


The three biggest risks are change orders that erode margin without formal repricing, unforeseen site conditions that invalidate your original scope assumptions, and material price volatility on multi-year projects that a fixed price won't absorb. Strong contract language around change order procedures, differing site conditions clauses, and clearly defined scope boundaries reduce exposure before you sign.


### How do I avoid scope creep disputes on a fixed price contract?


Document scope boundaries in precise language before signing, build a formal change order process that requires written approval and repricing for any additions, and keep contemporaneous records throughout performance. On government contracts, track every government-directed change or delay in writing; if a dispute arises, documented communications carry far more weight than memory.


### What's the difference between firm fixed price and fixed price incentive fee?


Firm fixed price (FFP) locks in one price with no adjustments; the contractor holds all cost risk and keeps all savings. Fixed price incentive fee (FPIF) adds a performance-based fee structure where you earn additional profit for hitting cost or schedule targets, sharing some risk with the government while maintaining overall price discipline.


### Can a fixed price contract adjust for cost changes during performance?


Only under specific contract types. A standard firm fixed price contract does not adjust; that's the point. Fixed Price with Economic Price Adjustment (FPEPA) allows limited price changes tied to labor or material cost indices on multi-year contracts, but the adjustment mechanism must be defined in the contract before award.
