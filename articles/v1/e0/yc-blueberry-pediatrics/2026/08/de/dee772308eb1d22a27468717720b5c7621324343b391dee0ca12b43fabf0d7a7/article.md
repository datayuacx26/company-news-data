# When Should Kids Get the Flu Shot? A Pediatrician's Timing Guide (2026-27)

Last medically reviewed: 2026-08-08


For most kids, the sweet spot is late September through October. The[American Academy of Pediatrics](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/flu/Pages/which-flu-vaccine-should-my-child-get-this-year.aspx) recommends a yearly flu shot for every child 6 months and older, with both doses done by October 31 if this is a first flu season (two doses, four weeks apart). If you miss October, still get it.[Flu often circulates into spring](https://blueberrypediatrics.com/health-tips/navigating-the-flu) .


Every September in our telehealth visits, we hear the same question: "Are we early, or are we already behind?" The honest answer is reassuring for almost every family. Below, a pediatrician walks you through the exact flu vaccine timing for kids, the two-dose rule most parents don't know about, and what to do if you missed the October window.

## The Short Answer: When Should Kids Get the Flu Shot?


The best time for kids to get a flu shot in 2026 is late September through the end of October. The[Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP)](https://www.cdc.gov/acip-recs/hcp/vaccine-specific/flu.html) says vaccination should ideally be offered in September or October, and everyone 6 months and older should be vaccinated by the end of October.


- **Not too early, not too late.** For a child who only needs one dose, July or August is not preferred, since the immune boost can wane before flu peaks in December through February. But if October comes and goes, do not skip. A November, December, or even January shot still protects.
- **First-time vaccinees are the exception.** They need two doses, four weeks apart, so dose one should be as soon as vaccine is available, usually early to mid September.
- **Same-visit is fine.** The flu vaccine can go in the same visit as the RSV antibody nirsevimab, the COVID vaccine, and routine childhood vaccines.


## When Is the 2026-27 Flu Vaccine Available?


Most pediatricians and pharmacies begin stocking the 2026-27 flu vaccine in August, and supply is usually broad by early September. If you call your pediatrician's office in mid August and vaccine has not landed, ask when the first shipment is expected, and the office can usually give you a date.


For most kids, August is possible but not preferred: a single-dose child risks the immune boost waning before flu peaks, and a first-time vaccinee is better off starting in early September. Retail pharmacies routinely give the flu shot to kids 3 and up (age minimums vary by state and chain); for babies and toddlers under 3, the pediatrician's office is almost always the right place.


## Does My Child Need One Dose or Two? The Two-Dose Rule Most Parents Miss


This is the piece of flu-vaccine timing that catches families off guard.


**The rule, in plain language:** Children 6 months through 8 years need two doses this season if they have not previously received at least two total lifetime doses before July 1. Everyone 9 and older gets one dose, regardless of history.


**Decision box: Has your child ever had at least two flu shots in their life?**


- **Yes:** One dose this year.
- **No, or not sure:** Two doses this year, four weeks apart.
- **Age 9 and up:** One dose, regardless of history.


The two lifetime doses do not need to be in the same season or in back-to-back years. What matters is that a child has been "primed" with two doses of any flu vaccine at some point, at least four weeks apart, before July 1 of this year.


Edge case: a child who is 8 at their first dose and turns 9 before dose two is due should still get both doses, because the rule is based on age at dose one.


### The Scheduling Math for First-Time Vaccinees


If your child needs two doses this year, the timing works out like this:


- Dose two must be given at least 28 days after dose one.
- To have both done by October 31, dose one needs to be no later than the last week of September.
- That is why our pediatricians tell first-time families to book dose one in early to mid September, which gives comfortable margin.


For a baby turning 6 months mid-season (November or December), you do not wait for next year. Dose one at the 6-month visit, dose two four weeks later, and they are protected for the second half of the season.


## When Can a Baby Get the Flu Shot?


The earliest age is 6 months. Every child 6 months and older without a medical contraindication should get an annual flu vaccine. That is the standing recommendation from the American Academy of Pediatrics and the federal ACIP.


Below 6 months, babies cannot receive the flu vaccine. Two layers of protection matter:


- **Vaccination in pregnancy.** A pregnant person can safely get the flu shot in any trimester; antibodies cross the placenta and protect the baby for the first few months of life.
- [Cocooning.](https://blueberrypediatrics.com/health-tips/how-to-keep-babies-and-toddlers-safe-from-rsv-flu-and-winter-germs-tips-from-a-pediatrician) Vaccinating everyone in the household, plus regular caregivers and grandparents, reduces the odds that flu reaches the baby. The[AAP HealthyChildren page on protecting babies from flu](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/flu/Pages/protecting-babies-and-young-children-from-flu-what-parents-should-know.aspx) walks through the same playbook.


A note that trips up new parents: the RSV antibody (nirsevimab, brand name Beyfortus) protects against RSV, not flu. For flu, the shot at 6 months is your baby's first real defense.


## Can My Child Get the Flu Shot With Other Vaccines?


Yes. This is one of the most common September telehealth questions. The flu vaccine can be given at the same visit as the RSV antibody (nirsevimab), the COVID vaccine, and routine childhood immunizations. Injectable vaccines go in separate anatomic sites, at least an inch apart.


Nirsevimab is a monoclonal antibody, not a vaccine. It goes in a separate syringe at a separate site; it cannot be mixed with the flu vaccine, but it does not need to be spaced apart. Safety and side effects match giving each alone.


For babies under 8 months at the start of RSV season, the fall visit is often where both protections happen: one trip, one appointment, two layers of defense.


## Shot vs. Nasal Spray: Which Is Better for Kids?


Both are acceptable, and neither is preferred over the other when both are age-appropriate. Pick what your pediatrician has in stock and what your child tolerates best.


- **Age matters for the nasal spray.** FluMist, the live-attenuated nasal spray, is only approved for ages 2 through 49. Under 2, the shot is the only option.
- **Not everyone can get the nasal spray.** It is not for pregnant people, kids with certain immune conditions, and a few other groups spelled out in the ACIP guidance. Your pediatrician will tell you if the spray is not an option for your child.
- **Same strains in both.** The shot and the spray protect against the same 2026-27 strains this year.


## What About Egg Allergy?


Egg allergy is a much smaller deal than parents expect, and a lot of families are still working from older, stricter advice. Any child with an egg allergy, mild or severe, can receive any age-appropriate flu vaccine (egg-based or egg-free) with no special precautions beyond those for any vaccine: no egg-free clinic, no observation period, no allergist visit required just because of egg. This matches[current AAP guidance on which flu vaccine children should get](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/flu/Pages/which-flu-vaccine-should-my-child-get-this-year.aspx) .


Egg allergy is not the same as a prior flu-vaccine reaction


If your child has ever had a severe allergic reaction to a flu vaccine itself (not to egg), that is different. Talk to your pediatrician or an allergist before this year's shot. A history of anaphylaxis to a previous flu vaccine is a contraindication.


Keep those two things separate: egg allergy, get the shot. Prior severe reaction to a flu vaccine, call your pediatrician first.


## What's New in the 2026-27 Flu Vaccine?


All three strains in this year's vaccine were updated. On March 13, 2026, the FDA recommended changing all three components to better match a fast-drifting strain called H3N2 subclade K, which spread widely during the 2025-26 season. The details are in the[CDC's ACIP flu vaccine summary of recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/flu/media/pdfs/2025-2026-summary-of-recommendations.pdf) .


- **New strains, same schedule.** Dosing, timing, age, and safety guidance for this year are the same as last year. Only the composition changed.
- **All U.S. flu vaccines are trivalent this season.** They cover two influenza A strains plus one influenza B strain. The second B strain (B/Yamagata) that was in older four-component (quadrivalent) vaccines was dropped a few years ago and remains dropped.
- **Supply is expected to be adequate.** The FDA anticipates a sufficient and diverse supply for 2026-27.


**A note on the 2026 federal vaccine schedule.** You may have seen news this year about changes to the federal childhood immunization schedule. Blueberry Pediatrics follows the American Academy of Pediatrics, which continues to recommend a yearly flu shot for all children 6 months and older. If your pediatrician recommends the flu shot for your child this fall, that recommendation is based on the AAP's 2026 schedule. If you have questions about how the news affects your child, ask us.


### How Well Does It Work?


In a typical season, the flu vaccine reduces a child's risk of getting sick from flu by roughly 40 to 60 percent, and it is even better at preventing severe illness and hospitalization. The[CDC's mid-season interim estimate](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/wr/mm7432a2.htm) for children in the 2025-26 season showed the vaccine cut outpatient visits by 38 to 41 percent and reduced hospitalization by 41 percent.


Season-specific numbers for 2026-27 will not exist until well into the season. Even in a lower-effectiveness year, the vaccine still does the most important job: keeping kids out of the hospital.


## What if We Missed the End of October? Is It Too Late?


No. This is one of the most common late-fall questions our telehealth pediatricians get, and the honest answer is reassuring.


Flu commonly circulates through winter and into spring, with peaks anywhere from December through March. A shot given in November, December, or even January still protects during the months you need it most. The reason to aim for October is to have full protection built up before flu really starts moving, not because the vaccine "expires" after that date.


If you missed October, do this now. It takes about two weeks after the shot for full protection to kick in, and every week you wait is a week of exposure with no coverage.


## When to Call Your Pediatrician


Reach out to your pediatrician (or start a telehealth visit) if any of these apply:


- Your baby is turning 6 months this fall and you want to plan the two-dose series.
- Your child had a reaction to a flu vaccine in a previous year (not the same as an egg allergy).
- Your child has a chronic condition (asthma, heart or lung disease, immune conditions, neurologic conditions) and you want to talk through timing.
- You are pregnant and want to talk through timing of your own flu shot.
- You missed October and are not sure whether to still get one.
- You are not sure if this counts as your child's first flu season, or how many lifetime doses they have had.
- Your child is coming down with something this fall and you want to[tell the difference between a cold and the flu](https://blueberrypediatrics.com/health-tips/cold-or-flu) before deciding what to do next.

## How to Book a Flu Shot for Your Child


You have three places to book:


- **Your pediatrician's office.** Best for babies, toddlers, and any child with a chronic condition, because your child's full history is on file, and the office can slot the flu shot into a scheduled well visit.
- **A retail pharmacy.** For older kids and teens (typical minimum is age 3; varies by state and chain), pharmacies are convenient and often have same-week openings. Check with your pediatrician first if your child has a complex history.
- **Blueberry Pediatrics.** If you're a Blueberry family, message us. We can pull up your child's history, confirm whether this is a one-dose or two-dose year, and help you plan the fall visit.


Flu-shot timing sounds simple until you sit down with a 5-month-old who will be 6 months in November, or a first-grader who has had exactly one flu shot in their life. You do not have to figure it out on your own.
