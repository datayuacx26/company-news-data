# Is Your Child's MMR Up to Date? A Pre-Camp and Travel Checklist

Most kids are up to date if they have had both MMR doses: one after the first birthday, and one around age 4 to 6. Not sure where your child stands? You can check in just a few minutes. This guide walks you through it, plus the timing rules for summer camp and travel.


## Key Takeaways


- The MMR (measles, mumps, and rubella) vaccine is given in 2 doses: one at 12 to 15 months, and one at 4 to 6 years.
- Two doses protect about 97 out of 100 kids from measles. One dose protects about 93 out of 100.
- Most summer camps ask for proof of 2 MMR doses with dates. Aim to be fully vaccinated at least 2 weeks before camp starts.
- Babies 6 to 11 months old traveling abroad can get one early dose, then still need their 2 regular doses later.
- Babies under 6 months are too young for MMR. If measles is spreading where you plan to go, consider delaying the trip.
- The MMR vaccine does not cause autism. Many large studies have confirmed this.
- Lost the records? It is safe to get vaccinated again, or a doctor can order a blood test to check protection.

## What Is the MMR Vaccine, and Why Does It Matter Right Now?


The MMR vaccine protects your child against three viruses: measles, mumps, and rubella. It is one of the safest and most studied vaccines we have. Right now it matters more than usual.


Measles is spreading more this year. The CDC reported 2,170 confirmed measles cases in the U.S. so far in 2026, as of July 2. That is already close to the total for all of last year. At the same time, MMR coverage among kindergartners has slipped to about 9 in 10, below the roughly 95% needed to keep the virus from spreading easily.


That level of protection is called herd immunity. It means enough people are protected that a virus has a hard time spreading. When coverage drops, measles spreads faster. Summer camp and travel bring kids together from many places, so a quick vaccine check now is worth the few minutes it takes.


## How Many MMR Doses Does My Child Need?


Most children need 2 doses of MMR, spaced years apart. The first dose comes in the toddler years, and the second before kindergarten. Here is the routine schedule the CDC recommends.


Dose Recommended age What it does


Dose 1 12 to 15 months Builds first protection; about 93 out of 100 kids are protected


Dose 2 4 to 6 years Boosts protection to about 97 out of 100 kids


After both doses, most kids are protected for life. You do not need a booster later for measles. If your child is on this schedule and has both doses recorded, they are up to date. MMR is one of several routine childhood shots; for another example of how these decisions get made, see our guide to[the Hep B birth dose](https://www.blueberrypediatrics.com/health-tips/the-hep-b-birth-dose-decision-why-pediatricians-still-urge-the-universal-shot-2026-guidance) .


Two special situations change the timing: summer camp and travel. We cover both next.


## Does My Child Need the MMR Vaccine for Summer Camp?


Yes, in most cases. School-aged campers usually need proof of 2 MMR doses, with the dates each dose was given. Camps keep these records in case a child gets sick, so they can act fast.


If your child is not fully vaccinated yet, here is what the CDC recommends before camp:


1. Get 2 doses of MMR, given at least 28 days apart.
2. Finish both doses at least 2 weeks before camp starts, so protection has time to build.
3. Bring written records with dates. Camps do not accept a verbal "I'm pretty sure they had it."


If your child has had only one dose, they usually just need the second, given at least 28 days after the first. If camp is only a week or two away and your child is behind, call your pediatrician right away. They can help you build the fastest safe catch-up plan and tell you what the camp will accept.


## What About MMR Before Travel?


Travel, both international and to U.S. areas with active outbreaks, is a reason to check MMR early. Measles is common in many parts of the world, and travelers are a frequent way it enters new communities. The rules depend on your child's age.


- Babies 6 to 11 months: For international travel, they can get one early MMR dose. This early dose does not count toward the regular series, so they still need their 2 later doses (one at 12 to 15 months, one at 4 to 6 years).
- Children 12 months and older: They should have 2 doses given at least 28 days apart before the trip. The second dose can be given early, before the usual 4-to-6-year timing, to finish the series in time.
- Timing for everyone: Try to finish vaccination at least 2 weeks before you leave, so protection has time to build.


MMR is not for babies under 6 months


Babies younger than 6 months are too young for the MMR vaccine. If you are planning to travel to a place where measles is spreading, talk with your pediatrician about whether to delay the trip until your baby is old enough to be protected.


## How Do I Check If My Child's MMR Is Up to Date?


You can usually confirm your child's MMR status in a few minutes. Work through these steps in order until you find the answer:


1. Find the vaccine record. Look at the yellow immunization card from birth, the packet from your pediatrician, or the paperwork from a past school or camp form.
2. Check the state immunization portal. Most states keep an online immunization registry. Search your state's name plus "immunization information system" to find and request your child's record.
3. Look for 2 MMR dates. You want to see two doses: one on or after the first birthday, and one around age 4 to 6. If both are there, your child is up to date.
4. Call your pediatrician's office. If you cannot find the record, the office can pull the vaccine history and print or send a copy.
5. Ask about a titer if needed. If no record exists anywhere, a doctor can order a simple blood test, called a titer, to check whether your child is already protected.


A question our pediatricians hear all the time during camp and travel season is, "Can you look at a photo of the vaccine card and tell me if we're covered?" It is a smart question, and often a quick telemedicine visit is all it takes to sort out.


## What If My Child Is Behind or I Can't Find Records?


Do not worry. Being behind is common and easy to fix. Your pediatrician can start a catch-up schedule that gets your child protected as quickly as is safe.


If you cannot find any records at all, that is fine too. If there is no written proof that your child had MMR, it is safe to give the vaccine again. Getting an extra dose when you were already protected does not cause harm. As an alternative, a doctor can order a titer, the blood test that checks for existing protection, so your child may not need another shot.


The takeaway: a missing card is never a reason to skip protection. There is always a safe path forward.


## Is the MMR Vaccine Safe? Common Parent Questions


Yes. The MMR vaccine has been used for decades and studied in millions of children. It is one of the most closely watched vaccines in the world. Asking questions about it is normal and responsible, so let's walk through the common ones.


What are the side effects? Most kids have none, or only mild ones. These can include a sore arm, a mild fever, or a light rash a week or so after the shot. These are signs the body is building protection. Serious reactions are very rare.


Is the MMR vaccine linked to autism? No. The MMR vaccine does not cause autism. This has been studied many times, in large groups of children across many countries, and the answer is consistent. The one small 1998 study that suggested a link was found to be flawed and was formally retracted. The concern is understandable, and the evidence is clear.


Is it safe to get MMR again if I'm not sure my child had it? Yes. If you cannot confirm a past dose, getting vaccinated is safe. An extra dose does not cause harm.


Is there anyone who should not get MMR? For most children it is very safe, but a few should check with their doctor first because MMR is a live vaccine. That includes children with a weakened immune system, such as those on cancer treatment, high-dose steroids, or with certain immune conditions, teens who are pregnant, and children who recently received blood products such as a transfusion or IVIG, which can affect when the dose should be given. A child who had a severe allergic reaction to a past MMR dose or one of its ingredients should not get another without specialist guidance. If any of these situations apply, your pediatrician will map out the safest plan.


According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, the MMR vaccine does not cause autism, a conclusion supported by many large studies across multiple countries.
