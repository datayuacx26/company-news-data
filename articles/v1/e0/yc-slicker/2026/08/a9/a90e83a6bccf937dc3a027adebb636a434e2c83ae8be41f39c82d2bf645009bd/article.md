## What Gross Margin Means in Business


Gross margin tells you how much revenue remains after subtracting the direct costs of delivering your product or service. For subscription companies, those direct costs typically include hosting and infrastructure, customer success headcount tied to onboarding, payment processing fees, and third-party software embedded in the product.


The number matters because it sets a ceiling on everything else. You cannot spend more on sales, marketing, or R&D than your gross margin allows and still reach profitability. A subscription business with 60% gross margin has far more room to grow than one running at 40%, even if both show identical top-line revenue.


## The Gross Margin Formula


Gross margin is calculated by subtracting the cost of goods sold (COGS) from revenue, then dividing by revenue:


**Gross Margin % = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue × 100**


For a subscription company generating $1,000,000 in revenue with $300,000 in COGS, gross margin is 70%. That number tells you how much revenue remains to cover operating expenses, interest, and taxes before you reach net profit.


### What Counts as COGS for Subscription Businesses?


COGS in a subscription context is narrower than most finance teams expect. It includes only the direct costs of delivering the service:


- Hosting and infrastructure costs tied directly to service delivery
- Customer support headcount allocated to onboarding and ongoing service
- Payment processing fees charged per transaction
- Third-party software or API costs embedded in the product


Sales, marketing, R&D, and general administrative expenses sit below the gross margin line. Misclassifying those as COGS will artificially suppress your gross margin and distort benchmarking comparisons.


## Gross Margin vs. Gross Profit


Gross margin and gross profit measure the same underlying relationship between revenue and cost of goods sold (COGS), but they express it differently. Gross profit is the raw dollar figure left after subtracting COGS from revenue. Gross margin converts that number into a percentage of revenue, making it comparable across companies of different sizes.


For subscription businesses, the distinction matters because investors and operators almost always benchmark margin percentages, not absolute profit dollars.

## What COGS Includes for Subscription Companies


For subscription businesses, cost of goods sold (COGS) looks different than in traditional product companies. Instead of raw materials or manufacturing costs, COGS covers the direct costs of delivering your service to customers.


Common COGS line items include:


- Hosting and infrastructure costs (cloud compute, storage, CDN fees) that scale with your customer base
- Payment processing fees, including per-transaction charges from your payment processor
- Third-party software or API costs embedded in your product delivery
- Customer support costs directly tied to service delivery, such as onboarding and technical support headcount


Expenses like sales, marketing, R&D, and general administration are operating expenses, not COGS. Keeping this separation clean is what makes gross margin a reliable signal of unit economics before overhead.


## Gross Margin vs. Operating Margin vs. Net Margin


Gross margin stops at the cost of delivering your product. The two margins below it tell a fuller story about where revenue actually goes.


### The Three-Layer View


These three margins sit at different points on the income statement:


- Gross margin measures revenue minus cost of goods sold (COGS), showing how much is left to cover all other expenses.
- Operating margin subtracts operating expenses like sales, marketing, and R&D from gross profit, revealing how much the business earns from its core activities before interest and taxes.
- Net margin takes everything out, including interest, taxes, and one-time items, producing the final bottom-line percentage.


For subscription companies, gross margin tends to be the most stable of the three because it isolates unit economics from the spending choices a company makes on growth. Operating and net margins fluctuate far more with investment cycles, making gross margin the cleaner signal for assessing whether the underlying business model is sound.


## Why Gross Margin Matters More Than Revenue for Subscription Companies


Revenue is a vanity metric if the unit economics underneath it are broken. A subscription company growing at 40% annually can still be destroying value if its gross margin sits at 30%, because there is simply not enough left over after fulfilling each subscription to cover sales, marketing, product, or any path to profitability.


Gross margin tells you how much of each dollar of revenue survives the cost of delivering your product. For software-based subscription businesses, that surviving dollar funds everything else: customer acquisition, retention investment, and eventually, profit.


Investors and operators track this number for good reason. SaaS businesses typically carry gross margins between 70% and 85%, while usage-heavy or service-heavy subscription models often run closer to 40% to 60%. A company at the low end of that range needs dramatically more revenue to reach the same operating performance as one at the high end.


The implication for subscription companies is direct: protecting gross margin means protecting your ability to grow without proportionally growing your cost base.


## Factors That Can Compress Gross Margin


For subscription companies, several structural cost pressures can quietly eat into gross margin over time.


- Infrastructure costs tend to scale with usage and not with revenue, so rapid subscriber growth can temporarily compress margins until engineering teams optimize resource consumption.
- Payment processing fees, including interchange and gateway costs, are typically treated as cost of revenue, reducing gross margin directly on every transaction.
- High support volume driven by onboarding friction or product complexity increases the cost of serving each customer, pulling margin down.
- Discounting and promotional pricing reduce recognized revenue without proportionally reducing delivery costs, which shrinks margin percentage even when subscriber counts grow.

## What Is a Good Gross Margin for Subscription Companies?


Gross margin benchmarks vary meaningfully by business model, so subscription companies need sector-specific reference points, not broad industry averages.


### Benchmarks by Subscription Type


The right target depends on what you deliver:


- [Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) companies](https://stripe.com/resources/more/saas-gross-margin-explained-what-it-is-and-why-it-is-important) typically see gross margins between 70% and 85%, reflecting low incremental delivery costs once the product is built.
- [Subscription e-commerce businesses](https://www.opexengine.com/post/a-deep-dive-into-e-commerce-gross-margin) (physical goods, boxes, consumables) generally run 30% to 50%, weighed down by product, packaging, and fulfillment costs.
- Streaming and digital media sit in the 50% to 70% range, with content licensing and delivery infrastructure as the primary cost drivers.


### What Signals a Healthy Margin


A gross margin above 60% gives a subscription business enough headroom to invest in sales, marketing, and retention without compressing operating income. Margins below 40% leave little room for growth spending and tend to attract investor scrutiny at scale. The right benchmark is less about a single number and more about whether your margin supports the unit economics your customer acquisition cost (CAC) and lifetime value require.


Your gross margin percentage is the starting point for that analysis, connecting directly to how much of each subscription dollar is available to cover the fixed costs that sit below the gross profit line.
