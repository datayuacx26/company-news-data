# National Financial Awareness Day 2026: Why fraud prevention is part of financial literacy

### Share


National Financial Awareness Day is observed every year on August 14. It’s a prompt to revisit budgets, savings goals, and long-term financial plans. But in 2026, there’s one topic that belongs right alongside the usual financial wellness conversation — and it doesn’t get nearly enough airtime:[fraud prevention](https://www.wexinc.com/resources/blog/protect-your-bottom-line-from-payments-fraud/) .


Knowing how to manage money isn’t just about compound interest and emergency funds anymore. It’s about knowing when someone is trying to take that money from you.


## **What is National Financial Awareness Day?**


National Financial Awareness Day, held annually on August 14, is an unofficial observance that encourages Americans to take stock of their financial health. It’s commonly used by financial institutions, educators, and nonprofits to promote budgeting, saving, debt management, and investment basics.


In 2026, fraud awareness deserves a seat at that table.


## **Why fraud prevention is a financial literacy issue**


Most financial education focuses on the positive side of the ledger: how to save, invest, and get out of debt. That’s essential. But it tends to assume a stable environment where the main risks are your own decisions.


The reality is messier. According to the[2026 AFP Payments Fraud and Control Survey Report](https://www.financialprofessionals.org/training-resources/resources/survey-research-economic-data/details/payments-fraud) , “For every 100 attempts by a fraudster, 20 are successful; and some companies are unable to recover targeted funds.”


[Scams have gotten more sophisticated](https://www.wexinc.com/resources/blog/payment-fraud-is-getting-smarter/) , more targeted, and increasingly hard to spot. AI-generated voice clones can now convincingly impersonate a family member in distress. Fake investment platforms look identical to legitimate ones. Romance scams unfold over months before a financial ask ever comes.


Financial literacy that doesn’t account for fraud risk isn’t complete. Think of it like insurance: you wouldn’t consider someone financially literate if they didn’t understand why insurance exists, even though it’s about managing bad outcomes rather than building wealth. Fraud prevention deserves the same status. It’s risk management.


## **Common financial scams to know in 2026**


Understanding prevalent scams is itself a form of financial literacy. In 2026, the ones doing the most damage include:


- **Impersonation scams** — fraudsters posing as the IRS, Social Security Administration, or a bank, often with spoofed phone numbers
- **Investment fraud** — fake platforms promising high returns, often using AI-generated testimonials and professional-looking websites
- **Business email compromise (BEC)** — fraudulent emails impersonating a colleague, vendor, or executive to trick someone into transferring funds or sharing sensitive information
- **AI voice cloning scams** — synthetic audio impersonating a family member claiming to be in an emergency
- **Phishing and smishing** — fraudulent emails and texts designed to harvest login credentials or payment details


Knowing these patterns matters because urgency and familiarity are the main levers scammers pull. Recognizing the script makes it easier to pause.


## **Practical fraud prevention habits that protect your finances**


This isn’t about living in a state of suspicion. It’s about a few habits that become second nature.


**Know how legitimate institutions communicate.** Banks don’t ask you to verify your account by calling a number in a text. The IRS doesn’t call demanding immediate payment. Any message that creates pressure to act before you can think is worth slowing down on.


**Slow down when urgency is involved.** Urgency is almost always a manipulation tactic — a limited-time investment, a grandchild who needs bail money wired immediately, a prize expiring in 24 hours. The legitimate version of almost anything can wait for a phone call to verify.


**Monitor your accounts regularly.** Most financial institutions offer transaction alerts. Setting them up takes ten minutes and catches a lot.


**Use multi-factor authentication.** On financial accounts especially, this is one of the highest-ROI security steps available.


## **Who is most at risk of financial fraud?**


Fraud doesn’t discriminate by intelligence or income, and the belief that “it won’t happen to me” can itself create a blind spot. While older adults are often targeted because they tend to have more accumulated savings, FTC data shows that younger adults are actually more likely to report losing money to fraud. The difference is that[older adults typically experience much higher losses](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/ftc-issues-annual-report-congress-agencys-actions-protect-older-adults?utm_source=chatgpt.com) when they are victimized. No demographic is off the table.


## **Using National Financial Awareness Day as a fraud-prevention check-in**


If August 14 is useful as a nudge to review your finances, it can also be a nudge to run a quick fraud-prevention check:


- When did you last review your credit report?
- Are your financial accounts using multi-factor authentication?
- Do the people you’d turn to in an emergency know what current scams look like?


None of this needs to be a project. It’s about folding fraud awareness into how we already think about financial health — because in 2026, they really are the same conversation.
