# The Mental Health Benefits of Pickleball for Older Adults

## 5 Ways Pickleball Supports Well-Being Later in Life


Most people take up pickleball for the exercise, but a growing body of research suggests its biggest payoff may be mental, and the benefits may be strongest later in life.


1.


#### It's built to help fight loneliness.


In a 2025 national survey of 825 U.S. adults over the age of 50, published in the[Journal of Primary Care & Community Health](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/21501319251385855) , those who had never played pickleball were about 1.5 times as


likely as


current players to report feeling lonely.


Pickleball players, both current and former, also reported more social connections than those who never played. Part of it may be the court itself: at roughly 30% the size of a tennis court, it keeps players close enough to talk and laugh throughout a match.


2.


#### The more you play, the better you tend to feel.


A 2025 study of 1,667 players in[Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1676695/full) found a dose-response relationship between pickleball and mental well-being. Those who played three or more times a week, and for longer sessions, reported higher well-being scores. Notably, the effect was strongest among older adults, particularly those ages 63 to 77. A[2023 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9988900/) came to similar conclusions, associating pickleball with improvements in life satisfaction along with lower stress and[symptoms of depression](https://lifestance.com/services/depression-treatment-counseling/#depression-symptoms:~:text=suitable%20treatment%20plan.-,Signs%20and%20Symptoms%20of%20Depression,-Symptoms%20of%20depression) .


3.


#### It keeps the mind engaged.


Pickleball asks a lot of the brain at once: tracking the ball, reading opponents, and adjusting shots quickly. Researchers call this kind of dynamic and reactive play open skill exercise, and a[growing body of evidence](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01707/full#S1) suggests it may benefit the brain more than closed skill activities like running or swimming. While this research spans racquet sports broadly rather than pickleball specifically, it points to why a fast, strategic game may do more for the mind than a repetitive one.


4.


#### It can pull you into the moment.


A 2026 study in the[Journal of Aging and Physical Activity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42297352/) found that adults 60 and older who played recreational doubles pickleball reported stronger flow states, or feelings of total concentration and losing track of time, than other activities. Pickleball may offer a welcome mental break from the day's worries.


5.


#### It offers routine and a sense of purpose.


Beyond the numbers, a 2025 qualitative study in the[Journal of Positive Psychology and Wellbeing](https://journalppw.com/index.php/jppw/article/view/19083/) found that older pickleball players described the sport as a source of autonomy, purpose, and belonging, with many prioritizing community and connection over competition. A standing game gives the week a rhythm and something to look forward to, weaving movement and connection into everyday life rather than leaving them to chance. That kind of built-in structure can matter most in[retirement](https://lifestance.com/blog/depression-in-retirement-what-to-do/) , when the routines and relationships of work tend to fall away.


Pickleball isn't a cure-all, or a replacement for[mental health care](https://lifestance.com/provider/) , but perhaps the game itself was never really the point. What seems to keep people coming back is each other, and as we age, that connection is worth showing up for.
