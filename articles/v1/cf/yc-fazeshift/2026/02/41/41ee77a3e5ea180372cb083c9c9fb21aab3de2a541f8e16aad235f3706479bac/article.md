# How to Create an Invoice: Templates and Best Practices for 2026

Invoices are the keystone of accounts receivable. They determine whether your sale becomes cash flow or gets stuck in payment limbo. Every subsequent process (such as payment reminders or collection calls) traces back to decisions made when that invoice was created. Get it right, and payments flow smoothly. Get it wrong, and you're fighting an uphill battle from day one.


The stakes are significant. Right now,[half of all B2B invoiced sales are overdue](https://atradius.us/knowledge-and-research/reports/b2b-payment-practices-trends-united-states-2024) across the United States—representing billions in delayed cash flow. While market conditions and customer behavior play a role, much of this payment friction stems from preventable issues that originate at the invoice level.


Unlike external factors you can't control, invoice optimization is entirely within your power. Small changes to how you structure, present, and deliver invoices can accelerate payment cycles and reduce the percentage of overdue accounts. This guide focuses on practical, proven techniques that convert revenue to cash faster—because a sale isn't complete until the money hits your account.


## **The Anatomy of an Invoice That Gets Paid**


A well crafted invoice guides the reader through every piece of information they need to process payment quickly. Yet most invoices fail this basic test, creating friction that transforms a simple transaction into a drawn-out collection process.


Every invoice requires the fundamentals like your complete business information, the customer's billing details, a unique invoice number, the invoice date, detailed line items, clear payment terms, and the total amount due. These are bare minimum for a document that accounting departments can actually process.


Invoices that get paid on time versus those that languish in approval workflows often differ in two critical elements that businesses routinely overlook: purchase order references and comprehensive payment instructions. When your invoice lands on an accounts payable clerk's desk without a PO number, it immediately gets flagged for additional review. When payment instructions offer only one method (say, checks only), you may be forcing customers into their slowest payment channel.


The stakes are higher than most business owners realize.[Invoice errors account for 61% of late payments](https://atradius.us/knowledge-and-research/reports/b2b-payment-practices-trends-united-states-2024) , mostly coming from simple mistakes. These include wrong addresses, missing purchase order numbers, incorrect pricing, and vague service descriptions. If someone in accounting needs to pick up the phone to clarify anything on your invoice, that invoice has failed. Every question they have to ask extends your payment cycle. Every missing piece of information adds days to your cash conversion timeline.


## **Invoice Templates by Use Case**


Different business models require different invoicing approaches. While the core elements remain consistent, you must present information to match how your clients expect to see their charges broken down.


### Standard B2B Invoice


The standard B2B invoice is your baseline. Every other template builds on this foundation, so getting these elements right matters more than any stylistic choice you'll make.


#### Your Business Information


Start with the basics at the top: legal business name, address, phone, email, and your EIN or tax ID. AP departments verify vendor identity before releasing payment. An invoice from an unrecognized sender gets flagged for review or discarded entirely. If you're not already in their vendor system, that missing tax ID blocks setup completely—and of course, no setup means no payment.


#### Customer Information


This is where invoices most commonly fail. You need the customer's legal billing name (not the contact's name, DBA, or the parent company unless that's who pays), the correct billing address, and their purchase order number.


Enterprise AP departments operate on three-way matching: they compare the PO, the invoice, and the receiving report before releasing payment. Without a PO reference, your invoice won’t match. Then it’ll sit in a queue until someone manually hunts down the paperwork. At many companies, that means it doesn't get paid until someone complains.


#### Invoice Identifiers


These establish the payment timeline. Your invoice number should be unique and sequential—duplicates trigger fraud alerts, and non-sequential numbers complicate reconciliation.


The invoice date starts the payment clock, but don't assume customers know when that clock ends. Spell out the explicit due date. Saying "Net 30" alone creates ambiguity. Does that mean Net 30 from invoice date? From receipt? From goods delivery? Finally, state the exact date payment is expected.


#### Line Items


Specificity matters a lot here. "Consulting services - $5,000" triggers clarification requests. "Strategy workshop, 8 hours @ $625/hr, December 15" gets approved without callbacks.


List quantities, unit prices, and line totals separately. Three-way matching compares these against the PO, and discrepancies of even a few cents can trigger manual review.


#### Totals


Make the amount due unmissable. AP analysts process dozens of invoices daily. If you bury your total in small text at the bottom of a dense page, it’s way more likely your invoice gets set aside for "later review."


Separate subtotal, tax, and final amount. We recommend that you bold the total due or otherwise make it visually prominent.


#### Payment Information


List every payment method you accept: ACH, wire, check, credit card. Include complete bank details for electronic payments such as routing number, account number, bank name.


Add a payment portal link if you have one; companies using embedded payment links see 3-5 day reductions in time-to-payment. And include a remittance address for checks. Over 40% of B2B payments are still paper, so don't make customers hunt for where to send them.


#### Terms


Close with late payment penalties (stated penalties create urgency and legal enforceability), early payment discounts (2/10 Net 30 represents a 36.7% annualized return for customers who take it), and dispute instructions (without a clear process, customers default to non-payment when they have questions).

### Progress/Milestone Invoice


Construction and project-based work operate on different payment rhythms than standard transactions. You're billing for work in progress, not completed deliveries, which means your invoice needs to document where the project stands beyond just what you're charging.


#### Billing Period and Completion Status


Every progress invoice must clearly state the billing period covered and the percentage of work complete. "Invoice for November 2024" isn't enough. State the specific work performed during that period and how it maps to the overall project scope.


Include cumulative totals showing what's been billed to date versus the total contract value. AP departments reviewing a $50,000 invoice need to see immediately that this represents billing 5 of 12 on a $600,000 contract, with $200,000 previously billed and $350,000 remaining.


#### Retention


Most construction contracts withhold 5-10% of each payment until project completion. Your invoice must show this math explicitly: gross amount earned, retention withheld, and net amount due.


Don't bury retention in footnotes. A contractor billing $100,000 with 10% retention should show the $100,000 earned, the $10,000 withheld, and the $90,000 due this period—plus cumulative retention held to date. When the retention release comes, that running total determines your final payment.


#### AIA Format


For commercial construction, the AIA G702/G703 forms are effectively mandatory. General contractors and owners expect this standardized format, and submitting invoices in any other structure creates friction. The forms document schedule of values, work completed by line item, stored materials, and retention calculations in a format every construction AP department recognizes.


If you're a subcontractor, your billing must align with how the GC bills the owner. Misaligned billing cycles or format mismatches delay your payment until the GC reconciles the discrepancy.


#### Change Orders


Project scope changes during construction—that's inevitable. Your invoice must clearly separate original contract work from approved change orders, referencing the specific change order numbers and approval dates. Unapproved changes billed as standard work get rejected. Approved changes billed without documentation get flagged for verification.

### Recurring Invoice


Subscription and SaaS billing looks simple on the surface since it’s the same amount, same customer, every month. But recurring invoices fail in ways that one-time invoices don't, and those failures recur, too.


#### Billing Period


State exactly what period this invoice covers. "Monthly subscription - $500" creates confusion. "Monthly subscription - January 1-31, 2025 - $500" eliminates ambiguity about what the customer is paying for and when service was delivered.


This matters to customers who are reconciling annual budgets and need to match invoices to service periods. Finance teams auditing software spend need clear date ranges. Vague billing periods create work for your customers' accounting teams, and annoyed accounting teams won't prioritize your invoices.


#### Usage and Overages


If your pricing includes usage-based components, break them out explicitly. Show the included allocation, actual usage, and any overage charges as separate line items.


A customer seeing "$500 base + $127 overage" will pay without question. A customer seeing "$627" with no explanation will email asking why their bill changed—and that invoice sits unpaid until they get an answer.


#### Prorated Amounts


Mid-cycle changes such as upgrades, downgrades, or seat additions will require clear proration math. Show the calculation: days remaining in billing period, daily rate, and resulting credit or charge.


Customers upgrading from a $100/month plan to a $200/month plan mid-cycle need to see how you calculated that $150 invoice. Hidden math looks like a mistake.


#### Payment Method on File


Recurring billing lives or dies on card-on-file and ACH authorization. Your invoice should confirm the payment method that will be charged and when. "This invoice will be charged to Visa ending 4242 on February 1" sets expectations and reduces failed payment disputes.


When cards expire or payments fail, your invoice becomes a dunning notice. Include clear instructions for updating payment information, and make that update process frictionless. This can help avoid involuntary churn.

### Time-Based Invoice


Professional services firms (such as consultants, agencies, law firms, accountants) bill for expertise delivered over time. Your invoice must document not just hours worked but value delivered, because clients scrutinizing a $15,000 invoice want to understand what they're paying for.


#### Hourly Breakdown


List each person who worked on the project, their role, their hourly rate, hours worked, and the resulting charge. Aggregate "professional services - 47 hours - $11,750" doesn’t tell the client enough. A breakdown showing Partner (8 hrs @ $400), Senior Associate (24 hrs @ $275), and Analyst (15 hrs @ $150) shows exactly where their money went.


We recommend grouping entries by date, by team member, or by project phase—whatever makes the work easiest for your client to follow. The goal is a clear narrative: here's who worked, here's when, here's what they accomplished.


#### Task Descriptions


Specificity also protects you in disputes and builds client confidence that hours billed reflect work performed. If your descriptions are too vague to defend in a billing dispute, they're too vague to put on an invoice.


For example, "Research - 4 hours" probably doesn't justify a $1,000 line item well enough. "Researched regulatory requirements for FDA 510(k) submission; reviewed three comparable predicate devices; summarized findings in memo - 4 hours" does.


#### Expense Reimbursement


Separate professional fees from reimbursable expenses. Travel, materials, software licenses, filing fees—these should appear as distinct line items with supporting documentation available on request.


For expenses requiring client approval over a threshold, reference the approval. "Airfare to Chicago - $487 (approved via email 11/3)" preempts questions about unauthorized spending.


#### Project Reference


Clients working with you on multiple matters need invoices that clearly identify which project they're paying for. Include the project name, internal client reference number, and matter number if applicable.


For retainer arrangements, show how this invoice draws against the retainer balance: opening balance, current charges, and remaining retainer. Clients shouldn't have to call you to understand how much of their prepayment remains.


#### Budget Tracking


If you're working against a quoted budget or not-to-exceed amount, show where this invoice falls relative to that cap. "Project budget: $75,000 | Billed to date: $52,000 | This invoice: $12,000 | Remaining: $11,000" gives clients visibility without requiring them to track it themselves, and prevents the unpleasant surprise of an over-budget invoice.

## Frequently Asked Questions


**What should be included on a B2B invoice?**


Every B2B invoice needs your complete business information (legal name, address, phone, email, EIN), the customer's legal billing name and address, a unique invoice number, invoice date, explicit due date, detailed line items with quantities and unit prices, tax breakdown, total amount due, and comprehensive payment instructions. The two elements businesses most often overlook—purchase order references and multiple payment methods—are also the two most likely to cause payment delays. Without a PO number, enterprise AP departments will flag your invoice for manual review. Without bank details for electronic payment, customers who want to pay today literally can't.


**What causes most late invoice payments?**


Invoice errors account for 61% of late payments, and most stem from preventable mistakes: wrong billing addresses, missing purchase order numbers, incorrect pricing, and vague service descriptions. If anyone in accounting needs to call you to clarify something on your invoice, that invoice has failed. Every question adds days to your payment timeline. The other major cause is payment friction—when you only offer one payment method (like checks only), you may be forcing customers into their slowest payment channel while they process internal exceptions.


**How do I invoice for construction or milestone-based projects?**


Progress invoices require additional elements beyond standard invoices. State the billing period covered and percentage of work complete, and include cumulative totals showing what's been billed to date versus total contract value. Show retention math explicitly: gross amount earned, retention withheld, and net amount due this period—plus cumulative retention held. For commercial construction, AIA G702/G703 forms are effectively mandatory. Always separate original contract work from approved change orders, referencing specific change order numbers and approval dates. Unapproved changes billed as standard work get rejected; approved changes billed without documentation get flagged.


**Should I offer early payment discounts?**


Yes, but strategically. The classic 2/10 Net 30 structure—2% discount for payment within 10 days instead of 30—delivers a 36.7% annualized return for customers who take it, making it nearly irresistible for cash-rich companies. However, don't offer discounts to customers who already pay promptly—you're giving away margin for behavior you'd get anyway. Track utilization: if fewer than 20% of eligible invoices use your discount, the program needs adjustment. Either customers don't understand the offer, the process is too complicated, or the discount isn't compelling enough.


**How does invoice automation improve collection times?**


Companies with AR automation average 29-day DSO compared to 44+ days without—a 15-day improvement that compounds across every transaction. The gains come from eliminating manual invoice errors, ensuring consistent delivery, automating follow-up sequences, and reducing payment friction through embedded payment links. Sixty-two percent of companies report measurable DSO improvements within their first year of implementing AR automation. Beyond faster payments, staff time shifts from manual invoice chasing to strategic analysis and customer relationship building.


**What's the difference between invoice date and due date, and why does it matter?**


The invoice date is when you issued the invoice; the due date is when payment is expected. The distinction matters because "Net 30" alone creates ambiguity—Net 30 from invoice date? From receipt? From goods delivery? Without an explicit due date, customers default to their own interpretation, often adding days or weeks to your collection timeline. Always state the exact date payment is expected: "Due: February 15, 2026" eliminates any confusion and gives AP departments a clear deadline to work toward.
