# Aspire Yield, Explained Through Yu'e Bao: A Guide for Chinese-Owned Businesses

## **The Core Idea Is the Same**


Both products solve the same basic problem: money sitting in a transaction account earns nothing, so why not put it to work without locking it away?


Yu'e Bao does this by automatically investing your Alipay balance into the Tianhong Yu'ebao Money Market Fund, which holds low-risk instruments like bank deposits, government bonds, and short-term securities.


Aspire Yield applies the identical logic to a business account. Idle SGD and USD balances sitting in your Aspire Business Account can be moved into the Fullerton SGD Cash Fund or Fullerton USD Cash Fund — both managed by Fullerton Fund Management, a Singapore-based asset manager under Seviora, which is owned by Temasek (the Singapore government's investment arm). These funds hold short-term, high-quality instruments such as government bills and fixed deposits with approved financial institutions — conceptually the same category of holdings as Yu'e Bao's underlying fund.


In other words: this isn't a deposit product, and it isn't a savings account paying a fixed rate. Both are money market fund structures where your capital is invested, not lent to a bank. That distinction matters for both products, and it's worth being upfront about it.


## **Key Differences to Understand**


**Who can use it.**


Yu'e Bao is built for individual consumers with an Alipay account. Aspire Yield is built exclusively for businesses — specifically, companies incorporated in Singapore that qualify as active Non-Financial Entities. It is not available to individuals, financial institutions, investment companies, or businesses majority-owned by U.S. persons.


**Currency exposure.**


Yu'e Bao operates purely in CNY. Aspire Yield lets you hold returns in SGD and USD, which matters if your business already operates internationally and wants to avoid unnecessary FX conversion just to earn a return on cash sitting between transactions.


**Fund manager, ownership, and oversight.**


The corporate structure behind each product is worth understanding, because they are quite different in design.


With Yu'e Bao, the platform, distributor, and fund manager are essentially one integrated corporate family. Alipay (the platform users interact with) is owned by Ant Group, which in turn holds a 51% controlling stake in Tianhong Asset Management — the company that actually manages the money. Alibaba acquired that stake in October 2013, just months after Yu'e Bao launched, specifically to bring the fund manager inside the group. This vertical integration is what made Yu'e Bao so seamless to use, but it also means a single corporate ecosystem controls the full chain from user interface to asset management.


Aspire Yield is structured differently. Aspire is the distributor — the company whose business account you hold and whose app you use. The actual fund management is handled by Fullerton Fund Management, a separately regulated, independently governed asset manager under Seviora, which is owned by Temasek. Aspire does not own Fullerton, and Fullerton does not own Aspire. The two companies operate at arm's length, with Fullerton's mandate being purely to manage the underlying funds according to its own investment discipline and regulatory obligations. This separation means your money is being managed by a dedicated asset manager whose only job is fund management — not a subsidiary built to serve a parent company's platform strategy.


The Yield product itself is offered by AFT SG 2 Pte. Ltd., which is regulated by the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) and holds a Capital Markets Services licence, meaning it operates under Singapore's securities regulatory framework with MAS oversight specifically for dealing in collective investment schemes.


**Where your money sits.**


Aspire is explicit that your funds are held in a trust account with a licensed bank, segregated from Aspire's own corporate assets — so in the unlikely event Aspire became insolvent, your money is protected and separate. Similarly, if Fullerton itself ran into trouble, the fund's assets are held by a third-party custodian and shielded from Fullerton's creditors. This segregated custody structure mirrors the protections Chinese investors are familiar with from regulated mutual fund structures, just under Singapore's regulatory regime instead of China's.


**Liquidity.**


Yu'e Bao offers same-day access to funds for spending or transfer. Aspire Yield offers same day access for USD if redemption request is submitted before 9am Singapore time and next-business-day access for SGD if redemption request is submitted before 4pm SGT. There's no lock-in period and no minimum investment amount required.


**Fees.**


This is one area worth being transparent about. Apart from the fund management fee of 0.16%, Aspire charges an annual fee of 0.5%, accrued daily on your average balance and deducted monthly — you're only charged for days you actually hold funds in the Yield account. Yu'e Bao's costs are baked into the fund's expense ratio rather than charged as a separate visible fee, which comprises management fee (0.3%), sales service fee (0.25%) and custodian fee (0.07%) but conceptually every money market fund carries some layer of management and custodian costs; Aspire's structure is simply more transparent about showing it to you upfront.


**Returns.**


Returns on both products move with prevailing interest rates and are not fixed or guaranteed — Yu'e Bao's yield has fallen sharply from its early years as Chinese rates have declined, while Aspire Yield's USD returns currently benefit from the higher US rate environment relative to SGD or CNY.


**How your returns are shown to you — and one important structural difference.**


This is worth understanding clearly, because the two products work differently in how they account for returns, and it can look unfamiliar if you're used to Yu'e Bao.


Yu'e Bao uses a constant NAV structure. Every unit of the Tianhong Yu'ebao fund is always worth exactly 1 yuan — that number never changes. When the fund earns returns, those returns are credited to you as additional units or accrued interest shown separately in your Alipay balance. You never see the per-unit value drop. This design makes it feel psychologically very close to a savings account: the number you see going in is the same number sitting there every day, and interest shows up as an add-on.


Aspire Yield's underlying Fullerton funds use a floating NAV structure — the standard approach for Singapore-regulated unit trusts. The fund's NAV (the price per unit) moves over time. Because Fullerton runs these as accumulating funds, returns are not paid out separately; instead, they are reinvested at the fund level and reflected as a gradually rising NAV. What this means practically: when you invest in Aspire Yield, your units are purchased at the current NAV price, and over time you will see that price rising steadily as the fund earns returns. That upward-trending NAV line is your return — it is not a sign that prices are fluctuating in a risky way.


The practical implication for how it feels day to day: with Yu'e Bao, you see a stable number plus interest accruing on top. With Aspire Yield, you see a unit price that trends steadily upward. Both reflect the same underlying reality — low-risk, short-duration investments earning short-term market rates — just displayed differently.


## **Why This Should Feel Familiar, Not Foreign**


For a Chinese-owned business evaluating Aspire Yield for the first time, the most useful mental model is: this is Yu'e Bao's business-account equivalent, operating under Singapore's regulatory and custody framework instead of China's. The mechanics you already trust — idle balance invested into a low-risk money market fund, no lock-in, daily/near-daily liquidity, returns that move with the market rather than being fixed — all carry over. What changes is the currency, the regulator, the fund manager, and the fact that it's purpose-built for a business account rather than a personal wallet.


## **A Few Things Worth Repeating Plainly**


- Aspire Yield is not a bank deposit and is not covered by deposit insurance. Capital is not guaranteed, and the value of your investment can fluctuate, in the same way it can with Yu'e Bao.
- Returns are not fixed — they move with market conditions, and past performance never guarantees future returns.
- Eligibility is limited to qualifying Singapore-incorporated businesses.
- This article is for general understanding only — it isn't financial, legal, or tax advice. Always check the current rate, fee structure, and terms on Aspire's official pages, or speak with your relationship manager, before making decisions about moving balances into Yield.


If anything above raises a question specific to your company's structure or ownership, that's exactly the kind of thing worth raising directly with the Aspire team before opening a Yield account.
