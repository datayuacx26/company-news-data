# Revenue management 101: What it is and how it works

## What is revenue management


Revenue management is a data-driven approach to maximising revenue by predicting customer behaviour and optimising pricing and product availability in response.


It answers 3 core questions:


- Who will buy (customer segmentation)?


- When will they buy (demand forecasting)?


- At what price (pricing optimisation)?


Originally developed by the airline industry, revenue management now spans hospitality, SaaS, e-commerce, healthcare, and manufacturing. In Singapore's high-cost, high-competition market, it has become a strategic necessity rather than a nice-to-have.

## How revenue management works


Effective revenue management follows a repeatable process.


### 1. Collect business and customer data


Gather data from every relevant source: transaction history, customer profiles, competitor pricing, inventory levels, and operational metrics. In Singapore, where PDPA regulations govern data collection and usage, businesses need to build rich customer profiles while maintaining compliant data practices from the outset.


### 2. Segment customers


Divide your customer base into groups with similar characteristics and willingness to pay:


- By behaviour: frequent buyers, seasonal shoppers, price-sensitive customers


- By value: high-LTV enterprise clients versus transactional SMB customers


- By channel: direct website purchasers versus marketplace buyers


[Singapore e-commerce data](https://www.databureau.com.sg/resources/publications/circular-02-2026-e-commerce-sector-intelligence-update) shows that the top 10 SKUs account for only 40% of revenue. Understanding which customer segments drive the remaining 60% is where targeted pricing creates the most value.


### 3. Forecast demand


Use historical patterns, leading indicators such as website traffic and inquiry volumes, and external factors like competitor promotions and seasonal events to predict future demand. Singapore hotels, for example, track occupancy patterns, enabling precise room allocation decisions well in advance.


### 4. Optimise pricing


Set prices that balance demand signals with revenue goals. This includes establishing price floors and ceilings for each customer segment, making dynamic adjustments based on real-time demand, and designing promotions that stimulate incremental revenue without training customers to wait for discounts.


### 5. Manage inventory or capacity


Allocate limited resources to maximise revenue across segments. Airlines reserve seats for high-fare business travellers while filling economy inventory early. SaaS businesses manage the capacity for trial-to-paid conversions and onboarding resources. Hotels protect rooms for peak-season bookings while discounting during low-demand periods.


### 6. Monitor performance and refine


Track KPIs daily and weekly. Run A/B tests on price points and promotional structures. Benchmark against competitors. Revenue management is not a one-time exercise; it is a continuous feedback loop.


## Core revenue management strategies


Revenue management combines several strategies to help you make better decisions about pricing, demand, customer segments, inventory, and sales channels.


### Market segmentation


Group your customers based on factors such as buying behavior, booking patterns, purchase frequency, or willingness to pay. This helps you understand how different customer groups make purchasing decisions and tailor your pricing, offers, and availability accordingly.


### Demand forecasting


Use historical sales data, booking trends, seasonality, and market signals to estimate future demand. More accurate forecasts help you make better decisions about pricing, inventory, staffing, and promotions before demand changes.


### Inventory and capacity management


Allocate your available inventory, services, or capacity based on expected demand and customer value. For example, a hotel can reserve rooms for periods when demand is expected to be higher, while an e-commerce business can adjust inventory levels based on forecasted sales.


### Channel optimization


Manage pricing, inventory, and availability across direct and third-party sales channels to improve profitability. This helps you understand which channels generate the most value after accounting for distribution costs and adjust your channel mix accordingly.


### Promotional pricing


Use discounts, bundles, or limited-time offers to stimulate demand during slower periods or move excess inventory. The goal is to generate incremental sales while protecting your margins and maintaining the perceived value of your offering.


## Pricing strategies for revenue management


Pricing is a central part of revenue management. The right approach depends on your demand patterns, customer segments, competitive position, costs, and business model. Common pricing strategies include:


### Dynamic pricing


- Adjust your prices based on changes in demand, availability, competitor rates, and market conditions.


- Capture more value during periods of high demand while keeping your offering competitive when demand slows.


### Tiered pricing


- Offer different price points for the same core product or service based on features, service levels, or conditions.


- Common in SaaS, where plans vary by features and usage, and hospitality, where room categories are priced based on their features and amenities.


### Best Available Rate (BAR)


- Set a publicly available base rate for a specific date or service.


- Structure other rates, such as member, package, and negotiated corporate rates, relative to the BAR.


### Discount pricing


- Offer reduced prices to stimulate demand during slower periods or encourage customers to commit earlier.


- Use discounts selectively based on demand and customer segments rather than applying them broadly.


### Positional pricing


- Set prices based on your desired market position, such as premium, mid-market, or budget.


- Align your pricing with the value and market position you want customers to associate with your brand.


### Competitive pricing


- Set your prices above, below, or in line with competitor rates depending on your positioning and objectives.


- Use competitor pricing as a reference, but avoid relying on it as your primary pricing strategy since it does not account for your own demand or customer data.


### Price per segment


- Offer different prices to customer segments based on factors such as willingness to pay, booking behavior, or customer value.


- Use rate fences, such as eligibility requirements or booking conditions, to differentiate offers between segments.


### Bundle pricing


- Combine multiple products or services into a package at a single price.


- Increase the perceived value of an offer while encouraging customers to purchase additional products or services.


### Cost-plus pricing


- Calculate your cost of delivering a product or service and add a target margin to determine the selling price.


- Maintain a predictable margin, but recognize that this approach does not account for changes in customer demand or willingness to pay.


### Price skimming


- Set a higher initial price for a new product or service to capture customers with a higher willingness to pay.


- Gradually reduce the price as the market expands.


- This approach can be useful for product launches and new market entries.


### Penetration pricing


- Launch a new product or service at a relatively low price to attract customers and build market share.


- Adjust pricing as your customer base and market position develop.


Choosing the right pricing strategy requires you to consider more than your competitors' rates or production costs. Your approach should reflect demand, customer segments, market conditions, and your broader revenue goals.


## Revenue management examples across industries


Revenue management is not limited to hotels and airlines. Businesses across industries use similar principles to make better decisions about pricing, demand, capacity, and customer segments.


### Marriott International: Hospitality


Marriott uses revenue management systems to adjust room rates based on factors such as demand, booking patterns, room availability, and market conditions. This allows properties to adjust pricing as demand changes, rather than relying on fixed room rates.


**What founders can learn:** If your demand changes frequently, your pricing should be able to respond to those changes rather than staying fixed throughout the year.


### Singapore Airlines: Airlines


Singapore Airlines uses revenue management to manage fares and seat inventory across its network. Airlines typically consider factors such as booking timing, remaining seat capacity, demand forecasts, and customer segments when determining fare availability.


**What founders can learn:** When you have limited capacity, allocating it to the right customers at the right price can be as important as increasing overall sales.


### Amazon: E-commerce


Amazon is known for using data and algorithms to adjust product prices based on factors such as demand, competition, inventory, and market conditions. Its large product catalog and high transaction volume make automated pricing particularly useful.


**What founders can learn:** If you operate at high transaction volumes, automated pricing can help you respond to market changes faster than manual updates.


### SaaS businesses: Subscription pricing


SaaS companies apply revenue management through tiered pricing, usage-based pricing, upgrades, renewals, and expansion offers. Instead of treating every customer the same, they can structure plans around different levels of usage, features, and customer value.


**What founders can learn:** Your pricing model can create opportunities to increase revenue from existing customers as their needs and usage grow.


### Uber: Ride-hailing


Uber uses dynamic pricing to adjust fares based on real-time supply and demand. When demand increases relative to available drivers, prices can change to help balance rider demand with driver availability.


**What founders can learn:** When demand and capacity fluctuate quickly, pricing can help you balance customer demand with the resources you have available.


#### What these examples mean for founders


You do not need the scale of Marriott, Amazon, or Uber to apply revenue management. The underlying principles can work for smaller businesses too.


Start by identifying where you have:


- **Variable demand:** When customer demand changes by season, day, time, or market condition.


- **Limited capacity:** When you have a fixed number of rooms, appointments, employees, production slots, or service hours.


- **Different customer segments:** When some customers have different needs, purchase patterns, or willingness to pay.


- **Flexible pricing:** When you can change prices, packages, or offers without significantly changing the product itself.


- **Useful data:** When you have enough historical sales and customer data to identify patterns and make informed decisions.


The goal is not simply to charge more. It is to understand your demand, customers, capacity, and costs well enough to make better revenue decisions.

## Common challenges for revenue management for Singapore businesses


Revenue management is not without friction. The most common obstacles Singapore businesses encounter include:


- **Data silos:** Customer, transaction, and operational data sitting in disconnected systems, preventing the unified view that effective pricing requires


- **Talent gaps:** Skilled revenue managers with analytics expertise are scarce and in high demand across the market


- **Technology costs:** Advanced systems require meaningful investment, which creates a barrier for SMEs


- **Channel conflict:** Balancing direct sales with marketplace and distributor relationships without triggering price erosion


- **PDPA compliance:** Data privacy regulations limit collection and usage, requiring privacy-by-design approaches from the start


- **Consumer expectations:** Singapore's consumers are sophisticated and expect pricing transparency; perceived price discrimination can damage trust quickly

## FAQs


### **What is the difference between revenue management and pricing strategy?**


Revenue management is a broader approach that combines pricing, demand forecasting, customer segmentation, inventory management, and channel optimisation. Pricing strategy focuses mainly on setting prices, while revenue management focuses on maximising total revenue across the business.


### **Is revenue management only for large companies?**


No. Businesses of all sizes can apply revenue management principles. Small businesses can start by analysing sales trends, customer behaviour, and pricing patterns, then adopt more advanced tools as they scale.


### **How can small businesses implement revenue management?**


Small businesses can begin by tracking demand patterns, identifying high-value customers, adjusting prices based on market conditions, and testing different offers. Revenue management software can help automate these processes as data volume increases.


### **What data is needed for revenue management?**


Revenue management relies on data such as historical sales, customer behaviour, competitor pricing, inventory levels, seasonal trends, and market conditions. Accurate data helps businesses make better pricing and demand forecasts.


### **Can revenue management work without AI or software?**


Yes. Businesses can use manual analysis and spreadsheets to apply basic revenue management strategies. However, AI-powered software helps process larger datasets, identify patterns faster, and automate decisions at scale.
