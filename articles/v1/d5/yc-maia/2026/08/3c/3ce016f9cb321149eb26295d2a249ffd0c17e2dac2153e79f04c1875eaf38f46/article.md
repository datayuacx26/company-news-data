# We pretend everything is fine in front of our families

You sit in the car outside the house a beat longer than you need to. One of you says something like "okay." The other one nods. Then you both get out, and somewhere between the driveway and the front door you start holding hands.


Three days of this. Maybe five.


Nobody in there is going to ask how you actually are, and if someone did, you would say good, busy year, we're great. Because the true answer is not a dinner-table answer. It is a six-month answer, and there is an aunt within earshot.


## What the performance is actually protecting


Most people assume they're doing it to keep the peace, or to avoid the questions. That's part of it. But underneath, the performance is usually protecting one of three things, and it's worth knowing which one is yours.


Sometimes it protects *them* : your mother would worry, your father would take it badly, your sister would never let it go. Sometimes it protects *your partner* : you don't want your family to think less of them for something you might work out by spring. And sometimes, quietly, it protects *you* , because saying it out loud to people who know you makes it real in a way it isn't yet.


None of those are dishonest. They are all reasonable. The problem is that they all cost the same thing, which is that for several days you and your partner are the only two people in the building who know the actual score, and you're not allowed to talk about it.


## Privacy is not the same as pretending


This is the distinction that helps most, and people mix them up constantly.


Privacy is choosing not to hand your family the details of your relationship. That is healthy. You don't owe anyone an account of your marriage, and "we're working through some things, I'd rather not get into it" is a complete sentence at any holiday table.


Pretending is different. Pretending is the performance: the inside jokes you don't actually feel, the hand on the back, the story about the trip you're planning that you both know you are not planning. Privacy costs you nothing. Pretending costs you energy all day, and then it sends you an invoice at night.


A useful test: are you declining to share something, or are you actively manufacturing something? Those two feel very different in the body by day three.


## The fight almost always happens in the car


In conversations people bring to Maia around family visits, the blowup rarely happens at the table. It happens twenty minutes into the drive home, when the performance ends and there is suddenly nowhere to put all the pressure.


And the opening line is almost never the real subject. It's "you barely spoke to my brother," or "why did you tell them we were fine," or nothing at all, just an hour of highway silence that says more than either version. What people describe afterward is a strange, specific loneliness: being[surrounded by people who love you](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/lonely-in-my-relationship-during-the-holidays) while the one person who knows the truth is the person you cannot say it to yet.


The other pattern we see a lot: both partners independently describe the pretending as something they're doing *for the other one* . Each thinks they are carrying it. Neither has ever said so.


## What to agree on before you walk in


You do not need to resolve the relationship before a family visit. You need a plan for the visit itself. A short conversation in the car, before the driveway, does more than people expect.


- **Agree on the one sentence.** Decide together what you're both saying if someone asks. Same sentence, both of you. Half of the tension in these weekends is the fear that your partner will say something you didn't expect.
- **Pick a signal.** Something small that means "I need ten minutes." A hand on the shoulder, a text, an offer to run to the store. Give each other permission to use it without explaining.
- **Name what is off the table until you're home.** Not to bury it. To schedule it. "We're not doing the money conversation at your parents' house" is a boundary, not avoidance, as long as there's an actual date on the other side of it.
- **Have a rule for the drive home.** Either you debrief, or you deliberately don't and put it on the calendar for the next day. What burns couples is the ambiguous version, where one of you is ready to talk and the other one just wants to survive the drive.
- **Give the performance an end time.** Out loud. "Two more hours and we're in the car." Something finite is much easier to hold than something open-ended.


## When the performing goes on too long


One weekend of this is manageable. A year of it is a different thing.


If you've been performing at every gathering for a while, notice what's happening to the story. The version your family believes gets more detailed and more distant from the truth, and at some point the gap itself becomes a thing you're managing. That's usually the sign that the visits are not the problem. The visits are just where you can no longer[avoid the hard conversation](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/we-avoid-hard-conversations) you've been not having at home.


It's also worth being honest with yourself about the timing question. Plenty of people decide to hold things together through a season and deal with it after, and that can be a legitimate choice when it's actually chosen. It stops being a choice when "after the holidays" has quietly moved three times. If you're already[weighing whether to wait until after](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/break-up-before-the-holidays-or-wait) , the useful thing is to name a real date rather than a vague one.


One more thing, said plainly. If the pretending isn't a mutual agreement but something you're doing because there would be consequences for you otherwise, if your partner controls what you can say to your family or how much time you get with them, that is not conflict management, and it deserves real support. In the US you can reach the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233, and telling one person you trust is a reasonable first step.


For everyone else: the fact that you're both still willing to put on the show is, oddly, information. It means you both still want the relationship to be worth protecting. That's something to work with, once you're back in your own house and can stop acting.


If you're heading into a stretch of this and dreading it, it can help to get clear on what you're actually protecting before you're standing in someone's kitchen. You can talk it through with Maia on your own, or the two of you together, and figure out what you want the week to look like instead of just bracing for it.
