# Can an app help you decide whether to break up?

Short answer: no. No app should decide this for you, and you should be a little suspicious of any that acts like it can. Nobody outside your relationship gets a vote on whether it ends.


But that is not really what people are asking when they type this into a search bar at one in the morning. The real question is closer to: is there anything that can help me think straight about this? There, the answer is a qualified yes. Most people trying to decide whether to break up are not missing information. They are missing somewhere to hear themselves think.


## Why this is so hard to work out inside your own head


Leaving someone is not one decision. It is the same decision made and unmade about forty times, usually in the shower, usually while rehearsing a conversation you will never actually have out loud.


Your own memory keeps switching sides, too. On a calm week you remember the good years, the way they take care of you when you are sick, the private language. On a bad night you can only find evidence for the prosecution. Both states feel like clarity. Neither one is.


Friends are not neutral either, and that is not their fault. Your best friend watched you cry in March and is quietly done. Your mother has been sitting on an opinion for two years. Ask enough people and you get a chorus, not an answer. So most of us go looking for a tiebreaker instead: a quiz, a therapist who will finally just say it, an app.


## The shift that tends to happen around week four


In Maia conversations, almost nobody opens with "should I break up." They open with something small and very specific. He didn't come to my dad's surgery. She looks at her phone the entire time I talk about work. Then they come back a week later with a different small thing, and again the week after that.


Somewhere around the fourth or fifth conversation, the tone changes. Not because new information arrived, but because they have started to hear the shape of their own story. That is the pattern we see most often here, and it is worth naming: people in this spot are rarely stuck on whether their partner is a good person. They settled that a long time ago. What they are actually stuck on is whether the thing they have been asking for is ever going to arrive.


Which is a different question from whether their partner is sorry. Plenty of people are sincerely, tearfully sorry and[still nothing changes](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/my-partner-apologizes-but-nothing-changes) . When someone finally says the plain version out loud, something like "I have asked for this for three years and I no longer believe it's coming," the decision stops feeling like a coin flip. It starts to feel like something they have known for a while and were working hard not to know.


## What a tool can honestly do


Used well, a conversation-based app is not an oracle. It is a place to think in full sentences. Specifically, it can:


- Hold the whole timeline instead of just tonight's mood, so you are weighing a pattern and not the last 48 hours.
- Ask the follow-up question your friends have stopped asking, because they already made up their minds about your partner.
- Be available at 1am, which is when this actually gets decided, rather than Thursday at 4pm in someone's office.
- Reflect your own words back at you. That is uncomfortable in a useful way. Most people recognize their own pattern before they can argue with it.
- Help you turn a vague dread into one specific, sayable sentence. That sentence is usually the entire decision.


## What it can't do, and shouldn't pretend to


It wasn't in the room. It doesn't know how their face looked when you said it. It can't tell you whether you'll regret this in five years, because nobody can. It won't carry the consequences, and it doesn't love anyone involved.


So be wary of anything that scores your relationship and hands back a ruling. A number, a compatibility percentage, a confident "you should leave" is entertainment dressed up as help. If a tool is willing to make this call for you off a few paragraphs of input, that tells you what kind of tool it is.[The same test applies to AI relationship help in general](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/is-ai-couples-therapy-any-good) : the useful kind helps you see, the useless kind issues verdicts.


## Three ways people misuse this


**Verdict-hunting.** Pasting in one fight and asking who was right. It feels great for about ten minutes. It is also close to useless, because being right about Tuesday tells you nothing about whether to stay for the next decade.


**Pro and con lists.** Reasons are not weights. You can have fourteen items in one column and a single thing in the other that quietly ends it, and the list will lie to you about which one matters.


**Looking for permission.** If what you actually want is for someone, anyone, to authorize the breakup so it isn't only your doing, notice that. It isn't a character flaw. It's information.


## Better questions than "should I stay"


These are the ones worth taking somewhere quiet, whether that's an app, a notebook, a therapist, or a long drive alone:


- What exactly have I asked for, in plain words, and what happened in the weeks after I asked?
- Am I unhappy with them, or unhappy with who I turn into around them?
- If nothing at all changes in two years, is this a life I would choose? Not could I survive it. Would I choose it.
- What am I most afraid of here: losing this person, or being alone, starting over, being the one who ended it?
- Is there a version of staying I would actively pick, or only a version I would tolerate?


If the answers point somewhere and you still can't move, that's normal, and it doesn't make you weak. It may just mean you aren't finished gathering.[Working out whether this is a rough patch or the end](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/should-i-break-up-or-keep-trying) takes most people months, and[taking real time apart](https://www.ourmaia.com/post/is-taking-a-break-the-fast-track-to-breaking-up) is sometimes an honest next step rather than a stalling tactic.


One thing that is not a gray area: if there is fear in this, if you are being controlled, threatened, or hurt, it isn't a decision to weigh quietly on your own with an app. Talk to someone you trust, or the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 (US), who can help you think through leaving safely.
