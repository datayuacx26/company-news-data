# Water Balance & AI: Driving Water Impact with Real-Time Data

# How Water Balance is Measured Today


In most industrial operations, water balance is the responsibility of site-level engineers or EHS managers. Data is typically collected manually through **daily walkarounds** , with **operators reading analog meters** and **recording values on paper or spreadsheets** . These readings may **later be digitized** , often in inconsistent formats, delaying insights and leaving room for error.


> “We currently operate with 22 water meters and **collect data manually every day, a task that takes about three hours** . The information is first recorded on paper, then transferred to a spreadsheet that’s not easily accessible for stakeholders. This process slows us down and limits timely decision-making,” said Daniel Hernández during our[webinar](https://www.waterplan.com/webinars/water-balance-ai-insights-from-brown-forman-journey) on Brown-Forman's water balance strategy.


Fragmented data ownership adds to the challenge. Water flow data may be managed by maintenance teams, utility coordinators, or quality control staff, depending on the site. Across different sectors approaches vary widely, both in measurement frequency and level of granularity. For some sectors, like the[mining](https://www.icmm.com/en-gb/guidance/environmental-stewardship/2021/water-reporting) industry, water balances are typically complex due to the need to manage large volumes of process water, tailings, and variable inflows from precipitation and groundwater. Mining operations often rely on site-specific models that incorporate hydrological and geochemical data.


In contrast, other sectors like the[beverage](https://www.bieroundtable.com/publication/2018-water-and-energy-use-benchmarking-study/) industry tend to focus more on the consistency of process inputs and product-related water use. Beverage companies use water balances to support water efficiency, set plant-level benchmarks, and drive reuse or recycling initiatives. These companies often employ frequent metering and real-time monitoring to detect anomalies and improve operational efficiency. Approaches vary widely, both in measurement frequency and level of granularity.


However, common gaps remain even across industries. These include:


-


Incomplete measurement coverage (e.g., missing meters on key inflows or reuse systems)


-


Lack of integration between flow data and operational data (e.g., production volumes)


-


Delayed anomaly detection due to infrequent monitoring


-


Difficulty identifying root causes of inefficiencies


-


Time-consuming manual processes


Another common issue is that water balance data, even when collected, remains siloed. The data may be stored in tools or folders that are difficult to access, preventing key stakeholders from using it effectively. Without timely visibility, even accurate data fails to drive decisions.


Even companies with robust data collection practices often struggle to act on the data in real time. Site managers may review water performance monthly, but leaks or abnormal consumption patterns can emerge and escalate within days or hours.
