# What is ADR? Dialogue replacement explained

The global film dubbing market is on track to reach[$7,048 million by 2032](https://www.globalgrowthinsights.com/market-reports/film-dubbing-market-102061) , per Global Growth Insights, and dialogue replacement sits underneath a large share of it. Capturing clean audio on set is close to impossible: there is always a passing truck, a rustling costume, or a take where the line just did not land.


Automatic dialogue replacement (ADR) is how the industry fixes that. It is the process of re-recording dialogue in a controlled space to replace or improve the audio captured during filming. sync. labs handles the visual half of the same job: when the words change,[sync-3](https://sync.so/sync-3) re-syncs the speaker’s lips to the new track so the picture still matches, without a reshoot.


## ADR re-records dialogue in a studio to replace audio captured on set


ADR, sometimes called looping, is the process of re-recording dialogue in a controlled environment to replace or improve audio captured on set. It is used everywhere dialogue has to be clean: film, TV, games, and podcasts. Actors return to a studio and re-perform their lines to picture, matching the delivery to the original take. When it is done well you do not notice it. When it is done badly you cannot unhear it.


## ADR fixes noise, performance, script changes, and localization


ADR gets reached for whenever the take you captured is not the take you need. The main reasons:


- **Noise interference.** Wind, traffic, HVAC, a dog in the next yard.
- **Poor audio quality.** Muddy, uneven, or off-mic recordings.
- **Performance fixes.** The line was right but the delivery was not, so the actor gets a second swing in a room where they can hear themselves.
- **Last-minute script changes.** Rewrites land after the camera is gone, and ADR gets the new line into the cut without a reshoot.
- **Technical faults.** A boom dips into frame, a lav mic clips, the wireless drops for two seconds.
- **Localization.** For international releases, ADR is how a film gets a second life in another language.


## ADR is used across film, TV, games, and creator content


ADR is no longer only a Hollywood process. In professional film, TV, and games it is a standard post-production tool, but it now applies to anyone who ships video or audio for a living. Podcasters use it to replace a line recorded in bad room tone. Game developers retune and localize voice work region by region. Online educators re-record the rough sections that quietly drop retention. Social creators swap a noisy on-location track so a strong hook is not undersold. Indie filmmakers treat it as the budget version of “fix it in post,” which keeps every frame usable without a reshoot.


## Traditional ADR is slow and studio-bound, which is why creators skip it


Most creators do not avoid ADR because they have never heard of it. They avoid it because it is a pain. It takes real audio-engineering instincts, treated rooms, and good microphones, all of which cost money and time. Re-performing a line three months later, in a different headspace, is harder than it sounds, and matching on-set energy in a sterile booth is genuinely difficult. Studio time is not cheap, and most creators do not have a soundproofed space.

## ADR carries copyright, consent, and performance-rights considerations


At any real scale, the paperwork matters as much as the audio. Producers usually own the copyright on ADR dialogue, but contracts should confirm it, and ADR sessions should be named explicitly in actor agreements, including any additional compensation union rules require. Translation or dubbing into other languages opens new licensing conversations. Treat unreleased ADR files like any other protected asset, credit voice actors to head off recognition disputes, and keep the replaced performance close to the original intent so no one argues about creative meaning later.


## Frequently asked questions


What does ADR stand for?


ADR stands for automatic dialogue replacement, also called looping. It is the process of re-recording dialogue in a controlled environment to replace or improve the audio captured during filming.


Why is ADR used?


ADR is used to fix location noise, poor audio quality, and off performances, to get last-minute script changes into the cut without a reshoot, and to localize a film into other languages.


What is the difference between ADR and dubbing?


ADR usually replaces a line in the original language to fix audio or performance. Dubbing replaces the dialogue in a new language. Both change the soundtrack, and with sync. labs both can also re-sync the lips so the picture matches the new track.


Can AI replace a traditional ADR session?


For the visual match, yes. You still record a clean replacement line, but sync. labs re-syncs the mouth to the corrected or translated audio in minutes, so you skip the frame-by-frame picture work and often the studio itself.


Does changing the dialogue make the lips look wrong?


With audio-only ADR in a new language, the lips no longer match the words. sync-3 re-times the lips to the new audio so the performance reads correctly across languages.
