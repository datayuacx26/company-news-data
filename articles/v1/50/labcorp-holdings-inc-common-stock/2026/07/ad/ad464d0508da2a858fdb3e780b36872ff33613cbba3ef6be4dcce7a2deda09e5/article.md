# Optimizing targeted CNS delivery: Five key considerations for using the Göttingen minipig in preclinical development

As neurodegenerative disease pipelines advance and gene, cell, and oligonucleotide therapies progress through clinical development, biopharma sponsors face a critical translational challenge: selecting the most appropriate preclinical species to evaluate targeted central nervous system (CNS) delivery.


Rodent models remain essential for early proof-of-concept and mechanistic studies, but significant differences in brain size, neuroanatomy, and cerebrospinal fluid dynamics can limit their predictive value for clinical translation. Nonhuman primates (NHPs) continue to provide the highest degree of translational confidence for assessing CNS delivery strategies due to their anatomical and physiological similarity to humans. However, increasing constraints on NHP availability and rising costs have prompted sponsors to evaluate alternative large-animal models whenever scientifically appropriate. As a result, the Göttingen minipig is gaining traction as a translationally relevant option that offers a practical balance between scientific rigor, scalability, and accessibility for advancing CNS therapeutics.


To better understand where the Göttingen minipig fits within the CNS drug development landscape and where its limitations remain, we spoke with Erin Slosarek, MBA, PhD, preclinical CNS lead and cell and gene therapy study director at Labcorp. She shares practical insights across five key areas: the foundational factors shaping any CNS development program, the criteria driving preclinical species selection, the unique advantages the minipig offers as a translational model, the realities of dosing and route-of-administration strategies, and the current state of gene therapy research in the minipig brain.


### 1. Foundational factors in CNS drug development


For sponsors developing gene, cell, or oligonucleotide therapies, successful CNS delivery requires far more than selecting a route of administration or a preclinical model. It begins with a series of interconnected scientific and translational decisions that collectively shape the development strategy.


At the center of these considerations is the blood-brain barrier (BBB), a highly selective interface that limits the ability of many therapeutics to reach the brain and spinal cord. The BBB influences nearly every aspect of CNS drug development, from modality selection to route of administration. Therapeutics delivered systemically by intravenous infusion must either possess intrinsic BBB-penetrating capabilities or leverage specialized delivery technologies. When adequate CNS exposure cannot be achieved through systemic administration, direct-to-CNS approaches, such as intracerebroventricular (ICV), intracisternal, or intrathecal delivery, often become necessary.


Equally important is defining the therapeutic target. This includes both the anatomical target region within the CNS and the molecular target, whether a specific receptor, gene transcript, protein, or cell population. These decisions guide downstream development choices, including therapeutic design, biodistribution requirements, delivery technologies, and the endpoints needed to demonstrate efficacy and safety. Target selection and biological barriers also directly influence drug characteristics such as molecular size, charge, vector design, formulation, and the potential use of carrier systems. Together, these factors determine the feasibility of different delivery strategies and help establish the nonclinical evidence package required to support regulatory submissions and eventual clinical translation.


The key takeaway for sponsors is that CNS delivery is not a single decision point. It is a sequence of interdependent decisions. Choices related to target biology, therapeutic modality, and route of administration create constraints and opportunities that shape every subsequent step in development. Selecting the most appropriate preclinical species is therefore not the starting point of a CNS program; it is the outcome of a thoughtful strategy built around the therapeutic’s biological and translational requirements.


### 2. Criteria for preclinical species selection


Species selection should be driven by scientific relevance, not convenience. Regulatory agencies expect sponsors to justify the choice of each nonclinical species based on its ability to characterize human risk, demonstrate pharmacological responsiveness to the therapeutic, and provide meaningful information on CNS distribution, safety, and tolerability.


In practice, an effective species-selection strategy evaluates four key areas:


**Biological and anatomical relevance to humans.** The degree to which a species reflects human CNS anatomy and physiology is a fundamental consideration. Brain size, structural organization, and neuroanatomical complexity can have a significant impact on therapeutic distribution, target engagement, and the performance of surgical or device-based delivery approaches. These factors become particularly important for direct-to-CNS administration routes, such as intrathecal, intracerebroventricular, or intraparenchymal delivery. Species-specific differences in BBB biology, including tight junction composition, transporter activity, and receptor expression, can also influence CNS penetration and the translational relevance of nonclinical findings.


**Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution.** Understanding how a therapeutic distributes, persists, and clears within the CNS is essential for predicting clinical performance. Pharmacokinetic and biodistribution profiles can vary substantially across species due to differences in brain size, cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, metabolism, and clearance mechanisms. Exposure levels and tissue distribution patterns observed in one species may not fully translate to humans, making it critical to evaluate these parameters in appropriately selected models before entering the clinic. These data help sponsors build confidence in dose selection, delivery strategies, and safety margins.


**Target biology and disease relevance.** The therapeutic target must be pharmacologically relevant in the chosen species. For biologics, oligonucleotides, and gene therapies, conservation of target receptors, gene sequences, and cellular pathways is often a prerequisite for generating meaningful efficacy and safety data. Without adequate cross-species homology, findings may be difficult to interpret or defend to regulators. Transgenic rodent models continue to play an important role in disease modeling and mechanistic studies, while larger animal models are frequently used to strengthen translational assessments of biodistribution, safety, and delivery feasibility.


**Route of administration feasibility.** Whenever possible, the nonclinical route of administration should mirror the intended clinical approach. If patients are expected to receive therapy through intrathecal, intracisternal, or intraparenchymal delivery, the selected species should support those procedures using clinically relevant dosing volumes, infusion rates, and delivery devices. This is particularly important for advanced therapeutics, where the route of administration can significantly influence biodistribution, target engagement, and safety outcomes.


No single species can answer every question relevant to CNS drug development. Each model brings distinct strengths and limitations, and the most informative preclinical programs leverage those complementary advantages. Rodents provide speed, scalability, and valuable mechanistic insight; larger species offer greater translational relevance for evaluating delivery strategies, biodistribution, and safety. Rather than searching for a single “best” model, sponsors are increasingly building integrated, bespoke programs that combine multiple species to generate the comprehensive evidence needed for clinical development.


### 3. The Göttingen minipig: Translational advantages and therapeutic fit


The Göttingen minipig has emerged as an increasingly attractive option for CNS-focused drug development, driven by its combination of translational relevance and practical advantages, particularly for evaluating direct-to-CNS delivery strategies and advanced therapeutics.


**Brain and CSF characteristics that more closely approximate humans.** The minipig exhibits a gyrencephalic brain with white-matter organization that is more comparable to humans than that of rodents. CSF volume, turnover, and flow dynamics better reflect the physiological conditions encountered in the clinic, enabling more relevant assessments of CNS distribution following direct administration.


**Feasibility of clinically relevant delivery procedures.** The size of the minipig supports evaluation of catheters, infusion systems, implantable pumps, and convection-enhanced delivery (CED) technologies at dimensions and dosing parameters that are difficult to replicate in smaller species. This makes the model particularly valuable for device-supported CNS delivery programs where procedural fidelity directly informs clinical translation.


**Practical advantages relative to NHPs.** For studies that do not require NHP-specific biology or endpoints, minipigs can provide a scientifically robust alternative with reduced cost and supply considerations.


**Growing regulatory familiarity and acceptance.** Regulatory agencies, including the FDA and EMA, are becoming increasingly receptive to minipig-generated data when supported by a clear scientific rationale. This is particularly true for studies where brain size, CSF volume, anatomical scaling, or delivery-device performance are critical considerations, and where the target is sufficiently conserved to support meaningful interpretation of the data.


The Göttingen minipig is not a replacement for NHPs in every CNS program. Rather, it is a fit-for-purpose model that can answer specific translational questions with a level of relevance that exceeds what is possible in rodents alone. Three therapeutic modality categories align especially well with its strengths:


**Gene therapies.** The minipig provides a valuable platform for assessing CNS biodistribution, transgene expression patterns, and expression durability following direct CNS administration. It also supports evaluation of local and regional neurotoxicity and the impact of clinically relevant dosing volumes and infusion parameters, translational questions that cannot be fully addressed in rodent models alone.


**Antisense oligonucleotides and other oligonucleotide-based therapies.** Because the minipig’s CSF compartment more closely reflects human physiology, it is well suited for evaluating intrathecal therapeutic delivery, CSF pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, target engagement, and chronic CNS tolerability. These characteristics make the model particularly useful for optimizing dosing strategies and understanding therapeutic exposure throughout the CNS.


**CNS-directed biologics and cell therapies.** For programs involving proteins, antibodies, engineered biologics or cell-based therapeutics, the minipig enables evaluation of delivery feasibility, procedural performance and local tissue tolerance using clinically relevant administration devices, infusion systems and dosing volumes. This provides critical information that can help refine delivery approaches before clinical testing.


Across all three modality classes, the common theme is the need to de-risk not only the therapeutic itself, but also the delivery strategy. In many CNS programs, successful clinical translation depends as much on achieving the right distribution, exposure, and administration parameters as it does on the properties of the molecule or cell product.


### 4. Dosing and route-of-administration strategies in the minipig


For CNS-targeted therapies, route of administration is often as important as the therapy itself. Selecting the appropriate delivery pathway requires balancing biodistribution goals, procedural complexity, safety considerations, and clinical translatability. In the Göttingen minipig, several established administration routes enable investigators to evaluate delivery strategies under conditions that closely mirror clinical practice.


**Intracerebroventricular (ICV) administration.** ICV delivery involves direct administration into the brain’s ventricular system and is supported by well-established neurosurgical techniques in the minipig. This approach can achieve broad CNS distribution and is commonly used when widespread exposure to CSF-bathed tissues is desired. However, because the delivery device must traverse brain parenchyma to access the ventricles, there is potential for tissue injury, procedure-related inflammation, and enhanced immune responses that may influence study outcomes.


**Intra-cisterna magna (ICM) administration.** ICM delivery provides direct access to the CSF space at the base of the brain and can support effective distribution to hindbrain and posterior CNS structures. Compared with ICV, ICM delivery avoids passage through brain parenchyma, reducing some procedural risks. However, this route presents its own technical challenges, including the potential for injury to nearby brainstem structures, and its limited use in routine clinical practice may affect translational relevance for some programs.


**Intrathecal (IT) administration.** Intrathecal delivery, typically through lumbar injection or catheter-based administration into the CSF space surrounding the spinal cord, is among the most clinically relevant routes for many CNS therapies. It is less invasive than intracranial approaches and is frequently used in outpatient settings. The primary challenge with intrathecal administration is achieving sufficient biodistribution throughout the CNS. Distribution can be influenced by CSF flow dynamics, dose volume, infusion parameters, and therapeutic properties. Sponsors must also carefully evaluate dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and spinal cord toxicity, which have become important considerations for several advanced therapeutic modalities, including antisense oligonucleotides and AAV-based gene therapies.


Beyond CSF-based approaches, MRI-guided stereotactic delivery is becoming increasingly important for therapies intended for specific brain regions. In the minipig, advanced neuronavigation platforms—such as ClearPoint Pre-Clinical Orchestra™ and Rogue Research Brainsight®, enable highly precise targeting of deep brain structures, including the putamen. Real-time imaging guidance provides confirmation of catheter placement and infusion parameters, improving confidence in dose delivery and target engagement. This capability is particularly valuable for evaluating gene therapies, cell therapies, and other regionally administered therapeutics that require accurate parenchymal delivery. Importantly, the anatomical scale of the minipig allows investigators to assess surgical techniques, delivery devices, and imaging workflows in a manner that closely resembles human stereotactic procedures.


Regardless of the route selected, the guiding principle remains the same: whenever possible, the nonclinical study should replicate the intended clinical approach. Route of administration can have a profound impact on biodistribution, efficacy, and safety, making translational fidelity a critical component of study design and a factor regulators will scrutinize during IND review.


### 5. Gene therapy research in the minipig: Emerging evidence


As interest in the Göttingen minipig continues to grow, sponsors increasingly want to understand whether the model can generate the biodistribution and transgene expression data needed to support CNS gene therapy development. Emerging evidence from both systemic and direct-to-brain delivery approaches indicates that when the vector, target region, and route of administration are appropriately aligned, the minipig can provide highly informative translational data.


**Systemic administration.** Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using the minipig to evaluate systemically administered adeno-associated virus (AAV) therapies targeting the CNS. In work by Lin and colleagues, intravenous administration of AAV9-CMV-GFP through the auricular vein of neonatal Bama pigs resulted in widespread and well-tolerated transgene expression throughout the brain and spinal cord. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed robust GFP expression in multiple CNS regions, including the striatum and hippocampus. These results establish the minipig as a viable model for systemically delivered AAV programs targeting the CNS, adding to the growing body of data supporting its use in IND-enabling studies.


**Direct parenchymal administration.** The minipig has also demonstrated value for evaluating regionally targeted gene delivery within the brain. Studies investigating direct administration of AAV9-mCherry to the putamen have shown expression patterns that closely parallel those observed in NHPs. Strong transgene expression was detected within the targeted putamen, with measurable but significantly lower distribution extending into connected neuroanatomical regions, including the caudate nucleus, thalamus, substantia nigra, and other functionally linked structures. Biodistribution assessments revealed a significantly smaller number of vector genome copies in expected peripheral tissues, including the liver and spleen, as well as detectable signal within dorsal root ganglia and spinal cord segments. These are precisely the tissues that regulators expect sponsors to evaluate when characterizing systemic exposure, off-target distribution, and potential safety liabilities in IND-enabling studies.


Taken together, these findings support a growing body of evidence that the minipig can serve as a clinically relevant large-animal model for CNS gene therapy development. They also help explain why regulatory agencies are becoming increasingly receptive to minipig data when supported by a strong scientific rationale. For sponsors seeking to evaluate CNS delivery, regional targeting, device performance, and transgene distribution in a large-animal model, the Göttingen minipig is emerging as a valuable translational bridge between rodent studies and clinical development.


### Designing successful minipig CNS studies


The value of a minipig CNS study depends on thoughtful study design and a clear scientific rationale. Programs that withstand regulatory scrutiny are typically built around the specific translational questions the model is intended to answer. They are not based on using the minipig simply as an alternative to another species.


Several characteristics consistently distinguish successful programs:


**A scientifically justified species-selection strategy.** The rationale for choosing the minipig should be established before study initiation and clearly linked to the therapeutic’s unique risk profile, including the vector, transgene, modality, target biology, and intended route of administration. Regulators expect species selection to be driven by scientific relevance rather than operational convenience, and programs that cannot articulate this rationale are likely to encounter questions during IND review.


**Alignment with the intended clinical delivery approach.** Whether the program involves ICV, ICM, IT, or MRI-guided intraparenchymal administration, the nonclinical study should closely replicate the planned clinical procedure. Whenever possible, the same class of delivery devices, catheters, pumps, and infusion systems intended for clinical use should be incorporated into the study design.


**A risk-based endpoint strategy.** Robust CNS programs assess more than therapeutic exposure alone. Endpoints should be selected to address the specific risks associated with the modality and should typically include CNS biodistribution, local and regional neurotoxicity, neuroinflammation, gliosis, tissue integrity, and pharmacokinetic assessments. For CSF-delivered therapeutics, repeat CSF sampling can provide valuable insights into exposure and durability. Long-term follow-up may also be necessary for chronic or progressive neurodegenerative indications.


**Integrated evaluation of delivery devices.** For therapeutics that rely on specialized administration systems, device performance should be assessed as part of the nonclinical program rather than deferred until later stages of development. Evaluating catheters, implantable pumps, and CED systems within the intended study species helps identify procedural and performance risks before clinical implementation, avoiding costly surprises during first-in-human dosing.


**Clinically relevant, translatable readouts.** The most informative studies prioritize endpoints that support human risk assessment and clinical decision-making. Findings should help predict safety, distribution, and performance in patients rather than simply generating data that is easy to collect in a preclinical setting.


When applied appropriately, the Göttingen minipig occupies an important and well-defined middle ground between rodent models and NHPs. It offers greater anatomical and physiological relevance for CNS drug delivery than rodents, while providing practical, ethical, and operational advantages. Its value lies in answering specific translational questions related to CNS delivery, biodistribution, device performance and safety in a large-animal setting. These questions are increasingly central to the development of gene therapies, oligonucleotide therapeutics, biologics and cell therapies, for which successful clinical outcomes depend on delivering the therapeutic to the appropriate CNS compartment.


Ultimately, the most successful programs are those that treat delivery as a critical component of the therapeutic strategy, not an afterthought. By enabling clinically relevant evaluation of both the therapeutic and its administration approach, the Göttingen minipig can help bridge the gap between proof-of-concept research and clinical translation, providing the evidence needed to move promising CNS therapies forward with greater confidence.
