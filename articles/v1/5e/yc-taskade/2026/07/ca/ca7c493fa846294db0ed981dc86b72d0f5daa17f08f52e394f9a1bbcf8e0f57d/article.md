## 📜 Origin of the Per-Technician Seat Tax


Field-service management software, often shortened to FSM, grew up around shops big enough to employ a customer service desk. Per-technician licensing made sense when the vendor funded a deep mobile app, a call-recording stack, a pricebook, and an accounting bridge. The seat fee bought infrastructure a two-truck shop could never build.


Three things shifted for small crews:


1. **Everyone became a data entry point.** Homeowners submit forms, techs update statuses, and the owner reads the board at 7 a.m. Paying a full license for every person who occasionally changes a status feels like a tax on visibility, not a payment for capability.
2. **Texting and spreadsheets became the real system of record.** Most shops under ten techs already coordinate in group chat plus a shared sheet. What they lack is not a suite. It is one board where a job points at a customer and an invoice points at a closed job.
3. **App builders collapsed the cost of a custom board.** In 2026 you can generate a work-order app from a prompt, then rename a column because your techs say "waiting on parts" and not "on hold." That moves the decision from "which FSM brand?" to "do we rent a schema or own one?"


None of that makes ServiceTitan wrong. It is a business model matched to packaged depth, and it is honest about who it serves. Small crews feel the mismatch when the modules they open daily are four, and the modules they pay for are forty.


That loop is what an owned board is built to hold. GPS breadcrumbs, card processing, and the general ledger are adjacent systems that stay where they already work.

1. **Pricebook and membership depth.** Flat-rate pricing with good-better-best options, tied to memberships and renewals, is a mature system inside ServiceTitan. Rebuilding it is a project, not an afternoon.

3. **A field app built for techs who lose signal.** Enterprise FSM mobile clients are funded to survive dead zones, barcode scans, and a tech who has never opened a settings menu.
