## 🧰 What Field Crews Actually Need From a Schedule


A field crew schedule answers five questions every morning. Hourly shift software answers two of them well and forces workarounds for the rest.


1. Who is on which truck today, and who is covering the callout?
2. Which jobs are committed to which arrival windows?
3. What does the tech need to know before knocking — gate code, dog, unit model, last visit?
4. Which customers are waiting on a status update, an estimate, or an invoice?
5. What slipped yesterday and has to be re-promised today?


Questions 1 and 2 are shift-shaped. Questions 3, 4, and 5 are job-shaped and customer-shaped. That is the whole split. A barista shift has no address, no equipment history, and no client who will call at 2pm asking where the truck is.

## 📜 Why This Problem Exists in the First Place


[Homebase](https://joinhomebase.com/) was designed around a shift. That was the right unit for the businesses it grew up serving: cafes, retail floors, restaurants, front counters. The schedule is a grid of hours, the compliance surface is breaks and overtime, and the payoff is a clean handoff to payroll.


Three things pulled field crews into that grid anyway.


1. **Field crews are small and price-sensitive.** A four-truck HVAC shop does not want a five-figure field-service suite. Hourly scheduling software is cheap, familiar, and available today, so crews adopt it for the schedule and improvise the rest.
2. **The improvisation always lands in group chat.** The shift grid holds who works. The group text holds which job, which address, which gate code, and what the customer was promised. Two systems, one of which nobody can search.
3. **The customer is nowhere in the schedule.** Retail scheduling has no customer object because the customer walks in. Field work is the opposite: the customer is the reason the truck moves, and the customer's history is the difference between a 20-minute call and a return visit.


That drift is the real cost. Not the seat price — the fact that dispatch truth lives in a thread and evaporates when the person who sent it takes a day off. In 2026 you can generate a board that holds jobs, customers, and crew assignment together, then reshape the fields yourself instead of filing a template request.


Every box after "assign" is job-shaped. That is the loop a dispatch board owns and a shift grid does not.

## 🛟 Who Should Stay on Homebase (Honestly)


Homebase is genuinely good at things Taskade Genesis does not attempt. Stay when any of these is the requirement you refuse to compromise:


- **Time clocks you can defend.** Homebase's clock-in, break tracking, and timesheet-to-payroll handoff is a mature, purpose-built path. If your labor compliance exposure runs through punches, that is a real product doing real work.

## 🧪 A Day in the Life on an Owned Dispatch Board


**06:50** The dispatcher opens the Board view. Yesterday's two unfinished jobs are at the top with age and reason. Nothing to reconstruct from a group text.


**07:10** The morning automation has already sent each crew its run sheet: three jobs, addresses, windows, and the access notes that used to live in someone's head.


**08:35** A customer calls with a no-heat emergency. Intake becomes a job in seconds. A triage agent suggests "emergency / HVAC / north route," a human confirms and assigns, and the customer gets a window by text.


**10:15** A tech closes a repair with three photos and a note that the capacitor is aging. That note lands on the **equipment** record, not just the job — so the next visit starts informed.

**17:30** Hours for the day go to payroll on the payroll provider, exactly as before. Nothing about the dispatch board touched that path.

## 🧠 How to Choose in One Afternoon


1. **Count hourly punches vs job dispatches** you run in a typical week. Whichever number is bigger picks your product category.
2. **List the four nouns** you cannot lose: customer, job, crew assignment, client visibility.
