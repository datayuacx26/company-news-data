## ❄️ What HVAC Shops Actually Need From a Dispatch Board


An HVAC shop with three to twelve techs does not need a field-service suite. It needs one board that survives a 96-degree Tuesday. The daily loop is narrow and repeats:


1. A homeowner calls or texts with no cool air.
2. Somebody triages: emergency today, or a slot Thursday morning.
3. A tech gets the address, gate code, equipment model, and filter size before the van leaves.
4. The tech closes with photos, refrigerant readings, and parts used.
5. Office staff sends the invoice and books the next maintenance visit.
6. An owner asks "what is still open?" and gets an answer without opening four apps.


That spine is a dispatch board. It is not a call center, a price book engine, or a fleet telematics platform. Confusing the two is how five-truck shops end up paying per-tech enterprise rates for a whiteboard replacement.

## 📜 Why HVAC Dispatch Breaks on Generic Tools


HVAC work mixes emergency no-cool calls, seasonal tune-ups, install days, warranty callbacks, and duct work. A board built for generic "jobs" misses the five fields that decide whether a truck roll succeeds: equipment model, serial, filter size, refrigerant type, and permit status. A tech who has to phone the office for a filter size has already lost fifteen minutes of billable time.


Three structural things went wrong, and none of them are anybody's fault:


1. **Field-service suites were priced for call centers.** Per-technician licensing made sense when the vendor funded a deep mobile app, a price book engine, marketing attribution, and a dispatcher's console. A shop with one owner-dispatcher funds all of that and uses a third of it.
2. **Spreadsheets have no concurrency model.** Two people editing the same Tuesday afternoon slot is not an error a shared sheet can catch. It is a callback, an angry homeowner, and a tech driving across town for nothing.
3. **The pain moved from scheduling to context.** In 2026 the bottleneck is rarely "who is free at 2pm." It is "does this tech know the unit is a 2011 heat pump in a crawlspace with a locked gate." Context lives in records, not in calendar blocks.


Spreadsheets fail specifically when:


- Two techs grab the same emergency slot and nobody notices until dispatch
- Office staff cannot see invoice status beside the job
- Homeowners call because there is no client-facing status page
- Maintenance contracts live in a separate tab nobody opens
- Equipment history is tribal knowledge that leaves when a tech leaves

## 🗄️ The Data Model Behind an HVAC Dispatch Board


Most dispatch failures are modeling failures. If equipment hangs off the job instead of the customer, you lose history the moment the job closes. Model it once, correctly, and the board answers questions you did not think to ask. Seven record types carry an HVAC shop, and the arrows are what keep history from evaporating:


Three modeling rules that save you a rebuild:


1. **Equipment belongs to the customer, not the job.** A 2011 heat pump outlives fifty service calls. Hang it off the address and every future tech inherits the history.
2. **PM visits are their own records.** A maintenance contract is a promise; a visit is a truck roll. Keep them separate so the daily board stays readable and the contract stays auditable.
3. **Estimates convert, they do not duplicate.** One record moves quote to job to invoice so nobody re-types a price. That is what the[estimate to invoice pipeline](https://www.taskade.com/generate/invoice/estimate-to-invoice) and[job to invoice flow](https://www.taskade.com/generate/invoice/job-to-invoice) exist for.
