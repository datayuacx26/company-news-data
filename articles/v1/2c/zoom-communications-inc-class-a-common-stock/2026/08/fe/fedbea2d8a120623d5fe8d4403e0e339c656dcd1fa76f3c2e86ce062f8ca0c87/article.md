# 5 best practices to make collaboration finally click

Ask most IT leaders whether their organization has capable collaboration technology, and the answer is yes. They've got phones, they've got chat, they've got Zoom Meetings, they've got AI capabilities, document tools, and workflow automation. What's harder to find is an environment where all of it works together in a way that's intuitive for employees and manageable for IT.


Research shows employees use fewer than a third of available collaboration features during meetings, and only half of the features available after them.* Most organizations don't have a capability gap on paper. In practice, the gap is strategic.


Organizations that build a deliberate collaboration strategy — one that defines how tools connect, how AI gets governed, and how employees actually adopt new ways of working — tend to create environments that get stronger over time. The ones that don't keep adding complexity and wondering why nothing feels simpler.


Here are five principles that separate the two.


## 1. Stop organizing your stack by tool. Organize it by how work actually moves.


Vendors have spent the last few years racing to connect their platforms into something more than a collection of features. The result is that the collaboration landscape has reorganized itself around workflows, not categories. The strategic question for IT isn't which tools you have anymore. It's whether they're connected in a way that reflects how work actually flows through your organization.


Before evaluating platforms or adding capabilities, map the workflows your employees depend on. Where does work start? Where does it stall? Where does context get lost between tools? Those answers should drive your platform decisions, not the other way around.


## 2. Govern AI before your employees do it for you


The AI adoption question has already been answered.[Employees are using AI in their collaboration tools whether IT planned for it or not.](https://www.zoom.com/en/blog/ai-note-taking-governance-for-it-teams/) The question is whether IT has a governance framework in place before that usage gets ahead of policy.


That means deciding which AI capabilities are available to which employees, how AI-generated content is handled under your data policies, and how you maintain visibility across tools. A platform that centralizes AI governance gives IT the controls to answer those questions. A patchwork of plugins and browser extensions tends to answer them for you, usually at the worst possible time.


## 3. Make integration a baseline requirement, not a bonus


No business is starting from zero. Your employees already use productivity suites, CRM platforms, and communication tools they depend on daily. A modern collaboration strategy connects those investments rather than replacing them.


Treat integration as a baseline requirement when evaluating any collaboration platform. The right question isn't whether a platform can integrate with your existing stack. It's how deeply, how reliably, and how much administrative overhead that integration creates over time. A platform that plays well with everything else you run compounds in value. One that requires constant maintenance to stay connected is just another thing to manage.


## 4. Adoption is a design problem, not a launch problem


Between a third and a half of available collaboration functionality, much of it AI-native, goes untapped.* Adoption was never truly designed, and that's one of the main problems.


Effective adoption strategies are persona-specific. Executives, managers, and individual contributors use collaboration tools differently and value different capabilities. A rollout that treats all employees the same tends to produce the same result: a few power users and a lot of idle features collecting dust in a settings menu nobody opens.


Build adoption into the platform decision. Ask vendors how they support change management, what in-product guidance looks like, and how they measure feature utilization over time, not just license counts.


## 5. There's likely more value in your collaboration environment than you're capturing


Most IT teams evaluate collaboration platforms through meeting counts and uptime metrics: a narrow lens that misses most of where value actually lives.


A more complete view includes time saved across the meeting lifecycle, reductions in repeated meetings, speed of decision-making, and how employees actually experience the tools day to day. Many CIOs acknowledge this gap. They know there's more value to measure, but they don't have the instrumentation to capture it.*


Platforms that provide visibility into how collaboration is actually being used, not just whether it's being used, give IT the data to demonstrate value, defend investments, and know where to improve before employees start working around the gaps.
