# The new rules of collaboration for IT leaders

Most enterprise collaboration environments didn't start with a blueprint. They started with a few urgent procurement decisions, a decade of incremental additions, and a lot of "good enough for now." Nobody designed the current state. It accumulated.


That was manageable when collaboration meant meetings plus email. But meetings, chat, phone, docs, AI, and workflows now operate as a single connected layer. Employees already treat them that way. They move from a meeting summary to a chat thread to a shared document to a task list without thinking about which tool they're in. IT governance, for the most part, hasn't caught up with that reality.


The gap between how work actually happens and how IT manages the tools behind it can create fragmentation, inconsistent security posture, and blind spots that compound quietly over time. This piece maps what changed, why it matters, and what a collaboration environment built for this reality actually requires.


## How collaboration stacks got so fragmented


Most enterprises didn't meticulously design their collaboration stack. They inherited one. Departmental preferences, vendor bundling, and organic employee adoption produced today's environment: Zoom for meetings, Slack or Microsoft Teams for chat, Google Docs or SharePoint for documents, and a growing layer of AI tools that nobody in IT approved.


According to BetterCloud's 2026 State of SaaS report, the average organization now deploys 27 AI-powered SaaS applications, making up roughly 22% of its overall software portfolio. The total number of apps per organization grew 11% year over year. And those are just the apps IT knows about. A 2026 Freshworks survey found that 71% of IT leaders acknowledge unapproved AI use is already common across their organizations, even as 92% believe they have full visibility into the tools employees are using.


The cost of this fragmentation can go beyond tool sprawl. Decisions made in meetings don't flow into project systems. Chat threads contain context that don't reach the people who need it. Documents live in silos that don't connect to the conversations that produced them. The tools coexist, but they don't connect. They share an org chart, not a data layer.


That distinction matters, because it changes what IT actually needs to solve for.


## Why IT's collaboration mandate looks different now


IT used to manage collaboration platforms. Keep them running, keep them patched, keep them licensed. That job still exists, but the surrounding scope has expanded significantly.


AI capabilities are commonly embedded inside collaboration platforms, generating content, summarizing conversations, and acting on data in real time. Governance scope now extends to AI-generated outputs, not just stored files. Employees expect seamless movement between meetings, chat, docs, and task management. When those handoffs break, some may route around IT with personal tools and unvetted apps. Security and compliance considerations include how AI processes meeting content, who can access summaries, and where generated data is retained.


IT's mandate today is to enable a more governed, integrated collaboration layer where communication, content, and AI share centralized policies and admin controls. That's a fundamentally different scope than managing a set of standalone platforms. And most collaboration environments weren't built to support it.


## What a modern collaboration environment actually requires


A collaboration environment built for the current reality has a few defining characteristics. None of them are about having the most features. All of them are about coherence.


- The first is **a shared governance layer** . AI, meetings, chat, docs, and workflows should operate within a shared governance framework that includes centralized admin controls, retention policies, and role-based access controls (RBAC).
- The second is **cross-platform awareness** . Enterprises don't run entirely on one vendor. The right collaboration environment acknowledges that and connects to Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Salesforce, ServiceNow, and the rest of the stack rather than ignoring them. A platform that only accounts for its own ecosystem leaves the majority of your collaboration surface unmanaged.
- The third is **AI that lives inside the collaboration flow** . AI capabilities that summarize meetings, compose messages, and capture action items should operate within the same platform employees already use for communication and content creation. When AI use requires a separate product, a standalone IT security review process, and a separate budget line, adoption fragments and governance gaps can widen.
- The fourth is **workflow continuity** . Meeting outcomes, chat decisions, and document approvals should be able to trigger downstream actions like task creation, CRM updates, and follow-up scheduling without manual re-entry or context loss.


These describe what's possible today. The question is whether your current environment delivers these capabilities in a coherent way, or whether you're assembling them piecemeal from disconnected tools.


## Consolidation without the rip-and-replace


"Consolidation" can make IT leaders uneasy because it implies migration, disruption, and political battles over entrenched tools. But the version of consolidation that matters here isn't about replacing everything with one vendor.


It's about creating a governable center of gravity. Keep the tools that work. Integrate them into a platform that allows for unified governance, a shared AI layer, and centralized admin controls. Consolidate where it reduces complexity, especially around AI, where ungoverned tools can create the most exposure.


Many organizations run Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, or both. A collaboration platform that ignores that reality isn't solving the problem. The right platform works alongside those environments and extends AI capabilities across them rather than competing with them. The goal is fewer ungoverned tools, fewer disconnected workflows, and fewer blind spots in how collaboration data moves through the organization.


## The gaps worth finding now


A few questions can reveal how much distance exists between your current collaboration environment and one that was intentionally designed.


Do you have visibility into which AI tools employees are using for meeting capture, summarization, and content generation? Can your collaboration environment move meeting outcomes into enterprise systems like your CRM, project management platform, or HR tools without manual re-entry? Are your AI governance policies applied consistently across every collaboration modality, or are they configured tool by tool? And if an employee leaves, do you know where their meeting data, AI-generated content, and chat context lives?


These aren't gotcha questions. They're the gaps that separate a collaboration environment that evolved from one that was designed. Closing them starts with understanding where you are today.
