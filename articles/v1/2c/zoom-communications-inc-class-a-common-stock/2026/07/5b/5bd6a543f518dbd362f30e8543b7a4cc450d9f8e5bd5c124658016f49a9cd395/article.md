# The collaboration environment got more intricate. Here's what's in it now.

Many IT leaders still organize their collaboration stack by category. Meeting software. Chat app. Phone system. Doc editor. Separate contracts, separate admins, separate strategies. It made sense when each tool had a single job.


But employees stopped working that way a while ago. A meeting generates a summary. That summary becomes a shared doc. The doc triggers a task. The task gets discussed in a chat thread. The chat thread references the original recording. That's not four tools doing four jobs — that's one workflow running across a platform, or trying to run across four disconnected ones.


The gap between how employees actually work and how IT manages the tools they work in has been widening for years. Let's find out what's on the other side of that gap.


## Meetings are still the center. The ecosystem around them expanded.


Meetings remain the gravitational center of enterprise work. That hasn't changed. What changed is the ecosystem orbiting them.


- Before the meeting: pre-reads shared in a channel, agenda docs, async video updates.
- During: real-time transcription, live AI summaries, in-meeting collaboration on shared files.
- After: auto-generated action items, follow-up threads, searchable recordings that can become institutional memory.


According to Deloitte, employees spend 25 hours per week on meeting-related work — and fewer than 10 of those hours are spent in the actual meeting.* The rest happens in the tools surrounding it. Which means the platform hosting your meetings can now help determine how much of that surrounding work is captured, connected, and usable — or lost between tabs.


## Chat stopped being casual


There was a time when enterprise chat was the informal channel. Quick questions. Birthday announcements. The occasional GIF. That era is over.


In many organizations, chat is where decisions get documented in real time. Where project updates live. Where files get shared, reviewed, and approved without anyone needing to open their email. It's a persistent workspace with a search bar and an institutional memory — if the platform supports it.


The shift matters for IT because persistent chat creates a data layer whether you planned for it or not. Conversations become searchable knowledge. Context accumulates. But only if chat is connected to the rest of the collaboration environment. Otherwise you've got another silo — just one that moves faster than the others.


## Shadow AI is what can happen when you don't offer the real thing


This one's straightforward. Employees aren't debating whether AI belongs in their collaboration tools. According to the 2025 McKinsey State of AI survey, 88% are already using it in at least one business function.† Meeting summaries, AI note-takers, smart compose, AI-generated follow-ups, voice translation. The question for IT isn't adoption. It's governance.


When AI lives inside the collaboration platform, IT is better positioned to manage access, set policies, and maintain visibility across the organization. When AI comes from a patchwork of browser extensions and third-party plugins, IT may not find out about it later — potentially from a security audit.


A plugin ecosystem gives you AI features. A platform gives you AI features and the ability to govern them. For ITDMs dealing with shadow AI (for many organizations, this is an increasingly common reality), the difference between those two things is the difference between a strategy and a cleanup job.


## Docs and workflows help close the loop


The newest layer in the collaboration stack: documents, whiteboards, and workflow automation living inside the platform itself. Not as replacements for Microsoft Office or Google Workspace — those aren't going anywhere, and nobody's asking them to. But as the connective tissue that turns meeting outputs into trackable, actionable work.


When a meeting summary generates a doc, and that doc creates tasks, and those tasks update in a shared workspace — that's a closed loop. When the summary lives in one tool, the doc lives in another, and the tasks live in a third, that's a plugin chain. It works until it doesn't. And IT is the one who gets the call when it doesn't.


## Skip the vendor checklist. Ask these instead.


If you're evaluating whether your collaboration environment is keeping up, here are the questions worth asking:


- **Does the platform connect the full workflow?** Meeting to summary to doc to task to follow-up — does that happen natively, or does it depend on integrations you have to maintain?
- **Can you govern AI centrally?** Or is AI access fragmented across plugins, browser extensions, and tools your employees found on their own?
- **Does it integrate without requiring a lifestyle change?** A modern collaboration platform should connect to Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Salesforce, and the rest of your stack — without asking employees to abandon what already works.
- **Can it grow without adding vendors?** Phone, contact center, events, webinars — if those needs emerge, can the platform expand, or are you back to procurement?
