# Enterprise collaboration quietly became infrastructure, and it's time to catch up

Most enterprises still treat collaboration as a category of tools. Meeting software over here. Chat apps over there. Phone system in the corner. Document editors somewhere else entirely. Separate line items, separate budgets, separate strategies.


But the way work actually happens has already moved past that framing. Employees move between a meeting, a chat thread, a shared document, and an AI-generated summary without pausing to think about which product they're in. The meeting generates context. The context feeds AI. The AI produces deliverables. The deliverables trigger workflows. It's one continuous flow, and the platform beneath it determines whether it holds together or falls apart at every seam.


That's what it means for collaboration to become a communication layer. And the distinction matters more than most IT strategies currently account for.


## Meetings, chat, and docs stopped being support tools and started being where work happens


For a long time, collaboration tools existed to support work that happened elsewhere. You had a meeting to discuss a project, then went back to your desk to do the actual work. The meeting was overhead. The chat was coordination. The real output happened in other systems.


That model is fading fast. According to a Deloitte study of 1,000 technology and business leaders, employees spend 25 hours per week preparing, executing, or following up on meetings, and fewer than 10 of those hours are spent in the actual meeting.* The majority of meeting-related work now happens before and after the conversation itself. Today, the meeting is where the decision gets made. The chat thread is where the project gets unblocked. The AI summary is the deliverable that the next team acts on. Collaboration has shifted from a supporting function to the operational layer where outcomes are produced.


This changes the calculus for IT. When collaboration was overhead, managing it as a set of disconnected tools was fine. When collaboration is the layer where decisions, context, and deliverables are created, the connections between those tools become critical infrastructure. Treating them as separate line items starts to cost you in ways that don't show up on a vendor invoice.


## Your meeting platform now decides what happens after the meeting


Meetings used to be self-contained events. Someone took notes. The notes lived in a doc somewhere. The meeting platform didn't matter much beyond audio and video quality.


That's no longer the case. Meetings can now generate structured data: summaries, action items, transcripts, and key themes and decision points. That data feeds AI. AI produces follow-up content, task assignments, CRM updates, and briefing docs. The meeting isn't the end of the workflow anymore. It's the starting line.


The platform where the meeting happens helps determine how much of that downstream value you can actually capture. If the meeting lives in one system and your documents, chat, phone, and workflows live in another, handoffs between them can create friction and information gaps. Context degrades. AI has less to work with. Workflows require manual re-entry instead of running automatically.


Over 90% of survey respondents reported using two or more collaboration platforms regularly.* Many run Microsoft 365 for productivity and document management. Many of those same organizations choose a different platform for meetings and real-time communication, because the meeting experience matters and because the platform that captures meeting context needs to connect it back into the broader ecosystem, including Microsoft. The strongest outcomes come from platforms that work with your existing investments rather than asking you to abandon them.


## The difference between tools that coexist and tools that connect


When collaboration operates as a true unified context layer, a few things change.


Conversations gain continuity across modalities. A discussion that starts in a meeting continues in chat, produces a document, and triggers a workflow without anyone needing to re-enter context or switching between disconnected systems. The thread holds.


AI gets the full picture. Instead of drawing from a single app's data, AI capabilities pull from meetings, chat, phone calls, and documents across the organization. The richer the context AI can access, the more useful its outputs can become. An AI tool that only sees your meetings, not your chats, documents, or calls, has a partial view of how your organization actually communicates.


Interoperability is structural, not cosmetic. The communication layer connects to the productivity and business applications your organization already uses, whether that's Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, Salesforce, or ServiceNow. It extends what you have rather than replacing it.


Customer conversations, partner interactions, supplier coordination: enterprise communication doesn't stop at the org boundary, and the platform should be built to support all of it.


Some organizations are already operating this way. The question is whether your current stack supports it or quietly works against it.


## The window to design your collaboration environment is open

Organizations that recognize this shift early are building connected environments, while others are still managing disconnected ones.
