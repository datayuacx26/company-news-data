Managing the psychological toll of working on existential risks \[00:01:00\]

But taking these issues seriously is very difficult for multiple reasons, including for me personally. They’re hard emotionally: we’re talking about suffering and death on an enormous scale. They’re also really hard to truly comprehend: I think the human brain struggles to actually fathom the things that we at 80K think might be true — everyone on Earth might die in the next decade or sooner, humans and other sentient beings might suffer extraordinarily at some point (or already). Even in the best case, the world might transform, maybe for the better, but beyond recognition in the span of a few years — which, despite that being a positive thing, I still find very scary.


I think all of that makes thinking about these issues emotionally fraught and also epistemically fraught.


And some of these issues are further exacerbated when you decide to spend your career trying to solve these problems.

I guess to start, do any of these things resonate with you? Do you struggle with any of them personally?

Topics like animal welfare, the amount of suffering involved is staggering, and even thinking about it can be really disturbing. Luisa and Spencer surveyed people working on existential risk \[00:04:23\]


**Luisa Rodriguez:** You have a lot of expertise on self-improvement and trying to be a better, more flourishing person in general. But neither of us, I would say, are experts on the particular intersection of mental health and existential risk — though I think your book is super relevant, even though it’s not tailored to it.


But to get a better sense of what issues come up for people working on x-risk, we did a bit of research ourselves: I spoke to a couple of therapists who mostly treat patients working on existential risks, and your team ran a survey for people working on very important but neglected global problems — so x-risks, factory farming, building the EA \[effective altruism\] community — and then we’ve got your book that also, again, is super relevant.


To start, can you say a bit about the[survey you ran](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/ycnBHFYrGdLGSwpfq/the-psychological-challenges-of-high-impact-work-please) ?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Yeah. We surveyed people who work on these high-impact cause areas like existential risk and, as you mentioned, animal welfare and AI. We ended up getting about 60 to 110 respondents, depending on the question — because some were optional and some people dropped out during the survey.


The basic questions we were asking about are:


- Do you experience psychological challenges in this work?
- How big are those challenges?
- What are the nature of those challenges?
- What kinds of things have you done that you think might be helpful to yourself, or that you think might help others?


And yeah, I think we have a lot of really interesting results from that.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Can you say a bit about the headline results?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Yeah. I’ll first note that these tended to be younger people that responded, typically aged about 25 to 45, and typically working for less than 10 years in the field.


What we found is that very few people were suffering from what you might call a clinical disorder or generalised anxiety disorder or major depressive disorder, but the majority of people do seem to find this work psychologically challenging. So usually it doesn’t get to the point of full-blown depression or anxiety, but it doesn’t mean it’s easy. In fact, not only did most people say that they experience at least somewhat difficult psychological challenges around it, but many people have even considered leaving or reducing their work in it because of these challenges.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, that is kind of surprising, especially the last part: people have considered leaving their work but aren’t currently reporting depression and anxiety. Do you think there could be some other explanation, and people actually are experiencing some depression and anxiety? Or do you think maybe those two questionnaires aren’t really capturing what makes this so hard for people? Or it’s actually just not that hard, and people aren’t depressed or anxious as a result of these topics?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Well, generalised anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder reflect certain patterns in the mind that can be really painful and difficult, but they don’t reflect all that can be really difficult psychologically. I think what we’re seeing here is that people are struggling psychologically; they’re just not getting to the point of having those particular disorders.


When we ran the survey, we asked people which of a variety of different psychological challenges they face from a list. In fact, the median number selected was six different psychological challenges of doing this work. Forty-five percent said they at least considered cutting back or leaving, and also 52% said that it at least moderately hurts their effectiveness or productivity.


So I think the answer is that people experience a lot of psychological challenges; they just usually aren’t these full-blown disorders.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Right. Were there any other findings that felt particularly surprising to you?


**Spencer Greenberg:** One was that the most common challenges people said they faced — which are not necessarily the most severe, but it’s still interesting to look at — were difficulty switching off or having boundaries around work: 68% of people said they had trouble switching off after work. I think that actually really is an issue, because it elevates your stress level, makes it difficult to relax. I think that can actually, even if it doesn’t get to a full-blown disorder, have pretty negative consequences over the long run.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, yeah. What were the next couple of most common things?


**Spencer Greenberg:**[Imposter syndrome](https://80000hours.org/2022/04/imposter-syndrome/) was really common, at 60%. For those of you that don’t know, that’s when you kind of secretly harbour the fear that you’re not good enough, or that you’re there by some kind of mistake and you know you shouldn’t really be there.


And then we also have burnout: 59% said they had some experience with burnout, which can mean different things to different people. It can be a loss of motivation, it can just be feeling too stressed to function. So a wide range of things can contribute to burnout.


Fifty-six percent said they experience guilt, and then 54% said they experience anxiety or worry around their work.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, OK. Those just feel like very high numbers to me. We’ll talk about most of those things, and a couple of others.


Before we start getting into some of the concrete issues, can you say a bit more about the book? Kind of what it covers and your process for writing it?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Yeah. So our new book — and we would really love for you to check it out if it sounds interesting — is called[The 12 Levers: The Complete Psychological Toolkit For Improving Your Life](https://80k.info/12-levers) .


The idea behind it is that my coauthor, Jeremy Stevenson, and I studied over 100 of the most popular self-improvement books, and over 20 of the most used therapies — so cognitive behavioural therapy, but also many others. What we were looking for is them saying any kind of method or technique or telling you to do something to change your life, and we made a giant database of everything they said — which is almost 500 different techniques.


And then our question was: what are all of the psychological strategies for improving your life? What’s the high-level meta pattern here? And I was worried we’d have 50 different high-level patterns, but actually, to my surprise, we were able to boil it down to just 12 high-level psychological strategies for improving your life. And that’s where the name of the book comes from, *The 12 Levers* .


We also looked at the evidence for these different levers. And the goal of the book is to teach you about what each lever is, how you can use it effectively, and give you specific concrete steps to put it into action. Each lever has multiple techniques around it, sometimes there are even dozens, so we try to pick what we think are the most useful techniques around each lever.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, nice. Again, it’s helpful for all sorts of issues you might have, but I was kind of blown away when I was preparing for this interview. It’s just a very relevant toolkit for this subset of issues too.

How to sustain your motivation \[00:11:13\]


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Let’s talk about some of the specific issues that come up for people working on x-risks. Actually I kind of want to start with a slightly different point, which is that I actually had a high when I first started getting really bought into existential risks being real. I guess not just x-risks, but there being very pressing global problems that people weren’t tracking, and that plausibly could get a lot better.


I think it felt like I learned something important and true about the world, and as a result I got to have this big opportunity to work on something high stakes that not many people were taking seriously. It felt exciting and it felt like an opportunity. And I think the ideas are also just intrinsically interesting, almost like a good sci-fi novel.


So I think there’s something enjoyable about that, which I feel kind of strange and awkward about. But from the conversations I’ve had with therapists who work with people working on these issues, this is kind of a common experience. Did you have anything like this, or do you know people that did?


**Spencer Greenberg:** I think it’s actually really common, because if you have new information that makes you feel like you have special knowledge that’s important to the fate of the world, that’s an incredibly attractive idea. And we can see that idea growing in popularity in many different domains, right? There’s nothing unique about these ideas in that way; people are drawn to special knowledge.


In addition, it can really give meaning to life. Having the sense of, “There’s something important and meaningful for me to work on,” that can be really motivating. Furthermore, it can make you feel connected to a community if there’s other people involved; you can feel connected to that community through these shared ideas.


So yeah, I think in a way it can be really appealing, while it could also be frightening if you kind of grapple with the gravity of it all.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** According to some of the therapists I spoke to — and it seems like the survey backs this up — after learning about these ideas, starting to work on them, maybe some people have a bit of a high, but then people can start to experience a kind of chronic, low-grade emergency state. So kind of the[threat system being perpetually on](https://80000hours.org/after-hours-podcast/episodes/hannah-boettcher-mental-health-challenges/) , because you’re regularly aware that humanity is under threat, or that factory farming is causing enormous unimaginable suffering right now, and we’re not really on track to solve these issues.


I think this can come with kind of hypervigilance, and compulsive monitoring of news sites and X, and the sense that you can’t slow down or stop working — because things are on fire, and we don’t have that much time before things get catastrophically bad.


Do you personally experience anything like this?


**Spencer Greenberg:** I, for my whole life, have had the kind of mind that tends to look for things that could go wrong. So I’m a kind of worrier[personality](https://programs.clearerthinking.org/personality-test.html) ; that’s always something that I’ve dealt with. And over the years I’ve improved tremendously on this, and it’s much less of a problem for me now than it was when I was younger. You know, like on the weekends I’d have trouble even relaxing or enjoying the weekend because I’d stress myself out with my thoughts.


So I feel like I’ve found a number of tools that really help, but I do think that’s my fundamental disposition. Like I don’t ever expect to have a different brain that’s not a worrier brain, but I do expect to have tools that make it all kind of easier that I can continue to use.


But I think, especially with topics like this — where they feel so big and important, where they feel like they’re changing, where people feel morally responsible to contribute — you’re going to find this at especially high rates, not just among people born with brains like me, but probably among a lot of other types of brains too.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah. Do you buy that this is a bad thing? I ask because there are plenty of things that I worry about in my day-to-day life that I give more worry than I think they deserve. But these problems are exceptionally big, and probably deserve more worry than we give them. If we were actually going to have worry that was proportionate to the size of the issue, how much anxiety is the right amount of anxiety?


**Spencer Greenberg:** There’s a lot to unpack there. And if you go to cognitive behavioural therapy, let’s say for anxiety, an exercise sometimes that you’ll engage in is one where you consider the evidence. So they’ll be like, “You’re worried about this thing. You’re worried about how your friend didn’t text you back. What’s the underlying thought there?” Maybe the underlying thought is like, “Maybe my friend doesn’t like me anymore.” And then they’ll be like, “OK, why don’t you consider the evidence for and against?” You’ll consider the evidence for and against, and often people will feel better after that, and it will help them have more realistic thinking.


And there’s a temptation, I think, if someone goes to a friend who doesn’t buy into these \[existential risk\] ideas, or even goes to a therapist who’s used to practicing that technique, where they’ll be like, “Consider the evidence.” And that’s actually really unhelpful in this situation, because chances are, if you’re listening to this podcast, you’ve already thought a lot about the evidence — and if someone’s like, “Consider the evidence for and against this problem that you think is a horrible problem being really bad,” you’re like, “Yeah, I’ve thought about the evidence, and I think it actually is really bad.” So that’s not the way to go in these kinds of situations, right?


But I think it’s also important to ask the question, what is hypervigilance, and what’s the point of it? Effectively it’s a state that’s preparing you to take drastic action. Like there’s something dangerous that’s coming: you’ve got to go, you’ve got to act. Unfortunately, it’s[really not a healthy state to be in all the time](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/randy-nesse-evolutionary-medicine-psychiatry/) . It’s not good for your body, it’s not good for your mind. It can literally mess up things like digestion, because it actually affects your digestion when you’re in a hypervigilant state.


You know, I like to think about professional athletes here. Do professional athletes want to be stressing their bodies all the time? If you’re training to be in the Olympics, and all you care about is the goal of winning the Olympics, are you just stressing your body to the absolute limit? The answer is absolutely no, because that is not the performance-optimising move. The performance-optimising move involves genuine rest; it involves variation of things. You’re not always working just on that one skill set. You’re going to have off days. You’re going to have days where you’re practicing other things or doing cross training or whatever — and that’s even if you just care about optimising for the goal.


So I think we have to be realistic about like, what is this state, what is it doing for us? And what kind of beings are we realistically?


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, I think the “what kinds of beings are we realistically?” feels really important to me. Part of my brain thinks that I can just not be a human. I don’t know what gave me this idea. I think it is something about this community really valuing self-improvement and feeling like changing parts about yourself is kind of possible.


I think some people think people can’t change, people can’t improve. Spending lots of time around the kinds of people who think they can make big improvements to the world, I think you also spend time around the kinds of people who think that they can better themselves — and that includes things like, “I can work harder; I can boost my productivity.”


**Spencer Greenberg:** Well, I think there are a few issues going on here.


One is that people can have a sense that the way they get social credit is by being the hardest worker or the one who’s doing the most good or whatever. If you’re in a community that values that, that can become a kind of optimisation metric — even to the point of it being unhealthy, and even past the point of it actually really being admired. It’s just sort of the sense that, “I’ll be admired more if I push this way.” And I think that can be an unhealthy dynamic of competing over who can grind themselves the hardest. I don’t think that is the way anyone would want the community to be. But sometimes, maybe people can perceive that, and that can have negative consequences.


The other thing is that, even if your goal were just to get the maximum performance,[you have to be realistic](https://80000hours.org/after-hours-podcast/episodes/luisa-keiran-free-will-guilt-shame/) about what kind of body you have and what kind of mind you have — and grinding all the way is not actually the way to get maximum performance. It’s a naive version of trying to get maximum performance. It’s like thinking that the way that you win a race is by going at maximum speed at every single moment. No, that’s actually how you get injured or burn out, right? That’s not the way you win the race.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** One thing that especially resonated with me was this idea that status is part of the motivation. I think I have this narrative that the reason I want to work harder is purely because of impact, but I think if I really reflect, you know, there is a part of me that wants people to think I work hard just because they’ll admire me more. And there’s a part of me that wants my manager to think I work hard because I want to be a good student.


So why does this feel important? I think it feels important because when I try to interrogate why I find it so hard to rest, I think the main motivation is impact. But I think that the fact that actually these other motivations are in there feels important for me to notice — because really, deep down, it isn’t all about impact. Yeah, it feels like there’s something there. I don’t know. What do you think?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Yeah. This goes back to the question of what kind of beings are we? I would argue that we’re beings with many different drives.


And some of them are very low level — like generally people will avoid taking on extra effort unless they have some reason to. Literally they’ll walk in straight lines instead of curved lines, right? They’ll take the path of least resistance. Well, why is that? I think we have some drive to reduce energy expenditure. Of course, you can override it. You could have a goal that overrides it. But I think it’s worth noticing that that’s a drive.


I actually think we have dozens of these drives. We have a drive to be accepted by our social community, probably because we would literally die if we weren’t 50,000 years ago.


I think we have all these overlapping drives, and then we have this kind of reasoning, more rational part of our minds that’s coming up with principles to live by and ideas that we want to follow and things we believe in, et cetera — but that’s operating amidst all of these drives. It doesn’t override all these drives. And that actually can create a lot of internal conflict when the drives and that more rational part are not gelling that well.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, I want to come back to the internal conflict stuff. I think a lot of what we’re going to talk about today has to do with that.


Before we do, I’m curious if there’s anything you found helpful in recognising and accepting that the type of person you are is a human with limitations. I just want to be the kind of being that can work as hard as my values make me feel like I should. Have you done anything, something like kind of dealing with the grief of it or something?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Well, I mean, it is a very powerful idea, that we’re such limited beings, and that no matter how hard we work to be unlimited beings, we’re never going to get there. All of these things are just ideals that we strive for and we won’t come close.


For example, I really care about trying to be rational, and I regularly see how far I am from that goal. And you could let that kind of undercut your motivation, being like, “Oh man, I’m so far from that,” or you can turn that around and you can say, “Hey, I can keep becoming a better version of myself and move incrementally towards this more ideal form.” And that can be exciting. I don’t think it has to be demotivating. It can be motivating, actually: being limited, but seeing how you can progress and improve.


But I would also say that failing to accept your limitations can actually be disastrous towards the goals that you care about. So the more accurately you can see yourself — what you are, what your limitations are — the better you can be at all the things that you care about. Denying them is actually a strategy that’s going to undercut you.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Yeah, totally.

Why you shouldn’t read the news \[00:23:54\]


**Luisa Rodriguez:** I guess there are a couple of threads there, and I want to put pins in a few of them and stay on this idea of the threat system always being on, and that being a thing that can feel maybe truth tracking and important — because in some ways it feels like the world is genuinely on fire — but also not good for you as a human being. Not good for your work, if you assume something like… Actually, not just assume: I believe that it is probably just bad for your work if your threat system is always on and you are constantly feeling chronically on fire.


One common way that people end up entering this threat system and staying in it is tracking world events. So maybe there’s the release of a new model that seems super impressive, or a blog post or an interview with someone talking about how AI timelines are even shorter than they thought, or bad news about policy, AI regulation stuff. What approach seems most helpful to you in handling these kinds of triggers?


**Spencer Greenberg:** I’ll use a parallel because I think it’s helpful: news consumption broadly.[Many people find news of the world stressful](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/bryan-caplan-stop-reading-the-news/) because the news tends to focus on bad things, and also there’s bad things happening in the world. So they read an article on X and they’re like, “Ahhh, things are falling apart!” And then a friend posts on Facebook and they’re like, “Ahh!”


So my strategy for helping deal with this challenge is I subscribe to a[service](https://www.theflipside.io/) that just gives one news story a day, and that is my news. Basically the idea is: if it’s not important enough to be the top news story of the day, it’s probably not important enough to care about. And in fact, funnily enough, many days that’s not even important enough for me to want to read it, and you know, they’re picking one news story a day. It’s kind of crazy to think about, but so much of this is noise, right?


And I find that helpful for me: staying on top of the important things happening in the world, but not getting sucked into it and not wasting time, and you’re not getting stressed out about things needlessly, but still knowing kind of what’s happening.


Now, let’s say you’re in the field of AI, and things are moving so fast. It may be even more difficult to deal with, just because there is information you really want to stay on top of and it is changing a lot. And there, I would say the same kind of strategy can be useful: being really aware of where am I getting my news, what are the good news sources, what’s the stuff that’s going to keep me up to date?


And then trying to block out all the noise, right? You don’t need to constantly scroll down and get every single new post on X about AI, a bunch of which are probably complete exaggerations anyway or bad takes. You know, figure out your trusted news sources and that’s where you’re going to get your information. That’s how you can stay on top of things, and then you don’t need to just constantly passively consume all this other information.


**Luisa Rodriguez:** Why do you think people are so inclined to doomscroll?


**Spencer Greenberg:** Well, it’s a bit of an irony, because often people go to social media as a form of avoidance or as a form of attempting to unwind, right? So they’re like, “I don’t want to think about this thing. I’m stuck on this thing. Let me open up social media.” Or like, “I’ve just been working hard, let me unwind.” And the irony is that they end up often in this sort of hypervigilant state when they’re on social media, where it’s like not even necessarily doing a good job of getting them to relax, or maybe they’ve avoided this one thing and now they’re stressed out about this other thing they’re looking at. So it often doesn’t serve the purpose that it’s intended to.


This actually connects to an idea from our book, *The 12 Levers* . In our beta testing, we were having people read the book. This idea is one of these ideas that our beta testers just kept bringing up, like this was an unlock for them.


It’s this idea that we call “glad you did it” activities. The idea is that often we do things that are serving some need, or that we believe are serving some need — like rest, relaxation, et cetera — but when we actually really get down to it, we could swap them with a different activity that serves the same need, but afterwards you’re like, “I’m glad I did that.” You’re not like, “That was a giant waste of time.”


I think a mistake people often make is that they try to swap out those activities with things that don’t serve that need. So you as a human have a need to rest, but what you might be doing to rest is going and scrolling social media — which, you know, maybe it is a little bit restful, but maybe it doesn’t even serve that person very well, and then afterwards you’re probably like, “Why did I do that? That was not a good use of time.”


Well, what else is restful? What else shares that same need? Maybe walking your dog or going for a walk in nature or having a call with a friend or whatever it is. And maybe it takes a little bit more planning to make that happen; it’s not quite as mindless — but if you could swap it out with something that serves the same need at least as well, hopefully better, but where afterwards you’re like, “I’m glad I did that; that was a good use of time,” it can not only serve our needs better, but actually make life more meaningful and fulfilling, and I think be more restorative so you are more ready when now you have to go take action in the future.

**Spencer Greenberg:** I have a particular approach to this, which is my life philosophy, which I call[valuism](https://www.spencergreenberg.com/2023/02/doing-what-you-value-as-a-way-of-life-an-introduction-to-valuism/) . So the first question is: how do you even figure out what you value? And this is actually a trickier question than it seems, because we’re quite good at figuring out what we value — and by that I mean things that we assign value to, like states of the world. Like you can imagine a state of the world where babies are being kicked in the face, and you’re like, “That’s a bad state of the world.” You can imagine the state of the world where babies are giggling, and you’re like, “That’s a better state of the world.” So we’re pretty good at thinking about states of the world and thinking about how much we value them.


What’s much more challenging is figuring out our[intrinsic values](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/your-intrinsic-values-why-they-matter-and-how-to-find-them) — because almost everything that we value, we value because it gets us other things, not because it’s an intrinsic value. You know, you might value having a car, but you value having a car because it gives you freedom to go places you want, or maybe it gives you status if it’s a really cool car, or whatever it is. But you don’t value the car for its own sake.


We deep dive on this in our book, *The 12 Levers* , about how to explore your values and use them. **Spencer Greenberg:** Lever 8 is about how to be more productive. And we call that “tuning the productivity equation.” There’s actually a mathematical equation we came up with; it’s actually literal. It’s about how much value you produce, and it splits into three factors:


- The time you put in: the more time you put into something, all else equal, you’re going to produce more of what you value
- The efficiency: how much \[output\] do you produce per hour
- The importance of the thing you’re working on: if it’s not important to you at all, according to what you value, you get no output in terms of what you value.
