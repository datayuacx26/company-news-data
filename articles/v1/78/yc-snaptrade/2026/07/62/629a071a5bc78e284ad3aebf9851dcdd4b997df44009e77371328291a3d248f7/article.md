# How to rebalance a portfolio

## **Highlights:**


- Rebalancing can take just minutes - but it’s hard to do if you can’t see all your accounts in one place
- Learn the one number that tells you if your whole portfolio is actually on track (most people never calculate it)
- See the easiest way to rebalance, *without* selling or triggering taxes


Rebalancing is simple when all your money is in one account. You look at it, see what's gotten out of line, and fix it.


It gets harder when your money is spread out: a TFSA, an RRSP, an FHSA, a taxable account, a workplace plan, maybe your partner's accounts too.


Now you're logging into five different places, trying to piece it all together in your head, and never quite sure if you're on track or just hoping.


If you can see all your investments in one place, rebalancing becomes easier.


## **What rebalancing actually means**


Rebalancing means moving your portfolio back to the mix you picked, after the market, your contributions, or your withdrawals push it off course. That drifting away from your target is called drift.


The goal isn't to predict the market. It's to keep your portfolio matched to how much risk you're comfortable with, how long you're investing for, and what you're aiming at.


Say you want 80% stocks and 20% bonds. Stocks have a great year, and now you're at 88% stocks, 12% bonds. You didn't touch anything, but your portfolio is riskier than you meant it to be.


Rebalancing keeps your risk in check - it's not a trick to boost returns. And it's not about timing the market either.


## **Why multiple accounts make this harder**


Each account can hold different investments. Some are tax-sheltered, some aren't.


Selling in a taxable (non-registered) account can trigger[capital gains](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/income/line-12700-capital-gains.html) . Workplace plans may give you limited investment options. Registered accounts come with contribution room you have to respect.


The tricky thing is that looking at each account separately can make your overall portfolio look balanced when it isn't, or look off when it's actually fine.


## **A step-by-step method**


**Step 1: Pick your target mix.** Decide what you're aiming for, like 80/20 or 70/30, based on your goals and how much risk you're okay with. If you don't have a target yet, stop here. You can't rebalance back to something you haven't decided on.


**Step 2: List every account and what's in it.** Your TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, non-registered account, workplace plan, locked-in accounts, and your partner's accounts if you manage them together. Use what they're worth today, not what you paid.


**Step 3: Sort everything into groups.** Lump your holdings into broad buckets, called asset classes: Canadian stocks, U.S. stocks, international stocks, bonds, cash, and so on. Watch out for all-in-one ETFs, since a single one can hold a bit of everything. Check the fund's own breakdown instead of guessing from its name.


**Step 4: Add it all up.** Total each group across every account, then divide by your whole portfolio's value. So $80,000 in stocks and $20,000 in bonds is an 80/20 portfolio, plain and simple, no matter how it's split between accounts.


**Step 5: See what's too high and too low.** Compare where you are now to your target. Then decide if it's off by enough to bother fixing. You don't need to hit your numbers down to the decimal.


**Step 6: Use new money first.** Before you sell anything, send new contributions toward whatever's running low. Dividends, distributions, and your regular contributions can all help close the gap, usually with less trading, fewer taxes, and less hassle.


**Step 7: If you still need to, sell carefully.** When new money isn't enough, you may have to sell. Selling investments that have gone up in a taxable account can trigger a tax bill on the gains, so many people prefer to make these trades inside registered accounts, where that doesn't happen right away. Keep an eye on trading fees, currency costs, and any fund rules too. If your situation is complicated, it's worth a chat with a tax pro or financial planner.


**Step 8: Pick a routine you'll actually stick to.** Some people check once or twice a year. Others only rebalance when something drifts far enough past their target. There's no single right answer. Just pick a rule you'll follow. Rebalancing too often only adds cost and busywork.


## **Common mistakes**


- Rebalancing each account on its own instead of looking at everything together.
- Forgetting about cash you've set aside to invest, or a workplace plan you almost never log into.
- Selling investments in a taxable account without checking the tax bill first.
- Treating an all-in-one ETF as pure stocks when it holds bonds too.
- Thinking of rebalancing as a way to time the market.


A useful mantra: close enough and consistent usually beats perfect and constantly changing.


## **Here’s an example**


Say you've got:


- A U.S. stock ETF in your TFSA
- Two Canadian funds in your RRSP that overlap more than you realized
- An all-in-one ETF you're pretty sure is mostly stocks but actually holds a chunk of bonds
- A few hundred in cash sitting in your taxable account that you keep forgetting about
- Your partner's RRSP, which you can sort of see if you log into the other account


Now do the math. Pull every balance. Figure out what each fund actually holds.


Add up your stocks across all five places, then your bonds, then divide by the total. Land on something like 72/28.


Decide if that's far enough off to bother with. Work out which account to buy in so you don't trigger a tax bill. Then place the trades, one account at a time.


And because you only do this once or twice a year, you've half-forgotten how it all works by the time you come back to it. Which account holds what again? So you put it off. And the longer you put it off, the further things drift from where you wanted them.
