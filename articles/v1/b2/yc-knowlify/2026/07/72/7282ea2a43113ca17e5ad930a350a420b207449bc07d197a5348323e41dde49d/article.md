# When AI Branding Goes Too Far: Over-Branded Training Video

## A practical identity-versus-instruction framework


Separate the visual system into four layers.


### Layer 1: Identity


This includes the approved logo, type family, core palette, tone, and signature motifs. Use it to establish provenance and continuity. Opening and closing cards, section markers, and selected framing elements are natural locations.


### Layer 2: Navigation


Progress markers, chapter labels, lower thirds, and repeated layouts help learners orient themselves. These can borrow brand styling, but they must remain readable and should not compete with the current step.


### Layer 3: Meaning


Status, risk, category, comparison, sequence, and feedback belong here. Meaning takes priority over decorative uniformity. Pair color with text or shape, and document semantic rules.


### Layer 4: Content


Screenshots, equipment, charts, people, and real environments should remain accurate. Do not recolor a product interface, safety sign, map, uniform, or real object just to match the palette if doing so reduces fidelity or changes meaning.


When layers conflict, prioritize content accuracy, instructional meaning, accessibility, navigation, and then decoration.

## Semantic color examples


### Example 1: Compliance knowledge check


An organization’s primary brand color is red. The first design uses red for the selected answer, the submit button, section headings, and the incorrect state.


That makes selection and error difficult to distinguish. A better system uses:


- brand red for identity framing and selected decorative emphasis
- a clear border plus “Selected” label for the chosen option
- an error icon, the word “Incorrect,” and an accessible error surface for wrong answers
- a check icon, the word “Correct,” and a separately tested success surface
- a neutral, high-contrast submit control


The exact hues can remain compatible with the identity, but the states no longer depend on hue alone.


### Example 2: Safety procedure


A brand palette contains yellow as an accent. The team wants to use yellow for every heading and transition. The safety procedure also uses yellow to mark caution.


The solution is to reserve the recognized caution treatment for actual cautions and move decorative emphasis to another brand element, perhaps a navy line, shape, or typographic weight. This protects the meaning of yellow within the lesson.


### Example 3: Data chart


A one-color chart technically looks branded, but its six series are hard to tell apart. Use a tested categorical palette and reinforce categories with direct labels, line styles, patterns, or symbols. Apply the brand through title treatment, typography, framing, and a selective highlight of the series that matters.


### Example 4: Logo on complex footage


The full-color logo disappears over a moving background. Adding a large opaque box behind it throughout the video preserves recognition but obscures the demonstration.


Better options include showing the logo on a clean opening and closing card, using an approved high-contrast variant in a safe area, or omitting the persistent watermark. W3C notes that logos are exempt from certain contrast requirements, but also recommends considering a sufficiently contrasting variant when a logo acts as a user interface component. The exemption does not make an unreadable mark useful.


## How to de-brand an overloaded video


Use this review sequence on a representative draft.


### Pass 1: Remove repetition


Temporarily hide every logo, slogan, border, corner shape, and branded transition. Add back only what establishes source or structure. Most teams discover that an opening mark, closing card, type system, and a few accents do enough.


### Pass 2: Restore hierarchy


Choose one focal point per scene. Reduce saturation and motion in secondary elements. Ensure the title, demonstrated action, or key diagram, not the brand furniture, receives the strongest emphasis.


### Pass 3: Audit meaning


List every color and what it communicates. If one color means “brand,” “selected,” “warning,” and “incorrect,” split those roles. If meaning is carried only by color, add words, icons, patterns, positions, or shapes.


### Pass 4: Test accessibility


Check text contrast using actual frames, not only palette swatches. Text over video can pass on one frame and fail on the next; use a stable surface, overlay, outline, or different placement. Test important graphics and controls as well.


### Pass 5: Test without sound and at small size


Turn off sound, enable captions, and view the video at the smallest expected player size. Confirm that captions are not blocked and that repeated branding does not consume the readable area.


### Pass 6: Ask learners


Show the revised video to representative users. Ask what they noticed first, what the colors meant, and what action they would take. Do not ask only whether it “looks on-brand.”

## A pre-publication over-branding checklist


- The learning objective is visible in the content, not buried under an identity sequence.
- Logos appear at a defined cadence and never obscure demonstrations or captions.
- Each scene has one clear focal point.
- Brand accents are selective enough to retain emphasis.
- Functional colors have documented meanings.
- Color is never the only cue for status, feedback, category, or required action.
- Normal and large text meet the chosen WCAG contrast target.
- Charts and diagrams remain distinguishable in greyscale and for users with color-vision differences.
- Real products, interfaces, controls, and safety markings are not misleadingly recolored.
- Motion and sound branding are brief and not repeatedly distracting.
- The design works in every intended aspect ratio and player size.
- An accessibility or learning reviewer can override brand usage with a recorded reason.


## FAQ


### How often should a logo appear in a training video?


There is no universal count. An opening card, closing card, and occasional unobtrusive identifier may be enough. Frequency should reflect distribution risk and brand policy without competing with the lesson.


### Are logos exempt from WCAG contrast rules?


Text that is part of a logo or brand name is exempt from WCAG 2.2’s text contrast requirement. That narrow exception does not cover ordinary branded text, diagrams, controls, or the rest of the corporate palette.


### Can brand colors be used for correct and incorrect feedback?


Yes, if the colors are distinguishable, meet applicable contrast needs, and are reinforced with non-color cues such as “Correct” and “Incorrect,” icons, shapes, or patterns.


### Does a less branded video look unprofessional?


Not necessarily. Consistent typography, composition, voice, pacing, and a restrained identity frame often feel more deliberate than constant logo and color repetition.


### Who should approve exceptions to brand guidelines?


Define this in advance. Typically the brand owner is consulted, while accessibility, legal, safety, or instructional requirements can justify a documented exception.
