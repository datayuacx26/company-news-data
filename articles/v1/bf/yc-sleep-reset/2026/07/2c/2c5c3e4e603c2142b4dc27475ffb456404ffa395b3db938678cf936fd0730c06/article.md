## So What Is Melatonin Really?


[Melatonin is actually a hormone that your brain produces](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/melatonin-what-you-need-to-know) in response to darkness in order to start your sleepiness state. It helps your body regulate your circadian rhythms (your natural sleep and wake cycles). How it works is darkness signaled through your eyes then increases your brain's natural melatonin production, which tells your body that it's time to go to sleep. In contrast, light, particularly blue light, decreases melatonin production, which signals to your body that it's time to wake up.


If you have trouble sleeping, you could have low levels of melatonin, or there may be other things that are affecting your body's melatonin production. You've probably heard this before but overexposure to bright or blue light can disrupt your body's natural melatonin production and disrupt your normal sleep patterns. Other factors, like[a lack of exercise](https://www.thesleepreset.com/blog/exercising-before-sleep-does-exercise-affect-sleep) , working night shifts, or even just simply getting older can also affect your body's melatonin production process. In these cases adding a melatonin supplement to your daily night routine might help you be able to[fall asleep more easily](https://www.thesleepreset.com/blog/how-to-fall-asleep-faster) and get enough rest.


## Understanding the Sleep Hormone


You might wonder where[melatonin](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534823/) is produced in your body. In fact,[melatonin is a hormone produced naturally by your pineal gland](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/sleep/art-20048379) , a pea-sized gland located just above the middle of your brain. When it's dark, your body produces more melatonin, signaling it's time to rest which we know from research on melatonin and the mammalian pineal gland.


As daylight fades and darkness envelops your surroundings, your body's internal clock, or what is called your circadian rhythm, kicks into high gear. A tiny gland in your brain, called the pineal gland, picks up on this cue and starts producing melatonin. Melatonin production ramps up throughout the night, reaching its peak in the dark hours, and then gradually tapers off as morning approaches.


Think of melatonin as your body's natural sleep promoter. It's the hormone that signals to your body when it's time to wind down and prepare for sleep, and when it's time to wake up. It doesn't actually make you fall asleep, but it's a powerful messenger that tells your body, "Hey there, it's nighttime now, get ready for bed!" This communication between your brain and body via melatonin is effectively how melatonin can be so helpful as a sleep aid.

## What Is a Safe Dosage of Melatonin?

In the United States, melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement, not a medication, so it's not as closely regulated by the Food and Drug Administration. Although it's easy and convenient to buy at your local pharmacy, grocery store, or online, you should be cautious about the brand of melatonin you buy and do your homework first. Some less reputable brands may list the dosage incorrectly on the label, while others may contain ingredients you don't want to consume. When in doubt, your doctor should be able to provide trustworthy recommendations for high-quality melatonin brands and guidance on the safety of melatonin.


Melatonin can also affect people differently, so it may take some time to determine the dose of melatonin that works best for you and has the fewest side effects. ### Frequently Asked Questions


#### Is it bad to take melatonin every night?


Taking melatonin every night is generally not recommended for long-term use. While melatonin is safe for short-term use, long-term melatonin use may lead to dependency, hormonal imbalances, and disrupted natural melatonin production. Most healthcare providers suggest using melatonin for no more than a few weeks at a time. If you find yourself needing melatonin nightly for extended periods, it's important to consult with a healthcare professional to address underlying sleep issues and explore alternative treatments like behavioral therapy or[improved sleep hygiene](https://www.thesleepreset.com/blog/why-your-sleep-hygiene-matters) . For more information about daily melatonin use, check out our detailed guide on[whether you can take melatonin every night](https://www.thesleepreset.com/blog/can-you-take-melatonin-every-night-what-to-know) .


#### Can melatonin make anxiety worse?


Yes, melatonin can potentially make anxiety worse in some individuals. While melatonin is generally calming for most people, some may experience heightened anxiety, mood changes, or irritability as side effects. The effects of melatonin use can vary significantly between individuals, and factors like dosage, timing, and individual sensitivity can influence whether melatonin helps or worsens anxiety. If you notice increased anxiety after taking melatonin, it's best to discontinue use and consult with a healthcare provider about alternative sleep aids or[anxiety management strategies](https://www.thesleepreset.com/blog/sleep-anxiety-why-it-happens-and-how-to-calm-your-mind) .


#### What are the long term effects?


The long-term effects of melatonin use are still being studied through ongoing clinical trials, but potential concerns include hormonal imbalances, disrupted natural melatonin production, and altered sleep architecture. Long-term melatonin use may interfere with your body's natural ability to produce melatonin, potentially leading to dependency. There are also concerns about impacts on reproductive hormones, insulin sensitivity, and cortisol levels. Additionally, prolonged use may mask underlying sleep disorders that require different treatment approaches. This is why most healthcare providers recommend focusing on sleep hygiene and behavioral therapy for long-term sleep management rather than relying on melatonin supplements indefinitely.


#### How much melatonin should you take?


The appropriate melatonin dosage varies by individual, but most healthcare providers recommend starting with 1-3 milligrams taken 30 minutes to 2 hours before bedtime. It's best to start with the lowest effective dose of melatonin and gradually increase if needed. Some people may need as little as 0.5 mg, while others may require up to 5 mg, though higher doses don't necessarily mean better results and may increase the risk of side effects. Since melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement in the United States, dosages can vary between brands, so it's important to read labels carefully and consult with a healthcare provider to determine the safest and most effective dose for your specific needs.


#### Is there a downside to long-term melatonin use?


Yes, there are several potential downsides to long-term melatonin use. Extended use may lead to decreased natural melatonin production, making it harder for your body to fall asleep without supplementation. Long-term use can also disrupt your natural circadian rhythms and sleep architecture, potentially leading to dependency. Other concerns include hormonal imbalances, reduced effectiveness over time, and the masking of underlying sleep disorders that may require different treatment approaches. The safety of melatonin use over extended periods hasn't been fully established through long-term clinical trials, which is why most healthcare providers recommend focusing on sleep hygiene, behavioral therapy, and addressing root causes of sleep problems rather than relying on melatonin supplements indefinitely.


#### How long before bed should you take melatonin?


Most experts recommend taking melatonin 30 minutes to 2 hours before your desired bedtime. The timing can vary depending on the type of melatonin supplement you're using - immediate-release formulations are typically taken 30-60 minutes before bed, while prolonged-release melatonin may be taken 1-2 hours before sleep. Taking melatonin too close to bedtime may not give it enough time to take effect, while taking it too early might cause you to feel drowsy before you're ready to sleep. The optimal timing also depends on your individual response to melatonin and your natural circadian rhythms. It's best to maintain consistency with timing to help regulate your sleep-wake cycle and consult with a healthcare provider to determine the[best schedule](http://www.thesleepreset.com/blog/sleep-calculator) for your specific situation.
