## What NBCMI and CCHI certification require of medical interpreters


Two national bodies sit behind the credentials you see on an interpreter's resume. The National Board of Certification for Medical Interpreters (NBCMI) and the Certification Commission for Healthcare Interpreters (CCHI) each set the bar before a name earns a title.


Entry requirements look similar across both. A candidate needs a high school diploma or its equivalent, proven proficiency in English and a second language, and at least 40 hours of formal[medical interpreter training](https://opalitehealth.com/blog/healthcare-ai-interpreter-what-to-require) . That training floor matters. It signals the person has studied medical terminology, ethics, and the mechanics of interpreting a clinical conversation, not simply that they speak two languages at the dinner table.

## What the exams test: accuracy, terminology, and clinical communication


Start with the written test. The NBCMI written exam runs 51 questions in English only, and per the February 2026 NBCMI Candidate Handbook, it weights 61% of its content toward medical knowledge, meaning terminology and clinical specialties, with roles, ethics, and standards making up 28%, and cultural competence and legislation covering the remaining 11%[according to the NBCMI exam guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/blog/nbcmi-cmi-certified-medical-interpreter-exam-guide-2026) . A passing score signals the interpreter knows what a myocardial infarction is before a cardiologist says it out loud.


The oral exam is where language actually gets tested. NBCMI adds bidirectional[consecutive interpreting](https://opalitehealth.com/blog/medical-translator-interpreter-ai-safety) , where the interpreter converts each speaker's turn back and forth, plus sight translation of a written document read aloud in the target language. CCHI's oral exam goes further with simultaneous interpreting, the real-time mode where the interpreter speaks while the provider is still talking.


For your operations team, the point is narrow. Passing proves an interpreter can hold clinical terminology, ethical boundaries, and live two-way interpretation together under pressure, in the moment a patient describes chest pain.


## The compliance framework: Title VI, Section 1557, and the certified interpreter standard


The reason certified interpreters show up in your budget at all starts with the law. Title VI of the Civil Rights Act and Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act require covered entities to give[patients with limited English proficiency](https://opalitehealth.com/blog/limited-english-proficiency-language-access-requirements) meaningful language access, free of charge, accurately, and in a timely manner. That obligation lands on you, not the patient.


The 2024 HHS Section 1557 update sharpened the expectation, with key compliance deadlines taking effect in 2025. Covered entities with 15 or more employees must now designate a Section 1557 coordinator and put written language access policies in place,[per the updated 1557 regulations](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/healthlawscan/2025/01/affordable-care-act-section-1557-new-language-accessibility-requirements) .


The definition of a qualified interpreter is where the compliance framework and the certification exams meet. Under these rules, a qualified interpreter must interpret effectively, accurately, and impartially, and must know specialized vocabulary,[per this Title VI and 1557 explainer](https://healthlaw.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/2025_12_11_T-VI-and-Sec-1557-explainer-2025-update.pdf) . Those are the same abilities board certification was built to test.


## What board-certified interpretation costs hospitals


Search "medical interpreter salary" and you get a wage figure. That number matters to the person taking the job. It tells you almost nothing about what interpretation actually costs the organization signing the checks.


The real number lives on a budget line, not a pay stub. Carolinas Healthcare System spent $3.3 million on contract interpreter expenses in 2015, more than half of its overall[language services budget](https://opalitehealth.com/why-it-matters)[according to this analysis](https://www.hfma.org/finance-and-business-strategy/patient-experience/57149/) .


Here is what drives that math. When you lean on[per-minute phone interpretation services](https://opalitehealth.com/blog/ai-vs-phone-interpreters-clinical-guide) , the meter runs during more than the conversation:


-


Time spent waiting for an interpreter to connect


-


Silences while a provider assesses a patient or reviews a chart


-


Administrative pauses between speakers


For a clinical operations leader, certified interpretation reads less like an hourly wage and more like a fixed cost that scales with every encounter.


## Where even certified human interpreters face structural limits


None of what follows is a knock on the interpreters themselves. The people who pass these exams are skilled clinical partners. The gaps are structural, and they surface most often at the worst moments:


-


After hours, when certified coverage thins out and a patient still needs care


-


In urgent situations, when a connection delay stretches while a provider waits


-


Across every department and shift, where maintaining certified coverage is a staffing problem no schedule fully solves


Then there is the language ceiling. NBCMI's full CMI oral exam covers Spanish, Mandarin, Cantonese, Russian, Vietnamese, and Korean. CCHI's full CHI oral exam covers Spanish, Arabic, and Mandarin. Together, the two bodies reach seven distinct languages; for all others, no full oral certification pathway exists.


For a patient who speaks one of those languages, the[certified standard for LEP patients](https://opalitehealth.com/blog/health-equity-language-gaps-lep-outcomes) was never an option to begin with.


## How AI interpretation is measured against the same quality standards


A board exam grades an interpreter once, on a testing day. AI interpretation gets graded on a different rhythm, encounter after encounter, against the same clinical dimensions those exams were built to protect.


The categories mirror what certification enforces. A serious evaluation scores an AI system on:


-


Omissions, where a piece of what was said drops out


-


Additions, where words appear that no one actually spoke


-


Negation errors, the difference between "take this" and "do not take this"


-


Numeral accuracy, so a 15 milligram dose never becomes 50


-


Clinically meaningful errors, the ones that change a diagnosis or a plan


-


[Cultural appropriateness and dialect handling](https://opalitehealth.com/blog/medical-interpretation-cultural-intelligence)


Certification bodies exist to hold a human to accuracy, fidelity, and terminology under pressure. When an AI tool is judged on the same axes, a clinical leader finally has a common yardstick for both.
