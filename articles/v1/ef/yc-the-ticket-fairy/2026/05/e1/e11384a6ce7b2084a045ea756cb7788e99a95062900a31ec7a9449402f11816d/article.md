# Using Attendee Data to Program Panels That Fill Seats Year After Year

## Why Data-Driven Convention Panel Scheduling Matters


Fan convention panel scheduling can make or break an event’s success. In an era where major cons host hundreds of panels and activities, using attendee data to guide programming is no longer optional – it’s essential. The biggest fan events, from San Diego Comic-Con’s 135,000+ attendees to New York Comic Con’s record[180,000 tickets sold](https://www.eventbrite.com/blog/ds00-fan-conventions-behind-the-mask/#:~:text=than%20100%2C00%20attendees,combined%20374%2C936%20people%20in%202016) , illustrate the stakes: packed panels lead to engaged, satisfied fans, while empty rooms or overcrowded halls signal missed opportunities. Convention veterans have learned that data-driven event programming isn’t just a buzzword; it’s a competitive edge that keeps fans coming back every year.


[Attendees vote with their feet at conventions](https://www.pcma.org/conference-metrics-that-matter/#:~:text=Participation%20is%20the%20single,of%20participation%20performance%20goals%20include) – showing up for content they love and skipping what doesn’t interest them. ### Harnessing Surveys and Fan Feedback


Raw attendance numbers only tell part of the story. To truly program panels that resonate, you need qualitative insights from attendees. That’s where post-panel and post-convention surveys come in. Effective organisers collect attendee feedback on questions like: *Which panels were your favourites? Which topics or guests would you like to see in the future? Did any sessions disappoint, and why?* Combining this feedback with attendance data paints a fuller picture of panel performance.


Many conventions deploy quick panel surveys via their mobile app or a QR code given out at the end of each session. Even a simple 1–5 star rating for each panel, plus an open comment field, can yield valuable pointers. For multi-track events, consider a short nightly survey during the con (when impressions are fresh) and a longer post-event survey afterward. Consistently, organisers find that response rates jump if you keep surveys short (5 questions or less) and perhaps incentivise them (e.g. a prize drawing for completed surveys). For example, a mid-sized sci-fi con in the UK saw survey response rates climb to 35% after offering a free signed poster to ten random respondents.


Attendee surveys can reveal hidden gems and pain points that numbers alone might miss. You might discover that a panel with moderate attendance still earned rave reviews from those who went – hinting at a niche hit that could grow if promoted better. Or perhaps a well-attended panel received poor feedback due to content issues, which is crucial to know before you simply repeat it next year. One[comprehensive industry report on event attendance](https://devnambi.com/data/pass-attendance.html#:~:text=Does%20a%20packed%20room%20,value%200.09) notes that organisers who blend quantitative data (attendance, sales) with qualitative input (satisfaction scores, comments) get the[clearest insight into event success](https://icv2.com/articles/columns/view/31899/new-eventbrite-survey-reveals-convention-demographics-spending-patterns#:~:text=More%20data%2C%20better%20shows,trends%20revealed%20in%20this%20survey) . In short, listen to your fans. Their direct feedback, combined with hard data, is a powerful guide for curating next year’s schedule.

### Identifying Hot Topics and Underperformers


Start by ranking your panels by attendance and by attendee satisfaction. Which sessions filled every seat, and which struggled to draw a crowd? Look for common threads among the top performers: Was it the theme (e.g., Marvel superhero panels outdrawing others), the format (Q&A sessions vs. lectures), or perhaps the star power of a guest? For example, at a large comic con in India, the organisers discovered that panels about new blockbuster film releases consistently had 80%+ fill rates, whereas classic comic lore panels averaged much lower. The takeaway was to increase content around upcoming movies and TV series – what’s hot *now* – to meet fan demand, while finding new hooks to boost classic content panels (like adding popular creators to those sessions).


At the same time, identify underperforming panels. If certain topics repeatedly see <30% attendance, that’s a red flag. It could mean that content is too niche, or maybe the panel was scheduled against a big main event (and thus was doomed by conflict, not lack of interest). Data needs context: cross-reference the timing and room location for low-attendance sessions. You might find a scheduling clash or a far-flung room suppressed an otherwise worthy panel. For instance, one anime convention noticed all their academic lecture-style panels were poorly attended – not necessarily because attendees disliked the topics, but because those panels ran parallel to the cosplay contest and were located in a separate building. The next year, they adjusted the schedule to avoid that conflict and saw those panel attendance numbers double.


It’s useful to categorise panels by type and genre when analysing. Break down your data by track, theme, and format. Maybe workshops and interactive panels had higher satisfaction scores than passive speaker panels – indicating fans crave more hands-on content. Or data might reveal that your gaming-related panels were 90% full while the science-fiction literature track lagged at 40% on average. These patterns help in reallocating emphasis. Many veteran organisers actually produce a simple report or heat map of their panel grid: highlighting which slots were most and least attended. This visualization can quickly show, for example, that Saturday mid-day sessions were jam-packed, while Sunday morning sessions (perhaps unsurprisingly) had sparse attendance. Informed by this, you might program lighter fare for early mornings and save blockbuster panels for peak times.

### Understanding Audience Segments and Interests


Another powerful use of attendee data is identifying *who* your audience is and how different segments behave. Your convention is likely composed of various fan subgroups – for example, hardcore cosplayers, casual pop-culture fans, gamers, anime enthusiasts, etc. Data can help tailor programming to these segments. Start by examining any demographic info you have (age ranges, first-time vs. repeat attendees, VIP ticket holders vs. general) and see if their panel choices differ.


For instance, repeat attendees might gravitate towards niche “insider” panels that go deeper into lore, while first-timers flock to celebrity Q&As and main attractions. A European gaming expo noticed that their attendees aged under 25 showed up in droves for esports tournament panels and game demo sessions, whereas those 25+ preferred developer talks and retro gaming discussions. By segmenting the feedback and attendance data, the organisers decided to create two new panel tracks the next year: one aimed at “pro gamers and esports fans” and another on “retro and industry topics” – effectively catering to both demographics more directly. Both tracks subsequently saw improved satisfaction scores as fans found content that spoke to them.


If your ticketing or registration system integrates with a CRM or analytics tool, you can even overlay purchase behavior or marketing data onto programming preferences. For example, you might discover that attendees who bought VIP badges (or higher-priced tiers) are the ones filling the front rows of academic or niche panels – perhaps because they’re the super-fans with broader interests. Understanding these nuances allows for data-driven content curation: you can ensure each key audience segment has something tailored to them, and you market those sessions to the people most likely to be interested.


Some advanced conventions experiment with attendee personas based on data. They cluster attendees into types (e.g. “The Cosplay Creator”, “The Collectibles Superfan”, “The Anime Binger”) and map out a sample itinerary of panels that each persona would love. Then they check if their schedule actually supports those ideal itineraries without conflict. This kind of analysis can highlight, say, that all the cosplay creator-oriented panels were bunched together, forcing a difficult choice, whereas they could be spread out. It’s an approach borrowed from corporate conferences, but it works for fan events too – ensuring your schedule offers a balanced journey for different interest groups. The more you understand your audience segments through data, the more precisely you can program content that *each* group will consider unmissable.

## Scheduling Panels with Data-Driven Insights


Collecting and analysing attendee data sets the stage – now it’s time to apply those insights to actually schedule your next convention’s panels. Data-driven scheduling means using evidence (not just gut instinct) to decide *what* panels to include, *when* and *where* to slot them, and *how* to manage them for maximum engagement. The result should be a programme that consistently draws full rooms and happy attendees. Here are key strategies to implement:


### Optimizing Room Allocation and Time Slots


One of the most immediate payoffs of data is smarter room assignments. By predicting which panels will be most popular, you can match high-demand panels to larger rooms and lower-demand ones to smaller spaces. This sounds obvious, but without data it’s guesswork – many organisers have been caught off guard by a “sleeper hit” panel in too small a room or an overhyped session not filling a giant hall. Look at last year’s attendance and any pre-registration or interest indicators for new content. If a similar panel last year hit 95% capacity in a 500-seat room, consider moving it to a 800-seat room this time. Conversely, if an experimental panel only drew 50 people in a 400-seat hall, you might downsize that session or combine it with another topic.

Data can help predict panel popularity surprisingly well. In fact, organizers of the PASS Summit tech conference used historical data and algorithms to forecast session attendance, successfully assigning rooms so that[overcrowded sessions dropped from 17% of sessions to just 8% year-over-year](https://devnambi.com/data/pass-attendance.html#:~:text=match%20at%20L185%202011%20,8) . They analysed factors like speaker popularity and topic trends to make these predictions. For fan conventions, you can similarly weigh factors such as: the popularity of the franchise/topic, social media buzz, guest star draw, and previous attendance for similar panels. Even a simple ranking (e.g., “Tier 1” headliner panels, “Tier 2” mid-level interest, etc.) based on data can guide your room allocation. It’s far better to have a slightly too large room than to turn away hundreds at the door. As experienced organisers often say, *empty seats don’t tweet angry complaints, but fans locked out of a full panel might.*


Aside from room size, use data to optimise time slotting. Your analysis might show certain time slots consistently outperform others. Is Saturday 11:00 AM a prime time when many fans are on site and energetic? Schedule a big-name panel or highly anticipated topic then. Perhaps your data shows that late afternoon sessions dip in attendance – that might be a good time for niche topics or secondary content, since fewer will attend anything then. Also consider patterns like meal times and other activities: for example, if a major cosplay contest or concert is at 7 PM, any panel scheduled opposite it could suffer. Stagger your “marquee” events so they don’t cannibalise each other’s audiences. A data-driven grid might entail anchoring each day with one or two tentpole panels (based on prior popularity) and filling around them with complementary content.


Another scheduling tweak informed by data is repeating popular sessions. If you have a panel that always fills up and still has excess demand, consider running it twice (maybe once each on two different days) or offering an encore on the final day. Some conventions do this with extremely high-demand content – for instance, a Japanese culture panel in New York might run on both Saturday and Sunday if the first session maxed out. Data from prior days (or previous years) can justify these encores. It’s a way to spread out attendance and give more fans a chance to see the content they want. Just be mindful to schedule repeats such that they don’t conflict with related content that the same audience would also want.


### Avoiding Conflicts and Managing Crowds


Your attendee data is also a map of fan interests, which can help you avoid scheduling conflicts that split your audience. A classic rookie mistake in programming is to schedule two super-popular panels at the same time, forcing fans to choose and leaving both half-full rather than one completely full. Data can prevent this. By reviewing which panels appealed to similar attendee segments (from surveys or pass scans), you can identify potential conflicts. For instance, if you know the attendees who went to the “Game of Thrones Fan Discussion” also largely attended the “Lord of the Rings panel” last year, don’t put those in the same time block this year – you likely have a significant overlap in audience. Instead, schedule them sequentially or at least far apart. Using data in this way can increase overall panel attendance by ensuring fans aren’t forced to skip one favourite for another.

Many large cons also adopt tools like a panel reservation system or “lottery” for the most in-demand sessions. This not only improves fan satisfaction by making access fair, but it gives organisers excellent data on interest levels. For example,[SXSW’s PanelPicker system allows the community to vote on proposed sessions](https://support.sxsw.com/hc/en-us/articles/360029576132-What-is-PanelPicker#:~:text=What%20is%20PanelPicker%3F%20PanelPicker%20is,speakers%20submit%20their%20session%20ideas) ahead of the event, directly telling organisers which topics have the most fan support. Fan conventions might use a simpler approach: during registration or via the event app, allow attendees to mark a few panels they’re most interested in (not as a guarantee, just as a gauge or RSVP). If 2,000 attendees pre-favourited a panel that only seats 500, you know to expect long lines or implement a ticketing mechanism for that panel. New York Comic Con, for instance, has used online lotteries for popular panels in limited rooms – the data from those sign-ups helps them anticipate crowd size and allocate security and line space accordingly. Embracing these tools, especially if your[convention ticketing platform](https://www.ticketfairy.com/event-ticketing/pop-culture-convention-ticketing) supports session reservations, turns potential crowd control headaches into manageable, data-informed logistics.


Even with great scheduling, the reality is some panels will be *very* popular. That’s where data-informed crowd management comes in. ## Case Studies: Data in Action at Fan Conventions


Nothing illustrates the impact of attendee data better than real-world examples. Here are a few cases from conventions globally that have leveraged data to boost panel success:


**SXSW (Austin, USA) – Community Voting Shapes the Schedule:** One of the most well-known examples of data-driven programming is South by Southwest’s PanelPicker® process.[SXSW invites its community to vote on proposed sessions months in advance](https://support.sxsw.com/hc/en-us/articles/360029576132-What-is-PanelPicker#:~:text=What%20is%20PanelPicker%3F%20PanelPicker%20is,speakers%20submit%20their%20session%20ideas) , effectively crowd-sourcing data on what content attendees most want to see. Organisers combine this fan input (which can total tens of thousands of votes) with internal curation. The result is a conference lineup that consistently matches attendee interests – because it was literally shaped by would-be attendees. For SXSW, this process has helped keep session rooms packed year after year; when fans have a voice in programming, they show up for those sessions. Convention organisers can take a page from SXSW by involving fans in programming decisions, whether via pre-con polls or advisory committees, to ensure the content resonates. **Anime Expo (Los Angeles, USA) – Refining Content via Surveys:** Anime Expo (AX), North America’s largest anime convention, gathers extensive attendee feedback to fine-tune its programming. After AX 2022, organisers noticed through surveys and panel attendance data that interest in industry insider panels (like discussions with anime producers and studios) was surging. In response, AX 2023 expanded that track with more sessions and larger rooms. They featured panels by major Japanese anime studios in the convention’s Petree Hall, which seats thousands. The outcome was phenomenal – those panels hit full capacity with lines out the door, and post-con surveys rated the industry track one of the year’s best additions. By contrast, AX also saw that some fan-run community panels had dipped in attendance. Rather than cutting them blindly, organisers cross-referenced feedback and discovered that scheduling was the culprit: many fan panels were slotted early morning when attendees were either in line for big events or simply not yet on-site. With that insight, AX 2024 shifted many fan-run panels to early afternoon slots and saw participation rebound strongly. This example shows how combining attendance stats with direct fan feedback allows a convention to pivot programming strategy and reap immediate results in attendance.


**Capturing the Voice of the Fan** —


Combining star ratings with direct feedback reveals the true sentiment and quality behind the raw attendance numbers.


**MidSizeCon (Global Examples) – Predictive Room Allocation:** A mid-sized gaming convention in Germany (let’s call it MidSizeCon) faced complaints in 2022 about overcrowded and half-empty panels – sometimes both on the same day. For 2023, the organisers harnessed their data to predict and allocate better. They noticed that every time they had a panel about “Retro Gaming Classics,” it drew a consistent 300+ people, despite being in a 200-seat room previously. Meanwhile, a series of “Indie Game Dev” panels were in a 500-seat theatre but only ever saw about 150 attendees on average. So MidSizeCon flipped their approach: they upgraded the retro gaming panel to the bigger room and moved the indie dev talks to a smaller one, aligning capacity with demonstrated demand. They also looked at time-of-day patterns – data showed that the retro panel drew even bigger crowds when scheduled right after lunch, so they gave it a prime early afternoon slot. The results were clear on site: no more turned-away fans for the retro session (the larger room accommodated everyone), and the indie sessions felt nicely full in the smaller space, creating better energy. Attendance for both tracks improved, and feedback was positive across the board. This case underlines that even without fancy algorithms, basic data analysis (past headcounts and time slot successes) can dramatically improve room utilisation and audience experience.


**Professional Conferences – Lessons for Fan Cons:** It’s worth noting that corporate conferences and trade shows have been employing data-driven session scheduling for years, and fan conventions are now catching up. For instance,[a tech conference used machine learning on its attendee data to anticipate interest levels](https://devnambi.com/data/pass-attendance.html#:~:text=match%20at%20L185%202011%20,8) , reducing overflow incidents significantly. They found that *speaker popularity was a top predictor of attendance* – a finding that resonates in fan events too (a famous actor or creator will spike numbers regardless of panel topic). Many such conferences also correlate session rating with attendance and sometimes find surprising results, like how[a packed room doesn’t guarantee high session quality ratings](https://devnambi.com/data/pass-attendance.html#:~:text=match%20at%20L348%20Does%20a,value%200.09) . Fan convention organisers similarly should balance popularity with quality – data can flag situations where a panel drew a crowd but got poor feedback, indicating a need to improve content or moderation next time. The crossover lesson is clear: more data leads to better programming decisions, whether for industry keynotes or cosplay sketch workshops.

## Continuous Improvement: Building a Year-Round Data Feedback Loop


The final piece of a data-driven programming strategy is making it continuous and iterative. The goal is not just to use attendee data for one event, but to build a feedback loop that makes each year’s convention better than the last. Veteran organisers treat data like a compass – guiding decisions not only in scheduling panels, but in marketing, ticketing, and overall event design. Here’s how to embed data into your year-round planning cycle:


**Managing Blockbuster Panel Demand** —


Implementing overflow rooms and fair access systems turns high-demand sessions into smooth, professional experiences for everyone.


**Post-Event Analysis and Debrief:** Right after your convention, do a thorough data review while everything is fresh. Generate reports from your ticketing system and registration platform with the panel attendance numbers, no-show rates for special events, and any other stats (e.g. how many people used the panel reservation system if you had one). Combine this with your survey summaries and team observations. Many teams hold a post-con “brain dump” meeting where department heads (programming, guest relations, operations) share impressions – backing those up with data prevents decisions from relying purely on anecdotal memories. For instance, instead of “Saturday felt really busy,” you can say “[Saturday 1-3 PM saw 20% higher panel attendance than any other period](https://devnambi.com/data/pass-attendance.html#:~:text=Year%20%C2%A0%C2%A0%20%20,%7C) , possibly due to the two blockbuster panels – next year let’s space those out.” Document these insights in a programming playbook for the next edition.


**Set Data-Backed Goals for Next Year:** Use insights to establish concrete goals. Maybe you want to improve average panel fill rate from 60% to 75%, or reduce the number of panels under 20% capacity to zero. You might aim for a certain uplift in satisfaction scores for panels, or to eliminate complaints about panel schedule conflicts. Having measurable objectives helps focus your strategy. For example, if feedback showed confusion about overlapping big panels, a goal could be “zero overlapping Tier-1 panels” in the schedule. If panel attendance on Sunday dipped to 50% of Saturday’s, a goal might be “increase Sunday panel attendance by 25%” – achieved by scheduling a must-see event Sunday morning or offering an incentive for fans to stick around. By tying goals to data, you can later quantify if your new programming strategy worked.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is a good panel fill rate for a convention?


A healthy panel fill rate for a convention is between 60% and 90% of the room’s capacity. This level indicates strong audience engagement without causing safety issues or turning fans away. Panels falling below 20% capacity usually require reevaluating the topic, while those exceeding 100% need larger venues.


### How do convention organizers track panel attendance?


Convention organizers track panel attendance using technology like badge scanning, RFID tracking at room entrances, and mobile app check-ins. These digital systems provide precise, real-time headcounts and individual attendee preferences, replacing manual click counters and allowing staff to monitor if rooms are filling up faster than expected.


### Why is data-driven panel scheduling important for fan conventions?


Data-driven panel scheduling ensures programming aligns directly with fan demand, leading to packed rooms and higher attendee satisfaction. Leveraging attendance metrics and surveys helps organizers allocate popular topics to larger rooms, avoid scheduling misfires, and boost overall event economics through increased on-site spending and happier sponsors.


### How can event organizers avoid scheduling conflicts for convention panels?


Organizers avoid scheduling conflicts by analyzing attendee data to identify overlapping fan interests and separating those panels into different time blocks. By reviewing past attendance and survey data, planners can ensure two highly popular sessions targeting the same audience segment do not run simultaneously, maximizing overall participation.


### How does attendee demographic data improve event programming?


Attendee demographic data improves event programming by revealing specific content preferences among different audience segments. Analyzing factors like age, ticket tiers, and registration interests allows organizers to tailor panel tracks directly to distinct groups, such as creating dedicated esports sessions for younger attendees or deep-lore panels for VIPs.


### How do large conventions manage crowds for highly popular panels?


Large conventions manage crowds for popular panels by utilizing advance reservation systems, online lotteries, and real-time attendance dashboards. Organizers also deploy on-the-fly solutions like opening overflow rooms with live video feeds or issuing wristbands to cap queues, ensuring fair access and safe line management for high-demand sessions.


### What is the best way to collect attendee feedback on convention panels?


The best way to collect attendee feedback is through short, incentivized post-panel surveys deployed via mobile apps or QR codes. Keeping surveys under five questions and offering rewards, like prize drawings, significantly boosts response rates, providing qualitative insights that raw attendance numbers cannot reveal.


### How do community voting systems influence convention panel schedules?


Community voting systems influence panel schedules by crowdsourcing direct fan input on proposed sessions months before the event. Platforms like SXSW’s PanelPicker allow organizers to combine thousands of attendee votes with internal curation, ensuring the final lineup matches actual audience interests and guarantees packed rooms.
