# What Is a Customs Broker? What They Do and When You Need One

The short answer


A licensed customs broker acts between an importer and CBP. The broker translates shipment and product data into a customs entry, checks classification and valuation, identifies admissibility requirements, calculates duties and fees, transmits the filing, and helps resolve questions during clearance.


Hiring a broker is not a transfer of all legal responsibility.[CBP says the importer of record remains ultimately responsible](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/basic-import-export/importer-exporter-tips) for entry accuracy and the duties, taxes, and fees owed.


## Key takeaways


Customs brokers are licensed and regulated by CBP.


They handle entries, classification, valuation, duty calculation, admissibility, and communication with CBP.


Qualifying owners and purchasers may self-file, but many use brokers because the process is complex.


The importer of record remains responsible even when a broker files the entry.


A customs broker handles customs compliance; a freight forwarder arranges transportation.


Broker fees vary by provider, shipment, and service scope. CBP does not set one universal fee.


## What does a customs broker do?


CBP says brokers need expertise in entry procedures, admissibility, tariff classification, customs valuation, duty rates, taxes, and fees.[See CBP’s broker definition](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/customs-brokers/becoming-customs-broker) .


01


### Review shipment documents


Check invoices, packing lists, transport documents, origin, parties, and product details.


02


### Classify merchandise


Determine the appropriate HTS code and flag questions that need deeper research or a ruling.


03


### Determine customs value


Apply the correct method and identify additions such as assists, royalties, or certain proceeds when relevant.


04


### Identify admissibility requirements


Check whether CBP or another agency requires permits, certificates, labeling, testing, or more data.


05


### Prepare and transmit the entry


Submit entry information and supporting data through CBP systems.


06


### Calculate duties and fees


Apply the tariff treatment in effect and arrange payment of estimated amounts.


07


### Coordinate release


Monitor status, respond to requests, and work with the importer, carrier, and CBP when an issue blocks release.


08


### Support post-entry work


Help with corrections, protests, reconciliation, records, and compliance reviews when included in scope.

## Do I need a customs broker?


Not always. CBP permits qualifying owners and purchasers to make entry on their own behalf. Many importers still hire a broker because filing, classification, valuation, admissibility, and recordkeeping decisions can be difficult to manage consistently.


Situation Practical approach


Occasional, straightforward imports with internal customs knowledge Self-filing may be workable if the importer can meet CBP requirements and maintain records


First commercial import A broker can help establish the entry, power of attorney, bond, classification, and document process


Frequent or high-value shipments A broker can provide repeatable filing and exception management


Regulated goods Use expertise familiar with the relevant partner-government-agency requirements


Uncertain HTS classification or customs value Get a documented review before filing


Time-sensitive inventory Use a broker with clear pre-arrival and exception workflows


No internal customs owner A broker can operate the filing process, but the importer still needs oversight and accurate source data


A freight forwarder, carrier, marketplace, or supplier cannot conduct customs business merely because it handles the shipment. It must also hold the appropriate broker license or work through a licensed broker.[Read CBP’s entry guidance](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/documents/icp073_3.pdf) .


## What remains the importer’s responsibility?


The importer of record must use reasonable care when making entry. Even when a broker submits the filing, the importer remains ultimately responsible for the entry and amounts legally due.[See CBP’s importing guide](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/documents/Importing%20into%20the%20U.S.pdf) .


- Provide complete and accurate product and transaction information.


- Review material classification, valuation, origin, and admissibility decisions.


- Pay duties, taxes, and fees that are legally due.


- Maintain required records.


- Correct errors and respond to CBP when necessary.


- Supervise the broker relationship and power of attorney.


A reliable broker asks detailed questions because missing specifications, unclear payment terms, unsupported origin claims, and incomplete invoices cannot be fixed by filing speed alone.

The services often work together: the forwarder moves the freight, while the broker gets the merchandise entered and released.


## How the broker relationship works


01


### The importer appoints the broker


The parties execute a customs power of attorney authorizing defined customs business.


02


### The importer supplies business and product data


Registration, bond details, specifications, valuation, origin support, invoices, and transport documents form the filing record.


03


### The broker reviews before arrival


Missing data and compliance questions are identified early enough to resolve.


04


### The broker files the entry


Required data and estimated duties are transmitted through CBP systems.


05


### CBP and other agencies review


They may release, request documents, inspect, or hold the shipment.


06


### The broker manages exceptions and records


The importer remains involved in material decisions and supplies additional evidence when needed.

## How much does a customs broker cost?


There is no universal fee. Pricing depends on the provider, shipment volume, entry complexity, tariff lines, agency requirements, disbursements, bond needs, exams, and additional advisory or post-entry work.


Ask for a written scope separating the base entry fee from pass-through charges and optional services. A low headline price is not useful if classification, agency filings, after-hours support, or exception handling are excluded.


## How to choose a customs broker


Use these questions to compare providers:


Is the person or company actively licensed by CBP and operating under the required permit?


Does the team understand your products, origins, and agency requirements?


Who reviews difficult classification, valuation, and admissibility questions?


Can you see entry status, documents, duties, holds, and release milestones?


How early does the team review documents before arrival?


What happens after hours or when a shipment is held?


Which fees are included, and which are pass-through or additional?


How are powers of attorney, records, user access, and sensitive data controlled?

## Frequently asked questions


Is a customs broker a government employee? +


No. Customs brokers are private individuals or businesses licensed and regulated by CBP. They represent importers and exporters rather than working for CBP.


Can I clear my own goods through U.S. Customs? +


A qualifying owner or purchaser may generally make entry on its own behalf. Self-filing means taking responsibility for the entry process, classification, valuation, admissibility, payment, and recordkeeping.


Does using a broker remove my liability? +


No. The importer of record remains ultimately responsible for entry accuracy and applicable duties, taxes, and fees, even when a broker files the entry.


What is a customs power of attorney? +


It is the authorization that allows a licensed broker to conduct defined customs business for the importer. Its scope and execution must follow CBP rules.


Is a freight forwarder the same as a customs broker? +


No. A forwarder arranges transportation. A broker conducts customs business. A company can provide both, but customs work must be performed through its licensed brokerage authority.


Does a customs broker choose the HTS code? +


A broker can research and recommend or apply the classification based on the facts provided. The importer should supply complete product information and review material decisions because it remains responsible for reasonable care.


Does a customs broker provide a customs bond? +


Brokers often help an importer obtain or use an appropriate bond through a surety, but the bond is a separate financial guarantee with its own terms and cost.


How early should I send documents to my broker? +


Before the shipment arrives—and ideally before it departs—so classification, valuation, origin, agency, bond, and documentation issues can be resolved without consuming arrival time.


How do customs brokers get paid? +


They charge clients for brokerage and related services. Pricing varies, so importers should review the entry fee, pass-through charges, disbursements, and optional services in writing.
