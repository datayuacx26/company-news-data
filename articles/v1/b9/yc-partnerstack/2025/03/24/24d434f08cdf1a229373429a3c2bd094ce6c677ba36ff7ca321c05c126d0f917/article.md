# What is Co-Selling in a Partner Ecosystem (And How Can You Do It?)

Partner ecosystems have moved from a nice-to-have to a core go-to-market strategy — and how companies sell has fundamentally changed as a result. We’re in a time where no one owns their customer, competitors are colleagues and companies share intellectual property with other companies in order to co-build solutions.


The shift toward an ecosystems-driven business world has changed how companies sell. And in this new world,[co-selling](https://partnerstack.com/articles/signs-youre-ready-for-co-selling-partnerships) sales teams are essential. Direct sales teams will hand over the torch to (or evolve into) partnered sales teams — and these co-sell partnerships will be one-to-many as opposed to one-to-one.


We spoke with[Nicolette Lopes](https://www.linkedin.com/in/nicolettelopes/) , Head of Partnerships at Carbon6, to learn about the important role that co-selling plays in an ecosystem.


**In this article you’ll learn:**


- **What co-selling is and what it’s not**
- **How it differs from current sales processes**
- **How to successfully pull co-selling off within your own partner ecosystem**


## What are co-selling and co-selling partnerships?


[Co-selling](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/co-selling) is a sales motion where two or more companies work together to close a deal — combining their relationships, credibility and expertise to win business neither could easily close alone. Unlike[reselling](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/reseller-partner) , where a partner buys and resells a product on your behalf, co-selling keeps the vendor in the deal while a partner adds influence, context or technical authority. In a B2B SaaS context, this typically means a software vendor and a systems integrator, agency or technology partner jointly engaging a prospect to accelerate the sale.


Suppose you’re the sales director at a company that sells database application software. You go to a healthcare organization and tell them that your company’s software is the perfect fit for their business needs. They’re intrigued, but they’re not sold just yet.


To help ease their concerns, you bring in an external person. This person is an employee at a systems integrator. As a temporary member of your sales team, they explain that this is actually the best solution on the market for the healthcare organization’s use case.


Having someone from the systems integrator lends credibility, because they’ve worked with both your company and your company’s competitors. They’ve also worked on database application software implementations for other healthcare companies.


All of this put together gives the prospect confidence that they should purchase the solution. This is an example of a co-selling sales team. But why is co-selling necessary in the first place?

### Why is co-selling effective?


“Statistically speaking, buyers do not trust sales reps. If a buyer is going directly to a sales rep, they assume that the sales rep is giving them false information, overselling the product, maybe they’re not able to fully represent the tech itself,” Lopes explains.


> “Given today’s buyer behavior in SaaS, buyers are able to do so much of the work in procuring software before they ever actually talk to the brand. There is significant value in being able to inject a partner who is able to reach and influence your clients before you have the ability to.”


The database software example we described above is an example of ad-hoc co-selling within a sales team. In that example of co-selling, you as the sales director may just be a proactive high performer who has nurtured relationships over a long career and can invite experts from other companies into your sales team’s conversations.


You may have understood that the prospect needed a little[social proof](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/social-proof-in-partnerships) . While this does wonders for deals that you’re involved in, co-selling on an ad-hoc basis means it only happens on deals you’re involved in, rather than spreading to the rest of the organization.


### Adding structure to co-sell motions


In reality, co-selling is more structured. Companies that know they work well together — like the database application software vendor and systems integrator in this example — could have a pre-existing relationship that reduces the amount of downstream work a sales professional has to do to close the deal.


The prospect can come in already knowing that:


- They’ve chosen the right solution for their specific business need
- They have an expert who can help them implement their solution, so they can start enjoying the business value immediately


There are also[partner managers](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/partner-manager) who help facilitate these deals.

## What is the difference between co-selling and reselling?


Co-selling and reselling sound similar, but they’re actually quite different. To tell the difference, just ask: who actually pays for it?


“The distinction here is really in who owns the client and who manages the contracting. When we talk about co-selling, this can often take a number of different forms. It’s important to align with each of your co-sell partners individually to determine who will do what within a deal cycle and how the co-sell partner will be expected to support. This may include a ramp period, not dissimilar to a new direct sales hire, and over time may become a very self-sufficient source of revenue,” Lopes explains.


“However, having your co-sell partners take on vendor contracting may not always be feasible for one reason or another,” Lopes adds. “Enter resellers. Resell partners take margin, versus the co-sell motion, who typically receives a commission, but they often provide much less in-deal visibility. Your relationship with your resellers is paramount here, especially if you are in an organization that is used to having full control over the incoming revenue to the business.”


## What are the benefits of co-selling in your ecosystem?


Co-selling introduces benefits for everyone in your ecosystem. It's the go-to-market fuel that goes beyond straightforward channel partnerships. First, co-selling allows deals to happen faster, at lower cost and while generating greater value.


As Jay McBain, chief analyst at Omdia,[explained in a 2022 conversation with Partner Insight](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s2AWBszaFiM) , the tech industry, specifically SaaS, is no longer at a place where it’s worried about hiding the total cost of ownership (TCO) of a solution. If a customer wants to buy, it’s “not the 20% of the deal that a partner can go win…it’s the 200% or 300% of the deal that they can go win at 75% margin,” he said.


The data backs this up. According to[PartnerStack’s 2026 Network Report](https://partnerstack.com/resources/research-lab/report-partnerstack-is-scaling-revenue-precision-in-2026) , mid-market and enterprise deals — where co-selling plays the biggest role — now drive 78% of all transaction volume across the PartnerStack network.


Customers get a one-stop-shop and all of the companies involved in the co-sell partnership get a larger recurring revenue stream at a cheaper acquisition cost.

## How do you practice co-selling within your partner ecosystem?


Want to start leveraging the power of co-selling in your ecosystem? Follow these steps.


### Make sure you can dedicate time and resources *before* getting started


Co-selling isn’t something you can simply delegate to an existing member of your team as a side-of-the-desk task. If they are already producing marketing resources or conducting direct sales, tacking co-selling process management or channel partnership management onto their existing responsibilities risks a mediocre program without the right resourcing.


“We see it a lot,” Lopes explains. “They’ll repurpose one individual from somewhere on the[go-to-market team](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/go-to-market) and say, ‘Okay, this is half your job now. Keep doing your entire other job, but now also focus on building an entire partnership function.’”


Why are headcount and resourcing so important? Because co-selling takes time and effort, and partners can tell when a vendor is not committing adequate time or energy to them. Partners work with lots of vendors, so it’s not difficult for them to spot a subpar co-sell partner program. Despite this, companies often want to wait until something breaks before they take action and commit real resources to their program.


*See more:*[The four stages of partnership maturity: A benchmarking framework for program leaders](https://partnerstack.com/articles/four-stages-of-partnership-maturity-benchmarking-framework-for-program-leaders) *.*


### Pay attention to your program before things go awry


“What ‘breaking’ means is that your partners are pissed off at you, your partners start firing you, deals start falling through, and that’s when you then realize, ‘Hey. We actually need to take this seriously,’” Lopes says. “When that happens, you’ve lost those relationships. They are not going to come back and work with you after you’ve burned the bridge.”


Since it’ll be difficult to get all of the technology set up at the beginning of your partner program, having a dedicated person who can engage with a partner’s sales team, listen to them and ensure they are heard is important.


### Find existing opportunities and turn them into formal partnerships


Another way for companies to get started is to look at “shadow” co-selling that’s already happening.


“People ask, ‘Where do you find these partners?’ The first place you should look is within your business,” Lopes says. “The reality is that partners are already influencing customers to work with you. You may just not be treating them like partners yet.”


Lopes explains that SaaS companies can usually go into Slack or other internal communication tools to discover underlying partner motions working within the business. Look for threads along the lines of “Hey, we have a current customer who wants to introduce us to someone. What rep should I loop in?”


This is a referral partner, not a direct sale motion. And with PartnerStack, that motion can be completely seamless —[using a simple slash command directly in Slack](https://support.partnerstack.com/hc/en-us/articles/43454135699347-How-to-capture-leads-from-Slack) to submit. No logging into portals. No missed opportunities.


*Learn more:*[The future of co-sell doesn't start in a PRM, it starts where your partners work](https://partnerstack.com/articles/co-sell-partner-experience-announcement) *.*


Lopes also points to instances where SaaS companies are doing an implementation with someone for a SaaS solution, but their email domain is different than that of the ISV who is the end customer. This reveals the people helping to implement specific solutions, AKA the partners that they should be working with.


“There’s a lot of that living under the surface,” Lopes continues. “It’s just a matter of going to your customers to understand, ‘How did you buy us? What did that process look like? Who did you leverage or lean on for industry insights? Are you working with someone now to help you?’ And then working with those people to maximize the relationships.”


### Identify your ideal partner


Next, you’ll need to identify who your ideal partner is.[Ideal partner profiles (IPPs)](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/ideal-partner-profile-ipp) aren’t new in the partnerships world, but when applying them to co-sell relationships, we recommend layering in another step: **analyzing your organization in terms of its fit for your ideal partner.** Conduct a SWOT analysis to identify:


- Strengths
- Weaknesses
- Threats
- Opportunities


For instance, if you’re a technology company with an excellent product (strength) but a weak implementation process or customer success team (weakness), there’s the threat that another business will come in and offer a smoother experience with a rival product. Your opportunity here is to tap into new markets and deepen relationships within your existing market by enhancing the experience around using your product.


This will also help you identify your unique value proposition. For instance, if your product is as good as you think, it solves a problem that managed service providers or systems integrators regularly have to solve for their customers. These are potential partners who would be happy to co-sell solutions to their customers, expanding the number of organizations that you can access.


*See more:*[How HubSpot and PartnerStack are powering co-sell at scale](https://partnerstack.com/articles/network-connect-cosell-2025) *.*


### Clarify exactly how much support you can provide


What exactly will you be offering your co-sell partners? Will they have access to a[PRM that makes it easy for them to track deals](https://partnerstack.com/platform/co-sell) and share and access information? Will there be an employee who’s specifically responsible for co-selling relationships who will guide them if they ever need assistance? Will there be a learning management portal that gives them access to product information and training?


Make it clear what this partner relationship will look like so there are no unrealistic expectations around your program. Again, partners are wary of “landing page only” co-sell partnerships, and the explosion of digital companies means they are spoiled for choice.


“People or organizations who are familiar with this co-sell model, they know what a good partner program looks like and they know what a bad one looks like. It’s important to understand that your prospective partners have options, and those options are your competitors. Partners will not invest in an organization who is not investing in them and often, vendors learn this lesson the hard way,” Lopes says.


“It's not that you need to have everything 100 per cent ready prior to launching your partner program, but it is important to prioritize partner success early on. This often means getting your partner's insights into your product, your market position, your co-marketing approach.”


> Your partners have the relationship with your end customers, lean on them to better understand how they are winning the business before you are.’”
> -Nicolette Lopes, Head of Partnerships at Carbon6


### Map out potential accounts and plan for your co-selling strategy


Once you’ve engaged with your ideal partner, get together to map out potential co-selling opportunities. This is a list of potential customers that you feel would be perfectly suited for your co-selling arrangement. Include people from different departments (like sales, marketing and product) in this conversation to get a complete picture.


Next, identify who holds the strongest relationships with these potential customers and who is best positioned to initiate these conversations. From there, develop account plans for each of these potential customers. These plans should outline potential pain points, the co-created solutions that will resonate most with this audience, the timing for sales team introductions and specific KPIs for tracking success.

### Develop co-marketing plans for your co-sell strategy


You and your partner may have determined that you’re a perfect match, but that doesn’t mean the market knows this. If you want to have a successful co-selling relationship, your partner manager will need to develop a co-marketing plan. This plan will outline the business goals and the joint message you want to deliver to your target audience. The tangible outcomes of this plan might include:


- A co-branded landing page or website
- A co-branded digital marketing campaign (e.g., paid ads, social media ads, ebooks)
- Guest articles on your partner’s site (and vice versa)
- [Podcast](https://partnerstack.com/articles/10-lessons-from-our-first-10-episodes-of-the-partnerstack-podcast) interviews
- Press releases


The goal is to build awareness of this useful, co-created solution within your target market.


This is also where social selling can come into play. Unlike social media marketing, social selling is about salespeople or influencer partners building deeper relationships with the people in their networks through social media. Some have even called it[a way to replace cold calling](https://blog.hootsuite.com/what-is-social-selling/) and[the future of networking](https://thecmo.com/demand-generation/social-media-sales/) .


Social selling can be done through a company’s brand page, but it’s often done by individual sales representatives via one-to-one conversations online, so that their brand is the first one customers think of when they’re considering a purchase. The key is to have real conversations and share user-generated content, since many buyers turn to peer recommendations and authentic reviews before they ever talk to a sales rep.

Co-selling is also a great opportunity to introduce team selling. Team selling is where multiple people work together to close an account, rather than one sales representative taking ownership of the account themselves. It could also be a team member from a particular department (such as product or engineering) who can answer specific questions a customer has and inspire confidence. Co-selling partners extend the number of team players you can bring into a call as well as the expertise each of those team players brings.


### Measure your co-selling partner relationship


Define metrics and[KPIs](https://partnerstack.com/glossary/key-performance-indicators-kpis) for your co-selling partner relationships so that you always have data to return to in order to refine your programs. Important metrics to track are:


- The number of qualified leads your co-selling partnerships have produced
- The dollar value of each qualified lead your co-selling agreements have produced
- Pre-qualified leads that your co-selling agreements have introduced into your pipeline
- How much revenue you’ve realized from partner-sourced or partner-influenced deals
- How much different partners contributed to a specific deal (which informs how much of a cut they get from the deal)
- The average deal size that each partner brings in

### Use your data to continue to improve and refine your program


As you build your co-selling relationships with your partners, you’ll gather more data, understand which partners bring the most value and identify opportunities to adjust your compensation. This is also an important way to start adding to your program without biting off more than you can chew. For instance, you may find that your partners have trouble accessing support when it comes to selling or servicing your products. Providing a better technical support solution like a dedicated line or inbox might be something that enriches your partner program during the next iteration.

This allows partner teams to focus on relationship building and avoid operational errors (such as failing to properly allocate a deal to the partner that brought it in) that erode trust.

## What are common co-selling mistakes to avoid?


As you’re building your co-selling program, you may find yourself stumbling from time to time. Here are a few pitfalls to avoid.


According to Lopes, the biggest mistake you can make is *not* operationalizing your program sooner. This means not putting people and technology in place. Co-selling programs can’t run themselves. You need a channel manager and someone who’s going to be there to physically support your partners through the sales cycle, be available for them, get them timely technical support and make sure that they can still be positioned as the industry expert in front of clients.


Eventually, you want to get to the point where you have an entire partner team. This partner team can manage different arrangements (co-selling,[co-marketing](https://partnerstack.com/articles/partner-co-marketing-strategies) , co-innovation, strategic partnerships, etc.) and ideally, this partner team won’t exist in a silo. They’ll be spread throughout the organization and regularly interact with other teams, driving predictable and consistent pipeline.
