## What causes melasma, and why is it so stubborn?


Melasma is a chronic pigment condition triggered by a combination of sun exposure, visible light, and hormones, which is why it resists quick fixes. Ultraviolet light and even visible light — the kind that passes through windows — switch on the pigment-producing melanocytes in your skin, and hormonal shifts from pregnancy or birth control pills make those cells more reactive. According to[Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/melasma-what-are-the-best-treatments-202207112776) , melasma has no outright cure and frequent relapses are common, so treatment is about steady fading and maintenance rather than a one-time cure.


Melasma most often appears as symmetric brown or gray-brown patches on the cheeks, forehead, upper lip, and bridge of the nose. It's far more common in women and in people with medium-to-deep skin tones, where more active melanocytes mean pigment forms readily and lingers. That same reactivity is why aggressive treatments can backfire and cause more darkening, making gentle-but-consistent the winning strategy.


## What ingredients actually fade melasma?


The most evidence-backed melasma ingredients are tranexamic acid, azelaic acid, and niacinamide, each interrupting pigment production through a different pathway. Per the[Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology](https://jcadonline.com/topical-treatments-melasma-mechanism-of-action/) , topical tranexamic acid works upstream of pigment by calming the plasmin pathway that links UV exposure to melanin, with studies showing significant improvement by weeks four to eight. Azelaic acid competitively inhibits tyrosinase, the key enzyme in melanin production, and in a 329-patient trial 65% of users on 20% azelaic acid achieved good-to-excellent results.


Niacinamide reduces the transfer of pigment into surface skin cells, with 4% formulas cutting melasma severity by around 62% over eight weeks. Supporting actives round out the picture: kojic acid and vitamin C also chelate the copper that tyrosinase needs, while alpha-hydroxy acids like glycolic and lactic acid speed cell turnover so pigment clears faster. The strongest serums combine several of these so more than one pathway is addressed at once, which is exactly how UNSPOTTED is built.


## How long does it take a serum to fade melasma?


Most people see visible melasma fading in about 8 to 12 weeks of nightly use, with the deepest patches taking longer. Clinical studies on tranexamic acid show measurable improvement starting around weeks four to eight, and azelaic acid trials report significant lightening within the first month or two of twice-daily use. Melasma sits partly deeper in the skin than ordinary dark spots, so patience matters — stopping early is the most common reason a serum "doesn't work."


The single biggest accelerator is sun protection. Without daily broad-spectrum SPF, fresh UV and visible-light exposure re-darkens the patches faster than any serum can lighten them, which is why dermatologists treat sunscreen as part of the treatment, not an optional add-on.

## How to use a melasma serum for the best results


Apply your melasma serum to clean, dry skin at night, then lock in daily sun protection every single morning. Because tranexamic acid, azelaic acid, and AHAs are the workhorses in most melasma serums, nighttime application avoids sun sensitivity and lets the actives work uninterrupted. Start every other night if your skin is reactive, then build to nightly over one to two weeks.


The non-negotiable step is a broad-spectrum tinted sunscreen containing iron oxides, which Harvard Health specifically recommends for melasma because iron oxides block visible light that ordinary sunscreens miss. Reapply through the day, add a wide-brim hat outdoors, and avoid picking or over-exfoliating, since inflammation and heat can both worsen melasma. Give any routine a full 12 weeks before judging it, and keep using a maintenance serum even after patches fade to hold your results.


## Frequently Asked Questions


### Can melasma be cured permanently?


No, melasma cannot be cured permanently, but it can be significantly faded and controlled. Dermatology sources describe melasma as a chronic condition prone to relapse, so the realistic goal is lightening the pigment and then maintaining results with ongoing sun protection and a maintenance serum. Stopping treatment and sunscreen usually allows the patches to return.


### Is tranexamic acid or hydroquinone better for melasma?


Tranexamic acid and hydroquinone can both fade melasma, and studies show topical tranexamic acid performs comparably to hydroquinone with fewer side effects. Hydroquinone is a powerful prescription-strength option but can cause irritation and, with prolonged use, rebound darkening. Tranexamic acid is gentler for long-term and maintenance use, which is why many modern serums feature it.


### Can I use a melasma serum while pregnant?


Some melasma ingredients are considered pregnancy-friendly, such as azelaic acid and niacinamide, but others are typically paused, so check with your doctor first. Pregnancy is itself a common melasma trigger, and many people simply focus on strict sun protection until after pregnancy and breastfeeding. Always confirm any active ingredient with your OB or dermatologist before use.


### Does melasma come back after it fades?


Yes, melasma frequently returns after fading, especially with renewed sun or visible-light exposure or hormonal changes. That's why dermatologists emphasize year-round sunscreen and a maintenance routine even after your skin looks clear. Consistent iron-oxide sun protection is the most effective way to keep faded melasma from darkening again.


### Do I still need sunscreen if I use a melasma serum?


Yes, sunscreen is essential and arguably matters more than the serum itself. Melasma is triggered by UV and visible light, so without daily broad-spectrum protection, fresh sun exposure re-darkens the pigment faster than any serum can lighten it. A tinted mineral sunscreen with iron oxides is the dermatologist-preferred choice for melasma.
