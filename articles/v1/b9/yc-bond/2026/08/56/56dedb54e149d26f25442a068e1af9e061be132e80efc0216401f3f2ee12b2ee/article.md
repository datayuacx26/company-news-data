# How to Prioritize Tasks When Everything Is P0

## How to Prioritize When Everything Is P0


Time to face a harsh truth: most prioritization advice online is useless.


You can force your to-do list into an Eisenhower Matrix, color-coded Kanban board, a list of alarms on your phone, or whatever productivity framework is having its moment. But none of them answers the question that actually makes prioritization hard:


What do you do when multiple things are genuinely P0?


A board deck is due Friday. Your biggest customer needs an answer today. The product team cannot move until you make a decision.


None of this is fake work. None of it can be ignored. And depending on who you ask, each one is correctly considered the company’s top priority.


When everything is P0, the goal is not to discover which tasks matter most because, spoiler alert, they all do.


The goal is to build two systems: one that forces tasks into an explicit order using consistent logic every time, and one that cleanly manages what gets delayed as a result.


The first tells you what happens now. The second ensures that everything still happens later.


### Why Everything Feels Urgent


Most productivity systems assume your work starts as a list, but executive work usually starts as a request: a board member wants revisions before Friday. Sales needs help saving a customer. Product is waiting on a decision before they can ship.


On top of being never-ending, these requests almost always arrive with an “urgent” label attached to them.


Sales sees the revenue at risk.


Product sees the team sitting idle.


The board sees the deadline.


You are the person expected to see all three.


That is what makes prioritization difficult: you are not just sorting one clean list of tasks by importance. You are reconciling competing commitments made to different people, often across meetings, Slack, email, and work you already delegated.


And because those commitments are scattered, the loudest or most recent request tends to win by default.


But—as you know very well—that is not true prioritization.


## **Two Systems for Dealing with P0 Overwhelm**


### **System One: Create an order**


#### **1. Capture every commitment—including the ones buried in meetings and Slack**


Your actual workload does not live in one task manager. It is spread across meeting notes, Slack DMs, email threads, documents waiting for approval, and promises you made out loud.


Before ranking anything, sweep every place where work enters your day. You cannot prioritize what you cannot see.


### **2. Separate what only you can do from what anyone else can do**


Before deciding which task comes first, remove the work that should not be competing for your time.


Separate tasks that require your judgment—a strategic decision, sensitive conversation, or final call—from tasks that require your role but not your hands, like gathering information, scheduling, drafting, or routine approvals.


### **3. Score what remains by the cost of waiting**


Once the list contains only work that genuinely requires you, compare each task using the same questions:


-


What gets worse if this waits?


-


When does that consequence begin?


-


What valuable work is blocked?


-


How difficult will the decision or consequence be to reverse?


This prevents the loudest, newest, or most uncomfortable request from automatically moving to the top.


### **4. When multiple tasks are genuinely P0, force them into a single order**


Several tasks can matter equally. They still cannot all happen first.


When two stakeholders both have legitimate urgent requests, make the tradeoff explicit:


“I can get X done by Thursday, but that moves Y to next week. Is that the right tradeoff?”


The goal is not to argue that one request does not matter. It is to decide which consequence you are more willing to accept.


### **5. Block real time for the true top priority**


Once you have chosen what comes first, make your calendar reflect it.


Short unblockers may need ten minutes and an immediate decision. Work that requires sustained judgment needs protected time during the hours when you think most clearly.


A priority that never receives time is only a preference.


## **System Two: Manage What Gets Delayed**


Choosing what comes first automatically pushes something else back.


That is not a failure of prioritization. Leaving the displaced work undefined is.


### **6. Give every delayed or delegated task a follow-up loop**


Every task that does not happen now needs:


-


An owner


-


A next action


-


A review date or trigger


-


An updated expectation for whoever is waiting


If the work is delegated, define what “done” means, when it is due, what could block it, and when it should return to your attention.


Delegation should remove the execution from your plate—not your visibility into whether the result is still on track.


## Bringing It Together


When everything is P0, you do not need another framework. You need visibility, an order, and a way to keep “later” from becoming “forgotten.”


Get every commitment in one place. Remove the work that does not require you. Rank what remains by the cost of waiting. Then force it into a real sequence.


That is prioritization: not pretending everything can happen now, but making a clear choice about what happens first while giving everything else an owner, a next step, and a reason to resurface.
