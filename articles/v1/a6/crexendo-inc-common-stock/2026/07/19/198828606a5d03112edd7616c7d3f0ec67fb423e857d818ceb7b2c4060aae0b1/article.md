## What Does Your POTS Shutdown Notice Actually Say?


A POTS shutdown notice can look fairly bureaucratic, but it typically holds a few pieces of information worth pulling out right away: the specific circuit or line numbers affected, the wire center or service area, a retirement or disconnection date, and a carrier contact for questions. Some carriers bundle every affected line into one letter; others send separate notices per circuit, which can make it easy to miss one if your business has lines at multiple locations.


***A shutdown notice functions less like paperwork and more like a countdown clock, even if the exact timing can vary by carrier and line type.***


It’s worth checking whether the date listed is a hard disconnection date or a deadline to complete a port or transfer request, since those aren’t always the same thing. It can also help to note whether the letter mentions any process for requesting more time, even if that process isn’t guaranteed to work. For background on the regulatory shift behind these notices,[the FCC’s March 2026 order and what it changed](https://www.crexendo.com/blog/pots-lines-shutdown-what-businesses-need-to-know/) is covered in more detail in an earlier post.

## What Can You Consider Doing in the First 48 Hours?


The first two days after a notice arrives tend to matter more than the weeks that follow, mostly because early missteps compound. A reasonable starting point is matching every line or circuit number on the notice against your internal records, so you know exactly what’s affected before assuming anything.


***Waiting for a full audit before acting on the parts you already know about can cost more time than it saves.***


Any line that might connect to a fire alarm panel, elevator phone, security system, or similar life-safety equipment could be worth flagging immediately, even before the rest of the inventory is complete. Looping in facilities and security teams the same day, rather than after IT finishes its own review, can help surface dependencies that IT alone might not know about. A shutdown notice can feel like pure disruption in the moment, but for many businesses, the migration it forces ends up addressing gaps, including outdated equipment, single points of failure, and missing redundancy that may have gone unaddressed for years otherwise.

## Are You Starting From a Plan, or Starting Closer to Zero?


What happens next can depend a lot on whether your business already did some of this groundwork. If you’ve already built out **[a POTS readiness plan with an inventory, an owner, and a target date](https://www.crexendo.com/blog/90-days-to-replace-your-pots-lines-does-your-business-have-a-plan/) ,** this notice mostly shifts you from planning mode into execution mode — confirming vendor timelines, scheduling the cutover, and notifying anyone who needs to know.


***A notice tends to reward whatever planning already happened and expose whatever didn’t.***


If there’s no plan in place yet, the same steps can still apply, just compressed. Instead of a quarter to complete an inventory, it might need to happen in days: pulling every carrier bill, listing every line, and assigning at least a temporary owner for each one. It doesn’t need to be an elaborate process to be useful; it can be as simple as a shared spreadsheet with names attached can move things forward faster than waiting for a more formal project structure to form.

## Who Might You Want to Contact This Week?


A few conversations can be worth having early, though the order may depend on your situation. Your carrier’s account representative can confirm whether the date on the notice is fixed and whether any extension process actually applies to your account. A replacement vendor conversation can help clarify realistic timelines for the lines you’re moving, whether that’s[a cloud-based phone system](https://www.crexendo.com/vip/pots-replacement/) for standard voice lines or a different approach for specialized equipment. It may also be worth asking what else that conversation could cover:[A McKinsey survey](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/b2b-customers-have-spoken-will-telcos-listen) found that nearly 60% of enterprise customers now prefer to buy core connectivity bundled with cloud communication and collaboration tools rather than as a standalone line item, which can make this a reasonable moment to look at more than a like-for-like swap.


***The businesses that move fastest after a notice tend to be the ones who know exactly who to call before they need to.***


For any line tied to fire alarms, elevators, or similar systems, it may also be worth checking in with whoever handles code compliance at your organization, since replacement equipment for those lines can carry its own approval requirements. None of these calls need to happen in a single day, but starting them the same week the notice arrives tends to leave more room for the unexpected.


---


## What if Your Timeline Feels Too Tight?


Sometimes the math doesn’t work — the notice period is short, and full replacement for every line isn’t realistic before the listed date. In that situation, a temporary bridge option might be worth exploring rather than treating the deadline as all-or-nothing. Cellular backup connections or compact on-site devices designed to stand in for analog lines can sometimes buy time for life-safety or specialized equipment while a more permanent solution gets put in place.


***A short notice window doesn’t necessarily mean there’s no room to maneuver, but it usually means the maneuvering needs to start immediately.***


It can also be reasonable to ask the carrier directly whether any extension or delay is possible, even if the answer isn’t guaranteed to be yes. Carriers vary in how they handle these requests, and a documented attempt to negotiate more time may matter later even if it doesn’t change the date. From here, the practical next question tends to become how the actual migration works, line by line and system by system, which is where the conversation naturally continues.

## FAQ


****Is it too late to build a plan once the notice has already arrived?****


Not necessarily. A plan built after the notice arrives just tends to move faster and cover less ground than one built ahead of time. The core pieces — an inventory, an owner per line, and a target date — can still apply, they may just need to be compressed into days rather than a full quarter.


****Should I call my carrier or my replacement vendor first?****


Either can be reasonable, depending on what you already know. If you’re unsure whether the date is negotiable, starting with the carrier might clarify that first. If you already know you’re moving forward, a vendor conversation could help you understand realistic timelines sooner.


****Can I actually negotiate more time with my carrier?****


It depends on the carrier and the type of line involved, and there’s no guarantee an extension will be granted. Some carriers may have a documented process for delay requests tied to life-safety equipment, while others may not. Asking directly tends to be worth the time either way.


****What if facilities or security doesn’t know what’s still running on copper?****


This is fairly common, since these lines often predate current staff. Asking directly — “what still uses a phone jack?” — across facilities, security, and IT can surface equipment that wouldn’t show up on a typical technology inventory.


****What’s a temporary bridge option if I can’t fully migrate in time?****


Cellular backup connections and compact devices built to emulate analog signals for fire alarms, elevators, or similar equipment can sometimes serve as a stopgap. These aren’t necessarily meant to be permanent, but they may help maintain code-required connectivity while a longer-term replacement gets finalized.
