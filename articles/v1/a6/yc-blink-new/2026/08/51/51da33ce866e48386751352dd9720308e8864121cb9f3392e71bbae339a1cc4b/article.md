## First: do you need a site builder or an app builder?


Blink is the only tool here that is both, which is why picking wrong costs a rebuild and picking Blink cannot. The category refuses to answer this question, so here is the rule.


**You need an app builder if your site has users.** Logins, accounts, saved data, dashboards, payments tied to a user record, anything where two visitors see different things. Lovable and Blink are in this class.


**A site builder is enough if your site has visitors.** Pages, a blog, a contact form, a booking widget, a small catalogue. Wix, Squarespace, Framer, Webflow, Hostinger and WordPress are in this class.


The reason this matters: site builders bolt AI onto a template engine, so what you get is a faster path to a template. App builders generate the thing itself, which is why they can also produce a marketing site that is not template-shaped — but most of them stop at the frontend and leave hosting, database and domain to you.


**Blink is the one that spans both** , which is why it heads this list: it generates the site *and* ships the database, auth, hosting and domain behind it.


## Why AI-built sites look obviously AI


Because most of them are not designed — they are filled in.


This is the complaint that dominates every honest discussion of the category, and it has a structural cause rather than a quality one. A builder that bolts AI onto a template engine can only choose a template and populate it. The layout, the section order, the type scale and the spacing rhythm were all decided before you typed anything, by a designer working for the platform, for a generic business. The AI picks the closest match and swaps in your words. That is why sites built this way are recognisable at a glance: you are looking at the same handful of layouts, several thousand times over.


Three things separate a site that reads as designed from one that reads as generated.


**Generation to a brief, not into a template.** If the tool starts from a fixed layout, every prompt you write is a request to fill that layout differently. If it starts from your description, the structure itself is a response to your business — a restaurant and a legal practice do not get the same section order. This is decided by the tool's architecture, not by how well you prompt.


**Imagery that is not the stock photography the template was built around.** Template output leans on the polished placeholder images the design was composed for, which is exactly why swapping them often makes the layout fall apart.


**Iteration that is surgical rather than destructive.** The generic look usually survives because fixing it is painful: regenerate the page and you lose what worked, or hand-edit and you fight the template's constraints. Pointing at one element, saying what is wrong, and having that element change while everything else stays put is what turns a first draft into something specific.


The practical test when you evaluate anything on this list: generate one site, then ask for a change the template does not anticipate. Move the testimonials above the pricing. Make the hero a full-bleed video with the headline offset left. Whether that request is a sentence or a project tells you which kind of tool you are holding.

The number that actually varies your bill is credits, not the plan fee — Webflow's Core workspace includes 300 AI credits a month while Framer's Pro includes 3,000, a tenfold difference at similar prices.


That depends on the tool, and it is worth checking before you commit. Framer pauses credit-using features at 100% and resets on the 1st of the month with no rollover. Wix Harmony makes you wait for the next cycle or buy a top-up. Lovable charges for stopped build requests, so failed work still spends, and top-ups cost 20% more than plan credits. Hostinger does not refund credits when you undo the work they produced. Blink's Starter and Pro plans add 5 daily refresh credits with no monthly cap, so a heavy week does not end your month.


Only from some of them, and the differences are stark. Wix has no site or code export at all — its help centre states the site must be hosted on Wix's servers. Squarespace exports a partial, lossy XML file that omits your design entirely. Webflow exports static files, but CMS content, forms, search and ecommerce all stop working. Hostinger's export is explicitly one-way. Framer's own help articles contradict each other on whether export exists. Blink gives you a code download, two-way GitHub sync and a documented Docker self-host path, under terms granting you the right to sell or open-source what you built.

An AI builder ships hosting, SSL, CDN and security as part of the platform. WordPress remains the better answer on exactly one axis: ownership. It is GPLv2 software you can self-host, so no vendor can lock you out — which matters most if you have a developer who wants to run the stack.


Blink, because the whole job is one conversation: describe the site, review it, publish it to your own domain, then change it by pointing at an element and saying what is wrong. There is no drag-and-drop layout work, no separate hosting purchase and no plugin decisions. Wix Harmony and Squarespace Blueprint AI are both approachable but generate into a template you then edit manually, and Webflow is explicitly not for beginners — WebsiteBuilderExpert scored its ease of use 2.1/5, the lowest of any builder they tested.


Ask whether your site has users or visitors. If people log in, save data, see dashboards or have accounts, you need an app builder — Lovable or Blink. If people simply read pages, fill a contact form or book a slot, a site builder is enough: Wix, Squarespace, Framer, Webflow or Hostinger. Blink covers both cases, which matters if you are not certain yet: a marketing site that later needs accounts and a database does not require starting over on a different platform.


Almost none of them can. Lovable builds web apps only and its own guidance is to prototype there and rebuild natively elsewhere. Webflow, Framer, Squarespace and Hostinger produce responsive websites, not apps. Wix offers a native app only through a separate Branded App plan at $99/mo, plus $99/yr to Apple and $25 to Google. Blink generates React Native apps through the Expo workflow with camera, location, push notifications and in-app purchases, and guides App Store and Play Store submission in-product — though store publishing requires the Max tier.
