## The 4-Stage Solopreneur Build Process


The $0-to-$10K MRR journey isn't random. The pattern repeats across enough indie hacker case studies that it has recognizable phases.

**Stage 1: Validate with a landing page (2 hours)**


Before building anything, you need a signal. A landing page with a waitlist button — or better, a "Pay $X to get early access" CTA — tells you whether the market exists. With Blink, you can describe the product in a prompt and have a landing page live in under an hour. No hosting config. No domain pointing. The URL is live the moment you ship.


The most important rule at this stage: charge from day one. Even $10/month provides better validation signal than free users. The discipline of charging shapes every product decision that follows.


**Stage 2: Ship the MVP (weekend)**


The MVP is not the product. It is the first conversation. Build the core workflow — the single thing that makes the product worth paying for — and nothing else. One feature, full-stack, working.


Post Bridge started as "upload once, post everywhere." No analytics, no team features, no advanced scheduling. Just the core loop. That minimal product hit $7K–$11K MRR in four months.


With Blink, you describe the app, and auth, database, and hosting come along automatically. The friction that used to eat the first two weekends of every MVP is gone.


**Stage 3: Iterate based on feedback (days, not months)**


This is where solo building compounds. When a customer says "I wish it did X," you can ship X the same day. Not in the next sprint. Not after the next planning cycle. The same afternoon.


The case study founders who hit $10K MRR fastest shared one pattern: they spent 80% of their time on customer development (not building) in the first 60 days. They let customer feedback drive what to build next.

Revenue is not the last step. It's the validation step. Paid customers have a fundamentally different relationship with your product than free users.


## What Solopreneurs Actually Build


The highest-success solopreneur products share a structural trait: they serve a niche that's too small for a VC-backed startup to care about, but large enough to generate $5K–$30K MRR.


**1. Niche scheduling tools** ($5K–$20K MRR common). Post Bridge targets solo creators and indie hackers. Not enterprise teams. This is too small for Hootsuite to optimize for — and exactly the right size for a solo founder.


**2. AI-powered workflow automators for a specific vertical** ($3K–$15K MRR). Tools that connect a specific industry's existing data sources to AI analysis. Tax preparers, real estate agents, e-commerce merchants — each has specific workflows with no off-the-shelf AI solution.


**3. "Scratch your own itch" developer tools** ($2K–$10K MRR). The indie hacker tradition. A specific pain point that you have, that other developers probably have too.


**4. Community analytics tools** ($1K–$8K MRR). Tools that pull Reddit, Discord, or Slack data and surface insights. Every community manager wants this; most SaaS platforms don't prioritize it.


**5. Lightweight CRM alternatives** ($3K–$12K MRR). Not Salesforce replacements. Tools for a specific kind of relationship: freelancer-client, coach-student, advisor-portfolio company. The niche is the moat.


**6. Content repurposing tools** ($2K–$8K MRR). One piece of content, many formats. The Jack Friks model, applied to long-form content instead of short videos.


**7. Niche research aggregators** ($2K–$6K MRR). Tools that watch a specific corner of the internet — job boards, patent filings, court records, academic journals — and surface signals for a specific professional.

## Common Vibe Coding Mistakes Solopreneurs Make


**Mistake 1: Building before validating.** The fastest path to $10K MRR is not coding — it's talking to customers. Most solopreneurs who fail do so because they spent six months building something nobody wanted. Two hours on a landing page before you write a line of app logic.


**Mistake 2: Choosing a frontend-only tool and discovering the infrastructure gap at 11 PM.** The "I'll just hook up Supabase later" trap has cost thousands of founders dozens of hours. Full-stack means full-stack from day one — not frontend now, database maybe later.


**Mistake 3: Waiting until the product is "ready" to charge.** Ready doesn't exist. Charge at MVP. Charge when you have five customers who want it. The first paying customer teaches you more than the first 100 free users.


**Mistake 4: Treating AI as the differentiator.** AI tools are infrastructure, not product strategy. The founders hitting $10K+ MRR are winning on customer understanding and distribution, not on having the best AI stack. The stack is a multiplier. Judgment is the input.


**Mistake 5: Building everything solo when AI agents can handle the support tier.** One Blink Claw agent handling FAQ-level support means you're not answering the same three questions every day. Automate what repeats; focus on what doesn't.


## How to Pick Your First App Idea


The frameworks that work for solo founders are different from the frameworks venture capitalists use to evaluate startups. You don't need a billion-dollar TAM. You need 500 customers who will pay $20/month.


**The overlap question:** Where does your professional knowledge, personal frustration, and observable market gap intersect? Jack Friks was a content creator who found cross-platform posting painful. He built for himself first, then for others like him.


**The niche test:** Can you describe your target customer in one sentence with at least three specific attributes? ("Solo SaaS founders with under $50K ARR who post on Twitter and LinkedIn daily" is a niche. "People who use social media" is not.)


**The "is this too small for enterprise?" filter:** If Salesforce, HubSpot, or Google could add this as a checkbox feature, it's not a good solopreneur business. If it's too specific for them to bother, you have a moat.


**The customer discovery shortcut:** Find 10 Reddit or Twitter posts where people complain about the exact problem you want to solve. If the posts exist and have replies, there's demand. If you can't find them, either the problem isn't painful or you're searching wrong.


**The "can I build the MVP in a weekend?" test:** If the MVP requires three months, it's not a solopreneur business — it's a startup. Good solopreneur businesses have a core workflow you can ship in 48 hours and improve forever.
