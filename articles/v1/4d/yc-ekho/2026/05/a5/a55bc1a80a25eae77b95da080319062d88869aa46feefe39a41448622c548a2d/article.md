# What Buyers Actually Say to an AI Sales Agent at 2:14pm on a Sunday

AI summary

This piece walks through two real, anonymized AI Sales Agent conversations from powersports dealerships - one a 15-minute Sunday-afternoon trade-in conversation that ended in a Tuesday showroom appointment, one a 9:37am inventory check that turned into a same-day phone call in three minutes - and pulls out what the back-and-forth reveals about how buyers actually want to be sold to.


The most useful thing about an AI Sales Agent isn't what it does. It's what it surfaces.


Every dealership knows the lead funnel leaks. Forms come in overnight, on weekends, during a busy Saturday on the floor - and the longer they sit, the colder they get. What's harder to see is what's actually inside those leads: the questions buyers ask before they pick up the phone, the hesitations, the small specifics that decide whether they walk in.


Below are two anonymized conversations from powersports dealers running the AI Sales Agent in the past two weeks. Both ended in a booked appointment.


##


Conversation 1: Two bikes, one garage, one decision


A returning rider pinged our partner dealer's website on a Sunday afternoon with five words:


> "do you purchase pre-owned motorcycles?"


That's the kind of message a lot of dealerships still treat as a tire-kicker. It's not. Within 15 minutes - ten back-and-forth turns - the AI Sales Agent had:


- Established that the buyer wanted to consolidate, not just sell - trading two bikes (a Concours 1400 tourer and a 650cc Yamaha custom) into one.
- Narrowed the engine displacement window ("smaller than the 1400, bigger than the 650").
- Pulled out the riding-position preference ("upright - not café, not chopper").
- Surfaced an ergonomic constraint the buyer hadn't volunteered until asked: a lower seat height was a hard requirement.
- Suggested specific in-segment models that fit those constraints - Bonneville T100, Z650RS - instead of pushing whatever was on the floor.
- Booked a showroom appointment at the buyer's preferred time, worked around a competing weekend project, and re-confirmed when the buyer hesitated about walk-in vs. appointment.


Most of those steps are qualifying steps - the work a strong BDC rep would do if they had unlimited time and zero other leads in the queue.


One moment is worth quoting. Six turns in, the buyer hesitated about giving up his contact info:


> "Do you work on an appointment only basis?"


The reflexive sales answer is "no, walk-ins welcome" - and lose the appointment. The agent did better:


> "We certainly welcome walk-ins, but setting an appointment ensures I have a specialist ready to focus on your two trade-ins and help you find that perfect upright fit. Since you mentioned 2:45 PM ET on Tuesday, would you like to lock that in so we can be ready for you?"


The buyer said "Sure. Thanks" and gave up his name and number on the next message.


Total cost to the dealership: zero minutes. By Tuesday morning, the floor team had a qualified appointment with a buyer who'd already spelled out his bikes, his budget envelope, his ergonomic constraints, and his preferred riding position.


##


Conversation 2: Three minutes, one phone call booked


The second conversation is the one that matters when people argue an AI Sales Agent can't move fast enough on a transactional buyer. Seven turns. Total elapsed time: three minutes. The buyer's first message:


> "850 sports available?"


That's a buyer with a specific bike in mind, almost certainly off a search result, checking if the store has it. Most sales rep follow-ups would arrive hours later, after the buyer has already pinged two other dealers within a 100-mile radius.


The AI Sales Agent answered the actual question in the first reply: yes, a new 2025 Tiger 850 Sport in stock - and asked whether he wanted it for daily commuting or longer touring. In-stock. Use-case discovery. One message.


What followed in the next 120 seconds:


- Buyer asked about trade-ins. Agent confirmed and asked what he had.
- Buyer answered: "2024 kawasaki klr650s 4000 miles." Agent registered the trade and pivoted to financing.
- Buyer said yes to financing. Agent offered same-day phone-call windows.
- Buyer picked 3:45pm. Agent confirmed and captured contact info.


By 9:40am - three minutes after the buyer's first message - there was a phone call locked in for that afternoon, with the bike, the trade, and the financing intent already documented. No human had touched the lead.


If a sales rep had picked up that lead at noon, the buyer would already have been at a competitor's showroom.


##


What the conversations actually show


Two conversations, two buyer profiles, two dealer types - same pattern.


**Buyers ask in their own words.** "850 sports available?" "do you purchase pre-owned motocycles?" These aren't form-field submissions. They're the actual sentences buyers type when they're talking to a system that responds. They're often misspelled. They're rarely complete. A traditional lead form would never capture them, because a traditional lead form doesn't ask follow-up questions.


**The qualifying detail almost always comes after the third turn.** The seat height. The 4,000 miles on the KLR. The "not café, not chopper" position. None would have shown up in a contact-form note field. They came out because the buyer was in a back-and-forth, not filling in a box.


**The booking is incidental, not the goal.** Both conversations end in a confirmed appointment, but the appointment isn't where the value is. The value is everything the buyer told the store before the appointment - context the salesperson walks in already knowing.


**Same playbook, different buyers.** A long-discovery buyer at one store and a transactional buyer at another ran the same agent. Both got an appointment. Neither relied on a human in the loop.


##


What this means for a dealer's lead funnel


The Sunday-afternoon consolidator didn't get a callback Thursday morning - he got a Tuesday-afternoon appointment. The 9:37am inventory-check buyer didn't wait until lunch for someone to pick up his form - he got an answer on the first message.


A salesperson walking into the Tuesday Concours appointment knows the seat height matters, the riding position is upright, and there are two bikes to appraise. That's 15–20 minutes of discovery the floor team doesn't have to do.


And when every inbound lead is captured as a real conversation - verbatim - the questions buyers are actually asking become a feed into how the store stocks, prices, and trains. The seat-height question wasn't unusual. The "do you take trades, here's mine" pattern wasn't either. A dealer reading those logs is learning what their buyers care about in real time.
