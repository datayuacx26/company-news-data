# Subscription Payments in Southeast Asia: What Businesses Need to Know

Subscription payments in Southeast Asia have grown from a niche billing option into a core growth model for businesses across the region. Consumers now pay on a


**recurring basis** for streaming, software, food delivery, beauty products, and everyday essentials, and they expect the payment step to match how they prefer to spend.


Any business planning to build predictable revenue here needs to understand how recurring billing works from one country to the next.


This guide explains what your business needs to collect recurring payments reliably across the region, so you can turn one-time buyers into long-term subscribers.


**Here is what this guide covers at a glance:**


- Subscription adoption is high and still rising across Southeast Asia, creating a large base of customers already comfortable with recurring payments


- Subscription payments give your business predictable revenue and stronger retention when the payment experience matches how local customers pay


- Payment preferences differ by country, with e-wallets and account-to-account transfers leading most Southeast Asian markets and cards remaining strong mainly in Singapore


- Reliable recurring revenue in the region depends on supporting local payment methods and handling renewals well, ideally through one integration


The sections below take these points in turn, starting with why demand for subscriptions is climbing so quickly.


## **The rise of subscription-based services in Southeast Asia**


Subscription-based services are becoming a familiar part of daily life across Southeast Asia, as consumers pay for ongoing access to digital content, physical products, and personalized services.


Adoption is already high in several markets. In the


**Philippines** and


**Vietnam** ,


**subscription adoption** has reached


[53% and 51%](https://retailasia.com/exclusive/asia-pacific-retailers-tap-subscribers-drive-growth) . For your business, these numbers point to a customer base that already understands and accepts recurring payments, which lowers the effort needed to introduce a subscription offer.


The depth of


[that adoption is clear in Indonesia](https://voiceofasean.com/newsroom/pr-business/the-demand-for-convenience-94-of-indonesian-consumers-want-a-single-hub-to-manage-all-subscriptions/) , where 93% of consumers hold more than two subscriptions and 20% hold more than six. This tells you that many customers are comfortable managing


**several recurring services at once** , so the opportunity reaches beyond a single flagship product into bundles, tiers, and complementary plans.


This behavior spans a


**wide range of categories** , from streaming and music to food delivery, sports, beauty, and repeat product purchases. Streaming shows how fast recurring access can scale, with


**paid streaming accounts** across the region growing


[19%](https://variety.com/2026/tv/news/southeast-asia-streaming-growth-indonesian-content-kdramas-1236658051/) year over year to more than 61 million in 2025, led by Indonesia. That breadth matters for you, because recurring billing now extends well past media platforms into retail, wellness, education, and service businesses that can package their offer around regular delivery.


As more services compete for the same budget, customers are also becoming more selective about which subscriptions they keep. That change, from signing up freely to actively managing renewals, sets the standard your subscription experience needs to meet, and the sections below break down what that involves.


## **What is a subscription payment model?**


[Recurring payments](https://www.xendit.co/en/products/subscriptions/) , also called


**subscription payments** ,


**autodebit** , or


**automatic payments** , are transactions processed automatically on a set schedule. Instead of asking customers to approve every charge, your business collects payment at agreed intervals after the customer grants permission once.


These cycles can run daily, weekly, monthly, biannually, or annually, with monthly being the most common. Customers already use recurring payments for utilities such as electricity and internet, and for services like gym memberships, streaming platforms such as Netflix or Spotify, and software subscriptions. That familiarity works in your favor, because customers rarely need convincing that automatic billing is safe or convenient. What they need is for it to work with the payment method they prefer.


The model benefits both sides. Your business gains


**predictable income** and


**stronger retention** , while customers avoid the effort of remembering and completing each payment by hand.


Companies that run subscription models see


[34% higher customer retention](https://eajournals.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/21/2025/05/The-Rise-of-Recurring-Payments.pdf) and


**46% less month-to-month revenue volatility** compared with those relying on one-off sales. For your planning, that


**stability** means more reliable cash flow forecasts and a customer base you can invest in over time instead of constantly rebuilding.

That predictability is the foundation for a much larger commercial opportunity across the region. The subscription model opens a wider set of commercial gains once you look at what recurring revenue does for a business over time.


## **The business potential of recurring revenue in Southeast Asia**


Retention is only part of what recurring revenue offers.


**Collecting payment** on a


**repeating schedule** reduces your dependence on one-time transactions and gives you a revenue base you can plan around each month, which matters in a market where digital spending keeps expanding.


The financial case strengthens over time. Subscription customers tend to


**stay longer** and


**spend more** , which raises


**customer lifetime value** . Analysis of the sector puts the lifetime value of subscription customers at roughly


[two and a half times](https://retailasia.com/exclusive/asia-pacific-retailers-tap-subscribers-drive-growth) that of traditional retail customers. For your business, that gap makes subscriber acquisition easier to justify, because each retained customer returns more value across the full relationship.


Recurring relationships also create room to grow revenue from customers you already have. As people keep using your service, you learn their preferences and can offer more relevant plans, upgrades, bundles, or complementary products. That opens


**three practical ways to grow revenue** per customer:


- Upselling customers to higher tiers as their needs expand


- Cross-selling complementary products alongside the core subscription


- Personalizing offers using the usage data that recurring billing generates


None of this is automatic. Capturing the opportunity across Southeast Asia comes down to one thing, whether your payment experience matches how customers in each market prefer to pay.


## **Why payment flexibility matters for subscription businesses**


Matching how


**customers prefer to pay** comes down to


**flexibility** , which means giving them real choice at checkout and at renewal. As more subscription services compete for the same budget, people are quicker to drop the ones that feel like a hassle, and an awkward payment step is a common reason they leave.


Frustration with rigid billing is already common.


[In Indonesia,](https://voiceofasean.com/newsroom/pr-business/the-demand-for-convenience-94-of-indonesian-consumers-want-a-single-hub-to-manage-all-subscriptions/) 68% of subscribers want an easier way to


**opt out** of auto-renewals, and 54% admit they keep paying for subscriptions they forgot were still active. For your business, this means


**clarity and control** at the payment stage directly affect whether customers trust your service enough to continue.


Flexibility also decides whether customers complete sign-up at all. When a


**preferred payment method is missing at checkout** ,


[up to 25%](https://blog.cleeng.com/local-payment-methods-for-global-subscription-growth) of customers


**abandon** the purchase. For a subscription business, a lost checkout costs more than one sale. It removes the full stream of recurring payments that customers would have generated over time.


That risk grows in markets where preference concentrates around a single method. In the Philippines, GCash holds


[89%](https://blog.cleeng.com/local-payment-methods-for-global-subscription-growth) of the mobile wallet market, so a checkout built without it asks most customers to pay in a way they rarely use.


**Offering the methods customers already reach for** , whether cards, e-wallets, direct debit, bank transfers, or QR, lowers that risk at both sign-up and renewal.


The catch is that a method customers reach for in one Southeast Asian market can be one they rarely use in the next, so flexibility has to be built market by market. Mapping out those differences is the next step.


## **How subscription payment methods vary across Southeast Asia**


Placing Southeast Asia next to more established subscription markets shows how different its payment habits really are.


According to


[Recurly's 2026 State of Subscriptions report](https://go.recurly.com/2026-state-of-subscriptions.html) ,


**recurring transactions in the United States** run mostly on debit cards (48.8%), credit cards (34.7%), and PayPal (9.6%). Across


**EMEA** , debit cards lead at 50.9%, followed by PayPal at 27.7% and credit cards at 12.8%. In these regions, a card-first setup reaches most customers, so a business can operate with a fairly narrow set of payment methods.


The card mix also affects where payments break down. In


**card-heavy markets like the US** , subscribers are twice as likely to see a decline at sign-up, while


**digital wallet-heavy EMEA** records roughly double the decline rate at renewal. That pattern shows the payment method itself shapes retention, so a region that leans on wallets and bank transfers needs close attention to renewal reliability.


***Also read:***[What to Look for in a Payment Partner When Expanding from the US to Asia](https://www.xendit.co/en/blog/what-to-look-for-payment-partner-expanding-us-to-southeast-asia/)


Southeast Asia does not follow the card-first pattern, and it cannot be treated as one market.


[Worldpay's 2026 Global Payments Report](https://www.worldpay.com/en/global-payments-report) shows how much


**payment behavior shifts by country** :


- In


**Indonesia** , digital wallets lead e-commerce at 42%, with account-to-account transfers close behind at 34%


- In


**Thailand** , account-to-account transfers dominate at 44%, the highest share in the region


- In


**Malaysia** , account-to-account transfers lead at 35%, ahead of digital wallets at 26%


- In


**the Philippines** , digital wallets reach 41%, while cash-based methods still account for 23%


- In


**Vietnam** , digital wallets account for 38%, with account-to-account transfers at 28%


- In


**Singapore** , credit cards remain strong at 34%, alongside digital wallets at 40%


The pattern holds across most of the region, where


**e-wallets** and


**bank transfers** carry more volume than cards and Singapore stands out as the clear exception. Low card ownership reinforces this. In Thailand, only


[23%](https://blog.cleeng.com/local-payment-methods-for-global-subscription-growth) of adults hold a credit card, and in the Philippines the figure is


[15%](https://blog.cleeng.com/local-payment-methods-for-global-subscription-growth) . For your business, a card-only subscription setup would quietly


**exclude a large share** of willing customers in these markets, capping growth before it starts.


Because so many customers across the region pay with wallets, transfers, or cash instead, a card-only setup runs into several problems:


- **Limited customer reach** A card-only checkout shuts out the large share of customers who hold no credit card or simply prefer wallets, bank transfers, and account-to-account methods. In markets like Thailand and the Philippines, that group is most of the potential audience.


- **Higher checkout friction** When someone reaches checkout and their usual payment option is missing, many abandon the sign-up instead of switching methods, so you lose the customer before the first charge is even made.


- **Harder renewal collection** A subscription only works if each renewal keeps going through. Methods customers rarely use are more likely to fail or be dropped over time, which quietly eats into recurring revenue month after month.


- **Market-by-market variation** A setup tuned for Singapore, where cards are common, can underperform in Thailand or the Philippines, where account-to-account transfers and cash lead. What works in one market does not carry over to the next on its own.


Supporting the right[local payment methods](https://docs.xendit.co/docs/available-payment-channels) in each market addresses these challenges directly. It widens reach, smooths sign-up, and gives recurring payments a better chance of clearing at renewal. Doing that across several countries at once is hard with fragmented tools, and a single regional infrastructure partner makes it manageable.


Handling that spread of payment methods across six or more markets is where most subscription setups start to strain. This is the gap Xendit is built to close.
