# How Businesses Can Optimize Payment Costs as They Scale

As a business grows, its payment costs grow with it. More transactions mean more processing fees. More accounts mean more maintenance costs. More payment methods mean more fee structures to track. What starts as a manageable line item can become a significant operational expense if it is not actively managed.


The good news is that scale also brings leverage. Higher transaction volumes unlock better pricing terms. A clearer understanding of your fee structure identifies where costs can be reduced. And the right payment infrastructure makes it possible to grow efficiently rather than expensively.


This article covers why payment costs increase as businesses scale, how to understand the different cost components involved, and the most effective strategies for keeping those costs in check without compromising on payment performance.


**Key Takeaways**


- Payment costs scale with transaction volume, but the relationship is not linear - the right pricing model and provider can reduce your effective rate as you grow


- Fixed and variable costs behave differently at scale, and understanding both is essential for accurate cost forecasting


- Common pricing models each have tradeoffs that make them more or less suitable depending on your business stage and transaction profile


- A long-term payments strategy treats fee optimization as an ongoing discipline, not a one-time exercise


## Why payment costs increase as businesses grow


Payment costs increase with scale for straightforward reasons: more transactions mean more fees, more accounts mean more maintenance costs, and more payment methods mean more fee structures to track and reconcile.


But the relationship between scale and cost is not simply linear. A business that grows its transaction volume without revisiting its payment setup often ends up paying more per transaction than necessary. Pricing models that made sense at lower volumes may be suboptimal at higher ones. Fee structures that were manageable with a handful of accounts become unwieldy across dozens of subaccounts.


Several specific cost drivers tend to emerge or intensify as businesses scale:


1. **Processing fee volume**


The most direct driver. More transactions mean more processing fees. But the composition of those fees matters as much as the volume. A growing business with a shifting payment method mix - more premium credit cards, more cross-border transactions, more high-value orders - may see its effective processing rate rise even if the headline rate stays the same.


2. **Account and maintenance fees**


Businesses that grow through platform structures or multi-entity setups accumulate accounts over time. Accounts that fall below minimum activity thresholds attract dormant account fees. At Xendit, a monthly minimum fee of USD 50 applies to dormant and inactive accounts. Having clear visibility of your portfolio of subaccounts, can help prevent unmanaged dormancy from becoming a material cost.


3. **Legacy API maintenance**


Businesses still operating on older API infrastructure may be subject to additional maintenance fees. At Xendit, a monthly maintenance fee of USD 250 applies to merchants using the legacy API. Migrating to current API versions eliminates this cost.


4. **Chargeback and dispute fees**


As transaction volume grows, so does chargeback exposure unless fraud prevention scales in parallel. Each chargeback carries a dispute fee - at Xendit, a chargeback dispute fee of USD 25 applies. Understanding how you can minimize chargebacks, can help to reduce this.


5. **Attempt fees on failed transactions**


At Xendit, a processing fee applies to every transaction attempt regardless of outcome. For businesses with growing transaction volumes, a high decline rate amplifies this cost. Improving authorization rates becomes more financially important as volume increases.

## Common pricing models used by payment providers


Your pricing model determines how your processing fees scale with volume. Understanding the most common models - and their tradeoffs - is essential for evaluating whether your current model is still appropriate as your business grows.


Flat rate pricing.


A single blended rate applies to every transaction regardless of payment method or transaction size. Simple to manage and predict, but often less cost-effective at higher volumes because the rate does not reflect the actual cost profile of your transactions.


Interchange-plus pricing.


The network and issuing bank fees are passed through at cost, and the provider adds a fixed markup on top. More transparent and typically more cost-effective at higher volumes, because the markup is the only negotiable component and it is visible separately.


Tiered pricing.


Transactions are categorized into tiers - typically qualified, mid-qualified, and non-qualified - with different rates for each tier. Less transparent than interchange-plus because tier classification can be opaque. Often less favorable than it appears when analyzed in detail.


Subscription or membership pricing.


A fixed monthly fee provides access to processing at near-cost network rates, plus a small per-transaction fee. Highly cost-effective for high-volume businesses where the monthly fee is outweighed by the savings on per-transaction rates.

## **Building a long-term payments strategy**


Payment cost optimization is most effective when it is treated as an ongoing discipline rather than a periodic exercise. The businesses that manage payment costs most effectively build a payment strategy that evolves with their growth rather than reacting to costs after they have already accumulated.


A long-term payment strategy includes several components:


1. **A clear owner for payment operations**


Someone in your organization - typically in finance, operations, or a dedicated payments function - should be responsible for monitoring payment costs, managing provider relationships, and identifying optimization opportunities. Without clear ownership, payment costs tend to drift upward unmanaged.


2. **Regular cost reviews**


Schedule a payment cost review at least quarterly. Review your effective processing rate, account portfolio, chargeback rate, authorization rate, and provider terms. Compare against the previous period and against benchmarks appropriate for your business type and volume.


3. **A provider relationship built on transparency**


Your payment provider should be a partner in cost optimization, not an obstacle. A good provider relationship includes open communication about fees, proactive advice on how to reduce costs, and willingness to revisit commercial terms as your business grows.


4. **Infrastructure that scales without friction**


The right payment infrastructure allows your business to add payment methods, markets, and transaction volume without adding proportionate complexity or cost. Evaluating your payment infrastructure not just for current needs but for where your business is heading is part of a long-term payments strategy.

## **Frequently Asked Questions**


### **Why do payment costs increase as businesses grow?**


Payment costs increase with scale because more transactions generate more processing fees, more accounts generate more maintenance costs, and a higher volume of chargebacks and refunds generates more dispute and refund fees. The composition of your transaction mix also matters - a shift toward higher-cost payment methods or more cross-border transactions can raise your effective rate even if your headline pricing stays the same.


### **What is the difference between fixed and variable payment costs?**


Variable costs scale with transaction volume, processing fees, chargeback fees, and refund fees are the primary examples. Fixed costs apply regardless of volume, monthly maintenance fees for legacy API access and monthly minimum fees on dormant accounts are fixed costs. Understanding the distinction helps your finance team forecast accurately and identify where optimization has the most leverage.


### **What account related fees apply at Xendit?**


At Xendit, a monthly minimum fee of USD 50 applies to dormant and inactive accounts. A monthly maintenance fee of USD 250 applies to merchants still using the legacy API.


### **How does improving my authorization rate reduce costs?**


A higher authorization rate means more of your payment attempts result in successful transactions. This reduces the volume of attempt fees paid on declined transactions and recovers revenue from customers who would otherwise abandon their purchase after a decline. At scale, even a modest improvement in authorization rate delivers a compounding financial benefit.


### **How can I reduce chargeback fees as my business grows?**


The most effective approaches are improving fraud detection to reduce fraudulent transactions, maintaining clear billing descriptors and refund policies to reduce friendly fraud, and responding to disputes quickly with strong evidence. At Xendit, a chargeback dispute fee of USD 25 applies on each dispute, so reducing chargeback volume has a direct impact on this cost line.
