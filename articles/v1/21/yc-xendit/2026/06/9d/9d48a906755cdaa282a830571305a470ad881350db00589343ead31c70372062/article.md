# Dormant Account Fees Explained: What They Are and Why Payment Providers Charge Them

Most businesses focus on the fees that come with processing payments. Fewer pay attention to what happens when an account stops processing - or slows down significantly. A dormant account fee is a charge that applies precisely in those circumstances, and for businesses with multiple accounts or subaccounts, it can quietly add up without drawing much attention.


Understanding what triggers a dormant account fee, why payment providers apply it, and how to manage it is a straightforward exercise that can prevent unnecessary costs from accumulating on accounts that are no longer actively generating revenue.


**Key Takeaways**


- A dormant account is one that has fallen below a minimum level of payment activity over a defined period


- Payment providers charge a monthly minimum fee on dormant accounts to cover the ongoing cost of maintaining inactive accounts in their systems


- Businesses with multiple accounts or subaccounts are most likely to encounter dormant account fees on accounts that have reduced or ceased activity


- Reviewing your account portfolio regularly and consolidating or closing inactive accounts is the most straightforward way to avoid these fees


## **What is a dormant account?**


A dormant account is a payment account that has fallen below a defined threshold of activity over a given period. What constitutes dormancy varies by payment provider, some define it by the absence of any transactions over a set number of months, others by transaction volume falling below a minimum level.


In payment processing, an account does not need to be completely inactive to be considered dormant. An account that was once processing a healthy volume of transactions but has since dropped to near-zero activity may still trigger a dormant account classification, depending on the provider's policy.


For businesses that operate multiple payment accounts - particularly those using platform structures with subaccounts, dormancy can occur on accounts that were active during a specific project or season but have since wound down. Without a regular review of account activity, dormant account fees can accrue on accounts the business may have forgotten about.

## **What is a dormant account fee?**


A dormant account fee is a recurring charge applied to accounts that do not meet a minimum level of payment activity within a billing period. It is not a transaction fee. It is an account-level fee that applies regardless of whether any payments are processed.


The fee reflects the cost of maintaining an account in the payment provider's system. Even when an account is not processing transactions, it occupies infrastructure, requires security monitoring, and must be maintained in compliance with applicable regulations. A dormant account fee ensures that the cost of maintaining inactive accounts is covered, rather than being subsidized by active merchants.


## **Why do payment platforms charge dormant account fees?**


Maintaining a payment account is not a zero-cost activity, even when no transactions are being processed. Payment providers incur ongoing costs for every account in their system, active or not.


There are four primary cost drivers that justify this fee:


1. **Infrastructure and system costs**


Every account requires storage, security monitoring, and system resources to remain operational. These costs do not disappear when transaction volume drops to zero.


2. **Compliance and regulatory obligations**


Payment providers are regulated entities. Maintaining accounts in compliance with anti-money laundering requirements, data protection regulations, and financial reporting obligations carries a cost that applies across all accounts, not just active ones.


3. **Risk monitoring**


Inactive accounts still require monitoring. A dormant account that suddenly becomes active with unusual transaction patterns needs to be identified and assessed. This ongoing monitoring has a cost.


4. **Resource allocation**


Payment providers allocate pricing infrastructure, support capacity, and technical resources based on their merchant portfolio. Accounts that generate no revenue but still consume resources create an imbalance that dormant account fees help address.


For businesses, this means the fee is not punitive - it reflects the actual cost structure of maintaining accounts at scale. Understanding this makes it easier to make rational decisions about which accounts to keep active and which to consolidate or close.

## **Best practices for managing business payment accounts**


Managing dormant account fees is largely a matter of maintaining good account hygiene. The following practices reduce the likelihood of unexpected fees accumulating on inactive accounts.


1. **Conduct regular account audits**


Review your full account portfolio on a quarterly basis. Identify accounts that have fallen below minimum activity thresholds and decide whether to reactivate, consolidate, or close them.


2. **Close accounts that are no longer needed**


An account that will not be used again should be formally closed rather than left inactive. This eliminates the ongoing cost of maintaining it and removes the compliance and security risks associated with unmonitored accounts.


3. **Assign account ownership**


Each account in your portfolio should have a named owner within your organization - someone responsible for monitoring its activity and making decisions about its status. Accounts without a clear owner are the most likely to drift into dormancy unnoticed.


4. **For platform businesses, monitor subaccount activity proactively**


If you operate a platform with subaccounts through XenPlatform, build subaccount activity monitoring into your regular operations. Identify subaccounts that have reduced activity before they reach the dormancy threshold and either reactivate them or initiate closure.


5. **Factor account maintenance costs into your pricing model**


If your platform or business model involves creating accounts that may not always be active, build the potential cost of dormant account fees into your operational cost projections. This prevents surprises and ensures that account management costs are accounted for in your unit economics.

The most straightforward approaches are maintaining a minimum level of payment activity on each account, closing accounts that are no longer needed, and conducting regular portfolio audits to identify accounts approaching dormant status before the fee applies.
