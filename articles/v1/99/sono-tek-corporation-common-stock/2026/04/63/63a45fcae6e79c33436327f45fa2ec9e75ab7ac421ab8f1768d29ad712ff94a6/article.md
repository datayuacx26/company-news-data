# Ultrasonic vs. Air Spray vs. Pressure Spray Nozzles: A Technical Comparison

For decades, industrial spray coating has been defined by three fundamentally different approaches to liquid atomization. Pressure spray nozzles force liquid through a small orifice at high velocity, relying on turbulence and shear forces to break the stream into droplets. The more commonly used air spray nozzles force compressed air to shear a liquid stream into droplets at the nozzle tip.


Ultrasonic spray nozzles take a different approach entirely: a piezoelectric transducer converts electrical energy into high-frequency mechanical vibration, and that vibration does the atomization work, with no pressure and no compressed air required.


At Sono-Tek, we’ve been engineering ultrasonic spray systems since 1975, when founder Dr. Harvey Berger patented the[ultrasonic atomizing nozzle](https://www.sono-tek.com/ultrasonic-coating/how-ultrasonic-nozzles-work/) . That five-decade foundation, which spans printed circuit board fluxing, fuel cell catalyst deposition, medical device coating, and advanced materials research, informs everything that follows.

This comparison draws on Sono-Tek’s application engineering expertise to examine where each technology performs, where each falls short, and how to choose the right tool for your process.


## How Each Industrial Spray Technology Works


Each technology achieves atomization through a fundamentally different mechanism.


### Pressure Spray Nozzles


Pressure nozzles have been a workhorse of industrial spraying for over a century, moving high volumes of liquid quickly across large surface areas. The mechanism is simple: liquid is forced through a small orifice at high velocity, and the turbulence created as it exits breaks the stream into droplets. Operating pressure and orifice diameter together determine droplet size, flow rate, and spray pattern, which makes pressure systems simple to set up but difficult to fine-tune. Their simplicity is their strength, but it’s also the source of their limitations when precision coating applications demand something more controlled.


### Air Spray Nozzles


Air spray nozzles, also called two-fluid or air atomization nozzles, use a stream of compressed air to shear a liquid into droplets at the nozzle tip. The liquid is delivered at low pressure through a central orifice, and high-velocity airflow surrounds it, breaking the stream apart through aerodynamic shear forces. The result is a finer, more controlled droplet than pressure spray alone can produce, which is why air spray became the standard in finishing, automotive coating, woodworking, and general industrial applications where surface quality matters.


### Ultrasonic Spray Nozzles


Rather than forcing liquid through a tiny orifice, ultrasonic nozzles deliver liquid to a vibrating atomizing surface at very low flow rates, where standing capillary waves break it into a soft mist of mathematically defined droplets. The result is a low-velocity spray plume that deposits gently onto any substrate without bounce-back, overspray, or pressure-driven clogging.


## Key Differences That Matter in Application


### Droplet Size and Uniformity


In pressure nozzles, droplet size is a function of orifice diameter and operating pressure, variables that interact unpredictably and produce a wide distribution of droplet sizes.


Air spray improves on this by using airflow to shear droplets more finely, but size distribution still varies with air pressure, liquid flow rate, and nozzle geometry.


In ultrasonic systems, droplet size is governed by a single variable: the resonant frequency of the nozzle.


[As Sono-Tek’s Technical Services Director Robb Engle documented](https://www.sono-tek.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Ultrasonic-Spray-of-Catalyst.pdf) in a 2011 paper presented at the ASME Fuel Cell Science and Technology Conference, ultrasonic atomization produces droplets “in a mathematically defined manner,” making droplet size tunable, predictable, and calculable before a single coating run begins.


### Flow Rates and Viscosity Handling


Pressure nozzles excel at high flow rates and can push viscous materials through with brute force, making them well suited for large-area, high-volume applications.


Air spray handles a broad range of viscosities but requires adjustment of both air and liquid parameters as viscosity changes.


Ultrasonic nozzles operate at ultra-low flow rates and use continuous vibration to keep particles uniformly suspended, handling nanoparticle suspensions and sensitive formulations that would clog or degrade under pressure or shear.


### Clogging and Maintenance


Pressure nozzles rely on small orifices and clog readily with high-solids or viscous materials.


Air spray nozzles share similar vulnerability at the liquid orifice, with dried or built-up material in the air cap adding another maintenance point.


Ultrasonic nozzles have no small orifices, and continuous high-frequency vibration is inherently self-cleaning.


Engle’s 2011 ASME paper puts the operational risk of conventional nozzle clogging precisely: “the core detriment of processing with nozzles that will clog is not when they fail, but in the event of slowly clogging, the spray characteristics are changing. Ultrasonic atomizers do not clog and therefore do not vary.”


### Energy Consumption and Efficiency


Pressure spray requires sustained hydraulic pressure, adding energy overhead.


Air spray compounds that with a compressor, regulator, and the energy losses inherent in accelerating a high-velocity air stream, and that air plume drives significant overspray.


Engle’s ASME paper documented that conventional hydraulic and dual-fluid nozzles commonly fail to achieve even 50% transfer efficiency to the substrate due to overspray and bounce-back.


Ultrasonic nozzles consume 1 to 8 watts per nozzle with no compressed air required, and transfer efficiencies can reach 97 to 99%.


### Material Compatibility and Coating Uniformity


Pressure nozzles handle high throughput but struggle with uniform thin-film deposition at the nanometer to micron scale.


Air spray produces a finer finish but still cannot achieve the coating thickness control required for advanced materials applications.


Ultrasonic spray coating delivers consistent coating thickness across complex substrates, delicate surfaces, and advanced materials including catalysts, drug formulations, and functional thin films.


## When to Choose Which: Practical Application


### Where Pressure Spray Excels


When the application demands high flow rates, large surface coverage, and speed over precision, pressure nozzles remain the right tool, including for agricultural spraying, industrial cleaning, tank washing, and large-area paint application.


These are environments where the high velocity, broad droplet distribution, and simple mechanics of pressure systems are advantages rather than liabilities.


### Where Air Spray Excels


Air spray is the right tool when surface finish quality matters but ultrasonic-level precision is not required. It handles a wide range of liquid viscosities, scales easily across large surface areas, and produces a finer finish than pressure spray alone, which is why it dominates in automotive refinishing, furniture and cabinetry coating, general metal finishing, and high-volume paint application where appearance is a primary specification. For applications where throughput and finish quality need to be balanced and compressed air infrastructure is already in place, air spray remains a practical and well-understood standard.


### Where Ultrasonic Nozzles Excel


Ultrasonic spray coating delivers its clearest advantage where pressure and air spray systems struggle: specifically when thin-film deposition with high uniformity is required, often on sensitive substrates, complex three-dimensional geometries using nanoparticle and catalyst coatings, and applications in[semiconductor processing](https://www.sono-tek.com/industry/semiconductor-wafers/) ,[fuel cell manufacturing](https://www.sono-tek.com/industry/alternative-energy/fuel-cell/) ,[medical device coating](https://www.sono-tek.com/industry/medical/) , and advanced materials research.


In these environments, the ability to control droplet size, minimize overspray, and deposit material gently and uniformly is a process requirement.


## The Bottom Line


Choosing among these three technologies is a question of what the application actually demands.


Pressure spray is the right tool for high-volume, large-area work where speed defines success. Air spray is the standard where surface finish quality matters and compressed air infrastructure is already in place.


For precision coating, sensitive materials, and complex substrates where uniformity and material efficiency are paramount, ultrasonic spray technology is the only tool built for the job.
