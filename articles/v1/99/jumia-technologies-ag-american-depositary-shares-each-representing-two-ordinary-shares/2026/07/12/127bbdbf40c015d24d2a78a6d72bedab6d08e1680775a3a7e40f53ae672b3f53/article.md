## Top Drizly Alternative Apps to Try in 2026


Drizly shut down in March 2024. Uber decided to fold its alcohol marketplace directly into Uber Eats. That single move reshuffled the entire on-demand booze delivery category almost overnight. Shoppers didn’t lose access to alcohol delivery, though. They just had to find a new home for it.


#### Here’s how the leading options stack up today.


[Uber Eats](https://www.ubereats.com/) is the most direct successor to Drizly. It absorbed much of Drizly’s original retailer network. If you already order food through the app, adding wine to that same cart is genuinely convenient.
**[Saucey](https://saucey.com/)** keeps things simple. It offers a roughly 30-minute delivery window in major metro areas, though it skips pickup entirely.


[Instacart](https://www.instacart.com/) shines when you want to fold alcohol into your weekly grocery order. It’s one of the few options offering both delivery and curbside pickup.
**[Minibar Delivery](https://minibardelivery.com/)** operates closest to how Drizly originally worked. It connects shoppers directly to local liquor stores for beer, wine, spirits, and party supplies.

For speed above everything, **Gopuff** stocks its own inventory in micro-fulfillment centers. That setup keeps delivery windows tight and predictable. It also bundles alcohol with snacks, household items, and other essentials in one order. If you’re hunting a hard-to-find bourbon or a limited-release wine, **Frootbat** specializes in exactly that. It even offers guides on distillery history and tasting notes.


Most of these apps overlap on the basics. Age[verification at signup](https://appscrip.com/blog/identity-verification-process/) and ID checks at the door are standard across the board.[Payment options](https://appscrip.com/blog/digital-wallet-integration/) are similar too, typically credit, debit, and digital wallets.


Where they differ is depth of selection. Some are tied to one retailer, others pull from a whole network of stores. That distinction matters more than most shoppers realize when an order needs to arrive fast.


## How to Pick the Right Alcohol Delivery App for You


With six solid options on the table, picking one can feel harder than it should. The truth is most shoppers don’t need to commit to a single app. Many people keep two or three installed. They choose based on what a specific order requires. Not every app fits every occasion, so match the tool to the moment:


- **Need it in 30 minutes or less?** Gopuff or Saucey are built for speed.
- **Already grocery shopping?** Instacart lets you add alcohol to the same cart.
- **Chasing a rare or allocated bottle?** Frootbat specializes in scarce inventory.
- **Want the Drizly-like local store feel?** Minibar Delivery is the closest match.
- **Ordering food and drinks together?** Uber Eats handles both in one app.
- **Comparing prices across multiple local stores?** Uber Eats and Instacart both pull from several retailers at once.
- **Value a smaller, curated retailer feel?** Minibar Delivery leans into that experience deliberately.


A quick gut check helps too: are you optimizing for speed, selection, or price? Most shoppers only need to answer that one question before the right app becomes obvious.


A recent hands-on[comparison of wine delivery apps](https://sommo.app/blog/best-wine-delivery-apps-2026/) tested Uber Eats, DoorDash, Instacart, and Total Wine side by side. It reached a similar conclusion. No single app won across every category. The best pick depended entirely on what mattered most that day.


## What Changed in Alcohol Delivery Since Drizly Closed


Drizly’s shutdown wasn’t really about alcohol delivery losing steam. Industry leaders at the time pointed to Uber’s own strategic priorities, not weakening demand. BevAlc e-commerce has kept growing since then. Multiple retailers moved quickly to fill the space Drizly left behind.


What did shift is how fragmented the market became. Instead of one dominant app, shoppers now juggle several options depending on location and occasion. That fragmentation is partly a regulatory story.[Alcohol delivery](https://appscrip.com/blog/how-to-build-a-delivery-app/) in the U.S. still runs through a three-tier distribution system. Every state layers its own rules on top of it.


**Factor** **What It Means for Delivery Apps**


Three-tier distribution Producers, distributors, and retailers must stay legally separate in most states


State-by-state variation An app available in California may not operate the same way in Pennsylvania or Utah


Age verification ID checks at signup and at the door are mandatory, not optional


Control vs. open states Some states run alcohol sales through state-owned agencies, limiting app options


This patchwork is exactly why no single app has replicated Drizly’s old scale. Building compliant, region-aware alcohol delivery software is genuinely harder than building a standard delivery app. That complexity keeps new entrants cautious.


According to the[National Alcohol Beverage Control Association](https://www.nabca.org/three-tier-system-modern-view-0) , no single entity can typically operate in more than one tier. That structure has held steady since Prohibition ended, and it isn’t going anywhere soon. For any Drizly alternative to succeed long term, it has to work within that framework.


There’s also a quieter shift worth noting. Curbside pickup has become a standard expectation, not a bonus feature. Shoppers who got used to curbside groceries during the pandemic now expect the same for alcohol. Apps that skip pickup, like Saucey, are becoming the exception rather than the rule.


## The Business Opportunity Behind Drizly’s Exit


Here’s the part most shopper-focused roundups skip. Drizly’s closure created real whitespace for founders, not just confusion for consumers. The U.S. alcohol delivery market has kept expanding since.


Competitive intelligence platform[CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/drizly/alternatives-competitors) tracks over a dozen active companies in this space now. They range from regional players like BevMo to niche specialists like Flaviar. All of them are competing for the audience Drizly once owned outright. That level of competition is a healthy sign for anyone building here, not a warning sign.


#### A few dynamics make this a genuinely attractive niche right now:


- Independent liquor stores still lack strong direct-to-consumer tools of their own.
- Regional and multi-store operators want an alternative to handing margin over to Uber Eats or Instacart.
- Consumers have shown they’re comfortable ordering alcohol online, so the habit is already built.
- Compliance requirements, while complex, are well-documented and solvable with the right software foundation.
- Smaller, focused apps can still compete on curation and service, even against giants like Uber Eats.


None of this means building an alcohol delivery app is simple. It means the demand is proven. The competitive field is also more open than it looks from the outside.


Grocery chains, regional liquor store groups, and even individual retailers are all testing their own branded delivery experiences. Rather than paying commission to Uber Eats or Instacart indefinitely, many are choosing to own the customer relationship directly.


That shift mirrors what happened in[food delivery](https://appscrip.com/blog/cost-to-develop-a-food-delivery-app/) years ago. Restaurants started building their own ordering apps alongside third-party marketplaces.
