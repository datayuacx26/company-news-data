# Exclusive: Jumia goes from turnaround to scale, according to CEO Dufay

Leading African e-commerce firm[Jumia Technologies AG (NYSE:JMIA)](https://www.investing.com/equities/jumia-technologies-ag) reported robust fourth-quarter 2025 earnings Tuesday, marking a pivotal transition in its corporate trajectory. In an interview with Investing.com, Jumia CEO Francis Dufay described the strategic journey that has moved the company from a turnaround phase toward stability and, ultimately, scale.


The company’s latest earnings materials revealed continued growth alongside narrowing losses. Gross Merchandise Value (GMV) surged 36% year-over-year, while Adjusted EBITDA losses were nearly halved. Management highlighted performance in key markets, particularly in Nigeria, which saw 50% GMV growth. Ghana also witnessed a significant breakout, with physical goods GMV erupting by 124%. Egypt, which had previously faced headwinds from declining corporate sales and currency devaluation, stabilized in what Dufay characterized as a “confirmation” of recovery.


While macroeconomic stabilization in several regions has bolstered consumer confidence, Dufay insisted that Jumia’s internal execution and increased market share remain the primary drivers. “Demand has always been there in those markets. What’s changing has been our execution,” the CEO noted.


Despite these operational gains, Jumia shares faced significant pressure during Tuesday’s trading session, falling 17.4%. Earnings per share (EPS) and revenue figures missed analyst expectations, and a shift in guidance specifications appeared to rattle some investors. While the company has long targeted breakeven by Q4 2026 and full profitability by 2027, previous projections were based on "loss before income tax." On Tuesday, Jumia shifted this guidance to Adjusted EBITDA.


The distinction is critical: Adjusted EBITDA does not account for share-based compensation, depreciation, or amortization. In Q4 2025, the gap between the two metrics was $2.4 million, with an Adjusted EBITDA loss of $7.3 million compared to a $9.7 million loss before income tax. Dufay emphasized that the change was intended to highlight Jumia’s core economic engine and operational leverage.


*“This business has changed. Over the past two years, we had significant amounts that came from corporate sales mostly in Egypt. That generated a significant amount of finance costs for cash repatriation below the EBITDA, so included in the net loss. This business has been completely deprioritized and is now accounting for negligible volumes. It makes a lot more sense given our push for operational leverage to focus on the EBITDA that doesn’t include some volatile items like finance costs or share-based compensation, and gives a much better representation of our operational improvement.”*


Investors also scrutinized Jumia’s planned exit from the Algerian market, following its departure from Tunisia and South Africa last year. Dufay cited a difficult consumer landscape and a desire to concentrate resources on core markets like Nigeria, Ghana, and Egypt.


*“It’s both. It’s been very hard for us to have access to the right supply, at the right price, and fight against the local, informal, offline market, and that’s very structural to the Algerian market. As part of this move, it enables us to streamline the organization, refocus on the eight core markets, and give those markets a bit more attention and a bit more focus.”*


When asked if the Algerian exit represents Jumia’s “final form” prior to reaching profitability, Dufay confirmed, “We believe so, yes… But we remain ruthless in the way we reevaluate and assess our footprint.” He added that no market entries or re-entries are planned until the company achieves a full year of profitability.


During the earnings call, management specifically identified Northern Nigeria as a target for expansion, an initiative that began in the second half of 2025. Dufay detailed these efforts to Investing.com: “We opened dozens of new pickup stations in the region, we increased frequency of deliveries, and made a targeted advertising push… That’s kind of our standard playbook… We know that it works and it’s starting to show results.”


Jumia intends to replicate this playbook in Ghana and Egypt. This strategy includes Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) services, which helped drive growth in Egypt despite the market’s declining corporate segment. On Monday, Jumia announced an expanded partnership in Egypt with fintech platform Mogo to launch a “triple zero” plan, an installment program featuring no down payment, no interest, and no administrative fees.


Central to Jumia’s rebound is the lean organizational structure Dufay was appointed to implement. Under his tenure, the company has exited several non-core markets, reduced headcount, and cut marketing and G&A expenses. Artificial Intelligence has become a primary driver of these efficiencies.


*“It is very obvious to us that AI will not destroy our business, which is based on very tangible assets. On the contrary, it will be a very powerful tool for us to maximize productivity and reduce all costs, including in tech and G&A.”*


According to a source familiar with the matter, Jumia had launched 150 AI tools and 100 custom assistants across 12 departments by May 2025. These tools have yielded a 25% boost in development efficiency and a 30% improvement in customer service resolution times, the source said.


Further gains are being driven by "upcountry" orders, which grew to 61% of total volume, up from 56% a year ago. These orders from rural and secondary cities serve as a key indicator of Jumia’s "stickiness." Dufay dismissed concerns that upcountry growth might destabilize margins, explaining that deliveries to these regions rely on pickup stations with fixed-cost structures, allowing the model to scale efficiently without eroding profitability.


First-party sales also provided a bright spot, growing 33% year-over-year to represent 49% of total revenue. These sales are increasingly bolstered by international partnerships, most notably with Elon Musk’s Starlink, which Jumia distributes in Nigeria and Kenya. While Dufay did not quantify the specific impact of Starlink, he confirmed Jumia hopes to expand the partnership into other overlapping African markets in 2026 as Starlink secures the necessary licenses.


Ultimately, while the revenue miss and guidance shift created short-term market friction, Jumia’s fourth quarter depicted a company that has successfully moved beyond a mere turnaround play. The entity now appears stabilized and poised to achieve the large-scale growth that has long been its primary ambition.


On the path to profitability, Dufay concluded, "It’s clear, it’s in the numbers, and it’s obvious it’s going to happen in a reasonable time frame. And I believe it will happen in Q4 2026. The growth rate we’re delivering today is nothing exceptional, there’s largely enough room in our markets to sustain it. And with that growth rate, we’re managing to get amazing operating leverage across the PnL. That takes us to breakeven."
