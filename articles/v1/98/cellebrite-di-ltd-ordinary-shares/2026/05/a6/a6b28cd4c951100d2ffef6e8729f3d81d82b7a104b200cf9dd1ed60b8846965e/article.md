## KeyPoints


- Childhood exploitation can have lasting effects on mental, emotional, and physical health into adulthood.
- Survivors are at higher risk for PTSD, depression, anxiety, substance abuse, and relationship difficulties.
- Long-term support systems are essential for helping survivors heal and rebuild stability.
- Trauma can impact education, employment, family dynamics, and overall quality of life.
- Early intervention, trauma-informed care, and community support can significantly improve recovery outcomes.


Survivors of childhood sexual abuse who share their stories in court or government inquiries often speak about the widespread and enduring personal effects that pervade many areas of their lives. One survivor explains, “[The depth of the pain is deeper.](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/nov/15/you-ruined-me-new-zealands-abuse-survivors-speak-at-landmark-inquiry?utm_term=Autofeed&CMP=twt_gu&utm_medium=&utm_source=Twitter#Echobox=1573863662) ” Research shows survivors are more likely to have long-term mental and physical health difficulties in adulthood, manifesting as a myriad of[mental health issues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31519507/) . One of the most devastating consequences is the cycle of victimization, where child abuse and neglect survivors are at an increased risk of further abuse in adulthood.


## The Silent Battles of Abuse Survivors


A study by[Australia’s royal commission](https://www.childabuseroyalcommission.gov.au/) into institutional responses to child sexual abuse revealed that it takes an average of 24 years for victims to speak about their abuse.


Craig Hughes-Cashmore, a survivor of child sexual abuse, grappled with societal stereotypes that often unfairly associate male victims with potential future perpetrators – making disclosure incredibly difficult. The abuse[inflicted lasting damage](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2019/jul/01/you-grow-up-hating-yourself-why-child-abuse-survivors-keep-and-break-their-silence) on Craig’s mental and emotional well-being. When interviewed by a journalist, he shares, “Trust is a massive issue for people who’ve been abused. \[The abuse\] can often leave people in a perpetual state of alarm, a heightened sense of who’s around me, what’s happening and constant vigilance,” he said. Similarly, Erin Delaney who bravely spoke about her harrowing childhood to The Guardian, says it was marked by physical and emotional abuse and left her feeling unsafe and ungrounded. The scars of abuse ran deep, leading to self-hatred and silence borne from internalized blame. Even when seeking help, hurtful reactions and dehumanizing labels from professionals reinforced her silence.


You grow up hating yourself and thinking you caused it and you deserve it,
~she painfully shares.


## Understanding the Impacts


**Sexual Abuse and Revictimization**


Child sexual abuse survivors face a traumatic reality: the possibility of revictimization later in life. Research paints a grim picture –[64% of survivors were re-victimized](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1077559520932665) , facing sexual assaults, physical assaults, threats and stalking.


Both men and women suffer cruel consequences and experience an increased risk of revictimization. For example, male survivors of child sex abuse were seven times more likely to be the victims of further sexual assault.


Factors like age at the time of the abuse, mental health conditions and the type of abuse suffered weave a complex web, making survivors vulnerable to further harm.


**Abuse and Neglect**


The consequences of child abuse and neglect are far-reaching. Research from the Australian Institute of Family Studies (AIFS) revealed that chronic maltreatment, involving recurrent incidents over time,[leaves lasting trauma](https://aifs.gov.au/resources/policy-and-practice-papers/effects-child-abuse-and-neglect-adult-survivors) . Additionally, complex trauma arising from prolonged and varied traumatic experiences leads to a range of cognitive, affective and behavioral outcomes.


Disturbingly, some survivors perpetuate the cycle, becoming abusive themselves in adulthood. Witnessing violence as children normalizes aggression, perpetuating the vicious cycle.


The same study also determined that an estimated one-third of children who are subjected to child abuse and neglect go on to repeat patterns of abusive parenting toward their own children. Abusive family environments teach children that the use of violence and aggression is a viable means of dealing with interpersonal conflict.


**Physical and Mental Health Impacts**


The relationship between childhood abuse survivors and subsequent physical health problems in adulthood is complex. AIFS also highlights studies suggesting that early life stressors can impact the immune system, making survivors more susceptible to health issues in adulthood.


These encompass a wide range of issues, such as heart disease, substance abuse, eating disorders and engaging in high-risk behaviors, which makes the path to recovery harder.


Furthermore, mental health problems are also a common consequence of child abuse and neglect. A peer-reviewed journal article showed that adults who had experienced child abuse were[two and a half times more likely to have major depression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19327835/) and six times more likely to have post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) compared to adults who had not experienced abuse.


## Prevention and Healing


Prevention stands as the first line of defense to combat this crisis. Raising awareness about abuse indicators, implementing robust reporting systems and establishing safe spaces for children are vital steps.


Equally important are mental health services. Therapy and counseling awareness can offer solace to survivors, aiding their recovery journey.


Research suggests that[group therapy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0190740920307349) is a highly effective and preferable way of treating young adults who have suffered from childhood abuse. Group therapy provides a safe space for individuals to share their experiences and connect with others who have been through similar traumas.


Out of the eight studies conducted, half found that victims who received group therapy showed significant improvements in comparison to those who did not. By sharing their experiences with others, victims can reduce their emotional, behavioral and trauma symptoms and prevent future symptoms from arising.


## Conclusion


The heartbreaking tales of child abuse and neglect etch indelible scars on the souls of survivors, shaping their adult lives in unimaginable ways. The specter of PTSD looms large, casting shadows on their mental and physical well-being.


Cultivating resilience, rebuilding self-esteem and fostering a sense of security are crucial healing components. Understanding the intricacies of abuse’s impact, society can actively work towards breaking the cycle to support victims and stand as a formidable force against this pervasive crisis.
