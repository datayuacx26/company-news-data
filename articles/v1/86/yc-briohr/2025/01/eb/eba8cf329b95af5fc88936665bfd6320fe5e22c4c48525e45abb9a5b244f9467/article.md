# Evaluating Potential Over Perfection: Finding the Right Talent

This is the second article in ourrecruitment series , building on the foundation of hiring trailblazers. If you haven’t already, explore the[first article](https://briohr.com/blog/recruitment-hiring-trailblazers/) to learn why trailblazers are essential for driving innovation and growth. In this piece, we delve into how evaluating potential over perfection can help identify candidates with the potential to transform your organisation.


Traditional interviews often focus on experience and surface-level qualifications, leaving little room to uncover a candidate’s true potential. This outdated approach can lead to hiring decisions that reinforce the status quo rather than propel your organisation forward. In today’s dynamic workplace, evaluating potential is far more critical than searching for a “perfect” fit. Building on the idea of **hiring trailblazers** , this article explores how to identify candidates who bring adaptability, creativity, and a growth mindset to your team.


### **The Problem with Traditional Interviews: Why Evaluating Potential Over Perfection Matters**


Traditional hiring mistakes can be costly. According to a[Harvard Business Review](https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbeshumanresourcescouncil/2024/03/29/what-leaders-get-wrong-about-hiring-and-why-it-matters/) study, **80%** of employee turnover stems from bad hiring decisions. Poor assessments not only waste resources but also damage team dynamics and morale.


Conventional interviews tend to **prioritise predictable, rehearsed answers and a polished demeanour** . While these qualities may indicate preparation, they don’t necessarily reveal a candidate’s ability to adapt, learn, or solve complex problems. Furthermore, traditional methods often favour candidates who fit predefined moulds, resulting in "safe" hires that **stagnate innovation** .


Another common issue is **overvaluing cultural or job fit** at the expense of diversity and fresh perspectives. Instead of discovering candidates who challenge assumptions and bring new ideas, organisations risk hiring individuals who simply reinforce existing practices.


### **What to Look for in Candidates**


To identify high-potential candidates, shift the focus from credentials to traits that signal long-term value. Here’s what to prioritise:


- **Adaptability**


: Does the candidate thrive in changing environments? Do they embrace learning new skills?
- **Problem-Solving Ability**


: How do they approach challenges? Are they proactive in finding solutions?
- **Constructive Challenging**


: Trailblazers often challenge the status quo, but there’s a distinction between constructive criticism and arguing for the sake of being difficult. Look for candidates who challenge ideas with the intent to improve outcomes, not to disrupt for attention.
- **Growth Mindset**


: Are they open to feedback, committed to continuous improvement, and willing to learn entirely new skills?


### **Techniques to Evaluate Potential**


Evaluating potential requires a departure from traditional methods. However, some modern approaches, like background checks on social media, raise ethical and practical questions. Candidates might use pseudonyms, keep accounts private, or feel such scrutiny invades their privacy. Instead, focus on structured evaluations and transparent techniques. Here are some **innovative ways** to uncover a candidate’s true abilities and mindset:


1. **Behavioural-Based Questions** :


- Ask open-ended questions to explore past experiences that demonstrate adaptability, problem-solving, team-work, and initiative.
- ***Example***


: “Tell me about a time you had to learn something outside your comfort zone to complete a task. How did you approach it?”


2. **Real-World Tasks** :


- Assign practical tasks or case studies related to the role. Focus on their thought process and creativity rather than the final result.
- ***Example***


: A marketing candidate could be asked to create a mock campaign for a fictional product.


3. **Hypothetical Scenarios** :


- Pose scenarios that encourage creative thinking and problem-solving.
- ***Example***


: “Imagine a key process in your last role was failing. How would you identify the problem and fix it?”


4. **Non-Traditional Metrics** :


- Evaluate traits like curiosity, collaboration, and emotional intelligence. These traits often indicate a candidate’s readiness to take on evolving challenges.


5. **Unexpected Shifts in Interview Questions** :


- Switching between work and non-work-related questions helps assess a candidate's ability to handle unexpected challenges, adapt quickly, and refocus. Introduce unexpected topics to observe how candidates adapt and think on their feet. Quickly transition back to work topics to test their mental agility.

### **Common Pitfalls in Candidate Assessments**


Even with structured techniques, some common traps can obscure a candidate's true potential. Avoid these pitfalls to ensure fair and accurate assessments. Here are some innovative techniques to help you uncover a candidate’s true abilities and mindset:


- **Relying on Resume Gaps as Indicators** :


- Candidates who leave roles after a year might not always be high performers. In some countries, extended probationary periods are required before termination, and short stints might reflect this rather than job-hopping tendencies.


- **Overemphasising Perfection** :


- Polished answers or a flawless interview performance can mask a lack of adaptability or willingness to grow. This could also result from candidates having faced dozens of interviews with repetitive, predictable questions, honing their ability to present polished but surface-level responses.


- **Misinterpreting Challenging Behaviour** :


- Constructive challengers aim to improve processes or outcomes. Be cautious not to dismiss them as disruptive without understanding their intent.


- **Ignoring Context** :


- Candidates from unconventional backgrounds or industries often bring unique perspectives. Don’t overlook them based on rigid criteria. The idea that someone from a smaller company cannot handle the challenges of a larger organisation is often misguided. In fact, smaller company employees often have broader skill sets due to less specialised roles, unlike their counterparts in larger organisations who may focus on narrower tasks within segmented teams.
