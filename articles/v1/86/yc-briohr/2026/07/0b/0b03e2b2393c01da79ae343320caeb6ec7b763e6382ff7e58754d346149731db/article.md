# The Difference Between Doing Your Job and Caring About It – Rejie Samuel

## The Job Description Problem


When Rejie arrived at Air International's Shanghai operation, he got rid of job descriptions. His problem with job descriptions was not that they were useless. It was that they had quietly become a tool for avoiding accountability. When something went wrong in an area slightly outside someone's assigned tasks, the document became a shield. "That is not my job." He had seen it happen across industries, across countries, across every level of an organisation.


"The job description is our biggest Achilles heel. It focuses people on 'this is my job' and creates silo mentality."


He replaced them with purpose statements, and he started with HR. His opening question to every person in the function was simple: why do you exist? Most of them responded by rewriting their job description back at him. The payroll person said their job was to process salaries accurately. Rejie kept pushing. Why does that role exist? What happens to the business when it runs well, and what happens when it does not?


The answer he was looking for: payroll exists so that employees are paid correctly and on time, which keeps them motivated, which contributes to the company hitting its sales, profitability, and cash flow targets. The same logic applied to every function. Finance does not exist to close the books. Recruitment does not exist to fill vacancies. Every role has a reason that connects back to the business, and once people understand that reason, the conversations in meetings change, the questions they ask change, and the decisions they make change.


## What the Numbers Cannot Tell You


Rejie is not arguing that spreadsheets are useless. He spent decades learning to read them and use them well. His argument is narrower than that: by the time a number appears in a report, the decision behind it has already been made. The cost has already been incurred. The quality issue has already reached the line.


Getting close to the work is how you catch things before they become numbers. Ms. Zhang had no financial training and no management title, but she understood the consequence of one unseated airflow door well enough to stop a production line over it. That is what Rejie means when he talks about care, not warmth, not wellness initiatives, but the kind of understanding that makes someone act differently when it matters.


"I encourage everyone, every leader, just ask the question: do you care? First of all, do you care enough? Are you there for the salary? And do your employees care?"
