# UK Blood Pressure Medicine Shortage: What Patients Taking Ramipril and Propranolol Should Know

A new warning about medicine supplies has raised concerns for people across the UK who rely on common blood pressure treatments. Ramipril and propranolol have been highlighted among medicines experiencing supply pressures, with some strengths reportedly more affected than others.


The situation does not mean that every patient taking these medicines will be unable to obtain their prescription. However, patients who depend on regular medication should be aware of the issue and speak with a pharmacist or healthcare professional if they experience difficulties obtaining their usual supply.


The Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) says medicine shortages can occur for a variety of reasons and are managed through established systems involving manufacturers, wholesalers, pharmacies and healthcare organisations.


## Which blood pressure medicines are affected?


Ramipril is an ACE inhibitor widely prescribed for high blood pressure and other cardiovascular conditions. It can also be used in people with heart failure and certain kidney conditions.


The warning concerns particular ramipril strengths rather than suggesting that every ramipril product is unavailable. Patients should therefore avoid assuming that their prescription will automatically be affected.


Propranolol is a beta blocker used for several conditions. Alongside high blood pressure and some heart-related problems, it can be prescribed for migraine, tremor and anxiety-related symptoms.


Reports have indicated supply pressure involving some propranolol capsule strengths. Availability can vary between pharmacies and suppliers, so anyone having difficulty obtaining the medicine should ask their pharmacist what options are available.


## Why are medicine shortages happening?


Medicine shortages are rarely caused by one single problem. The UK relies on a complex international pharmaceutical supply chain, meaning disruptions in manufacturing, transportation, raw materials, demand or distribution can affect supplies.


The DHSC explains that medicines licensed for use in the UK are sourced through a highly regulated global supply chain. Government departments work with manufacturers, wholesalers and other partners to assess shortages and reduce their impact on patients.


In some cases, demand can rise unexpectedly. Manufacturing problems can also reduce the amount of medicine available, while changes in global production and distribution can create additional pressure.


The recent situation involving ramipril also illustrates why patients should distinguish between a **medicine shortage** and a **medicine recall** . A shortage relates to availability, while a recall generally concerns specific batches or products that need to be removed from circulation for safety or quality reasons.


For example, the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) issued precautionary recalls involving particular ramipril batches in 2026 because of manufacturing or packaging problems. These recalls applied to specified batches and should not be interpreted as a recall of every ramipril product.


## What should patients do if their pharmacy has no stock?


The most important advice is not to stop taking prescribed medication simply because you have heard about a shortage.


If your pharmacy tells you that your usual medicine or strength is unavailable, ask what the pharmacist recommends. Depending on the circumstances, they may be able to source the medicine from another supplier or advise you about an appropriate alternative.


In England, serious shortage protocols can sometimes allow pharmacists to provide an alternative medicine or quantity under specific circumstances. The government says these protocols are designed to help manage shortages where an alternative can be supplied safely and appropriately.


However, an alternative is not automatically suitable for every patient. Your pharmacist, GP or other qualified healthcare professional should make that decision based on your prescription and individual circumstances.


Patients should also avoid stockpiling medication. Government guidance includes restrictions designed to prevent hoarding and exporting certain medicines when supply problems exist.


## Should you stop taking ramipril or propranolol?


No. Patients should not change their dose, stop treatment or switch medicines without professional advice.


Blood pressure medicines are generally taken regularly to control a medical condition over time. Suddenly changing treatment can create health risks, depending on the medicine and the reason it was prescribed.


If you are running low on tablets or capsules, contact your pharmacy before you completely run out. This gives the pharmacy and your healthcare team more time to identify an appropriate solution.


Anyone who has already missed doses or is worried about symptoms should seek advice from a pharmacist, GP or another appropriate healthcare professional.


## What is being done about UK medicine shortages?


The UK has established procedures for monitoring and responding to medicine supply problems. Pharmaceutical companies have reporting responsibilities when certain shortages or discontinuations are expected, allowing government authorities to assess potential effects on patients.


The DHSC says it works with NHS England, manufacturers, wholesalers and other organisations to assess supply problems and decide what action may be necessary. Healthcare professionals can also receive medicine supply notifications and access specialist information about current shortages.


Official UK statistics on medicine supply disruption are also published periodically. The latest 2026 release covers medicine supply issue and discontinuation notifications reported to the DHSC by pharmaceutical suppliers.


These systems are intended to reduce disruption and help maintain access to essential treatments even when individual products become difficult to obtain.


## The key message for patients


The reported shortage of some ramipril and propranolol products is a reason to stay informed, but it is not a reason for patients to panic.


If you take either medicine, continue following your prescription unless a healthcare professional tells you otherwise. Check with your pharmacy if your usual supply is unavailable, particularly if you are approaching the end of your current prescription.


It is also important to remember that shortages can affect particular strengths, manufacturers or batches rather than an entire medicine. The availability of a product may therefore change between pharmacies and over time.


Patients should rely on their pharmacist, GP and official NHS or government information rather than social media posts or unverified online claims.
