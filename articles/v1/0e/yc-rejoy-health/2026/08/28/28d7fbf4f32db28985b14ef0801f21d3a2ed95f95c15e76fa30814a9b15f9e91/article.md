# Drought Spreads Across England as Swimming Pools and Car Washes Could Face New Bans

England is facing growing pressure on its water supplies as prolonged dry weather and another spell of extreme heat intensify drought conditions across much of the country. Water companies are now considering tougher restrictions that could affect businesses such as car washes, swimming pools and other activities that rely heavily on mains water.


The situation has developed rapidly following an exceptionally dry July. According to the Met Office, England recorded only 6.5mm of rainfall during July, making it the driest July since records began in 1836. Wales also experienced its driest July on record, receiving just 9.3mm of rain.


With temperatures expected to climb again this week, concerns are increasing about whether water resources will be able to recover before demand rises further.


## Businesses Could Face New Restrictions


Southern Water has applied for permission to introduce a drought order and a non-essential use ban covering Hampshire and the Isle of Wight. The proposed measures could place additional restrictions on businesses that use significant amounts of water for activities considered non-essential.


This could include filling certain swimming pools, washing vehicles and other commercial uses of water.


The company has indicated that the application is precautionary and that it does not currently intend to introduce all of the proposed restrictions immediately. However, the move demonstrates how seriously water shortages are being treated as the summer progresses.


A non-essential use ban would go beyond the restrictions already affecting many households. Existing hosepipe restrictions have already been introduced in several areas, limiting activities such as watering gardens, washing cars and filling private swimming pools.


The potential expansion of restrictions is particularly significant for businesses whose normal operations depend on regular water use.


## Millions Already Affected


The drought is no longer confined to a small part of southern England. Government figures reported on 10 August indicated that approximately 71.3 per cent of England was officially experiencing drought conditions. Around 45 million people are estimated to live in affected areas.


The Environment Agency has described the rapidly developing situation as a "flash drought". This term refers to drought conditions that develop or intensify unusually quickly, generally because of a combination of very low rainfall and unusually high temperatures.


Several regions have recently been added to the list of areas experiencing drought, including parts of the East Midlands, Lincolnshire and Northamptonshire, Kent and East Sussex, and the Solent and South Downs.


The expansion means pressure is being placed on rivers, reservoirs and underground water sources across a much wider part of England.


## Why Has the Situation Become So Serious?


The main problem is the combination of several weather conditions occurring at the same time.


Rainfall has been exceptionally low, while temperatures have repeatedly climbed to unusually high levels. At the same time, extended periods of sunshine have increased evaporation and dried out soil and vegetation.


The Met Office described July as an extraordinary month for the UK's climate records. England received only around 10 per cent of its long-term average July rainfall, while Wales received approximately 9 per cent of its normal rainfall.


The Met Office's wider analysis also found that England and Wales received only around 4 per cent of their normal July rainfall when considered together.


This matters because water supplies cannot necessarily recover immediately when rain eventually arrives. Reservoirs, rivers and underground aquifers can require significant rainfall over an extended period before they return to healthier levels.


## Another Heatwave Adds to the Pressure


The water crisis is developing just as the UK prepares for another period of very hot weather.


Forecasters expect temperatures to rise significantly this week, with some areas potentially reaching the mid-30s Celsius. This is expected to be the fifth heatwave of the summer.


Hot weather creates additional demand for water. People naturally use more water during warm conditions, whether for drinking, bathing, gardening or cooling down.


Businesses can also experience increased demand. Leisure facilities, hospitality businesses and other industries may use more water as temperatures rise.


This creates a difficult situation for water companies. They must maintain supplies for essential household and public needs while also protecting rivers and other natural ecosystems from excessive abstraction.


## What Could a Non-Essential Use Ban Mean?


A non-essential use ban is designed to reduce water consumption where the activity is not considered vital.


The exact restrictions can vary depending on the conditions and the specific order approved by regulators. Potentially affected activities can include filling swimming pools, washing vehicles and other commercial uses involving substantial quantities of water.


For a car wash, for example, restrictions could mean that traditional water-intensive cleaning methods become temporarily unavailable or that the business has to use alternative systems.


Swimming pools could also face difficulties if operators need to top up or refill pools using mains water.


It is important to note that the possibility of restrictions does not mean every swimming pool or car wash in England is about to close. Measures depend on the water company, geographical area and drought conditions.


## Household Restrictions Are Already in Place


Millions of people are already living with some form of water restriction.


Hosepipe bans have been introduced by several water companies, affecting customers in areas where supplies are under particular pressure.


These restrictions are generally intended to reduce unnecessary outdoor water consumption. Depending on the rules in a particular area, customers may be prohibited from using a hosepipe to water gardens, wash vehicles or fill swimming pools.


The restrictions are not necessarily tied to a fixed end date. Water companies generally assess conditions based on the recovery of rivers, reservoirs and groundwater supplies.


That means restrictions could remain in place until water resources have improved sufficiently.


## Environmental Impact Is Another Concern


The drought is not simply a question of whether households will have enough water coming through their taps.


Low river levels can put considerable pressure on wildlife and aquatic ecosystems. Fish, amphibians, insects and plants can all be affected when rivers and wetlands become warmer and shallower.


Dry landscapes can also increase the risk of wildfires. Fire services have already been dealing with major incidents in several parts of the country during the current period of hot and dry weather.


Agriculture is another sector facing significant challenges. Farmers depend on reliable water supplies for crops and livestock, while dry soils can make growing conditions more difficult.


The combination of drought, heat and wildfire risk therefore creates a much broader environmental and economic problem.


## A Warning About Britain's Water Future


The current situation has also renewed questions about whether England has enough infrastructure to cope with increasingly severe periods of drought.


The UK has historically been associated with frequent rainfall, but that does not mean water supplies are automatically secure. Population growth, rising demand, leakage and changing weather patterns can all increase pressure on available resources.


Long-term resilience may require investment in reservoirs, water recycling, leakage reduction and more efficient water use.


For now, however, the immediate priority is managing the current shortage.


## What Happens Next?


The coming weeks will be closely watched by water companies, government agencies, businesses and households.


If significant rainfall returns and water sources recover, some restrictions could eventually be eased. If the dry weather continues, however, additional measures may become necessary.


The potential restrictions on commercial water use highlight the seriousness of the situation. What began as a period of exceptionally dry weather has developed into a nationwide concern affecting households, businesses, farmers and the environment.


For the public, the message is straightforward: water should be treated as a limited resource, particularly during periods of extreme heat.


The latest drought conditions are a reminder that Britain's water security cannot be taken for granted. With another heatwave approaching and rainfall remaining uncertain, the pressure on the country's water supplies is likely to remain a major issue throughout the rest of the summer.
