# White-Tailed Eagles Return to Exmoor After Two Centuries in Historic Conservation Comeback

White-tailed eagles have returned to Exmoor after more than 200 years. Discover how this conservation project aims to restore one of the UK’s most iconic birds and strengthen biodiversity.


## White-Tailed Eagles Make a Historic Return to Exmoor


A remarkable chapter in British wildlife conservation has begun as six white-tailed eagles have been released in Exmoor National Park for the first time in more than 200 years. The ambitious reintroduction project marks a major milestone in efforts to restore one of the UK’s largest birds of prey to landscapes where it once thrived.


The release is part of a long-term conservation programme led by Forestry England and the Roy Dennis Wildlife Foundation. The initiative aims to rebuild the population of white-tailed eagles in southern England and encourage the species to establish a sustainable breeding presence in the South West.


Known for their enormous wingspans of up to eight feet, white-tailed eagles are among the most impressive birds found in Britain. Sometimes described as “flying barn doors” because of their size and distinctive shape, these magnificent predators were once widespread across parts of England before persecution drove them to extinction in the region.


## Why White-Tailed Eagles Disappeared From Southern England


White-tailed eagles were historically a natural part of Britain’s wildlife. However, centuries of hunting, habitat changes, and human persecution caused their numbers to decline dramatically.


By the 18th century, the species had disappeared from southern England entirely. Their absence represented a significant loss for local ecosystems, as these birds play an important role as top predators and scavengers.


The return of white-tailed eagles is not only about restoring a lost species. Conservation experts believe their presence can help improve biodiversity and restore natural ecological processes across coastal and rural areas.


## Exmoor Chosen as a New Home for Eagle Reintroduction


Exmoor National Park, which stretches across parts of Devon and Somerset, was selected as a suitable location for the new releases after research showed that previously released birds were already exploring the area.


Satellite tracking data from white-tailed eagles introduced on the Isle of Wight revealed that some birds frequently travelled to Exmoor. The region offers many of the features these large predators need, including coastal habitats, rivers, open landscapes, and access to food sources such as fish and other wildlife.


The area also has historical significance, as white-tailed eagles would have naturally bred across parts of south-west England before disappearing.


Before the release took place, conservation organisations carried out feasibility studies, public consultations, and environmental assessments. Local groups and community representatives have also been involved through a project steering group designed to support the long-term success of the programme.


## A Continuing Conservation Success Story


The Exmoor release builds on the progress of a wider white-tailed eagle restoration project that began with releases on the Isle of Wight in 2019.


Since then, 45 young white-tailed eagles have been released as part of the southern England reintroduction effort. The programme achieved a major breakthrough in 2023 when the first wild white-tailed eagle chick was born in southern England for more than 240 years.


This successful breeding event demonstrated that released birds could adapt, survive, and eventually contribute to the recovery of the species.


Conservationists expect the newly released Exmoor birds to travel widely while they mature. White-tailed eagles usually begin breeding when they are around four to five years old, meaning the full impact of the project will develop over time.


## More Eagles Planned for Exmoor


The conservation team has announced plans to release up to 20 additional young white-tailed eagles from the Exmoor site over the next three years. Releases will also continue from the Isle of Wight as part of the broader effort to rebuild the species’ population.


Experts will closely monitor the birds using tracking technology to understand their movements, survival rates, and future breeding behaviour.


Zoe Smith from the Roy Dennis Wildlife Foundation said the return of white-tailed eagles to Exmoor represents a special moment after centuries of absence. The team will continue following the birds’ progress as they explore the region and eventually establish themselves within the landscape.


## Boost for Biodiversity Across the UK


The return of white-tailed eagles is expected to bring wider environmental benefits. As a key species within ecosystems, these birds help maintain natural balance by influencing populations of other animals and contributing to nutrient recycling.


Ali Hawkins, senior ecologist with Exmoor National Park Authority, described the release as an exciting moment for the national park and a significant boost for biodiversity.


The project reflects growing efforts across the UK to restore native species that have disappeared due to historical pressures. Similar conservation programmes have focused on bringing back endangered or extinct-in-the-wild species, while improving habitats for many other plants and animals.


## Government Support for Wildlife Recovery


The latest eagle release has also received support from the UK Government. Environment Secretary Angela Eagle welcomed the return of the birds, highlighting it as part of wider commitments to protect nature and reverse wildlife declines.


The Government has also pointed to funding initiatives aimed at supporting threatened species recovery and improving biodiversity across the country.


The return of white-tailed eagles demonstrates how carefully managed conservation projects can help repair damage caused by centuries of environmental change. While the birds still face challenges, including habitat pressures and the need for successful breeding, their return offers hope for the future of British wildlife.


## What the Return of White-Tailed Eagles Means for Exmoor


For local communities, visitors, and wildlife enthusiasts, the arrival of white-tailed eagles marks an exciting new era for Exmoor. These birds are expected to become an important part of the region’s natural identity and could attract greater interest in conservation and wildlife tourism.


The project is also a reminder that species once lost from landscapes can sometimes return through scientific research, public support, and long-term commitment.


After more than two centuries away, white-tailed eagles are once again flying above Exmoor. Their journey back represents not only the recovery of a remarkable bird but also a wider effort to restore the connection between Britain’s landscapes and the wildlife that once called them home.
