# WHO Cancer Agency Classifies Three Common Medicines as Carcinogenic: What Patients Need to Know

The World Health Organization’s (WHO) cancer research agency has classified three commonly prescribed medicines as “carcinogenic to humans,” raising questions among patients and healthcare professionals worldwide. However, experts have emphasised that the classification does not mean people should stop taking these medicines. Instead, it highlights the need for careful monitoring, responsible prescribing, and informed medical decisions.


The three medicines identified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) are hydrochlorothiazide, voriconazole, and tacrolimus. These drugs are widely used for conditions including high blood pressure, serious fungal infections, and prevention of organ transplant rejection.


The findings were published in Volume 137 of the IARC Monographs, a scientific assessment series that evaluates whether substances or exposures can cause cancer. The latest review follows conclusions previously reported in The Lancet Oncology in 2024.


## Understanding the IARC Group 1 Cancer Classification


The IARC places substances into categories based on the strength of scientific evidence showing whether they can cause cancer. Group 1 means there is sufficient evidence that an agent can cause cancer in humans.


However, this classification identifies a potential hazard and does not measure the actual risk faced by individuals using a medicine at prescribed doses.


Medical experts have explained that a substance being classified as carcinogenic does not automatically mean that every person exposed to it will develop cancer. Many factors influence cancer risk, including dosage, duration of exposure, genetics, lifestyle, environmental exposure, and overall health.


For patients, the key message remains clear: prescribed medicines should not be stopped suddenly without medical guidance.


## Hydrochlorothiazide and Skin Cancer Risk


Hydrochlorothiazide is a commonly prescribed diuretic used to manage high blood pressure and fluid retention. It works by helping the body remove excess salt and water, thereby lowering blood pressure.


According to the IARC evaluation, there is sufficient evidence linking hydrochlorothiazide use with squamous cell carcinoma of the skin and lip cancer.


Research, including studies from Denmark and the United States, has found increased rates of certain non-melanoma skin cancers among long-term users of the medicine. Scientists believe the risk is associated with the drug’s phototoxic effect, which can increase sensitivity to ultraviolet (UV) radiation.


When the skin becomes more vulnerable to UV exposure, repeated sun damage may increase the likelihood of DNA changes that contribute to cancer development.


Experts say this concern is especially relevant for people with high sun exposure, including outdoor workers such as farmers and individuals living in regions with intense sunlight.


## Voriconazole and Increased Skin Cancer Concerns


Voriconazole is an antifungal medication used to treat severe fungal infections, particularly in people with weakened immune systems, such as transplant recipients and patients undergoing certain medical treatments.


The IARC review found sufficient evidence linking prolonged voriconazole use with squamous cell carcinoma of the skin, especially among transplant patients.


Similar to hydrochlorothiazide, researchers have associated the risk with increased sensitivity to sunlight and UV-related skin damage.


Doctors have also raised concerns about inappropriate or unnecessary use of voriconazole in some situations. In countries where antifungal medicines may be easily available, misuse or prescribing outside approved indications can create additional challenges.


Healthcare professionals stress that voriconazole remains an important medicine when used appropriately for serious fungal infections.


## Tacrolimus and Cancer Risk After Transplantation


Tacrolimus is an immunosuppressant that plays a critical role in preventing organ rejection after transplantation. It reduces immune system activity, allowing the body to accept a transplanted organ.


The IARC classified tacrolimus as carcinogenic based on evidence linking it to non-Hodgkin lymphoma and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder. There was also limited evidence connecting it with certain blood cancers and skin cancers.


The increased cancer risk is believed to result from long-term immune suppression. A weakened immune system may have a reduced ability to identify and eliminate abnormal cells before they develop into cancer.


Despite these concerns, tacrolimus remains essential for many transplant recipients. Without immunosuppressive treatment, organ rejection can become life-threatening.


## What This Means for Patients in India


The classification has particular relevance in India, where all three medicines are widely used.


Hydrochlorothiazide is commonly prescribed as a first-line treatment for hypertension. Voriconazole is used for serious fungal infections, especially among immunocompromised patients. Tacrolimus is a key component of post-transplant care.


Dermatology experts have highlighted that the risks associated with hydrochlorothiazide may be more important among people with high UV exposure, including outdoor workers and populations living in sunny regions.


Doctors also point out that India faces challenges related to over-the-counter availability of medicines, self-medication, and inappropriate prescribing practices. These factors make responsible medicine use and regular medical supervision especially important.


## Should Patients Stop Taking These Medicines?


Doctors strongly advise against stopping these medicines without consulting a healthcare professional.


These drugs are prescribed because their benefits can be significant and, in many cases, lifesaving. For example:


- Hydrochlorothiazide helps control high blood pressure and reduces the risk of complications such as stroke and heart disease.
- Voriconazole can be vital in treating dangerous fungal infections.
- Tacrolimus helps transplant patients maintain functioning organs.


Stopping treatment suddenly may create serious health risks.


Instead, patients should discuss their individual risk factors with their doctors. Healthcare providers may consider factors such as treatment duration, alternative medicines, medical history, and cancer screening needs.


## Safety Measures Recommended by Experts


For people taking medicines associated with increased cancer risk, doctors recommend preventive measures rather than panic.


Patients using hydrochlorothiazide or voriconazole may benefit from:


- Regular use of sunscreen
- Reducing unnecessary UV exposure
- Wearing protective clothing outdoors
- Reporting unusual skin changes to a doctor
- Periodic skin examinations when appropriate


Transplant recipients taking tacrolimus are generally already monitored closely because of their increased risk of certain cancers and complications related to immune suppression.


## The Importance of Balanced Medical Decisions


The WHO cancer agency’s classification serves as an important scientific warning, but it should be interpreted correctly. It does not suggest that these medicines should be abandoned. Instead, it encourages doctors and patients to use them carefully and responsibly.


The decision to prescribe any medicine involves weighing benefits against potential risks. For millions of patients worldwide, hydrochlorothiazide, voriconazole, and tacrolimus continue to provide major health benefits when used under medical supervision.


The focus should remain on informed prescribing, regular monitoring, and patient awareness rather than fear-driven decisions.
