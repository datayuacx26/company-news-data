# How to Make Healthy Food Choices Easier for Families

Discover practical ways families can make healthier food choices, from affordable shopping and better food labelling to healthier school meals and industry action.


## How Can Families Make Healthier Food Choices?


Helping children and families eat a healthier diet is not simply about telling people what they should and should not eat. For many households, the reality is much more complicated. Food prices, busy schedules, confusing labels, advertising and the availability of convenient products can all influence what ends up on the family table.


Recent discussions involving nutrition experts, health organisations, food companies and retailers have highlighted an important point: healthier eating needs to become easier, more affordable and more practical for families.


The issue is particularly significant in the UK, where concerns about childhood obesity and diet-related health conditions continue to grow. The World Obesity Atlas 2026, cited in a recent Guardian feature, reported that around 3.8 million children in the UK have a high BMI.


Rather than placing responsibility entirely on parents, experts argue that supermarkets, manufacturers, policymakers and wider society all have a role to play.


## Why Healthy Eating Can Be Difficult for Families


Most parents want to provide nutritious meals for their children. However, good intentions do not always translate into easy decisions at the supermarket.


Parents often have to balance nutrition with price, convenience and what their children will actually eat. Food packaging can also make choosing between products difficult, particularly when claims such as "natural", "high in fibre" or "no added sugar" appear prominently on the front of a packet.


Paediatric dietitian Lucy Upton has highlighted how confusing food information can leave parents feeling uncertain about whether they are making the right choices.


Affordability is another major concern. Research highlighted by the Food Foundation suggests that healthier diets can be significantly more expensive for families on lower incomes. This means that simply advising households to buy healthier foods may not address the underlying problem.


A successful approach needs to consider household budgets as well as nutritional guidelines.


## Making Healthy Food More Affordable


One of the most effective ways to encourage healthier eating may be to make nutritious food easier to afford.


Fresh fruit and vegetables, wholegrains, pulses and other nutritious ingredients should be accessible to families across different income levels. Promotions and pricing strategies can influence purchasing decisions, especially when households are trying to manage a weekly food budget.


Retailers can contribute by increasing the availability of affordable healthier options and making these products easier to identify.


Tesco, for example, has reported initiatives designed to increase the proportion of its food sales classified as healthy. The supermarket has also highlighted promotions involving fruit, vegetables and everyday essentials.


However, affordability should not depend entirely on individual supermarket initiatives. Government policy and wider food-system changes may be necessary if healthier diets are to become genuinely accessible to everyone.


## Helping Children Develop Healthy Eating Habits


Healthy eating for children is not about creating a perfect diet. Children need a balanced range of foods, and their preferences can change over time.


Families can encourage healthier habits by regularly offering fruit and vegetables, choosing wholegrain foods where appropriate, including sources of protein and keeping sugary foods and drinks as occasional choices.


Presentation can also make a difference. Children are often attracted to food because of its appearance, branding and familiarity. Making nutritious foods appealing and enjoyable can therefore be an important part of encouraging better choices.


Experts have suggested that healthier foods need to compete with the excitement created by heavily marketed packaged products.


This does not mean turning every meal into a complicated recipe. Simple ideas such as colourful fruit, vegetable snacks, homemade sandwiches or easy family meals can make healthy food feel normal rather than restrictive.


## Understanding Ultra-Processed Foods


Ultra-processed foods, commonly referred to as UPFs, have become a major part of discussions about modern diets.


Some experts are concerned about the high proportion of ultra-processed foods consumed by children and teenagers in the UK. The challenge, however, is that not every packaged food should automatically be treated as unhealthy.


Nutrition is more complicated than simply dividing food into "good" and "bad" categories. Families can instead focus on overall dietary patterns and aim to include a wide variety of minimally processed foods.


Reading nutrition information, comparing products and paying attention to ingredients can help, but food labelling needs to be clear enough for busy shoppers to understand quickly.


## What Can Supermarkets and Food Manufacturers Do?


Food companies have considerable influence over what consumers see, buy and eat. Reformulating products to reduce excessive sugar, salt or saturated fat can therefore make a difference without requiring families to completely change their routines.


Tesco has reported efforts to increase fibre, reduce sugar in selected products and modify children's yoghurt products. Such initiatives demonstrate how changes made at the manufacturing and retail level can potentially affect large numbers of consumers.


Food manufacturers can also help by improving product information and making healthier products more visible.


Clearer standards could provide companies with greater certainty about what constitutes a healthier product. Consistent government guidelines could also make it easier for consumers to compare foods.


## The Role of Government Policy


Experts involved in the recent discussion argued that education alone may not be enough to create large-scale improvements in population health.


People can be given nutritional advice, but their choices are still influenced by the environment around them. If healthier foods are expensive, difficult to find or heavily out-marketed, information alone may have limited impact.


Government policy could therefore focus on measures that encourage healthier products, improve food standards and reduce the marketing of less healthy foods to children.


Mandatory reporting and measurable targets have also been suggested as ways to encourage businesses to make meaningful changes.


The goal should not be to make healthy eating complicated. Instead, the food environment should make healthier decisions easier by default.


## Small Changes Can Add Up


Improving family nutrition does not require every household to completely transform its diet overnight.


Small, sustainable changes can be easier to maintain. Families might start by adding an extra portion of vegetables to dinner, replacing some sugary drinks with water or choosing wholegrain alternatives more often.


Planning a few meals in advance can also reduce reliance on takeaway food during busy evenings. Cooking larger portions and using leftovers for another meal may help families save both time and money.


The most important point is consistency rather than perfection.


## A Shared Responsibility for Healthier Families


The challenge of improving children's diets cannot rest entirely on parents. Families make choices within an environment shaped by food prices, product availability, advertising, packaging and public policy.


Supermarkets can make healthier options more affordable and visible. Manufacturers can reformulate products. Schools can support nutritious meals and food education. Governments can create clearer standards and policies. Parents can encourage balanced eating habits at home.


When these efforts work together, healthier choices can become part of everyday life rather than another source of pressure for busy families.


Creating a healthier food environment will take time, investment and cooperation. But the principle is straightforward: families should not have to be nutrition experts, spend excessive amounts of money or devote hours to cooking every day simply to provide their children with a balanced diet.


Making healthier choices easier, clearer and more affordable could be one of the most practical ways to support the health of future generations.
