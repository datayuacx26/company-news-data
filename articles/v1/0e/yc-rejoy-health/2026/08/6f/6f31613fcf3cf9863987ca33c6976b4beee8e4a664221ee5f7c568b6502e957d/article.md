# Cocoa Crunch Cereal Recall Expands Across 10 States Over Undeclared Soy

A ready-to-eat cereal product has been recalled in 10 states after federal officials identified an undeclared soy allergen on its label. The recall affects Nutrient Foods’ Protein Cereal Cocoa Crunch, a product marketed as having a long shelf life and sold in individual metalized pouches.


The U.S. Food and Drug Administration classified the recall as a Class II event on Aug. 14, 2026. The company had initially initiated the recall on Aug. 3. According to the FDA enforcement information, the reason for the recall was that soy was not declared in the product’s required “contains” statement.


For most people, consuming a product containing soy is unlikely to cause a serious health concern. However, the situation is different for people with a soy allergy. An undeclared allergen can expose consumers to an ingredient they are actively trying to avoid, which is why accurate food labeling is an important part of food safety.


## Which Cocoa Crunch Cereal Was Recalled?


The recalled product is **Protein Cereal Cocoa Crunch** , manufactured by Nutrient Foods of Reno, Nevada.


According to information provided to the FDA, the recalled product consists of 16 kits. Each kit contains an 8.9-ounce metalized poly pillow pouch. Company representatives reportedly stated that the product has a shelf life of up to 25 years.


The cereal was distributed across 10 states:


- Florida
- Indiana
- Maryland
- Massachusetts
- Missouri
- New Jersey
- North Carolina
- Oklahoma
- Pennsylvania
- Texas


Consumers who purchased the product in these states should check their pantry supplies carefully, particularly if anyone in the household has a soy allergy.


The FDA's enforcement database provides the official recall information and regulatory details.


## Why Is Undeclared Soy a Food Safety Concern?


Soy is one of the major food allergens that U.S. regulators require manufacturers to identify on food labels.


For people who are allergic to soy, exposure can result in an allergic reaction. The severity of food allergy reactions varies from person to person, and some reactions can require medical attention.


The FDA recognizes nine major food allergens that must be properly declared on food labels. They are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame.


This means that an ingredient that may be harmless to most consumers can represent a significant health concern for people with an allergy.


The Cocoa Crunch recall illustrates why consumers with food allergies need to pay close attention to recall announcements and ingredient information. Even products that appear familiar or suitable for a particular diet can potentially contain an allergen that was not properly identified on the packaging.


## What Does a Class II Recall Mean?


The FDA uses different classifications to communicate the potential health risks associated with recalled products.


A **Class II recall** generally applies when exposure to a product may result in temporary or medically reversible adverse health consequences, or when the probability of serious harm is considered remote.


A Class II classification is different from a **Class I recall** , which represents the FDA's highest level of recall risk. A Class I situation involves a reasonable probability that exposure to the product could cause serious adverse health consequences or death.


The FDA also uses a **Class III** classification for products that violate regulatory requirements but are unlikely to cause adverse health consequences. Certain minor labeling problems can fall into this category.


For the Cocoa Crunch cereal, the Class II designation reflects the potential concern associated with the undeclared soy ingredient, particularly for consumers who have a soy allergy.


## What Should Consumers Do?


Consumers who have purchased the recalled Cocoa Crunch cereal should avoid eating it if they have a soy allergy.


The safest approach is to check the product against the recall information and avoid consuming the cereal until its status has been confirmed. Consumers should also be cautious about serving recalled products to children, guests, or other household members who may have known food allergies.


If someone accidentally consumes a product containing an allergen and develops symptoms of an allergic reaction, they should follow the emergency plan provided by their healthcare professional. Severe allergic reactions require immediate medical attention.


Consumers without a soy allergy may not face the same level of risk, but they should still pay attention to recall instructions and product identification information.


## Undeclared Soy Has Triggered Other Recent Recalls


The Cocoa Crunch cereal recall is not an isolated example of an undeclared soy allergen creating a food safety issue.


The FDA recently reported another recall involving prepared breakfast burritos after an undeclared soy ingredient was identified.


Fresh & Ready Foods LLC voluntarily recalled two varieties of spicy breakfast burritos. The affected products included the 8.3-ounce Sprig & Sprout Spicy Breakfast Burrito and the 8.2-ounce Fresh and Ready Spicy Breakfast Burrito.


The products were distributed through grab-and-go markets and vending machines in several states.


The labeling issue was particularly notable because the products could have been perceived by consumers as vegan or otherwise free from certain animal-derived ingredients. The ingredient information listed items including scrambled eggs, salsa, potatoes, jalapenos, shredded cheese, green pepper, and onion.


The comparison highlights a broader issue for consumers: food labels can change, products can be reformulated, and manufacturing or labeling errors can result in allergens being present without adequate disclosure.


## Why Food Recall Awareness Matters


Food recalls can sometimes appear routine, especially when the affected ingredient does not pose a concern for the general population. For people with food allergies, however, a recall involving an undeclared allergen can be much more significant.


Consumers can reduce their risk by regularly checking FDA recall notices, reading ingredient labels and keeping track of known allergens in their household.


The Cocoa Crunch recall also demonstrates why consumers should not assume that a long shelf-life food is automatically safe to keep indefinitely. Products stored for years can remain in household pantries long after their original purchase, making recall awareness particularly important for shelf-stable foods.


Anyone who believes they may have purchased the recalled Protein Cereal Cocoa Crunch should review the official FDA information before consuming it.
