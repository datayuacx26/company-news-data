# Fauci COVID Vaccine Texts Reveal Pregnancy Safety Concerns Discussed in 2021

Newly released text messages from Dr. Anthony Fauci's government-issued cellphone are drawing renewed attention to how U.S. health officials discussed COVID-19 vaccine safety for pregnant women during the early months of the vaccine rollout.


The messages, released on Aug. 10 by Republican Sens. Ron Johnson of Wisconsin and Rand Paul of Kentucky, are part of a much larger collection of communications from Fauci's government phone. According to Johnson's office, the device contained more than 34,000 text messages and hundreds of voicemails.


The most significant messages concern conversations in January 2021 involving Fauci, then-CDC Director Dr. Rochelle Walensky and then-Surgeon General Dr. Vivek Murthy. Their discussion centered on a basic but important question: How much was actually known about the safety of COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy at that point?


## What Did Fauci Say About Pregnancy and COVID Vaccines?


In a Jan. 25, 2021, exchange, Murthy asked whether there was any data or theoretical reason to prefer vaccination earlier or later during pregnancy. He also asked when more substantial information about vaccine risks for pregnant women would become available.


Walensky responded that, at the time, her understanding was that the available information concerning vaccine risks during pregnancy was extremely limited. She pointed to thousands of pregnant women enrolled in CDC safety monitoring as an important source of information that was still being collected.


Fauci similarly acknowledged that there was not enough evidence to determine whether one stage of pregnancy was safer than another for vaccination.


One part of the exchange has received particular attention. Fauci said that immune responses and fever following a second vaccine dose could theoretically be associated with miscarriage during the first trimester. Walensky and Murthy acknowledged the theoretical concern.


That wording is important. Fauci was discussing a **theoretical possibility** , not reporting that vaccination had been demonstrated to cause miscarriage. The distinction matters when interpreting historical medical communications.


The newly released messages therefore provide evidence that senior health officials were actively discussing uncertainties surrounding vaccination during pregnancy. They do not, by themselves, establish that COVID-19 vaccines caused miscarriages.


## The Data Gap Was a Central Issue


Another exchange from Jan. 26 focused on a World Health Organization statement concerning Moderna's vaccine and pregnancy.


The discussion reflected a problem that existed during the earliest phase of the vaccine rollout: pregnant women had generally been excluded from the original clinical trials, leaving policymakers to make decisions while direct trial data were limited.


Fauci wrote that when data were lacking for a vaccine in pregnant women, officials had to weigh potential risks against potential benefits. He also pointed to the experience of more than 10,000 pregnant women who had chosen to receive vaccination and said that no safety issues had emerged from that experience at the time.


Animal toxicology studies, he noted, had also not raised major warning signs.


This part of the exchange reveals something more nuanced than a simple claim that officials either knew the vaccines were safe or believed they were dangerous.


Instead, the messages show officials working with incomplete information, weighing theoretical risks against the potential benefits of vaccination during a pandemic.


## Why the Messages Matter


The significance of the texts is partly about what they reveal regarding scientific uncertainty.


Public health recommendations often need to be made before every question has been answered. During the COVID-19 pandemic, that challenge was especially pronounced because vaccines were being developed and deployed at unprecedented speed.


Pregnancy presented an additional challenge because pregnant women are frequently excluded from early clinical trials for ethical and safety reasons. That meant policymakers had less direct evidence available when vaccines first became available.


The newly released messages demonstrate that senior officials were aware of this information gap.


They also show that the officials were discussing possible risks internally rather than treating every question about vaccination during pregnancy as completely settled.


That distinction could influence the continuing debate over how public health agencies communicated uncertainty to the public.


## Internal Discussions and Public Messaging


The release has also prompted questions about whether the uncertainty expressed privately was adequately reflected in public statements.


Johnson and Paul have highlighted the difference between the officials' internal discussion of limited pregnancy data and their broader public support for COVID-19 vaccination.


However, the texts need to be considered in their full context.


A private discussion acknowledging uncertainty does not necessarily contradict a public recommendation. Medical decisions frequently involve comparing uncertain risks rather than choosing between a completely safe option and a demonstrably dangerous one.


For pregnant women during the pandemic, officials were weighing the potential risks of vaccination against the known risks posed by COVID-19 infection itself.


That risk-benefit calculation was particularly important because COVID-19 infection could also have serious consequences for pregnant women.


The key question raised by the documents is therefore not simply whether officials knew there was uncertainty. The messages indicate that they did.


The larger question is **how that uncertainty was communicated to the public and incorporated into official recommendations.**


## The Broader Fauci Investigation


The release of the pregnancy-related texts comes as congressional scrutiny of Fauci's pandemic-era decisions intensifies.


The Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, chaired by Johnson, recently obtained a copy of Fauci's government-issued iPhone from the Department of Health and Human Services. The phone is expected to provide investigators with additional communications surrounding the government's COVID-19 response.


The messages are also being released after Fauci appeared before senators in July and declined to answer questions by invoking his constitutional right against self-incrimination.


The ongoing investigation has expanded beyond vaccine policy to include questions about Fauci's broader management of the pandemic and his communications while serving as one of the most prominent U.S. public health officials.


## What the Texts Do and Do Not Show


The newly released communications provide useful insight into the thinking of senior health officials in January 2021.


They show that:


- Direct clinical data on COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy were limited at the time.
- Officials were actively discussing the absence of pregnancy-specific data.
- Fauci raised a theoretical concern about fever and immune responses potentially being associated with miscarriage during the first trimester.
- Officials also considered early real-world vaccination experience among pregnant women.
- Animal toxicology studies had not produced major warning signals according to Fauci's message.
- The officials were attempting to balance potential vaccine risks against the risks and benefits associated with COVID-19.


What the messages **do not establish** is that COVID-19 vaccines were proven to cause miscarriage.


That distinction should remain central to any discussion of the documents.


## Why Transparency Matters


Perhaps the broader lesson from the texts is the importance of transparent communication when scientific evidence is incomplete.


During a public health emergency, officials may have to make recommendations using the best evidence available at the time. As new evidence emerges, those recommendations can change.


But public trust depends heavily on people understanding what officials know, what they do not know, and why they are making a particular recommendation.


The Fauci texts offer another glimpse into how complicated those decisions were in early 2021.


They also show why government communications from the pandemic continue to attract public and congressional scrutiny years later.


As additional messages and records are reviewed, the most useful approach will be to distinguish documented facts from political interpretations and theoretical concerns from demonstrated medical outcomes.


For readers trying to understand the latest release, the central takeaway is straightforward: **the texts show that senior U.S. health officials knew pregnancy-specific COVID-19 vaccine data were limited and discussed theoretical risks while also weighing those uncertainties against the potential benefits of vaccination.**


The continuing debate is now focused on whether that uncertainty was communicated clearly enough to the American public and whether policymakers made the right decisions with the evidence available at the time.
