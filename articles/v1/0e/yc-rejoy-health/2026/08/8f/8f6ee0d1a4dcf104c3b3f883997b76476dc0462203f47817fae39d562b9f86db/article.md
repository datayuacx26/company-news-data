# Rising High Blood Pressure Rates During Pregnancy Reported Across All US States, CDC Study Finds

## Gestational Hypertension Increasing Nationwide as Experts Highlight Maternal Health Risks


High blood pressure during pregnancy is becoming an increasingly common health concern in the United States. A new study from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) found that rates of gestational hypertension increased in every U.S. state and the District of Columbia between 2016 and 2024.


Gestational hypertension refers to high blood pressure that develops during pregnancy, usually after 20 weeks of gestation, in women who did not previously have hypertension. According to the CDC research, the nationwide rate of gestational hypertension increased by 73 percent over the eight-year period, rising from 6 percent in 2016 to 10.4 percent in 2024.


This means that approximately one in every ten pregnant women in the United States experienced gestational hypertension in 2024.


## CDC Reports Significant Increases in Every State


The CDC analysis examined all registered births in the United States from 2016 through 2024. Researchers found that the increase in gestational hypertension occurred across all states, although the size of the rise varied by location.


The smallest increase was reported in Idaho, where cases rose by about 21 percent. Hawaii recorded the largest increase, with rates climbing by 144 percent during the study period.


Eight states experienced increases of 100 percent or more, including Hawaii, California, Colorado, Wyoming, Arkansas, Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Massachusetts.


In 2024, gestational hypertension rates were below 9 percent in the District of Columbia and several states, including New Jersey, California, Massachusetts, Utah, Nevada, New York, Idaho, Tennessee, and New Mexico.


Meanwhile, some states reported rates of 13 percent or higher. These included Minnesota, Oregon, Wyoming, New Hampshire, Ohio, Alabama, Alaska, and Louisiana.


## Why High Blood Pressure During Pregnancy Is Dangerous


Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy are among the leading causes of maternal health complications and pregnancy-related deaths in the United States.


For pregnant women, high blood pressure can increase the risk of serious health problems, including organ damage, stroke, cardiovascular disease, and pregnancy complications. In severe cases, it can develop into preeclampsia or eclampsia.


Preeclampsia occurs when a pregnant woman develops high blood pressure after 20 weeks of pregnancy along with signs of organ problems, often involving protein in the urine. Eclampsia is a more severe condition where seizures occur in a woman with preeclampsia and require immediate medical attention.


High blood pressure during pregnancy can also affect the baby. When blood flow through the placenta is reduced, the baby may receive fewer oxygen and nutrients. This can increase the chances of premature birth, low birth weight, and stillbirth.


## Older Maternal Age Linked to Higher Risk


The CDC study also examined differences in gestational hypertension rates based on age and other demographic factors.


Researchers found that women aged 40 and older had the highest rates of gestational hypertension in 2024. Younger women under age 20 and women between ages 30 and 34 had lower rates compared with some other age groups.


The increase in pregnancy-related hypertension comes as the average age of first-time mothers in the United States continues to rise. A CDC report published in 2025 found that the average age of first-time mothers increased from 26.6 years in 2016 to 27.5 years in 2023.


The percentage of first births among teenagers declined during that period, while births among women aged 35 and older increased.


## Differences Seen Among Racial Groups


The CDC report also identified differences in gestational hypertension rates among racial and ethnic groups.


In 2024, the lowest reported rate was among Asian non-Hispanic mothers, at 7.1 percent.


The highest rate was observed among American Indian and Alaska Native non-Hispanic mothers, at 13.2 percent.


Health experts continue to study the reasons behind these differences, including the role of access to healthcare, underlying medical conditions, social factors, and other pregnancy-related risks.


## Normal Blood Pressure Levels During Pregnancy


According to the Office on Women’s Health, normal blood pressure during pregnancy is generally considered to be 120/80 millimeters of mercury (mm Hg) or lower.


A reading of 140/90 mm Hg or higher on two separate occasions after 20 weeks of pregnancy may indicate high blood pressure.


Regular prenatal care and blood pressure monitoring are important because pregnancy-related hypertension can sometimes develop without obvious symptoms.


## Common Risk Factors for Gestational Hypertension


Several factors may increase a woman’s risk of developing high blood pressure during pregnancy. These include:


- Diabetes
- Overweight or obesity
- Being older than 35 years of age
- First pregnancy
- Autoimmune disorders
- A family history of preeclampsia
- Previous blood clotting problems
- A gap of 10 or more years between pregnancies


Managing these risk factors before and during pregnancy can help reduce the likelihood of complications.


## Prevention and Healthy Pregnancy Practices


While not all cases of gestational hypertension can be prevented, maintaining healthy habits may lower the risk.


Doctors often recommend eating a balanced diet, staying physically active when medically appropriate, attending regular prenatal appointments, and managing existing health conditions such as diabetes, kidney disease, or high blood pressure.


Women who are planning pregnancy may benefit from discussing their health history with a healthcare provider. Early medical guidance can help identify possible risks and create a plan for a safer pregnancy.


## Growing Concern for Maternal Health in the United States


The rise in gestational hypertension rates highlights the importance of improving maternal healthcare and increasing awareness about pregnancy-related blood pressure conditions.


Because high blood pressure during pregnancy can affect both the mother and baby, early detection and proper medical management remain essential.


Healthcare providers encourage pregnant women to monitor their blood pressure, attend prenatal visits, and seek medical advice if they experience warning signs such as severe headaches, vision changes, swelling, chest pain, or sudden changes in health.


As pregnancy-related hypertension continues to rise nationwide, researchers and health officials are focusing on understanding the causes behind the increase and developing better strategies to protect maternal and infant health.
