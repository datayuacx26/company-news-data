# Blood-Thinning Drugs and Alzheimer’s: Could Anticoagulants Slow Cognitive Decline?

New research suggests some blood-thinning drugs may be linked to slower cognitive decline in people with atrial fibrillation and Alzheimer’s. Here is what the findings mean.


## Could blood-thinning drugs slow Alzheimer’s disease?


A new study has raised an intriguing possibility in the search for ways to slow the progression of Alzheimer’s disease. Researchers have found that certain modern blood-thinning medications may be associated with a slower decline in cognitive function among people who have both atrial fibrillation and Alzheimer’s.


The findings are particularly interesting because anticoagulant medicines are already widely prescribed for people with atrial fibrillation, a common heart rhythm condition that can increase the risk of blood clots and stroke.


However, the research does **not** mean that people should start taking blood thinners to prevent or treat Alzheimer’s disease. The researchers say further investigation is needed before these medicines could be considered specifically for their potential effects on dementia.


## What did the new Alzheimer’s study find?


Researchers from Sweden examined health information from more than 7,000 people diagnosed with both atrial fibrillation and Alzheimer’s disease.


The participants were grouped according to whether they were taking newer anticoagulant medicines, the older blood thinner warfarin, or no anticoagulant treatment.


The researchers then assessed cognitive performance, including areas such as memory, language and motor skills.


The results suggested that people taking newer anticoagulants experienced a somewhat slower decline in cognitive function than people taking warfarin or those who were not receiving anticoagulant treatment.


The difference was relatively small over a single year. People taking newer blood thinners scored just under 1 per cent higher on the cognitive assessment after one year.


Although that may appear modest, researchers say even small differences could potentially become more meaningful over longer periods.


## Why could blood thinners affect the brain?


The possible explanation centres on the relationship between the heart, blood vessels and the brain.


Atrial fibrillation can interfere with the heart's normal rhythm and increase the likelihood of blood clots. Those clots can travel to the brain and cause a stroke. Even very small disruptions to blood flow may potentially contribute to damage in the brain over time.


Researchers believe that anticoagulant treatment could potentially protect brain health indirectly by reducing clot formation and helping to prevent vascular events.


The idea is particularly relevant because Alzheimer's disease does not occur in isolation from other health conditions. Changes affecting blood vessels and circulation can also contribute to cognitive problems.


According to the researchers, improving blood flow and reducing small-scale vascular damage could potentially have a positive effect on cognition.


Importantly, this remains an area of scientific investigation rather than an established treatment for Alzheimer's.


## Newer anticoagulants appeared to perform differently


One of the most notable aspects of the research was the difference between newer anticoagulants and warfarin.


Newer medications, sometimes referred to as direct oral anticoagulants, include drugs such as rivaroxaban. These medicines are increasingly used instead of warfarin for many people with atrial fibrillation.


The study associated newer anticoagulants with slower cognitive decline compared with both warfarin and no anticoagulant treatment.


The researchers also observed that people receiving newer anticoagulants had lower rates of some serious outcomes, including stroke and premature death, compared with people who were not receiving anticoagulant therapy.


Warfarin was also associated with lower risks of certain outcomes compared with receiving no anticoagulant treatment, although it was linked with a greater risk of major bleeding.


These findings highlight why anticoagulant treatment needs to be carefully tailored to each individual.


## Does this mean blood thinners could prevent Alzheimer’s?


Not at this stage.


The study was observational, meaning researchers examined existing health data rather than assigning participants randomly to different treatments. This type of research can identify associations, but it cannot prove that a medication directly caused the slower cognitive decline.


There may also be other differences between people taking different medicines that influence the results.


For this reason, researchers have stopped short of recommending anticoagulants as an Alzheimer's treatment for people who do not otherwise need them.


Blood thinners can have serious side effects, particularly bleeding. Taking an anticoagulant without a medical reason could therefore create significant risks.


Anyone already taking an anticoagulant should not stop or change their medication based on this study. Medication decisions should always be discussed with a doctor or qualified healthcare professional.


## The connection between atrial fibrillation and dementia


The research also draws attention to the relationship between heart health and brain health.


Atrial fibrillation becomes more common with age and is associated with an increased risk of stroke. Several cardiovascular risk factors, including high blood pressure, obesity and smoking, can also affect long-term brain health.


This means looking after cardiovascular health may be an important part of protecting cognitive health as people get older.


Maintaining healthy blood pressure, avoiding smoking, staying physically active, eating a balanced diet and managing conditions such as diabetes can all form part of a broader approach to reducing dementia risk.


However, no single lifestyle change can guarantee that someone will avoid Alzheimer's disease.


## What should people take from the research?


The most important message is that the study provides another potential avenue for Alzheimer's research, rather than a new treatment recommendation.


The findings suggest that medicines already used to manage cardiovascular conditions may have effects on brain health that scientists do not yet fully understand.


Future clinical trials will be important to determine whether newer anticoagulants genuinely slow cognitive decline and, if so, which patients could potentially benefit.


Until then, people should continue taking prescribed medicines as directed and speak to their healthcare professional before making changes.


The research also reinforces a broader lesson: heart and brain health are closely connected. Managing cardiovascular risk factors may be an important part of maintaining cognitive health throughout later life.


## Final thoughts


Alzheimer’s disease remains one of the major health challenges associated with ageing, and researchers continue to investigate treatments that could slow its progression.


The latest Swedish research offers an interesting clue. Among people with atrial fibrillation and Alzheimer's, newer blood-thinning medicines were associated with a slightly slower decline in cognitive function.


But an association is not proof of cause and effect. More research is needed before anticoagulants could be considered specifically as a strategy for treating or preventing Alzheimer's disease.


For now, the findings should be viewed as promising research rather than medical advice. People with atrial fibrillation should discuss their individual treatment options with their doctor, particularly because anticoagulants can reduce the risk of dangerous blood clots but can also increase the risk of bleeding.
