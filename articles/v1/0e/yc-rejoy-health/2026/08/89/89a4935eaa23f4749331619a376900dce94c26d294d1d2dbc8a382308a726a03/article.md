# Suspected Botulism Warning at Bristol Park Lake: Symptoms, Risks and Safety Advice

Bristol City Council is investigating suspected botulism at a lake in St George’s Park. Find out what botulism is, the warning signs and what visitors and dog owners should do.


A health and safety warning has been issued following reports of suspected botulism at a lake in St George’s Park, Bristol. Local authorities are investigating concerns about the potentially dangerous illness after reports of sick wildlife in and around the water.


People visiting the popular park have been advised to avoid the lake and not to approach sick, injured or dead animals. Dog owners have also been told to keep pets away from the water and prevent them from coming into contact with affected wildlife.


The situation is still being investigated, meaning the reported presence of botulism should be treated as a suspected incident rather than a confirmed human outbreak.


## What is happening at St George’s Park?


According to reports published on August 18, 2026, Bristol City Council said it was aware of concerns about suspected botulism at the lake in St George’s Park and was investigating.


Residents had reportedly noticed wildlife becoming unwell, raising concerns about the possible presence of botulinum toxin in the area.


There have also been questions from local residents about the condition of equipment used to circulate or aerate the lake. However, there is currently no established evidence that a faulty aerator caused the suspected illness.


The safest approach for visitors is therefore to follow official advice and avoid the lake until authorities provide further information.


People should not touch sick or dead birds or other animals found near the water. Anyone who finds affected wildlife should report it to the relevant local authority rather than attempting to rescue or handle it themselves.


## Why is botulism dangerous?


Botulism is a rare but serious illness caused by toxins produced by certain bacteria, most commonly *Clostridium botulinum* .


The toxin affects the nervous system and can cause severe muscle weakness and paralysis. In serious cases, the paralysis can reach the muscles responsible for breathing, creating a medical emergency.


The NHS describes botulism as rare but potentially life-threatening and says it requires immediate hospital treatment.


Although the reported incident in Bristol concerns a lake and wildlife, people should not assume that simply being near the area means they have developed botulism. The authorities' warning is primarily intended to reduce unnecessary contact with potentially affected water and animals while investigations continue.


## How can people become infected?


There are several forms of botulism, and the routes of exposure vary.


Foodborne botulism can happen when someone eats food containing botulinum toxin. It can be associated with food that has not been prepared, preserved or stored safely.


Wound botulism occurs when the bacteria enter a wound and produce toxin.


Infant botulism is different and can occur when babies ingest spores of the bacteria. This is why the NHS advises that babies under 12 months should not be given honey.


The fact that botulism can occur in different ways is important because suspected contamination of a lake does not automatically mean members of the public will become infected.


## What symptoms should you know about?


Botulism can affect the nerves controlling muscles, particularly those involved in the face, eyes, swallowing and breathing.


According to the NHS, possible symptoms include blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing or speaking, facial muscle weakness and dry mouth. Paralysis can develop if the illness progresses.


Symptoms can be serious because weakness may spread through the body. Breathing difficulties are particularly concerning and require emergency medical attention.


Anyone who develops symptoms that could indicate botulism should not wait for the condition to become worse before seeking help.


The NHS advises calling 999 if you think you or your child may have botulism, particularly if there are symptoms such as facial weakness, difficulty swallowing or speaking, inability to move part of the body or severe breathing problems.


## What should visitors to the park do?


The most important precaution is to follow the instructions issued by local authorities.


Visitors should avoid entering or deliberately approaching the lake. People should also avoid touching wildlife that appears sick, injured or dead.


Parents should keep children away from the water and explain why animals in the area should not be handled.


Dog owners should take particular care. Dogs can investigate or drink from water sources and may approach dead birds or other wildlife. Keeping dogs away from the lake and on a lead where advised can reduce the risk of unwanted contact.


If someone discovers a dead or visibly unwell bird, they should report it to Bristol City Council rather than attempting to move the animal.


## Is this a confirmed human outbreak?


At this stage, it is important to distinguish between a **suspected botulism incident involving wildlife** and a confirmed outbreak of botulism among people.


The information available indicates that authorities are investigating reports concerning the lake. It does not establish that there has been a confirmed outbreak of human botulism linked to St George’s Park.


That distinction matters because botulism is rare in the UK. The NHS says all forms of botulism are uncommon in Britain.


People should therefore remain cautious without becoming unnecessarily alarmed.


## When should you seek medical help?


If you believe you may have symptoms of botulism, urgent medical advice is essential.


The condition can become life-threatening because paralysis may affect breathing. Treatment is carried out in hospital and may include medication to stop the toxin from causing further damage, alongside supportive treatment if muscle weakness or breathing problems develop.


For emergencies, call 999. For urgent but non-life-threatening medical concerns, NHS 111 can provide guidance and direct people to the appropriate service.


Above all, people visiting St George’s Park should follow the latest guidance from Bristol City Council while the investigation continues.
