# Common Sugar May Help Cancer Spread After Chemotherapy, New Study Suggests

## New research reveals how fructose could influence cancer recurrence


A new scientific study has uncovered a surprising link between fructose, a common type of sugar, and the spread of cancer after chemotherapy. Researchers found that fructose may act as a biological signal that encourages surviving cancer cells to become more aggressive, raising fresh questions about nutrition, cancer treatment, and even the use of cholesterol-lowering medications.


While the findings are still in the early stages and have not yet been confirmed in human patients, they offer an important new direction for cancer research. Scientists believe the discovery could eventually help improve treatment strategies and reduce the risk of cancer recurrence.


## What did the study discover?


The research, published in the journal *Nature Ageing* , focused on ovarian cancer, one of the deadliest forms of cancer among women. Scientists from the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia studied how cancer behaves after chemotherapy.


Chemotherapy aims to destroy cancer cells, but not every tumour cell dies during treatment. The remaining cells enter a state where they stop dividing but continue to stay biologically active. These surviving cells release various molecules into the surrounding environment, influencing nearby cancer cells.


Researchers found that one of these signalling molecules is fructose.


According to the study, fructose produced by chemotherapy-surviving tumour cells appears to encourage neighbouring cancer cells to spread more easily. This discovery is one of the first to identify a nutrient acting as a communication signal between cancer cells after chemotherapy.


## Why is fructose important?


Fructose is a naturally occurring simple sugar found in fruits, honey, and some vegetables. It is also widely used in processed foods and sugary beverages, often in the form of high-fructose corn syrup.


Scientists discovered that fructose released by surviving tumour cells affects cholesterol levels inside neighbouring cancer cells.


Cholesterol does much more than affect heart health. Within cells, it helps maintain the structure of cell membranes and allows cells to stick together. Researchers believe that lower cholesterol inside cancer cells weakens these connections, making it easier for cancer cells to detach and spread throughout the body.


This mechanism may help explain why ovarian cancer often returns after initially successful chemotherapy.


## Why ovarian cancer frequently comes back


Ovarian cancer is particularly difficult to treat because it often recurs after chemotherapy.


Although many patients respond well to platinum-based chemotherapy, the disease frequently returns months or years later. When ovarian cancer spreads through the abdominal cavity after treatment, it becomes significantly more difficult to manage.


Researchers estimate that recurrence and metastatic spread account for approximately 90 percent of ovarian cancer-related deaths.


The new findings suggest that chemotherapy-surviving cells may actively contribute to this recurrence by sending fructose-based signals that promote cancer spread.


## Could diet play a role?


One of the most interesting aspects of the study is the possibility that dietary sugar intake could influence cancer progression.


Researchers found evidence suggesting that consuming large amounts of fructose, particularly through sugary drinks and highly processed foods, might also activate similar pathways that encourage cancer cells to spread.


This does not mean that eating fruit causes cancer or that sugar alone leads to cancer recurrence. Natural fruits contain fibre, vitamins, antioxidants, and other nutrients that affect how the body processes sugar.


Instead, the findings raise questions about excessive fructose consumption from processed foods and sweetened beverages, especially among individuals undergoing cancer treatment.


Scientists say more clinical research is needed before dietary recommendations can be made.


## What about statins?


The research also introduces another unexpected possibility involving statins, the widely prescribed medications used to lower cholesterol.


Because fructose appears to reduce cholesterol within cancer cells, researchers are investigating whether statins might unintentionally influence the same biological pathway.


This has prompted scientists to explore whether combining cholesterol-lowering medications with chemotherapy could affect cancer progression in some patients.


However, experts strongly emphasise that there is currently no evidence that patients should stop taking statins.


The study has not tested this effect in humans, and researchers caution against making any changes to prescribed medications without consulting a healthcare professional.


## Could this apply to other cancers?


Although the current research focused on ovarian cancer, scientists believe the findings may have broader implications.


Many types of cancer survive chemotherapy through similar biological mechanisms. Researchers are already planning follow-up studies to determine whether fructose signalling also contributes to recurrence in breast, colorectal, pancreatic, and other cancers.


If confirmed, the discovery could reshape scientists' understanding of how cancer spreads after treatment and potentially lead to new therapies designed to block these signalling pathways.


## What this means for patients


While the study has generated considerable interest, it is important to understand its limitations.


The research was conducted using preclinical models rather than human clinical trials. Although the results are promising, they do not prove that reducing fructose intake will prevent cancer recurrence.


Patients undergoing chemotherapy should continue following advice from their oncology team and should not make major dietary or medication changes based solely on this research.


Future clinical trials will be needed to determine whether limiting dietary fructose or targeting these signalling pathways can improve patient outcomes.


## Key takeaways


The study provides compelling evidence that fructose may do more than simply serve as a source of energy. It appears capable of acting as a biological messenger that helps surviving cancer cells spread after chemotherapy.


Researchers also identified a potential link between fructose, cholesterol regulation inside tumour cells, and cancer metastasis. These discoveries open new opportunities for developing treatments that interrupt this process.


Although much more research is required, the findings highlight the complex relationship between nutrition, metabolism, and cancer biology.


For now, maintaining a balanced diet, following medical advice, and attending regular follow-up appointments remain the most effective strategies for cancer patients.
