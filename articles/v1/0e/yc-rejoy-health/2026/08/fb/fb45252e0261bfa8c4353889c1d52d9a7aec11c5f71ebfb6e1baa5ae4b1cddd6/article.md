# Why Andy Burnham Should Reconsider UK International Aid Cuts

UK aid cuts could have consequences for global health, conflict prevention, British influence and humanitarian efforts. Here are four reasons Andy Burnham should reconsider the policy.


The United Kingdom is facing difficult choices over public spending. With pressure on the NHS, education, transport, social care, defence and household finances, governments are being asked to deliver more while keeping borrowing and taxation under control.


Against that backdrop, reducing international aid can appear to be a straightforward way of finding money for domestic priorities. However, the decision to cut the UK's overseas development budget carries consequences that are not always immediately visible.


Prime Minister Andy Burnham has inherited a particularly challenging fiscal environment. Using international development funding to support domestic commitments may provide short-term financial flexibility, but critics argue that the longer-term costs could affect Britain's security, international influence and ability to respond to global crises.


Here are four major reasons why the government should reconsider further reductions in UK aid.


## 1. Global health threats do not respect national borders


The Covid-19 pandemic demonstrated how quickly a health emergency in one part of the world can become an international crisis. Disease surveillance, laboratory capacity and early intervention are therefore not simply charitable concerns. They can also form part of Britain's own public health protection.


International programmes have helped poorer countries improve their ability to detect infectious diseases, strengthen health systems and respond to outbreaks. Reductions in these programmes could make it harder to identify emerging threats before they spread internationally.


The UK's withdrawal from the Pandemic Fund has attracted criticism from scientists and public health experts. Concerns have also been raised about reductions affecting efforts against antimicrobial resistance, a growing global problem that threatens to make previously treatable infections much harder to manage.


For Britain, investing in global disease prevention can therefore be viewed as a form of prevention rather than merely expenditure overseas. A comparatively modest investment in early detection and preparedness could potentially reduce the enormous economic and human costs associated with another major pandemic.


In a world where international travel and trade are routine, the UK's health security cannot be separated completely from the health security of other countries.


## 2. Development spending can support conflict prevention


Military strength is an important part of national security, but it is not the only tool available to governments.


Development programmes can address some of the conditions that contribute to instability, including poverty, weak institutions, displacement and lack of economic opportunity. Diplomacy and development can also complement military efforts in fragile states.


Cuts to programmes dealing with conflict prevention, extremism and stabilisation have therefore raised concerns among people who argue that development funding should be considered part of Britain's broader security strategy.


The consequences can extend beyond the countries receiving assistance. Where conflict continues or populations cannot safely return home, displacement can increase, creating additional humanitarian and political pressures.


Demining provides another example. Organisations involved in removing landmines and unexploded weapons often employ local workers while drawing on specialist international expertise. Reducing their activities can affect employment, civilian safety and the ability of displaced communities to return home.


The argument is not that every aid programme should automatically be protected from scrutiny. Instead, policymakers should consider whether cutting preventative programmes today could create much greater costs tomorrow.


## 3. UK aid cuts could weaken Britain's global influence


Foreign aid is also closely connected to diplomacy.


Britain has historically used international development assistance as one way of building relationships with governments, communities and institutions around the world. Long-term partnerships can help create opportunities for trade, diplomacy and cooperation.


Africa is particularly important in this context. Many African economies are growing rapidly, and their political and commercial importance is increasing. If Britain substantially reduces bilateral assistance, other countries may have greater opportunities to deepen their own relationships across the continent.


China, in particular, has expanded its economic and diplomatic presence in many developing countries. If the UK wants to remain an influential international partner, it needs to consider how reductions in aid could affect its ability to build lasting relationships.


There is also a reputational issue. Britain has traditionally promoted itself as a major international actor with a strong commitment to humanitarian assistance and development. A significant reduction in aid spending may make it harder for the UK to maintain that reputation.


Soft power cannot easily be measured in the same way as defence spending, but its effects can be significant. Trust, relationships and diplomatic credibility can take years to build and can be difficult to restore once lost.


## 4. The humanitarian consequences could be severe


The most immediate argument against major aid reductions concerns the people who depend directly on international development programmes.


Funding supports projects involving maternal health, child survival, education, food security, women's rights, disease prevention and humanitarian assistance. When budgets are reduced, programmes may have to close or serve fewer people.


Women and girls are particularly vulnerable when development funding disappears. Projects supporting women peacebuilders, preventing gender-based violence and protecting girls from practices such as female genital mutilation can all be affected by funding decisions.


Cuts to programmes designed to prevent avoidable deaths among women, babies and children are especially significant. These initiatives often focus on basic healthcare and interventions that can save lives at relatively low cost.


Research cited in the debate over Britain's aid policy has suggested that maintaining the UK's development spending at the previous target of 0.7 per cent of gross national income could have contributed to millions of lives being saved over the decade.


Such estimates should be considered carefully, but they illustrate the scale of the potential consequences.


## A difficult decision for Andy Burnham


Andy Burnham's government faces genuine financial constraints, and public spending decisions cannot ignore domestic pressures. People want better public services, improved transport, stronger healthcare and greater economic security.


However, the debate should not be framed simply as overseas aid versus spending at home.


International development can also serve Britain's long-term interests by supporting global health security, reducing instability, maintaining diplomatic relationships and preventing humanitarian emergencies from becoming larger crises.


The key question is therefore whether cutting aid provides genuine savings once these wider consequences are taken into account.


As Britain prepares to engage with other major economies through international forums such as the G20, the government has an opportunity to reconsider its approach. A more strategic aid policy could focus resources on programmes that deliver measurable humanitarian and security benefits while ensuring taxpayers receive value for money.


Rather than treating international development as an easy source of savings, ministers could view it as part of a wider national strategy.


The UK's influence abroad, security at home and reputation internationally are closely connected. Decisions made about aid today could shape all three for years to come.
