# Early Menopause May Raise the Risk of High Blood Pressure, New Study Finds

Menopause is a natural stage of life that marks the end of a woman's reproductive years. While most women experience menopause between the ages of 45 and 55, some go through it much earlier. According to new research, women who experience menopause before the age of 40 may face a greater risk of developing high blood pressure later in life.


The findings add to growing evidence that the timing of menopause plays an important role in long-term cardiovascular health. Researchers believe healthcare providers should consider early menopause as an important risk factor when evaluating a woman's heart health.


## What Is Premature Menopause?


Premature menopause occurs when a woman stops having menstrual periods before the age of 40. Early menopause refers to menopause that occurs between the ages of 40 and 45. Both conditions result in lower estrogen levels much earlier than expected, which may affect several aspects of health, including bone strength, brain function, and heart health.


Although genetics can influence the age of menopause, other factors such as autoimmune diseases, certain medical treatments, smoking, and some health conditions may also contribute.


## Study Links Early Menopause to High Blood Pressure


A recent study published in the journal *Menopause* examined whether the age at menopause influences the risk of developing hypertension, also known as high blood pressure.


Researchers analyzed health information from nearly 108,000 women enrolled in the UK Biobank, one of the world's largest long-term health research projects. Participants joined the study between 2006 and 2010 and were followed until the end of 2023.


The results revealed a clear pattern. As the age of menopause became younger, the likelihood of developing high blood pressure increased.


The study found:


- Nearly 23 percent of women with premature menopause had high blood pressure.
- Around 19 percent of women who experienced early menopause developed hypertension.
- Fewer than 17 percent of women who reached menopause after age 45 had high blood pressure.


After adjusting for other cardiovascular risk factors, women with premature menopause still had a 12 percent higher risk of hypertension compared with women who experienced menopause after age 45.


## Why the Risk May Be Higher


Although the study did not prove a direct cause and effect relationship, researchers believe declining estrogen levels may play an important role.


Estrogen helps maintain healthy blood vessels and supports normal blood pressure regulation. When estrogen levels fall years earlier than expected, the protective effects on the cardiovascular system may also decrease. Over time, this could contribute to higher blood pressure and an increased risk of heart disease.


Researchers also observed that the highest increase in hypertension risk was seen among women who entered menopause between the ages of 25 and 35. This suggests that cardiovascular risks may begin earlier than previously recognized for women with extremely early menopause.


## An Important Finding for Women's Heart Health


The research team concluded that age at menopause should be considered an independent cardiovascular risk factor during routine medical assessments.


Doctors already evaluate factors such as smoking, obesity, diabetes, cholesterol levels, and family history when estimating heart disease risk. The new findings suggest that menopause timing may deserve similar attention.


Identifying women at increased risk allows healthcare providers to recommend earlier blood pressure monitoring, healthier lifestyle changes, and preventive strategies before complications develop.


## Surgical Menopause Showed Different Results


Interestingly, the researchers found one notable exception.


Women who experienced menopause because of surgical removal of their ovaries did not show the same increased risk of high blood pressure after accounting for other health factors.


While additional research is needed to understand why this difference exists, the findings suggest that natural premature menopause and surgically induced menopause may affect cardiovascular health differently.


## Importance of Regular Blood Pressure Screening


High blood pressure is often called the "silent killer" because it usually develops without noticeable symptoms. Left untreated, it can increase the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, and heart failure.


Women who experience menopause before age 40 may benefit from more frequent blood pressure checks, even if they feel healthy.


Routine screening allows doctors to detect elevated blood pressure early and recommend treatment when necessary.


## Can Hormone Therapy Help?


Medical experts continue to recommend hormone therapy for many women who experience premature menopause, provided there are no medical reasons to avoid it.


Hormone therapy helps replace estrogen that the body loses earlier than normal. In addition to reducing symptoms such as hot flashes and vaginal dryness, treatment may also provide benefits for bone health and potentially reduce some long-term health risks associated with early menopause.


The decision to begin hormone therapy should always be made in consultation with a qualified healthcare professional after evaluating individual medical history and personal risk factors.


## Ways to Support Heart Health After Early Menopause


Women who experience premature or early menopause can take several practical steps to protect their cardiovascular health.


Some healthy habits include:


- Monitor blood pressure regularly.
- Eat a heart healthy diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins.
- Stay physically active for at least 150 minutes each week.
- Maintain a healthy body weight.
- Avoid smoking and tobacco products.
- Limit alcohol intake.
- Manage stress through relaxation techniques and adequate sleep.
- Attend regular medical checkups and discuss any cardiovascular concerns with a healthcare provider.


These lifestyle measures benefit everyone, but they may be especially important for women who enter menopause at a younger age.


## What This Research Means


This large study strengthens the evidence that premature menopause is associated with a greater likelihood of developing high blood pressure. While more research is needed to understand the biological reasons behind this connection, the findings emphasize the importance of early cardiovascular screening and preventive care.


Women who experience menopause before age 40 should discuss their long-term heart health with their healthcare provider. Regular monitoring, healthy lifestyle choices, and appropriate medical treatment can help reduce future cardiovascular risks.


As researchers continue to explore the relationship between menopause and heart disease, one message is becoming increasingly clear. The age at which menopause occurs is more than a reproductive milestone. It may also offer valuable insight into a woman's future cardiovascular health.
