# Flavoured Vapes During Pregnancy: New Study Raises Concerns Over Vanillin and Early Development

A new laboratory study has raised concerns about a flavouring chemical commonly found in some e-cigarette liquids, particularly in relation to pregnancy and early human development.


Researchers have examined **vanillin** , a chemical widely used to create vanilla-like flavours in vaping products. Their findings suggest that exposure to the substance may interfere with processes that are important during the earliest stages of development. However, experts have stressed that the research was conducted using cells in a laboratory, rather than pregnant women, meaning the results should not be interpreted as proof that vaping causes the same effects in human pregnancies.


The study has nevertheless prompted calls for greater awareness, more research and clearer information about the chemicals contained in vaping products.


## What is vanillin and why is it used in vapes?


Vanillin is the principal compound responsible for the familiar flavour and aroma associated with vanilla. It is used in a wide range of food, cosmetic and consumer products, and it can also be added to e-cigarette liquids to create or enhance flavour.


Researchers led by Professor Prue Talbot at the University of California, Riverside, focused on vanillin because of its presence in some vaping liquids and its interaction with a cellular receptor known as TRPV4.


The research team exposed embryonic stem cells to different concentrations of vanillin to investigate how the chemical affected their behaviour.


According to the findings, very low concentrations were associated with changes in the ability of early-stage cells to develop into different cell types. At higher concentrations, the researchers observed cell death.


These observations are potentially important because early embryonic development depends on cells multiplying, specialising and organising themselves correctly.


## Why are the findings relevant to pregnancy?


Pregnancy involves a series of highly complex biological processes. During the earliest stages, embryonic cells gradually develop into the different tissues and organs needed for normal growth.


The researchers argued that some of the effects observed in their experiments occurred at concentrations that could potentially be relevant to exposure from vaping.


They therefore suggested that healthcare professionals should discuss the possible risks of vaping with people who are pregnant or planning to become pregnant.


The team also called for regulators to consider requiring e-cigarette manufacturers to provide more detailed information about the ingredients contained in their products.


This is particularly relevant because vaping liquids can contain multiple ingredients, including nicotine, flavouring chemicals and other substances. The exact composition can vary between products.


## Experts urge caution when interpreting the research


While the findings have attracted attention, specialists have emphasised that laboratory research involving isolated cells cannot automatically be translated into health outcomes for pregnant people.


Professor Jacob George, professor of cardiovascular medicine at the University of Dundee, described the research as a concerning pre-clinical finding that deserves further investigation. He also noted that more research is needed to establish whether laboratory observations correspond to measurable effects in humans.


Professor Marian Knight, professor of maternal and child population health at the University of Oxford, made a similar point.


She highlighted the difference between exposing cells directly to a chemical under laboratory conditions and the much more complicated process through which a developing embryo could encounter a substance following maternal vaping.


That distinction is crucial.


A laboratory experiment can identify a potential biological mechanism and provide an important warning signal. It cannot, on its own, establish that pregnant people who vape will experience the same biological effects or that their babies will suffer developmental problems.


## Does this mean vaping during pregnancy is proven to be dangerous?


The new study does **not** prove that vanillin in e-cigarettes causes developmental problems in human pregnancies.


Instead, it adds to a wider body of scientific concern about exposure to vaping chemicals during pregnancy.


It is also important to distinguish between smoking traditional cigarettes and using e-cigarettes. Cigarette smoking exposes the body to thousands of chemicals produced through combustion and is well established as a major health risk. E-cigarettes are generally considered less harmful than combustible tobacco for adult smokers who switch completely, but that does not mean they are harmless.


Pregnancy creates an additional consideration because substances inhaled by the mother can potentially affect the developing fetus.


The new research therefore raises an important question: should people be exposed to flavouring chemicals during pregnancy when the long-term consequences of many of these substances are not fully understood?


## Why flavouring chemicals deserve more research


Flavour is one of the major features that distinguishes vaping products from traditional cigarettes. Sweet, fruit, dessert and beverage-inspired flavours can make vaping more appealing, particularly to people who have never smoked.


At the same time, the safety of inhaling many flavouring compounds over long periods remains an area of scientific investigation.


A substance can be considered safe for one type of exposure without necessarily being safe when inhaled. Chemicals used in food, for example, are not automatically proven safe when heated and inhaled into the lungs.


This is one reason researchers have called for greater scrutiny of flavouring ingredients.


Better ingredient disclosure could also help researchers understand which chemicals are being used and allow consumers and healthcare professionals to make more informed decisions.


## What should pregnant people do?


Anyone who is pregnant or trying to conceive and is concerned about vaping should speak with a doctor, midwife, pharmacist or other qualified healthcare professional.


People should not interpret one laboratory study as evidence that a specific outcome will happen to them or their baby. Equally, the absence of definitive evidence about every vaping ingredient should not be taken as proof that exposure is risk-free.


For smokers who are pregnant, stopping smoking is particularly important. Healthcare professionals can provide advice about evidence-based ways to quit and can help people weigh the risks and benefits of different approaches.


The key message from the latest research is therefore one of caution rather than certainty.


## A developing area of scientific research


The vanillin findings highlight how much scientists are still learning about the chemicals used in modern vaping products.


Further studies will be needed to determine how vanillin behaves in the body after vaping, what concentrations can reach maternal blood, whether meaningful amounts can reach a developing fetus, and whether the cellular effects observed in laboratory experiments occur during real pregnancies.


Until those questions are answered, the findings should be viewed as an early warning signal rather than definitive evidence of harm.


For pregnant people, the safest approach is to discuss vaping and smoking with a healthcare professional rather than relying on assumptions about the safety of individual ingredients.


As vaping continues to evolve, research into flavouring chemicals may become increasingly important. The latest study adds to calls for transparency, careful regulation and stronger evidence about what happens when these substances are inhaled during pregnancy.
