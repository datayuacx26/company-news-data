# RFK Jr. Brings the “Real Food” Message to the Kitchen

U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is taking his campaign for a return to whole, minimally processed foods beyond policy announcements and into the kitchen. Through a new government-backed cooking series called *The Real Food Show* , Kennedy is working with professional chefs to promote affordable meals made with everyday ingredients.


The series is part of the Trump administration’s broader “Make America Healthy Again” agenda, which places greater emphasis on nutrition, home cooking and reducing Americans’ reliance on highly processed foods. HHS describes the program as an effort to make healthy cooking practical, accessible and affordable for families.


There is, however, an important detail to clarify about the show's launch. The premiere did not feature celebrity chef Geoffrey Zakarian preparing a French omelet with Kennedy. According to HHS, the first episode featured chef Andrew Gruel, who joined Kennedy to prepare crispy salmon cakes with an apple, white bean and greens salad. HHS says the recipe costs approximately $5 per serving when made with frozen wild salmon, and it can cost even less when canned salmon is used.


Geoffrey Zakarian is connected to the broader initiative. The chef appeared in promotional material for *The Real Food Show* , teasing a future appearance. He has also been involved in the administration’s effort to improve food served in hospitals. In July, Zakarian joined Kennedy and Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins at Tampa General Hospital, where he helped promote a healthier approach to hospital meals.


## Turning Nutrition Policy Into Everyday Meals


The concept behind *The Real Food Show* is straightforward. Rather than discussing nutrition only in terms of government guidelines, Kennedy and his culinary guests demonstrate how those principles can be applied at home.


HHS says every recipe featured on the show is designed around whole-food ingredients, simple preparation and affordability. The recipes are intended to work in ordinary home kitchens without specialized equipment. The department also says the meals are designed to cost less than $5 per serving whenever possible.


That approach reflects the federal government’s updated Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025 to 2030. Released in January, the guidelines place a strong emphasis on what the administration calls “real food.” They encourage Americans to eat more vegetables and fruits, prioritize protein, choose whole grains, consume healthy fats from whole-food sources and reduce highly processed foods, added sugars and artificial additives.


The cooking-show format gives those recommendations a more familiar setting. Instead of presenting a list of nutritional targets, the program shows viewers how ingredients can become an affordable meal.


## Why Ultra-Processed Foods Are Central to the Message


Kennedy has made criticism of highly processed foods a major part of his public health agenda. His administration argues that Americans should move toward meals built around recognizable ingredients rather than products containing long lists of additives and refined ingredients.


That message is also appearing in other HHS initiatives. In July, HHS and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services launched the “Make Hospital Food Healthier” pledge, encouraging hospitals to reduce highly processed foods and emphasize nutrient-dense options. The initiative calls for limiting processed meats and foods high in added sugars, sodium and artificial additives while encouraging whole grains and minimally processed proteins.


The strategy is therefore larger than a television-style cooking program. The administration is attempting to make food and nutrition part of a broader public health conversation, from hospital cafeterias to family kitchens.


## The Farming Connection


The administration’s food policy also extends beyond what consumers put on their plates. In June, President Donald Trump signed an executive order aimed at advancing regenerative agriculture and strengthening American farm resilience.


The order directs the Agriculture Department to expand its regenerative agriculture pilot program and develop partnerships that can help farmers adopt regenerative practices. It also calls for greater research into agricultural chemicals and technologies that could reduce reliance on conventional crop-protection tools.


That means the “real food” message is being presented as part of a larger food-system argument. Supporters of the approach say improving health requires attention not only to individual eating habits but also to farming practices, food production and the availability of affordable ingredients.


## Affordability Remains a Major Challenge


One of the most important themes in *The Real Food Show* is cost. Advising people to eat healthier can be difficult when healthier ingredients are perceived as more expensive or less convenient than packaged alternatives.


By highlighting recipes that can be prepared for around $5 per serving, HHS is attempting to challenge that perception. The salmon cake recipe featured in the premiere, for example, combines salmon with beans, greens and fruit to create a complete meal without relying on expensive specialty ingredients.


Whether every family will be able to reproduce those prices depends on local food costs, household size and shopping habits. Still, the focus on budget-friendly cooking addresses a practical obstacle that often gets overlooked in conversations about healthy eating.


## A Political Message Served With Dinner


There is also a political dimension to the project. *The Real Food Show* is explicitly connected to the Trump administration’s “Make America Healthy Again” agenda. That makes the series more than a conventional celebrity cooking program.


For Kennedy, the kitchen provides another platform for communicating his health priorities. The use of professional chefs gives the message a practical and accessible format, while the recipes provide viewers with something they can potentially try themselves.


Geoffrey Zakarian’s involvement adds another layer to that strategy. As a prominent chef and television personality, his participation brings culinary credibility and entertainment value to a campaign that otherwise might be viewed primarily through a political lens.


Ultimately, the success of the initiative will depend less on the celebrity appearances and more on whether Americans find the recipes useful, affordable and enjoyable enough to make regularly.


The central idea is simple: cooking more meals at home, choosing minimally processed ingredients and making nutritious food affordable could have meaningful benefits for families. *The Real Food Show* is the administration’s latest attempt to turn that idea from a policy slogan into something people can experience at the dinner table.
