# PFAS Fears Grow in Thornton-Cleveleys as Residents Seek Answers

Concerns about environmental contamination are growing in Thornton-Cleveleys, Lancashire, where residents are undergoing blood tests after PFAS chemicals were found in soil around a former chemical manufacturing site. The situation has raised questions about historic industrial emissions, possible exposure through homegrown food, and whether local residents have received enough information about the potential risks.


The concerns centre on PFOA, a type of PFAS commonly described as a “forever chemical” because it can persist in the environment and the human body. According to reports, soil testing has identified PFOA at 71 properties surrounding the former ICI site, now operated by AGC Chemicals Europe Ltd.


## Residents Seek Answers Through Blood Testing


Around 56 residents are reportedly taking part in blood testing as they seek greater clarity about their possible exposure. For people who have lived close to the former industrial site for years, the discovery has created understandable anxiety.


Some residents had used their gardens to grow fruit and vegetables, while others kept animals and consumed products such as eggs. Andrew Molloy, whose garden sits close to the factory boundary, said he had regularly eaten strawberries and herbs grown at his property.


The issue is particularly sensitive for families who believed they were producing healthy food at home. Sam Linacre-Hammond and her son have undergone blood testing, with Sam describing the experience as extremely distressing. She said residents want answers about the potential impact of contamination on their families.


The situation illustrates why environmental contamination can have effects that extend beyond the immediate discovery of chemicals in soil. When people have lived in an area for decades, questions naturally arise about what they may have been exposed to through food, soil, water or air.


## What Are PFAS?


PFAS refers to a large group of chemicals that have been used in many industrial and consumer applications. PFOA, the chemical identified in the Thornton-Cleveleys investigation, is one member of this group.


These substances are known for their persistence, which is why PFAS are often called “forever chemicals.” The report states that PFAS can accumulate in blood and have been associated with health concerns including cancer, immune system effects and high cholesterol.


However, it is important to distinguish between the presence of PFAS in a person's blood and proof that the chemicals caused a particular illness. A blood test can indicate exposure, but it cannot establish exactly where that exposure came from or predict an individual's future health problems.


A multi-agency response has stressed this point, explaining that PFAS exposure can come from many sources, including food, drinking water, air and everyday products. It also noted that measurable PFAS levels are likely to be found in many people in the UK.


## Historic Industrial Activity Under Investigation


The contamination investigation began after information obtained by the Environment Agency indicated historic PFOA emissions from the former ICI site. The investigation, involving the council and Environment Agency, was launched in 2024.


The report states that the chemical was believed to have been released from the site over several decades, from the 1950s until it was phased out in 2012. At the time, the substance was reportedly being used in accordance with UK legislation, although it was subsequently classified as carcinogenic.


This history highlights one of the difficulties associated with long-term industrial contamination. Chemicals and industrial practices that were permitted or considered acceptable in the past can later become subject to greater scrutiny as scientific understanding develops.


For communities living close to former industrial sites, this can leave difficult questions about historic exposure and environmental responsibility.


## Guidance for Residents Has Changed


Residents were warned in 2024 not to eat produce grown in their gardens. Earlier guidance had suggested that washing and peeling homegrown produce could help remove potentially contaminated soil or dust.


Two local allotments have since been closed, adding another visible consequence for residents who rely on community growing spaces.


The report also states that most properties tested did not require further action, while seven properties were classified as high risk and required additional investigation.


These findings show why environmental investigations need to consider individual locations rather than assuming that every property in a wider area carries the same level of risk.


## Debate Over PFAS Testing


One of the biggest points of disagreement is whether residents should have access to PFAS blood testing.


Some residents believe testing is important because they would rather understand their exposure than remain uncertain. A legal team involved in PFAS investigations has been providing testing to residents, arguing that the results could help people better understand their individual exposure.


Authorities, however, have taken a more cautious position. The multi-agency response says that PFAS biomonitoring cannot identify the source of exposure and cannot predict future health problems. It therefore states that such testing is not currently recommended through the NHS or private blood tests.


This difference in perspective creates a difficult situation. Residents may see testing as a way of gaining information, while health authorities may be concerned that results could cause anxiety without providing clear medical answers.


## What Happens Next?


For residents, the investigation is ultimately about obtaining reliable information. Soil contamination has already been identified at numerous properties, while further testing is being carried out to understand exposure among people living in the area.


Results from the community blood-testing programme are expected to become available during autumn, according to the report.


Meanwhile, AGC Chemicals Europe has announced that it is consulting employees and their representatives about a proposal to cease operations at the manufacturing plant. The company has said that no final decision has been made and that the site has experienced losses in recent years.


For Thornton-Cleveleys residents, the priority remains understanding what happened, determining the extent of contamination and receiving clear information about what the findings mean.


The story also demonstrates the broader challenge posed by PFAS contamination. These chemicals can remain in the environment for long periods, while their presence may raise questions that cannot be answered immediately. As investigations continue, transparent communication between residents, regulators, health authorities and industry will be essential.
