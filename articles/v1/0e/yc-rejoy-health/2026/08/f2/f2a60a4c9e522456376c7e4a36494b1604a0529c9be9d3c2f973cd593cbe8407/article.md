# UK Baby Makes Medical History With First In-Womb Surgery for Rare Gastroschisis Condition

A UK baby has become the first from Britain to undergo pioneering womb surgery for gastroschisis, a rare birth condition where intestines develop outside the body. The procedure could transform future treatment options.


A baby boy from the UK has made medical history after becoming the first British child to receive pioneering surgery for a rare birth condition while still inside the womb.


The groundbreaking procedure was carried out in the United States and could represent a major step forward in the treatment of gastroschisis, a condition where a baby’s intestines develop outside the body during pregnancy.


The baby, named Theo, underwent the experimental operation before birth after doctors identified the condition during pregnancy. His mother, Maisie Savage, 29, travelled from London to Texas when she was 26 weeks pregnant so her unborn child could take part in a specialist clinical trial.


The operation was performed at Texas Children’s Hospital through an international collaboration involving Great Ormond Street Hospital (GOSH) in London and KU Leuven in Belgium. Medical experts believe the technique could eventually change the way severe cases of gastroschisis are treated around the world.


## What Is Gastroschisis?


Gastroschisis is a rare congenital condition that affects the development of a baby’s abdominal wall. During pregnancy, an opening forms near the belly button, allowing the intestines to grow outside the baby’s body.


In many cases, gastroschisis is detected during the routine 20 week pregnancy scan. Around one in every 3,000 babies born in the UK is affected by the condition each year.


Currently, babies with gastroschisis usually receive surgery shortly after birth. Doctors carefully place the intestines back inside the abdomen and close the opening. While many babies recover well, some experience serious complications.


Severe cases can involve damaged intestines, repeated surgeries, long hospital stays, feeding difficulties, and extended periods of medical care. In some situations, the condition can become life threatening.


Researchers have been exploring whether treating gastroschisis before birth could improve outcomes and reduce the challenges faced by newborn babies and their families.


## A New Approach to Foetal Surgery


Theo’s operation used a technique inspired by successful foetal procedures developed for other conditions, including spina bifida.


During the surgery, doctors carefully accessed the womb and worked to reposition Theo’s intestines back into his body before birth. The aim was to protect the developing organs and reduce complications that can occur when intestines remain exposed to amniotic fluid for a long period.


The procedure was carried out as part of a clinical trial designed to understand whether this approach could become a future treatment option for babies with complex gastroschisis.


Professor Paolo De Coppi, a paediatric surgery specialist at the UCL GOSH Institute of Child Health and consultant at Great Ormond Street Hospital, said the early results were encouraging.


Experts involved in the research hope that, if the trial continues to show positive results, this type of surgery could eventually become available to selected families in the UK.


## Theo’s Journey After Surgery


Following the successful operation, Theo remained in the womb until he reached full term. He was born in Texas in February and has continued to make strong progress.


His mother described him as a “little miracle” after the challenging journey her family experienced.


Theo is currently one of only a small number of babies worldwide to have received this type of treatment before birth. His case provides researchers with valuable information about whether prenatal surgery could offer better outcomes for children diagnosed with severe gastroschisis.


Medical teams involved in the project say Theo is doing remarkably well, giving hope to other families who may face a similar diagnosis in the future.


## Could This Become a Standard Treatment?


The success of Theo’s surgery has created optimism among specialists in maternal and child healthcare.


If further research confirms that the procedure is safe and effective, doctors believe it could eventually become an important option for babies with complex forms of gastroschisis.


Professor De Coppi explained that previous advances in foetal surgery, particularly for spina bifida, have shown that operating before birth can sometimes significantly improve a child’s health outcomes.


The long term goal is to help babies affected by gastroschisis begin life with fewer complications, reduce the need for multiple operations, and allow families to spend less time in hospital after birth.


However, experts have stressed that more research is needed before the procedure can become widely available. Clinical trials must continue to evaluate the safety, effectiveness, and potential risks involved.


## A Major Moment for UK Medical Research


Although the surgery took place overseas, the involvement of Great Ormond Street Hospital highlights the important role of UK specialists in international medical innovation.


The collaboration between hospitals in the UK, United States, and Belgium demonstrates how global partnerships can help develop new treatments for rare conditions.


For families receiving a gastroschisis diagnosis, advances like this offer renewed hope and show how quickly medical science continues to evolve.


Theo’s story represents more than an individual medical achievement. It is part of a wider effort to improve care for unborn babies and create better treatment pathways for future generations.


As research continues, this pioneering procedure could mark the beginning of a new chapter in the management of rare birth conditions.
