# Pesticide Exposure and ALS Risk: What a Major Study Reveals About a Possible Environmental Link

## New research explores the connection between pesticides and ALS


A major scientific review has raised fresh concerns about the possible connection between pesticide exposure and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), a devastating neurological disease that gradually destroys the nerve cells responsible for movement.


The study suggests that people who regularly work with pesticides may face a significantly higher risk of developing ALS compared with those who have little or no exposure. Researchers found that occupational exposure to pesticides was linked with an increased risk of the disease, with some groups showing risk levels up to 70 percent higher.


ALS, also known as Lou Gehrig’s disease, is a progressive condition that affects motor neurons. These specialised nerve cells control voluntary muscle movement, including walking, speaking, swallowing and breathing. As these cells deteriorate, patients often experience increasing weakness, loss of independence and severe disability.


Although ALS remains relatively uncommon, its impact can be life changing. Thousands of people in the United States are living with the condition, and many patients experience rapid progression after diagnosis. In many cases, survival is measured in only a few years, although some people live much longer.


## What the latest ALS pesticide study found


Scientists from the Robert Sauvé Institute for Research in Quebec reviewed existing research examining the relationship between pesticide exposure and ALS.


The research team analysed data from previous studies and selected 11 studies involving more than 2,000 people diagnosed with ALS. Their findings indicated that individuals exposed to pesticides through their work were around 60 percent more likely to develop ALS compared with people who had not experienced similar exposure.


The researchers also found a stronger association among people exposed mainly to herbicides, which are chemicals designed to kill unwanted plants and weeds. This group showed a potential increase in ALS risk of approximately 70 percent.


Even lower levels of pesticide exposure were associated with a possible rise in risk, although scientists stressed that more research is needed to fully understand the relationship.


The study also reported that the association appeared stronger among men when researchers examined male and female participants separately. However, the reasons behind this difference remain unclear and require further investigation.


## Why pesticides are being studied as a possible ALS risk factor


The exact cause of ALS is still unknown in most cases. Around 10 percent of cases are believed to involve inherited genetic factors, while the majority appear to result from a complex combination of biological and environmental influences.


Scientists have increasingly investigated environmental factors that may contribute to ALS development. These include chemical exposure, pollution, lifestyle factors and workplace hazards.


Pesticides are one area of particular interest because they contain chemicals designed to affect living organisms. Farmers, agricultural workers and others who handle these products regularly may experience higher exposure levels than the general population.


Some pesticides have already been linked to concerns about neurological health. For example, paraquat, a herbicide used in agriculture, has been the subject of scientific debate because of possible connections with certain neurological conditions. The chemical is restricted or banned in many countries but remains approved for use in some regions.


Another pesticide, chlordane, was previously used for termite control before being banned in the United States due to health concerns. Earlier research has investigated possible links between chlordane exposure and neurological diseases, including ALS.


## Experts urge caution when interpreting the findings


While the study provides important information, researchers and outside experts say the results should be interpreted carefully.


The review identified an association between pesticide exposure and ALS risk, but it does not prove that pesticides directly cause the disease. Many factors can influence health outcomes, and studies based on past exposure records can have limitations.


Dr Jonathan Cooper-Knock from the University of Sheffield’s School of Medicine and Population Health noted that the research was valuable because it combined information from multiple studies involving thousands of participants. However, he also highlighted that measuring past pesticide exposure can be difficult.


Researchers often rely on workplace histories, questionnaires or estimates of exposure rather than direct measurements taken over many years. This means other factors connected with pesticide use could potentially contribute to the observed association.


Future research will need to examine specific chemicals, exposure levels, genetic factors and biological changes that may explain why some people develop ALS while others do not.


## Protecting workers from potential pesticide risks


The study authors have called for stronger measures to reduce pesticide exposure, particularly among people who use these chemicals as part of their jobs.


Agricultural workers and others who handle pesticides can reduce exposure by following safety guidance, using appropriate protective equipment and following product instructions carefully.


Workplace safety policies, improved training and continued research into safer alternatives may also help reduce possible health risks.


At the same time, experts emphasise that pesticides remain important tools in modern agriculture when used responsibly. The current evidence does not suggest that occasional exposure from normal daily activities automatically leads to ALS.


## ALS continues to challenge researchers worldwide


ALS has received increased public attention following diagnoses among well-known figures, including former professional athletes and actors. These cases have highlighted how the disease can affect people who appear healthy and active before symptoms begin.


Despite decades of research, scientists still do not fully understand why ALS develops. The condition likely involves multiple causes rather than one single trigger.


The new pesticide research adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that environmental exposures may play a role in some cases. However, scientists say continued investigation is essential before firm conclusions can be made.


Understanding possible risk factors could eventually help researchers develop better prevention strategies, earlier detection methods and improved treatments for people living with ALS.
