# Can You Get Seasonal Affective Disorder in Summer? Symptoms, Causes, and How to Cop

Most people associate **Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)** with dark winter mornings and shorter days. However, fewer people realize that some individuals experience the opposite pattern. Known as **summer-onset Seasonal Affective Disorder** or **reverse SAD** , this condition can trigger low mood, anxiety, irritability, and sleep problems during the warmest months of the year.


While sunshine is often linked to happiness, summer does not improve everyone's mental wellbeing. For some, longer days, rising temperatures, disrupted routines, and increased social expectations can have the opposite effect.


If you find yourself feeling emotionally drained when everyone else seems excited about summer, you are not alone. Understanding reverse SAD can help you recognize the signs and take practical steps to protect your mental health.


## What Is Summer Seasonal Affective Disorder?


Seasonal Affective Disorder is a type of depression that follows a seasonal pattern. While winter SAD is much more common, **summer-onset SAD** affects a smaller number of people whose symptoms appear during spring or summer and improve once autumn arrives.


Mental health professionals believe that changes in routine, temperature, sleep quality, and environmental factors can all contribute to this seasonal shift in mood. The condition varies from person to person, and symptoms can range from mild emotional changes to more significant disruptions in daily life.


## Common Symptoms of Reverse SAD


Summer Seasonal Affective Disorder may include:


- Persistent low mood
- Increased anxiety
- Irritability
- Difficulty sleeping
- Fatigue despite longer daylight hours
- Loss of interest in enjoyable activities
- Trouble concentrating
- Feeling overwhelmed by social events
- Restlessness or agitation


These symptoms can interfere with work, relationships, and overall wellbeing, especially if they continue for several weeks.


## Why Can Summer Make Some People Feel Worse?


Although research is still evolving, mental health experts believe several factors may contribute to reverse SAD.


### 1. Increased Social Pressure


Summer often comes with expectations of holidays, festivals, barbecues, and outdoor gatherings. Social media can amplify the idea that everyone should be enjoying themselves.


When your emotional state does not match these expectations, feelings of guilt, loneliness, or inadequacy may become stronger. Constant invitations and busy schedules can also leave people feeling exhausted instead of refreshed.


### 2. Changes to Daily Routine


Many families experience significant routine changes during school holidays. Parents may juggle childcare, vacations, or altered work schedules.


Even adults without children often experience changes in commuting, working hours, or social calendars during summer. Losing a predictable routine can affect emotional stability and increase stress.


### 3. Poor Sleep Caused by Heat


Warm nights can make it difficult to fall asleep or stay asleep. Poor sleep affects nearly every aspect of mental health.


Lack of quality rest may lead to:


- Increased irritability
- Reduced concentration
- Higher stress levels
- Lower emotional resilience
- Increased anxiety


Over time, chronic sleep disruption can worsen symptoms of depression and anxiety.


### 4. Dehydration


Many people underestimate how much hydration affects mood.


Even mild dehydration may contribute to:


- Fatigue
- Headaches
- Irritability
- Brain fog
- Reduced energy


Drinking enough water becomes especially important during hot weather when the body loses more fluids through sweating.


### 5. Childhood Memories and Expectations


For some people, summer brings nostalgia. Looking back on childhood holidays or time spent with loved ones may trigger sadness, particularly if important people are no longer present.


Parents may also feel pressure to recreate "perfect" summer experiences for their own children, adding emotional and financial stress.


### 6. Heat and Sensory Overload


Not everyone enjoys hot weather. High temperatures can feel physically uncomfortable and mentally exhausting.


People who are sensitive to sensory experiences may struggle with:


- Heat
- Humidity
- Sticky skin
- Bright sunlight
- Sunscreen textures
- Crowded outdoor spaces


These factors can increase discomfort and emotional distress.


### 7. Body Image Concerns


Summer clothing often reveals more of the body, which can increase self-consciousness.


Individuals living with body image concerns, eating disorders, or visible scars may find summer especially challenging. Feeling pressured to wear lighter clothing can increase anxiety and reduce confidence in social situations.


## How to Manage Summer Seasonal Affective Disorder


While reverse SAD can be difficult, there are practical strategies that may help reduce symptoms.


### 1. Slow Down Your Schedule


Avoid trying to attend every event or fill every weekend.


Give yourself permission to decline invitations and schedule downtime. Rest is productive when it supports your mental health.


### 2. Protect Your Sleep


Healthy sleep habits are essential.


Try to:


- Keep consistent bedtime and wake-up times
- Use blackout curtains
- Keep your bedroom cool
- Limit screen time before bed
- Use a fan if needed


Good sleep improves mood, concentration, and stress management.


### 3. Stay Connected Without Overcommitting


You do not need to attend every large gathering.


Smaller, quieter activities can feel more enjoyable, such as:


- Meeting one friend for coffee
- Cooking together at home
- Taking an evening walk
- Having a picnic in a shaded park


Meaningful connection matters more than a packed social calendar.


### 4. Exercise in Cooler Conditions


Physical activity supports both physical and mental health.


Swimming can be particularly helpful because it combines exercise with relief from the heat. Walking early in the morning or later in the evening can also make exercise more comfortable.


### 5. Drink Plenty of Water


Hydration supports both brain and body function.


Carry a reusable water bottle throughout the day and increase fluid intake during particularly hot weather or after exercise.


### 6. Build Recovery Time Into Your Week


Think about your personal limits before accepting plans.


Setting aside evenings or weekends for relaxation can help prevent emotional burnout. Reading, listening to music, gardening, or simply spending quiet time indoors may help you recharge.


### 7. Seek Professional Support When Needed


If symptoms persist for several weeks or begin affecting your work, relationships, or daily life, speak with a healthcare professional.


Consider seeking support if you notice:


- Ongoing sleep problems
- Persistent sadness
- Increased anxiety
- Social withdrawal
- Loss of interest in normal activities
- Feelings of hopelessness


Early intervention often leads to better outcomes.


## When Should You Be Concerned?


Everyone experiences occasional low moods, especially during stressful periods. However, if emotional changes continue for more than two to four weeks, become more severe, or interfere with normal life, professional support is recommended.


Mental health conditions are treatable, and you do not have to wait until symptoms become overwhelming before asking for help.


## Final Thoughts


Summer is often portrayed as the happiest season of the year, but that experience is not universal. Reverse Seasonal Affective Disorder is a real condition that can affect mood, sleep, and overall wellbeing despite bright sunshine and longer days.


Recognizing the signs, maintaining healthy routines, prioritizing sleep, staying hydrated, and giving yourself permission to slow down can make a meaningful difference. If symptoms continue or worsen, reaching out to a qualified mental health professional is an important step toward feeling better.
