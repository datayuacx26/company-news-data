# UK Gel Nail Ban 2026: What the TPO Rules Mean for Gel Manicures

The UK gel nail ban targets TPO, an ingredient used in some gel polishes. Find out when the rules take effect, why TPO is banned and what salons will use instead.


If you enjoy long-lasting gel manicures, recent changes to UK cosmetic regulations may have caught your attention. Headlines about a "gel nail ban" have caused concern among manicure fans, but the reality is more specific.


The new UK rules do not ban gel nails altogether. Instead, they restrict the use and sale of products containing a particular chemical called trimethylbenzoyl diphenylphosphine oxide, better known as TPO.


The ingredient has been used in some gel nail products because it helps polish cure when exposed to ultraviolet light. With the new rules now taking effect, both consumers and nail technicians are understandably asking what happens next.


## What is the UK gel nail ban?


The phrase "gel nail ban" is somewhat misleading. The regulation does not prohibit gel manicures or all gel nail polish.


Instead, the change concerns TPO, a photoinitiator used in some nail products. Photoinitiators help gel polish harden when it is exposed to UV or LED light during a manicure.


According to information reported by The Independent, the UK restrictions prevent manufacturers from placing new TPO-containing products on the market. The rules apply in England, Scotland and Wales.


This means customers can still have gel manicures, provided the products being used comply with the relevant regulations.


## When does the TPO ban take effect?


The UK restrictions have already begun. The latest rules prevent manufacturers from selling new cosmetic products containing TPO.


However, salons have a longer transition period.


According to the source article, nail salons have until **14 February 2027** to stop using existing TPO-containing products. This means some salons may continue using their remaining stock before the deadline, but they cannot replenish their supplies with newly sold TPO products.


The approach gives businesses time to move towards alternative formulations and update their professional product ranges.


For customers, this means the availability of certain gel polishes may vary between salons during the transition period.


## Why has TPO been banned?


The regulatory change is linked to concerns about the potential reproductive and fertility effects associated with TPO.


The ingredient has been classified as a substance with hazardous properties following evidence from animal studies. These findings contributed to restrictions on its use in cosmetics in the European Union.


However, the safety discussion is more complicated than simply saying that gel manicures containing TPO are dangerous.


The Cosmetic, Toiletry and Perfumery Association has argued that the concentration used in nail products is significantly lower than levels associated with harmful effects in studies. It has also pointed out that gel products are applied to the nail rather than directly consumed.


This distinction is important for consumers. A regulatory ban does not necessarily mean that every previous use of the ingredient presented an immediate danger to everyone who had a gel manicure.


Instead, cosmetic regulations can take a precautionary approach when an ingredient meets certain hazard classifications.


## Does this mean gel manicures are no longer available?


No. Gel manicures are not disappearing from UK salons.


The key difference is that salons will need to use compliant products that do not contain TPO once the transition period ends.


Many manufacturers already offer TPO-free gel systems, meaning customers are unlikely to need to abandon gel manicures completely.


For people who prefer gel polish because it lasts longer than conventional nail varnish, this is potentially good news. The main change may be the formulation used by the salon rather than the overall manicure experience.


Customers can ask their nail technician whether the products being used are TPO-free and compliant with current UK cosmetic requirements.


## What will salons use instead?


There are already TPO-free gel polishes available on the market.


Manufacturers have been developing alternative formulations as regulations have changed in different parts of the world. Some salons may therefore have already switched to alternative systems, while others are using their existing stock during the transition period.


It is important to remember that replacing one chemical does not automatically mean that every alternative has exactly the same characteristics.


Different formulations can have different curing requirements, durability, application methods and safety profiles. Nail technicians should therefore follow the manufacturer's instructions carefully and use the appropriate lamp and curing process for the product.


For customers, the simplest approach is to choose a reputable salon and ask what product system it uses.


## What should gel manicure customers do?


There is no need to panic or assume that your next manicure has been cancelled.


If you regularly have gel nails, consider asking your salon three straightforward questions:


1. Does the gel product contain TPO?
2. Has the salon moved to a TPO-free formulation?
3. Will the new product require any changes to the manicure or removal process?


These questions can help you understand what is being applied to your nails.


It is also worth remembering that nail health involves more than the presence or absence of one ingredient. Incorrect application, inadequate curing, aggressive filing and poor removal techniques can all affect the condition of natural nails.


A trained technician should follow professional instructions and avoid unnecessary damage to the nail plate and surrounding skin.


## What does the ban mean for the beauty industry?


For salons and nail technicians, the biggest impact is likely to be the transition to alternative products.


Businesses may need to review their existing stock, speak to suppliers and update their product ranges before the February 2027 deadline. Training may also be necessary where new formulations have different application or curing requirements.


The change also highlights a wider issue within the beauty industry. Consumers increasingly want to know which chemicals are contained in cosmetics and why particular ingredients are being restricted.


For salon owners, clear communication could become particularly important. Customers who have heard about a "gel nail ban" may arrive expecting the treatment to disappear entirely.


Explaining that the regulation concerns TPO rather than gel manicures as a whole can help prevent unnecessary confusion.


## The bottom line


The UK's new gel nail rules do **not** mean the end of gel manicures.


The main change is the restriction on TPO, a photoinitiator used in some gel nail products. Manufacturers can no longer introduce new TPO-containing products under the relevant UK rules, while salons have until **14 February 2027** to stop using existing TPO-containing stock.


For customers, the most practical step is to ask their nail technician whether the products used are TPO-free and compliant with current regulations.


With alternative formulations already available, gel manicures are expected to remain an option for UK beauty customers. The biggest change is likely to happen behind the scenes, as salons and manufacturers replace affected products with compliant alternatives.
