# Breast Cancer Risk Checks May Miss 95% of High-Risk Women Under 50, Study Warns

## New research highlights gaps in current breast cancer referral guidelines


Breast cancer remains one of the most serious health challenges affecting women in the UK, particularly among younger women. New research from the University of Cambridge suggests that current referral guidelines used by GPs may fail to identify many women under the age of 50 who are at increased risk of developing breast cancer.


The study found that existing National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) family history referral rules may miss up to 95% of younger women who later develop breast cancer within a decade. Researchers say that improving early identification of women at higher risk could provide opportunities for earlier monitoring, personalised support, and potentially better outcomes.


## Current breast cancer referral system may overlook younger women


In England, women are usually referred for specialist breast cancer risk assessment when they meet specific NICE criteria. These guidelines mainly consider factors such as family history and known genetic risks.


However, researchers from the University of Cambridge analysed data from 1,258 women under 50 who participated in the Breast Cancer Now Generations Study between 2004 and 2011. The study examined whether current referral methods could accurately identify women who had a greater chance of developing breast cancer.


The findings suggested that NICE criteria identified only a small proportion of women who were later diagnosed with breast cancer. According to the research, the current approach would refer around 1.4% of women under 50 for additional assessment, including only 4.4% of those who later developed breast cancer.


Researchers compared this with a wider risk assessment approach called Boadicea, which considers multiple factors rather than relying mainly on family history.


## Risk prediction tools could improve early detection


The Boadicea model uses information including genetics, family history, reproductive history, and lifestyle factors to estimate an individual’s breast cancer risk.


Using this system, researchers identified eight times more women under 50 who developed breast cancer compared with the current referral approach. The model classified around 26.5% of women in the study group as having an above-average risk.


Importantly, it identified almost 35% of women who went on to develop breast cancer within ten years. Experts believe that tools like Boadicea could help healthcare professionals provide more personalised advice and support to women who may benefit from closer monitoring.


Dr Juliet Usher-Smith from the University of Cambridge said that healthcare providers need better methods for identifying women at the highest risk of breast cancer. She explained that earlier action could provide more options for treating or even preventing disease.


## Experts call for careful review of breast cancer guidelines


The findings have received attention from cancer researchers and charities, who say that improving risk identification could play an important role in reducing the impact of breast cancer.


Dr Simon Vincent, chief scientific officer at Breast Cancer Now, said that new approaches to identifying women at increased risk could help prevent some breast cancers or detect them earlier, when treatment is often more effective.


However, experts also highlighted challenges involved in changing the current system. A broader risk assessment approach may require additional NHS resources, including specialist staff, genetic testing capacity, and improved access to healthcare services.


Professor Montserrat Garcia-Closas from the Institute of Cancer Research in London said there would need to be a balance between improving accuracy and managing practical challenges. While existing NICE criteria are easier to apply, they may not identify many women who have elevated risk.


A wider assessment process could provide more detailed information but may require greater investment and careful planning.


## The importance of personalised breast cancer prevention


Breast cancer risk is influenced by many different factors. While family history and inherited genetic changes can increase risk, they are not the only contributors.


Lifestyle factors, reproductive history, age, and other personal health information can also affect an individual’s likelihood of developing breast cancer. This is why researchers are increasingly studying models that combine multiple types of information rather than focusing on one risk factor.


Dr Sowmiya Moorthie from Cancer Research UK said that exploring improved methods for identifying women at higher risk could support more personalised healthcare.


She also noted that any future changes must consider accessibility, fairness, and the possible emotional impact of additional referrals. Receiving a high-risk assessment may cause anxiety for some women, so healthcare systems would need to provide appropriate guidance and support.


## NICE response and future research


NICE has acknowledged the potential value of multifactorial risk assessment models but said that current evidence does not yet justify immediate changes to existing familial breast cancer guidelines.


The organisation stated that it will continue reviewing new evidence and consider future updates as more information becomes available about the effectiveness, costs, and practical impact of these approaches.


Further research will be needed to understand how risk prediction tools could be introduced across the NHS and whether they improve outcomes for women.


## What women should know about breast cancer risk


Women concerned about their breast cancer risk should speak with their GP, especially if they have a strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer, known genetic risks, or other concerns about their health.


Regular breast awareness and seeking medical advice about unusual changes remain important. Symptoms that may require medical attention include a new lump, changes in breast shape or size, skin changes, nipple changes, or unusual discharge.


Early detection can improve treatment options, but risk assessment and screening decisions should always be based on individual circumstances and professional medical advice.


## Conclusion


The University of Cambridge study highlights potential weaknesses in current breast cancer risk referral methods for women under 50. While existing NICE guidelines provide a practical system, researchers suggest that broader risk assessment tools could identify more women who may benefit from early support.


The challenge ahead will be finding a balance between improved detection, NHS resources, and ensuring that every woman has fair access to appropriate care.
