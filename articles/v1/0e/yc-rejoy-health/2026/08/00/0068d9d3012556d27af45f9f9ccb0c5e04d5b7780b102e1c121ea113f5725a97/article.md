# Why Period Poop Happens: Understanding Menstrual Digestive Issues and How to Manage Them

For many women, menstruation is about much more than cramps and mood swings. It often comes with an uncomfortable digestive surprise that has become widely known as "period poop." Whether it is diarrhea, stomach cramps, bloating, constipation, or an urgent need to use the bathroom, digestive changes during menstruation are surprisingly common.


Although millions of women experience these symptoms every month, the topic remains under-researched. Scientists are still uncovering exactly why menstrual cycles affect the digestive system and what can be done to ease the discomfort.


In this article, we explore why period poop happens, the science behind menstrual digestive symptoms, available treatment options, and practical ways to improve gut health during your cycle.


## What Is Period Poop?


"Period poop" is an informal term used to describe digestive changes that occur before or during menstruation. These symptoms can include:


- Diarrhea
- Frequent bowel movements
- Stomach cramps
- Bloating
- Gas
- Nausea
- Constipation
- Abdominal discomfort


Research suggests these symptoms are extremely common. One study found that approximately 73 percent of healthy premenopausal women experienced at least one significant gastrointestinal symptom before or during their menstrual cycle.


While diarrhea and abdominal pain are the most frequently reported issues, many women experience different digestive symptoms depending on the stage of their cycle.


## Why Does Your Digestive System Change During Your Period?


The answer lies in hormonal changes that occur throughout the menstrual cycle.


As menstruation approaches, levels of estrogen and progesterone decrease rapidly. This hormonal shift signals the body to begin shedding the uterine lining, resulting in menstrual bleeding.


At the same time, the uterus releases hormone-like compounds known as prostaglandins.


### Prostaglandins Affect More Than the Uterus


Prostaglandins help the uterus contract so it can expel its lining. However, these compounds do not only affect the reproductive system.


According to researchers, prostaglandins may enter the bloodstream and influence nearby organs, including the intestines. Since the digestive tract contains similar smooth muscle tissue, these chemicals may trigger stronger intestinal contractions.


This increased activity can lead to:


- Diarrhea
- Stomach cramping
- Increased bowel movements
- Urgency to use the bathroom


These digestive symptoms often peak during the first one or two days of menstruation when prostaglandin levels are highest.


## Can the Nervous System Play a Role?


Researchers are also exploring another possible explanation involving the vagus nerve.


The vagus nerve serves as a communication pathway between the brain and the digestive system. It is highly responsive to inflammation and stress.


When activated, this nerve can increase intestinal contractions and fluid secretion inside the gut. These changes may contribute to diarrhea and stomach discomfort.


Scientists believe menstruation may activate this pathway in some women, although additional research is needed to confirm exactly how this process works.


## Why Some Women Experience Constipation Before Their Period


Not every woman experiences diarrhea.


Some notice constipation during the week or two before menstruation begins. Researchers believe higher progesterone levels during this phase may slow bowel movements by relaxing smooth muscle throughout the digestive tract.


Although this theory is supported by some evidence, researchers say more studies are needed to determine exactly how common this symptom is and why it affects some women more than others.


## Who Is More Likely to Experience Severe Period Digestive Symptoms?


While digestive changes can happen to anyone during menstruation, certain medical conditions may make symptoms significantly worse.


Women with the following conditions often report more severe menstrual digestive issues:


- Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)
- Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)
- Endometriosis
- Crohn's disease
- Ulcerative colitis


However, even women without any diagnosed digestive disorder frequently experience period-related bowel changes.


Researchers emphasize that although these symptoms are common, they should not automatically be dismissed as something women simply have to tolerate.


## Can You Prevent Period Poop?


Unfortunately, there is currently no guaranteed solution. However, several approaches may help reduce symptoms.


### Stay Hydrated


Diarrhea can increase fluid loss, making dehydration more likely. Drinking plenty of water throughout your menstrual cycle helps maintain hydration and supports digestive function.


### Eat More Fiber


Nutrition experts recommend increasing fiber intake through foods such as:


- Fruits
- Vegetables
- Whole grains
- Beans
- Lentils


Fiber supports healthy digestion and may help regulate bowel movements throughout the menstrual cycle.


### Limit Greasy Foods


Many people crave fried or high-fat comfort foods during their period. Unfortunately, these foods may worsen digestive upset and diarrhea.


Choosing lighter, balanced meals may reduce gastrointestinal discomfort.


### Exercise Regularly


Although research is still limited, regular physical activity appears to help some women reduce bloating, constipation, and menstrual cramps.


Even gentle exercise such as walking or yoga may provide relief.


## Do Probiotics Help?


Researchers have begun investigating whether probiotics can improve menstrual digestive symptoms.


One randomized clinical trial found that probiotics improved symptoms of indigestion. However, they did not significantly reduce diarrhea, which remains the primary complaint for many women.


More research is needed before probiotics can be recommended as a reliable treatment for period poop.


## Can Birth Control Help?


Some healthcare providers suggest hormonal birth control because it reduces the growth of the uterine lining.


In theory, less uterine tissue should produce fewer prostaglandins, potentially reducing cramps and digestive symptoms.


However, current evidence has not confirmed that oral contraceptives consistently improve gastrointestinal symptoms during menstruation. Some women using birth control still experience period poop.


## Should You Take Ibuprofen or Naproxen?


Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and naproxen reduce prostaglandin production.


This can help relieve menstrual cramps and may also reduce intestinal muscle contractions.


However, these medications come with limitations.


NSAIDs can irritate the stomach and intestines, occasionally causing digestive upset or diarrhea. They may also worsen symptoms in people living with Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis.


Anyone with chronic digestive conditions should consult a healthcare professional before using these medications.


## When Should You See a Doctor?


Occasional digestive changes during menstruation are common.


However, you should seek medical advice if you experience:


- Severe abdominal pain
- Persistent diarrhea
- Blood in the stool
- Significant weight loss
- Symptoms that interfere with work or daily activities
- Digestive problems that continue long after your period ends


A healthcare provider can determine whether another medical condition is contributing to your symptoms.


## Why More Research Is Needed


Despite affecting millions of women worldwide, menstrual digestive health remains an understudied area of medicine.


Researchers say this reflects a broader lack of investment in women's health research. As a result, many women continue to experience uncomfortable symptoms without clear guidance or effective treatment options.


Greater awareness and increased scientific funding could improve understanding of period-related digestive disorders and lead to better therapies in the future.


## Final Thoughts


Period poop is real, common, and backed by scientific evidence. Hormonal changes, prostaglandins, and communication between the nervous system and digestive tract all appear to play important roles in why bowel habits change during menstruation.


Although there is currently no single cure, healthy eating, proper hydration, regular exercise, and speaking with a healthcare provider about persistent symptoms can help improve quality of life.


Most importantly, women should know that digestive symptoms during menstruation deserve attention and should not be ignored simply because they are common.
