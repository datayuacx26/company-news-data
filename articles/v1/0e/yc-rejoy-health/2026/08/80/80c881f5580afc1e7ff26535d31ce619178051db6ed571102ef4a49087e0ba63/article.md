# Three Midlife Health Habits That May Help Add More Dementia-Free Years, Study Suggests

## Protecting brain health may start decades before symptoms appear


Dementia is often viewed as a condition associated mainly with older age, but researchers continue to find that decisions made during middle age can have a major influence on future brain health. A new long-term study suggests that maintaining healthy blood pressure, avoiding diabetes, and not smoking between the ages of 45 and 65 may help people enjoy significantly more years of life without dementia.


The research found that people who avoided all three major risk factors experienced almost 13 more years without dementia compared with those who had all three risks. The findings add to growing evidence that protecting the heart and blood vessels can also protect the brain.


## Long-term research highlights the importance of midlife choices


Researchers from New York University Langone Health followed more than 12,000 adults who did not have dementia at the beginning of the study. Participants were assessed at around age 55 and monitored for an average of 26 years.


The study focused on three important health factors linked to dementia risk:


- High blood pressure
- Diabetes
- Smoking


During the follow-up period, more than 3,000 participants developed dementia, while around 5,200 people died without developing the condition.


Researchers compared dementia-free survival rates among people based on the number of risk factors they had. The results showed a clear difference between those who maintained healthier lifestyles and those who had multiple risk factors.


Participants with none of the three risk factors lived an average of 30.1 years without dementia after the study began. Those with one risk factor had around 26.3 dementia-free years, while people with two risk factors had approximately 21 years. Those with all three risk factors experienced about 17 years without dementia.


The difference between the healthiest group and those with all three risks was close to 13 years.


## Why blood pressure, diabetes, and smoking matter for the brain


The brain depends on a healthy blood supply to function properly. Conditions that damage blood vessels can also affect brain health over time.


High blood pressure can contribute to damage in the small blood vessels that supply the brain. This damage may increase the likelihood of problems affecting memory and thinking.


Diabetes can also influence dementia risk because high blood sugar levels may contribute to inflammation, blood vessel damage, and changes in brain function.


Smoking affects the body in many ways, including reducing oxygen supply, increasing inflammation, and damaging cardiovascular health. Since the brain relies heavily on a strong circulation system, smoking can have long-term consequences for cognitive health.


The study’s findings support the idea that preventing or controlling these conditions earlier in life could help delay the onset of dementia.


## Experts say heart health and brain health are closely connected


Experts have increasingly emphasized the connection between cardiovascular health and cognitive health.


Dr Josef Coresh, director of the Optimal Ageing Institute at NYU Langone, said the research suggests people should actively work to avoid these risk factors during middle age to help preserve brain health for many years.


Other researchers have highlighted that healthy habits such as regular physical activity, avoiding smoking, limiting excessive alcohol consumption, and staying socially connected may also support brain health.


Dr Jacqui Hanley from Alzheimer’s Research UK noted that while there is no guaranteed way to prevent dementia, reducing risks linked to poor cardiovascular health may help lower the chances of cognitive decline.


Professor Ian Maidment from Aston University said the findings add to previous research showing that what benefits the heart often benefits the brain as well.


## Women and dementia-free years


The study also identified differences between groups. Researchers found that women generally lived longer without dementia than men, regardless of the number of risk factors they had.


The research also reported differences in dementia-free years between racial groups, with white participants having more dementia-free years than black participants in the study population.


Researchers noted that dementia risk is influenced by many factors, including genetics, environment, healthcare access, and social conditions. These findings highlight the importance of continuing research into how different populations experience dementia risk and prevention.


## Simple steps that may support brain health


Although no lifestyle change can completely eliminate the possibility of dementia, experts recommend several actions that may support long-term brain health.


### Keep blood pressure under control


Regular health checks can help identify high blood pressure early. Managing blood pressure through lifestyle changes and medical advice when needed may reduce risks linked to vascular damage.


### Prevent or manage diabetes


Maintaining healthy blood sugar levels through balanced nutrition, physical activity, and appropriate medical care can support overall health and may help protect the brain.


### Avoid smoking


Stopping smoking or never starting can provide benefits for circulation, heart health, and brain health at any age.


### Stay physically active


Exercise supports cardiovascular health and may contribute to better cognitive function as people age.


### Maintain social connections


Relationships, social activities, and mental engagement may also play a role in supporting healthy ageing.


## Dementia prevention begins before old age


The latest research reinforces an important message: brain health is not only an issue for later life. The choices people make during their 40s, 50s, and 60s may influence how they age and how long they remain free from dementia.


While researchers continue to study the complex causes of dementia, reducing known risk factors remains one of the most practical steps individuals can take. Maintaining healthy blood pressure, avoiding diabetes, and not smoking may provide benefits that extend far beyond physical health.


Protecting the brain starts with protecting the body, and midlife may be a crucial opportunity to make changes that support healthier ageing.
