# Two Melbourne Children Suffer Kidney Failure After E. coli Infections Linked to Petting Farm Visits

Two young children in Melbourne have reportedly suffered severe health complications after contracting Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC), with both cases linked to visits to the same petting farm. One child reportedly developed kidney failure and spent more than two weeks receiving dialysis, while another has suffered serious complications, including permanent brain damage.


The cases have raised renewed concerns about the potential health risks associated with contact with animals at petting farms and similar attractions, particularly for young children.


According to a report by *The Age* , the two children visited the Melbourne-area farm before becoming seriously ill. The cases emerged as Victoria continues to record an increase in reported STEC infections.


## What is STEC and why can it be dangerous?


Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* , commonly known as STEC, is a group of bacteria that can cause serious gastrointestinal illness. Although some *E. coli* bacteria are harmless and normally live in the intestines of humans and animals, certain strains can produce Shiga toxins that damage cells in the body.


STEC infections may cause symptoms including severe stomach cramps, diarrhoea, vomiting and nausea. In some cases, diarrhoea can become bloody.


Young children are among those who can be particularly vulnerable to serious complications. One of the most concerning complications is haemolytic uraemic syndrome, or HUS.


HUS can develop after an STEC infection and can cause damage to red blood cells and the kidneys. Severe cases can result in acute kidney failure and may require dialysis or other intensive medical treatment.


## Two children reportedly required intensive treatment


One Melbourne child, aged two, reportedly became seriously ill after visiting the farm in May. According to *The Age* , the child's mother initially believed he had gastroenteritis after he developed persistent vomiting and diarrhoea.


The situation became more concerning when blood was found in his stool. He was taken to Frankston Hospital, where testing reportedly identified haemolytic uraemic syndrome.


The child spent 17 days on dialysis and also received two blood transfusions. He has since reportedly recovered.


A second child, aged three, has experienced a considerably more serious course of illness. The child reportedly spent four weeks in intensive care and suffered brain damage as a result of the illness.


Doctors are also reportedly assessing whether the child's kidneys will recover sufficiently or whether a kidney transplant may eventually be necessary.


The cases highlight how an infection that initially appears similar to ordinary gastroenteritis can, in some circumstances, develop into a serious medical emergency.


## How can E. coli spread at petting farms?


STEC bacteria can be carried in the gastrointestinal tracts of animals. Infection can occur when people accidentally come into contact with contaminated faecal matter and then transfer the bacteria to their mouths.


Petting farms, animal exhibits and agricultural attractions can create opportunities for this type of exposure. Children may touch animals, fences, feeding equipment, gates or other surfaces and then eat food or touch their faces without washing their hands.


Importantly, an animal does not necessarily have to appear sick for it to carry bacteria.


This is why thorough handwashing is an important precaution after touching animals or visiting areas where livestock are present.


## Health authorities respond to reported cases


Victoria's Department of Health has reportedly advised the petting farm to introduce additional handwashing facilities and provide clearer warnings about the potential risk of infection.


Deputy Chief Health Officer Dr Clare Looker said the Department of Health investigates reported cases of HUS to identify possible sources of infection and help prevent additional cases.


Health authorities have also noted that increased STEC and verotoxin-producing *E. coli* notifications can partly reflect improvements in testing and reporting.


However, the potential consequences of severe STEC infection remain significant, particularly when HUS develops.


## What symptoms should parents watch for?


Symptoms of an STEC infection can initially resemble other stomach illnesses. They may include:


- Diarrhoea
- Bloody diarrhoea
- Severe abdominal cramps
- Nausea
- Vomiting
- Fever in some cases


Parents and carers should seek medical advice if a child develops severe or persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly if there is blood in the stool, signs of dehydration or a significant deterioration in their condition.


Children who become seriously unwell may require urgent medical assessment.


It is important not to assume that every case of diarrhoea or vomiting is caused by STEC. Many infections and other illnesses can produce similar symptoms. Testing and professional medical assessment are needed to determine the cause.


## How can families reduce the risk at petting farms?


Families do not necessarily need to avoid animal attractions altogether, but good hygiene practices are important.


Adults should supervise children around animals and make sure they wash their hands thoroughly with soap and running water after touching animals or entering animal areas.


Hand sanitiser may be useful when soap and water are unavailable, but proper handwashing is preferable after animal contact.


Children should also avoid eating or drinking while interacting with animals. Food should be consumed in designated areas away from livestock and animal enclosures.


Parents should take particular care with young children, who may be more likely to put their fingers, toys or other objects in their mouths.


Petting farms can also help reduce infection risks by providing accessible handwashing stations, clear hygiene instructions and appropriate separation between animal areas and food consumption areas.


## Why these Melbourne cases matter


The reported illnesses demonstrate why infection-control measures at animal attractions are important. Petting farms provide educational and recreational experiences, but contact with animals can carry a risk of exposure to bacteria and other pathogens.


The two Melbourne cases are particularly concerning because of the severity of the complications reported. One child required prolonged dialysis before recovering, while another remains seriously affected and may face further treatment for kidney damage.


The incidents also underline the importance of recognising potentially serious symptoms early. Bloody diarrhoea, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting or signs of dehydration should not simply be dismissed as an ordinary stomach bug.


As health authorities continue monitoring STEC infections in Victoria, families visiting farms and animal attractions can reduce their risk by following basic hygiene measures and seeking medical advice when concerning symptoms develop.
