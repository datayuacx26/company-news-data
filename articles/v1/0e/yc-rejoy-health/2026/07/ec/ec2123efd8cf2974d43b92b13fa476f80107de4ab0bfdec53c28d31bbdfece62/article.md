# The Most Common Emotion People Experience at Work, According to New Research

For years, workplaces were expected to be places where emotions stayed out of sight. Employees were encouraged to remain professional, focus on productivity, and separate personal feelings from business decisions. Today, that mindset is rapidly changing. Organizations are beginning to understand that emotions influence every aspect of work, from decision making and teamwork to creativity and performance.


A recent study has revealed a surprising finding. Despite growing concerns about workplace stress and burnout, happiness remains the most commonly experienced emotion at work. At the same time, negative emotions such as anger, sadness, fear, and disgust continue to shape employee experiences in important ways.


The findings highlight why emotional wellbeing should be a priority for employers and employees alike.


## Why Workplace Emotions Matter


Emotions are more than temporary feelings. They are intense responses to meaningful events that influence how people think, behave, and interact with others.


In a workplace setting, emotions can affect:


- Productivity
- Collaboration
- Communication
- Problem solving
- Employee engagement
- Job satisfaction


For example, sadness may reduce motivation and concentration, while anger can increase workplace conflict. Positive emotions, on the other hand, often improve creativity, teamwork, and resilience.


Simply saying someone feels stressed or unhappy does not tell the full story. Identifying the specific emotion provides valuable insight into what triggered it and how it may affect workplace behavior.


## What the Research Found


Researchers studied 102 employees working across different industries. Participants recorded important workplace events over a period of 15 days, creating nearly 1,500 reports describing experiences that stood out during their workdays.


Each event was evaluated based on whether it was positive or negative and how significant or unexpected it felt.


The results painted an interesting picture of emotional life at work.


### Happiness Was the Most Common Emotion


The biggest surprise was that happiness appeared in approximately 67 percent of reported workplace events, making it the most frequently experienced emotion.


Researchers suggest that people often find satisfaction from:


- Completing meaningful tasks
- Achieving goals
- Working with supportive colleagues
- Receiving appreciation from managers
- Feeling valued by their organization


While many conversations focus on workplace stress, this finding suggests that positive experiences remain a regular part of many employees' daily lives.


## Surprise Came Second


Surprise was the second most common emotion, appearing in more than half of workplace events.


Unexpected situations triggered this response, including:


- Sudden management decisions
- Unplanned organizational changes
- Unexpected praise
- Team celebrations
- Clients changing plans


Surprise is not automatically positive or negative. Instead, it reflects moments that disrupt expectations and require employees to quickly adapt.


## Negative Emotions Still Have a Powerful Impact


Although less frequent than happiness, negative emotions tended to be stronger and often appeared together.


Researchers found that:


- Anger appeared in nearly 40 percent of significant events.
- Sadness occurred in around 32 percent.
- Fear affected about 26 percent.
- Disgust was reported in roughly 20 percent.


Rather than occurring individually, these emotions frequently overlapped, particularly anger, sadness, and disgust.


This suggests that difficult workplace experiences rarely produce just one emotional response. A single negative event can trigger several emotions at once.


## Strong Events Create Strong Emotions


One of the study's most important findings was that emotional intensity depends on more than whether something is good or bad.


Events that are unexpected, disruptive, or highly significant produce much stronger emotional reactions.


For example:


- Receiving an unexpected promotion creates stronger happiness than routine praise.
- Being publicly criticized generates more intense anger than minor disagreements.
- Major organizational changes create more fear than everyday uncertainty.


The more significant an event feels, the greater its emotional impact.


## Fear Behaves Differently


Fear stood apart from every other emotion examined in the study.


Unlike happiness or anger, fear depended more on uncertainty and disruption than whether an event was positive or negative.


Employees were more likely to experience fear when facing:


- Organizational instability
- Unpredictable leadership
- Job insecurity
- Major structural changes
- Safety concerns


Researchers also found that personal characteristics influenced fear levels. Individuals with more pessimistic personalities or higher levels of emotional sensitivity experienced fear more frequently.


## Minimal Gender Differences


Contrary to long standing stereotypes, researchers found very few gender differences in workplace emotions.


Men reported happiness slightly more often, while women experienced disgust somewhat more frequently.


However, the intensity of emotional experiences remained largely consistent across genders.


The findings suggest that workplace experiences themselves influence emotions more than gender does.


## What Triggers Workplace Emotions?


The study also identified common situations that generate different emotions.


### Happiness


Employees experienced happiness after:


- Completing important work
- Achieving goals
- Receiving recognition
- Collaborating successfully
- Feeling appreciated


Recognition from managers, even something as simple as a thank you, had a noticeable positive impact.


### Surprise


Surprise occurred when expectations suddenly changed.


Examples included:


- Clients unexpectedly ending contracts
- Positive company announcements
- Unexpected rewards
- Last minute schedule changes


### Anger


Employees commonly experienced anger when they felt:


- Treated unfairly
- Publicly disrespected
- Humiliated
- Prevented from achieving goals


Poor communication and unfair treatment were significant contributors.


### Sadness


Sadness often followed experiences involving loss or disappointment, including:


- Failed projects
- Missed opportunities
- Career setbacks
- Colleagues leaving the company


These situations often affected morale long after the event itself.


### Disgust


Disgust was usually linked to unethical behavior.


Employees reported this emotion when witnessing:


- Workplace harassment
- Dishonesty
- Toxic behavior
- Corporate hypocrisy
- Ethical violations


Such experiences damaged trust within organizations.


### Fear


Fear was typically triggered by uncertainty.


Common examples included:


- Job insecurity
- Organizational restructuring
- Unpredictable managers
- Safety concerns
- Economic instability


Fear often served as a warning signal that employees perceived potential threats to their wellbeing or future.


## Why Emotional Wellbeing Should Be a Business Priority


The research reinforces an important message for organizations.


Every workplace interaction has the potential to influence employee emotions. Feedback, scheduling decisions, leadership communication, recognition, and company policies all contribute to the emotional climate.


Organizations that actively encourage positive experiences while reducing unnecessary stress may benefit from:


- Higher employee engagement
- Better teamwork
- Improved productivity
- Lower turnover
- Stronger organizational culture


Managers cannot eliminate every negative experience, but they can build workplaces where employees feel respected, supported, and appreciated.


## Final Thoughts


The modern workplace is far more than a place where people complete tasks. It is a social environment where daily interactions shape emotions, relationships, and performance.


While happiness remains the most common workplace emotion, the study also shows that negative emotions can have lasting effects when organizations ignore employee wellbeing.


Understanding what triggers workplace emotions allows employers to create healthier work environments and helps employees better recognize how their experiences influence performance and mental health. As businesses continue placing greater emphasis on wellbeing, emotional awareness may become just as valuable as technical skills in building successful and resilient workplaces.
