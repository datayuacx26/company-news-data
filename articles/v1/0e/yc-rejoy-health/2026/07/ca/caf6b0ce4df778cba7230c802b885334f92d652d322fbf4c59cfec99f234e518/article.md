# Eating Earlier May Help Protect Brain Health as You Age, Early Research Suggests

As people search for practical ways to reduce the risk of cognitive decline and dementia, new research suggests that **when you eat** may be just as important as **what you eat** . A preliminary clinical study presented by researchers at Rutgers University indicates that limiting food intake to a nine-hour daily window and avoiding meals in the four hours before bedtime could provide modest benefits for memory, planning, and problem-solving abilities in older adults.


Although the findings are still in the early stages and require confirmation through larger, peer-reviewed studies, they add to a growing body of evidence linking time-restricted eating with improved metabolic and overall health.


## What the Study Investigated


Researchers from Rutgers University in New Jersey conducted a six-month clinical trial involving **47 women between the ages of 50 and 79** who were living with overweight or obesity.


All participants followed a calorie-reduced eating plan, cutting approximately **500 calories per day** . However, they were divided into two different eating schedules:


- One group consumed all meals within a **nine-hour window** , typically between **10 a.m. and 6 p.m.**
- The second group spread their meals across roughly **12 hours** each day.


The primary goal was weight loss, but researchers also examined whether meal timing influenced cognitive performance.


## Weight Loss Was Similar in Both Groups


Interestingly, both groups achieved nearly identical weight loss during the six-month program.


Participants lost an average of around **7 kilograms (15 pounds)** regardless of whether they followed the shorter eating window or the longer schedule.


This suggests that simply reducing calories was effective for weight management. However, the cognitive outcomes revealed notable differences.


## Better Performance on Some Cognitive Tests


At the conclusion of the trial, participants completed several standardized cognitive assessments.


Researchers observed that individuals following the nine-hour eating schedule performed better in tests measuring:


- Spatial planning
- Problem-solving
- Executive function


There were also indications of fewer mistakes during memory and learning exercises, although improvements were not consistent across every cognitive measurement.


Some assessments, including multitasking ability and reaction time, showed little difference between the two groups.


The improvements were described as **modest** , but researchers believe they warrant further investigation.


## Why Meal Timing Might Matter


Scientists are increasingly interested in how eating schedules influence the body's internal clock, also known as the circadian rhythm.


Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed benefits:


### Improved Blood Sugar Regulation


Eating earlier in the day may help maintain healthier blood glucose levels, reducing spikes that can contribute to inflammation and vascular damage over time.


Stable blood sugar has long been associated with healthier brain function.


### Better Circadian Alignment


The human body follows natural daily rhythms that regulate hormones, metabolism, digestion, and sleep.


Late-night eating may interfere with these rhythms, while earlier meals appear to better align with the body's biological clock.


### Reduced Inflammation


Chronic inflammation has been linked to cognitive decline and several age-related diseases.


Longer overnight fasting periods may help reduce inflammatory processes that affect both metabolic and brain health.


### Enhanced Metabolic Health


Previous research has shown that time-restricted eating can improve several metabolic markers, including:


- Blood pressure
- Waist circumference
- Body mass index (BMI)
- Insulin sensitivity


Better cardiovascular and metabolic health often supports healthier brain aging as well.


## Dementia Prevention Remains a Major Health Priority


Dementia continues to be one of the leading causes of death worldwide.


While factors such as genetics and aging cannot be changed, experts estimate that a significant proportion of dementia cases could potentially be delayed or prevented through healthier lifestyle choices.


These include:


- Maintaining a healthy body weight
- Regular physical activity
- Managing blood pressure
- Controlling diabetes
- Eating a balanced diet
- Avoiding smoking
- Getting sufficient sleep
- Staying socially and mentally active


Obesity during midlife has also been associated with a higher risk of developing dementia later in life.


## Expert Opinions on the Findings


Professor Sue Shapses, a nutritional scientist involved in the research, explained that weight loss alone may support cognitive health.


However, she suggested that limiting eating to an eight or nine-hour daily window could provide additional benefits beyond simple calorie reduction.


The researchers believe future studies will help determine whether meal timing directly affects cognitive performance or whether the improvements result from broader metabolic changes.


Professor Wendy Hall, Head of Nutritional Sciences at King's College London, who was not involved in the research, also said the findings are biologically plausible.


According to Hall, avoiding large meals late in the evening could improve:


- Blood sugar control
- Vascular health
- Inflammation


All of these factors have previously been linked to better brain function.


However, she emphasized that these findings remain preliminary and should not be considered definitive until larger, peer-reviewed studies are completed.


## Growing Evidence Supporting Time-Restricted Eating


The latest study aligns with an expanding body of research examining intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating.


In many Western countries, people often consume food across a period of approximately **14 hours** each day, leaving only around **10 hours overnight without eating** .


Some recent reviews suggest extending the overnight fasting period may improve several health outcomes.


Earlier research has linked finishing meals before early evening with improvements in:


- Body weight
- Blood pressure
- Waist circumference
- Blood sugar regulation
- Other markers of metabolic health


Scientists continue to investigate whether these physical health improvements also translate into better long-term cognitive function.


## Should You Change Your Eating Schedule?


Although the results are encouraging, experts advise against viewing time-restricted eating as a guaranteed method for preventing dementia.


The Rutgers study involved a relatively small number of participants, included only older women with overweight or obesity, and has so far been presented at a scientific conference rather than published in a peer-reviewed journal.


More extensive clinical trials involving diverse populations will be needed before healthcare professionals can make broad recommendations.


For many people, however, avoiding frequent late-night eating and maintaining consistent meal times may be a practical habit that complements other healthy lifestyle choices.


Anyone considering significant dietary changes, particularly individuals with diabetes, chronic medical conditions, or those taking medications that affect blood sugar, should first consult a qualified healthcare professional.


## Final Thoughts


The timing of meals is emerging as an important area of nutrition research. While calorie reduction and healthy food choices remain essential, early evidence suggests that limiting eating to a shorter daytime window and avoiding food several hours before bed may provide additional support for brain health.


Although this research is still preliminary, it reinforces the broader message that everyday lifestyle habits, including diet, sleep, exercise, and weight management, all play an important role in healthy aging. As scientists continue to explore the relationship between meal timing and cognitive function, future studies may provide clearer guidance on whether changing eating schedules can meaningfully reduce the risk of age-related cognitive decline.
