# Early Exposure to PFAS Chemicals May Increase Gut Inflammation Risk in Children, New Study Suggests

A new scientific study has found that exposure to PFAS, often called "forever chemicals," during pregnancy and early infancy may increase the risk of intestinal inflammation in children later in life. Researchers followed mother-child pairs for more than a decade and discovered that higher levels of these persistent chemicals around birth were associated with biological markers linked to inflammation in the digestive system.


The findings add to growing evidence that early-life exposure to environmental pollutants can have lasting health effects. While previous studies have connected PFAS exposure to intestinal diseases in adults, this research is among the first to investigate how prenatal and newborn exposure may affect children's gut health during critical stages of development.


## What Are PFAS?


PFAS, or per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are a large group of synthetic chemicals widely used in consumer and industrial products. They are valued for their resistance to heat, grease, stains, and water, making them common in non-stick cookware, waterproof clothing, food packaging, firefighting foams, and stain-resistant fabrics.


These chemicals are commonly known as "forever chemicals" because they break down very slowly in the environment. Over time, they accumulate in soil, water, wildlife, and the human body. Scientists have identified thousands of PFAS compounds, many of which remain poorly understood.


Previous research has associated PFAS exposure with several health concerns, including certain cancers, liver damage, immune system dysfunction, kidney disease, developmental issues, and reproductive problems. Researchers are increasingly focused on understanding how these chemicals affect children, whose developing bodies may be more vulnerable to environmental exposures.


## Inside the New Study


The research was conducted by scientists from the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The team followed 107 mother-child pairs from the United States and Mexico, collecting biological samples during pregnancy, at birth, and throughout childhood.


Researchers analyzed maternal blood collected during pregnancy, umbilical cord blood, and newborn blood samples to measure PFAS exposure. They identified hundreds of PFAS-related metabolites and concentrated their analysis on the 46 compounds most commonly detected.


Rather than examining one chemical at a time, the scientists evaluated mixtures of PFAS. This approach better reflects real-world exposure because people are typically exposed to multiple chemicals simultaneously rather than individually.


The researchers later measured fecal calprotectin in the children as they grew older, with follow-up extending up to 11 years. Fecal calprotectin is a recognized biomarker that indicates inflammation in the intestines.


## Key Findings


The study found a strong association between higher PFAS exposure during prenatal development or shortly after birth and elevated levels of fecal calprotectin later in childhood.


Although increased fecal calprotectin does not diagnose a specific disease, it signals intestinal inflammation, which is linked to conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. Persistent intestinal inflammation may also contribute to a greater risk of colorectal disease over time.


Researchers emphasized that these findings highlight the importance of early developmental periods. Exposure to environmental contaminants before birth may influence long-term health outcomes years later.


The study also detected well-known PFAS compounds such as PFOA and PFOS, along with newer replacement chemicals that have entered the market following restrictions on older PFAS varieties. Scientists note that much less is known about the long-term health effects of many of these newer compounds.


## Why Chemical Mixtures Matter


Traditional toxicology studies often focus on individual chemicals. However, humans encounter complex mixtures of environmental pollutants every day through drinking water, food, household products, and the surrounding environment.


By analyzing PFAS mixtures rather than isolated compounds, the researchers aimed to provide a more realistic picture of how exposure occurs in everyday life. This method may improve understanding of the cumulative effects of multiple chemicals acting together.


As scientific knowledge expands, many researchers believe mixture-based analyses will become increasingly important for evaluating environmental health risks.


## Understanding the Limitations


While the findings are significant, the researchers caution that the study has limitations.


The sample size included only 107 mother-child pairs, which is relatively small compared with larger population studies. The research also demonstrates an association rather than proving that PFAS directly caused intestinal inflammation.


Additional studies involving larger and more diverse populations will be necessary to confirm the findings and determine the biological mechanisms involved.


Even so, experts say the study contributes valuable evidence supporting concerns about prenatal exposure to environmental contaminants.


## Can Exposure Be Reduced?


Although eliminating PFAS exposure completely is difficult because these chemicals are widespread, some experts recommend practical steps that may help reduce exposure:


- Use certified water filtration systems designed to remove PFAS where contamination is known.
- Limit the use of stain-resistant and waterproof consumer products when possible.
- Reduce reliance on food packaging that may contain PFAS coatings.
- Consider fresh foods and thoroughly wash fruits and vegetables.
- Stay informed about local drinking water quality reports.


Researchers also stress that individual actions alone cannot solve the problem. Broader regulatory measures and stronger environmental policies are considered essential for reducing PFAS contamination and protecting future generations.


## Why This Research Matters


The study strengthens growing scientific evidence that environmental exposures during pregnancy and infancy may shape health outcomes later in life. Identifying these risks early could help guide public health strategies aimed at preventing disease before symptoms develop.


Scientists hope that continued research into PFAS exposure will improve understanding of how these chemicals affect children's immune and digestive systems. Such knowledge may also support better regulations governing chemical manufacturing and environmental contamination.


As awareness of PFAS continues to grow worldwide, studies like this highlight the importance of monitoring environmental pollutants and investing in preventive healthcare measures that safeguard mothers, infants, and future generations.
