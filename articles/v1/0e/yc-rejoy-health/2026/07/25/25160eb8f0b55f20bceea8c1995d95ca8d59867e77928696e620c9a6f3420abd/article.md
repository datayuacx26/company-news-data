# School Cafeteria Favorites May Need a Healthy Makeover as New Nutrition Rules Approach

## Popular School Meals Face Changes to Reduce Sugar and Sodium


School cafeterias across the United States may soon see changes to some of the meals students know and love. Foods such as pizza, tacos, chicken wings, breakfast smoothies, sausage biscuits, and other popular cafeteria choices may need healthier recipes to meet updated nutrition standards scheduled to take effect during the 2027 to 2028 school year.


A recent study published in *Nutrition Reviews* found that many commonly served school meals contain high amounts of added sugar and sodium. Researchers say the goal is not to remove students’ favorite foods, but to improve their nutritional value while keeping meals enjoyable, familiar, and appealing.


School meals play an important role in children’s daily nutrition. For many students, breakfast and lunch served at school provide some of the most balanced meals they receive throughout the day. Because many children eat these meals several times each week, even small improvements in ingredients and preparation methods could have a meaningful impact over time.


## Researchers Examine Common Cafeteria Foods


The research team reviewed current school cafeteria offerings to understand which foods may require updates under upcoming United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) nutrition limits.


Researchers surveyed nearly 1,100 school nutrition professionals to learn which foods schools serve most often and which meals students choose regularly. They then analyzed recipes for popular items to identify those with the highest levels of added sugar and sodium.


The study found that several breakfast foods commonly served in schools may contain more sugar than recommended. Items such as muffins, yogurt parfaits, and breakfast smoothies were among the foods identified as higher in added sugars.


Other breakfast choices, including sausage biscuits and breakfast pizzas, were found to contain significant amounts of sodium. At lunchtime, foods such as flavored chicken wings, tacos, and nachos were also identified as meals that may need recipe adjustments.


One example highlighted by researchers was chicken wings. Some recipes averaged more than 2,100 milligrams of sodium per serving, which is close to the daily sodium limit of 2,300 milligrams recommended for adults.


## Healthier Recipes Without Removing Student Favorites


Nutrition experts say improving school meals does not have to mean eliminating popular foods. Instead, schools can focus on creating healthier versions of familiar dishes.


Researchers suggest that cafeteria professionals can reduce sodium by using herbs, spices, and other flavor-building ingredients. Garlic, rosemary, thyme, paprika, and other seasonings can enhance taste without relying heavily on salt.


For sweet foods, ingredients such as cinnamon, nutmeg, and ginger may help provide flavor while reducing dependence on added sugars.


The challenge, however, is making sure students actually enjoy the updated meals. A healthier recipe will only succeed if students choose to eat it.


Taste testing, student surveys, and feedback programs can help schools understand which changes work best. When students are involved in the process, cafeterias may have a better chance of creating meals that are both nutritious and popular.


## Why Reducing Sugar and Sodium Matters for Children


Eating too much added sugar and sodium regularly can contribute to health concerns over time. Developing healthy eating habits during childhood can help children make better food choices as they grow older.


School cafeterias are one environment where children are exposed to food choices repeatedly. Researchers say these daily experiences can influence what students consider normal and enjoyable.


The goal is not to make school meals boring or restrictive. Instead, the focus is on helping children become comfortable with foods that are lower in sugar and sodium while still being flavorful.


Experts emphasize that school meals are only one part of a child’s overall diet. Family meals, personal preferences, activity levels, and many other factors also influence long-term health. However, improving the quality of school meals can provide an additional opportunity to support children’s well-being.


## Schools Face the Challenge of Balancing Nutrition and Taste


Updating cafeteria menus will require creativity from school nutrition teams. Food professionals must balance federal nutrition requirements with student expectations.


A meal that meets nutrition standards but goes uneaten does not provide the intended benefits. This is why researchers stress the importance of maintaining familiar flavors and appealing textures while making gradual improvements.


Small recipe changes can make a difference. Reducing sodium little by little, adjusting sweetness levels, and introducing new flavors can help students adapt to healthier versions of their favorite foods.


Schools may also use education programs to help students understand why certain changes are being made. When children learn about nutrition and how food choices affect health, they may become more open to trying updated meals.


## The Future of School Cafeteria Food


The upcoming nutrition standards represent an opportunity for schools to rethink cafeteria menus. Instead of viewing changes as restrictions, experts say they can be seen as a chance to improve the foods children eat every day.


Popular meals do not have to disappear. With thoughtful recipe development, schools can continue serving foods students recognize while improving their nutritional quality.


Researchers believe that exposing children to healthier meals consistently can help shape lifelong eating habits. While school food alone cannot determine a child’s future health, it can contribute to building a stronger foundation for better choices.


As schools prepare for the updated USDA requirements, nutrition professionals will continue exploring ways to create meals that are healthier, satisfying, and enjoyable for students.
