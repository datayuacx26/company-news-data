# AT&T’s copper line shutdown has begun

## Copper lines are disappearing—and your emergency systems may be at risk.


AT&T has officially started phasing out its copper POTS (Plain Old Telephone Service) lines. This is the infrastructure that businesses and communities have relied on to power critical infrastructure for more than a century, but it’s finally coming to an end. For organizations, the question was never if copper will go away, but when. And the answer is finally here.


The urgency around copper retirement is only increasing. In March 2026, the FCC took additional steps to accelerate the transition by removing key regulatory hurdles that once slowed providers down from retiring legacy networks. These changes eliminate requirements for network change disclosures, streamline the approval process for discontinuing copper-based services, and reduce the need for case-by-case filings when phasing out legacy lines. This means carriers now have fewer barriers to discontinuing copper line service. Combined with AT&T’s 2025 to 2029 shutdown timeline, this shift signals that the window for maintaining traditional POTS lines is shrinking faster than many businesses expect.


## FCC filing update addresses timing and scale of the shutdowns


A recent[FCC filing](https://www.fcc.gov/ecfs/document/1201492902836/1) from AT&T reinforces that the shift away from legacy copper POTS phone lines is actively moving forward. In the filing, AT&T requests authority to discontinue legacy TDM-based voice services (“POTS”) on or after November 15, 2026, impacting approximately 90,000 customers across portions of certain wire centers in 18 states. For organizations still relying on POTS lines for fire panels, elevator phones, alarm panels, and other critical lines, this is a clear signal to identify remaining analog dependencies now and put a replacement plan in place before retirements in your region create disruption, delays, or limited options.


## The shutdown timeline


The first big milestone hit October 15, 2025. From that date on, AT&T no longer supports new installations, moves or changes to copper lines across nearly 20 states. This is the start of a nationwide phase-out, with the company planning to retire nearly all copper services by 2029.


AT&T previously announced plans to shut down[copper-based services](https://www.ooma.com/blog/business/are-traditional-copper-landlines-still-working/) across the country by the end of 2029 at an investor event in December 2024.


“The bottom line is we expect to no longer provide copper-based services across the vast majority of our footprint by the end of 2029,” said Susan Johnson, AT&T’s head of wireline transformation and supply chain.


This commitment to retire the bulk of the company’s copper lines by 2029 was reinforced by AT&T CEO John Stankey during a Q4 earnings call. It also includes an initial target of 1,300 central offices. The company has already received regulatory approval to begin this massive transition, with the FCC recently approving AT&T to discontinue legacy services in about 10% of its footprint.


## June 2026: The first permanent copper shutdowns begin


While October 2025 marked the end of new orders for many legacy copper services, June 2026 is the real turning point in AT&T’s copper retirement strategy.


Beginning in June 2026, AT&T is expected to start permanently decommissioning copper infrastructure in approximately 500 wire centers nationwide—roughly 10% of its network footprint—following FCC approval for the first wave of discontinuance projects. This is not simply a restriction on new service orders. It is the physical retirement of the underlying network infrastructure.


Once a wire center is decommissioned, every POTS line served by that facility is permanently shut down. Businesses can no longer assume that existing copper lines will continue operating indefinitely, even if they have been grandfathered in. Affected customers receive discontinuance notices, but there is no automatic replacement service provided for many legacy applications.


Recent FCC filings show this process is already accelerating. Rather than repairing hundreds of damaged copper network segments, AT&T is seeking permission to permanently discontinue service at those locations. It’s another indication that aging copper infrastructure is increasingly being retired instead of restored, making it more important than ever for organizations to proactively replace POTS lines before an outage or shutdown affects critical systems.


The FCC recently authorized another phase of AT&T’s copper retirement in California, covering approximately 184,000 residential customers and 15,000 business customers. Although California continues to challenge AT&T’s Carrier of Last Resort (COLR) obligations, the retirement of legacy copper networks is moving ahead. Organizations that still depend on POTS lines should identify their critical analog systems and begin planning their migration before service changes create unnecessary risk.


For organizations still relying on analog lines for elevator phones, fire alarm panels, security systems, fax machines, emergency call boxes, point-of-sale backup connections, and other mission-critical systems, the message is clear: identify and replace those copper-dependent connections before your local wire center reaches retirement. Waiting until service termination notices arrive may leave insufficient time to evaluate alternatives, maintain compliance requirements, and complete a smooth migration.


## The hidden dependencies on copper phone lines


It’s tempting to shrug and say, “We don’t use landlines anymore.” But copper powers far more than phone calls. Many critical systems still depend on it, including:


- **[Fire alarm panels](https://www.ooma.com/business/airdial-pots-line-replacement/fire-alarm-panels/)** that alert emergency services to potential disasters.
- **[Elevator emergency phones](https://www.ooma.com/business/airdial-pots-line-replacement/elevator-emergency-phones/)** for passenger safety and building code requirements.
- **Public safety phones** across parking lots, campuses, and other public spaces.
- **Security alarm systems** that secure facilities and keep tenants protected from intruders.
- **Emergency call boxes** and[blue light safety phones](https://www.ooma.com/business/airdial-pots-line-replacement/blue-light-emergency-phones/) .
- **Fax machines** that are still used by many property managers and in healthcare applications.
- **Medical monitoring devices** , for at-risk patients in hospitals.


If these connections disappear without a plan in place,[organizations risk](https://www.ooma.com/blog/business/risks-of-waiting-to-replace-pots-lines/) losing essential safety and compliance tools overnight which will come with some serious risks.


## The risk of inaction


As AT&T phases out copper lines, organizations that haven’t prepared will face serious consequences, including:


- **Safety code violations:** Many local fire and building codes specifically require working emergency communication systems. A non-functional elevator phone or fire alarm connection could result in citations, fines, or even building closures.
- **Insurance issues:** Property insurance policies often mandate functional fire and security alarm systems. Loss of monitoring capability could void coverage or drastically increase premiums.
- **Service disruptions:** AT&T has already received approval to stop all new copper-based inward orders in 60 wire centers across 13 states, with many more approvals pending. Once AT&T stops supporting copper lines in your area, getting landline service restored becomes increasingly difficult—and eventually impossible.


## The financial reality driving change


AT&T reportedly spends $6 billion annually to maintain its copper network, which spans the company’s 500,000-square mile local service footprint, yet only 5% of customers still use copper voice technology. The economic case for transitioning away from copper is compelling for AT&T, and that’s before you factor in the people cost, as copper networks require a significant amount of labor to maintain. To top that off, copper lines are highly valued and subject to ever-rising rates of[copper theft](https://www.ooma.com/blog/copper-thefts-are-impacting-critical-infrastructure-for-business/) , a growing problem since the early 2000s, with the FBI deeming it as a threat to critical US infrastructure. Thieves stealing copper wires are causing power and phone outages, exposing live wires, and even disrupting emergency services.


Companies such as AT&T and BT Group are trying to recover 800 million metric tons of copper lines over the next decade, worth over $7 billion.


Between 2021 and 2023, AT&T recycled more than 14,000 tons of copper and expects this part of its business to increase. The cables are being pulled out of the ground, stripped for the copper then sold at profits that top 30% after extraction, recovery and processing.


Clearly, carriers want out of the copper line business to increase their overall bottom lines.
