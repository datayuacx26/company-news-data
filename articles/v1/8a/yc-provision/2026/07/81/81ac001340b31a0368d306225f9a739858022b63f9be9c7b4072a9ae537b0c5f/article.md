# Who Groutes the Base Plates? Division 03 Scope Conflicts GCs Miss at Bid

## TL;DR


- Base plate grouting is one of the most common Division 03 / Division 05 scope conflicts in commercial construction.
- Neither the concrete sub nor the steel sub explicitly owns it in most bid packages — and neither prices it unless told to.
- If your scope sheets don't assign it, you absorb it or fight over it at closeout.
- The fix is upstream: drawings-first scoping, explicit trade assignments, and a pre-issue scope review before bid day.
- This conflict appears in[Chapter 5 of the Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) alongside a dozen other trade-specific gaps that show up across commercial GC bids.


## The Question Nobody Answers at Bid


Structural steel is going up. The iron workers have bolted the first columns. The concrete sub has finished the slab pour. Then someone asks: "Who's grouting the base plates?"


Silence.


This is not a rare situation. It plays out on commercial, institutional, and industrial projects across North America — every year. The concrete sub says it's structural steel work. The steel sub says it's concrete finishing. The GC is stuck in the middle with a field crew waiting and a change order forming.


The problem started at bid, not in the field. And it started because nobody wrote it down.


## Why Base Plate Grouting Is a Structural Gap, Not an Afterthought


Grouting base plates is load-bearing work. It transfers column loads to foundations. It's not a finish — it's structural. If it's done wrong or late, it affects the entire steel erection sequence.


The material is typically non-shrink cementitious grout. The work requires tight tolerance, proper curing, and coordination with the steel erector's schedule. It is not something a concrete crew can just swing back to do between pours.


Structurally, this work belongs to the concrete scope. Sequentially, it belongs to the steel erection schedule. That tension is exactly why it falls through the cracks.


### What the Specs Say (and Don't Say)


Most project specs reference base plate grouting in Division 03 (Concrete) under section 03 60 00 (Grouting) or 03 62 00 (Non-Shrink Grouting). Some specs reference it in Division 05 under structural steel installation notes.


Here is the problem: specs often describe the product and the method. They rarely assign the trade. When a spec says "Contractor shall install non-shrink grout at all base plates per manufacturer's instructions," it uses "Contractor" — not "concrete subcontractor" or "steel erector."


In a design-bid-build delivery model, "Contractor" means the GC. The GC then needs to assign that scope to a sub. If the GC doesn't explicitly do that in the scope sheet, nobody prices it.


## How the Conflict Unfolds on a Real Project


Here is a typical sequence. It's not hypothetical — it's pulled directly from patterns documented across 200+ GC interviews conducted for the[Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook) .


1. **Bid phase:** GC sends bid docs to the concrete sub and the steel sub. Neither scope sheet explicitly assigns base plate grouting. Both subs exclude it — sometimes explicitly, sometimes silently.
2. **Buyout phase:** GC reviews leveling sheets. Grout line items aren't there. No one flags it because both subs' numbers look competitive.
3. **Construction phase:** Steel erection starts. Base plates are set. The steel sub requests grouting so they can continue erection. The concrete sub says it wasn't in their scope.
4. **Change order phase:** GC absorbs the cost or negotiates a split. The fight usually runs $30K to $150K depending on project size and grout volume.


This is a textbook scope gap. It was predictable. It was preventable.


## The "As Per Plans and Specs" Problem


The most common anti-pattern in GC scope sheets — documented repeatedly in the[Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) — is the phrase "as per plans and specs." It sounds complete. It isn't.


"As per plans and specs" doesn't assign a trade. It doesn't tell a concrete sub that base plate grouting is their scope. It tells them to read 2,000 pages and figure it out. Most subs read what they know and price what they see. If they've never owned base plate grouting before, they won't price it.


A Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC put it plainly: *"It's descriptive — bread, put it on a plate, use the open jar. You have to get to that level of detail or else they'll just be like, 'you didn't tell us that.'"*


That's the peanut-butter test. If your scope sheet doesn't spell out that the concrete sub is responsible for non-shrink grouting at all structural base plates per Section 03 62 00, you have not been descriptive enough.


## Division 03 Scope Conflicts Beyond Base Plates


Base plate grouting is one of several concrete-related scope gaps that show up regularly across commercial bids. The pattern is consistent: the spec describes the work, nobody assigns the trade, and the GC absorbs it at closeout.


### Slab Assembly Conflicts


On projects with composite steel decks, conflicts arise between the steel sub and the concrete sub over who places the shear studs, who installs the edge form, and who is responsible for concrete mix placement above deck. Each sub assumes the other is pricing it.


### Concrete Pumping


Pumping is frequently excluded from concrete sub bids. Some subs price direct chute or buggy placement. When the building height or pour location requires a pump, the GC either pays for it separately or finds it missing at buyout. If your scope sheet doesn't say "pumping included," assume it isn't.


### Grouting Embed Plates and Anchor Bolts


Structural embeds set in concrete and anchor bolt chases are another grey zone. The steel sub installs the embed. The concrete sub pours around it. Who groutes the void? Same conflict, smaller dollar value — but it adds up across a full structure.

## The Five Habits That Prevent This Gap


The firms with the tightest margins on concrete-heavy work share a set of scoping habits. These align directly with the Eight Habits framework from the[Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook) .


### 1. Drawings-First, Not Boilerplate-First


Pull the structural drawings before you touch last project's scope template. Look at the base plate schedule. Count the columns. Note the grout specification. Then write the concrete sub's scope to match what's on this project — not what was on the last one.


### 2. Specific Document References


Instead of "all concrete work as per plans and specs," write "non-shrink grouting at all structural base plates per Section 03 62 00, including all locations shown on Structural Drawings S-series, columns and base plates as noted on the Base Plate Schedule." That is not bureaucratic over-engineering. That is protection.


### 3. Front-Load the Buyout Conversation


Flag base plate grouting at the leveling meeting. Ask the concrete sub directly: "Have you included non-shrink grout at base plates?" Get a written confirmation. If they excluded it, get a price before award — not after the steel erector is standing around waiting.


### 4. Sub-Specific Tailoring


An Estimating Manager at a Canadian ICI GC noted that subs have become more likely to call out scope exclusions explicitly: *"We have less subs who just kind of a gentleman's agreement — they've become more quick to clarify that we're not including that one piece of scope."* That shift works in your favor only if your scope sheet gives them something specific to respond to.


### 5. The Pre-Issue Scope Review


Before you send the scope to the sub, have someone review it against the structural drawings. Not a five-minute skim — a real check against the spec sections and the structural engineer's notes. If your team is covering too many pursuits to do this consistently, that is a workflow problem worth solving at the process level.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is base plate grouting in construction?


Base plate grouting is the installation of non-shrink cementitious grout beneath structural steel column base plates. It fills the gap between the base plate and the foundation to ensure full, uniform bearing and proper load transfer. It's structural work — not a finish — and it directly affects column stability and the steel erection sequence.


### Who is responsible for grouting base plates — the concrete sub or the steel sub?


There is no universal rule. Most project specs place base plate grouting in Division 03, which points to the concrete subcontractor. But the work is coordinated with the steel erection schedule, which creates ambiguity. The GC must explicitly assign the trade in the scope sheet. If it isn't assigned, neither sub prices it.


### Why do Division 03 scope gaps happen so often?


Division 03 work touches nearly every other trade — civil, steel, MEP, envelope. Each interface is a potential gap. Most scope sheets are built from templates, not from a drawings-first review. When nobody reads the structural notes against the Division 03 spec sections, items like base plate grouting fall through. The anti-pattern is generic scope language like "as per plans and specs" with no trade assignment.


### What does a base plate grouting scope conflict cost on a real project?


On a mid-size commercial structure with 80 columns, the direct grout cost can run $40K to $100K. Add crew mobilization, coordination delays, and any schedule impact on steel erection, and total exposure often exceeds $100K. Arcadis reports the average U.S. construction dispute is now worth $60.1 million — and scope omissions are the top driver.


### How do I prevent this gap in my concrete scope sheets?

Confirm at the buyout leveling meeting that the sub has priced it. Then do a pre-issue scope review before the package goes out. These five steps take under an hour and eliminate the gap entirely.
