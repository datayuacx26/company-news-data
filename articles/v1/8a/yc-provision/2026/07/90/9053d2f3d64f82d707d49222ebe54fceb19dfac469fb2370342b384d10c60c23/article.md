Drywall buyout looks straightforward. One trade, one scope letter, one sub. But drywall scopes hide more ambiguity than almost any other trade package — and the disputes that follow are predictable and avoidable.


Wall blocking ownership, gypcrete naming collisions, and hollow metal door frame supply-and-install splits are the three items that generate the most friction between drywall subs and GCs on commercial projects. None of them are exotic. All of them get missed repeatedly.

## Why Drywall Scopes Are Harder Than They Look


Drywall is a coordination trade. It touches framing, MEP rough-in, specialties, envelope, and finishes. Every one of those intersections is a potential ownership gap.


The problem compounds because drywall specs are often split across multiple spec sections: 09 21 16 (gypsum board assemblies), 09 83 00 (acoustical room components), 03 54 00 (cast underlayments, where gypcrete often lives), and Division 08 for hollow metal frames. When scope items live in different sections, no one sub reads all of them — and GCs often don't reconcile them during takeoff.


According to the[Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) , built from interviews with 200+ general contractors, drywall is one of the highest-frequency categories for scope disputes at buyout. The three items below show up on the list consistently.


## Gap 1: Wall Blocking — Who Installs It and Who Supplies It?


### What the gap looks like


Wall blocking is wood or steel backing installed inside stud cavities. It supports grab bars, handrails, casework, toilet accessories, TV mounts, signage brackets, and anything else that gets bolted to a wall.


Blocking locations are typically shown on architectural or specialty drawings. But the scope letter often just says "framing and drywall as per drawings." That sentence says nothing about who supplies the blocking material, who installs it, or who is responsible for coordinating locations with the specialty subs.


The drywall sub assumes the GC will supply blocking, or that another sub is handling it. The GC assumes the drywall sub is covering it as part of rough framing. Neither party is wrong — the scope letter created the ambiguity.


### Where it shows up


- Washrooms: grab bars, toilet paper holders, paper towel dispensers
- Corridors: handrails, ADA accessories, signage
- Tenant spaces: millwork, casework, TV and display mounts
- Healthcare: medical gas panels, equipment rails, overhead lifts


On a mid-size commercial project, blocking scope can run $15,000–$60,000 depending on the number of specialty items. That's a real change order if it's not assigned.


### What the scope letter must say


The scope letter needs to explicitly state:


1. **Who supplies the blocking material** — drywall sub, GC direct-supply, or specialty trade.
2. **Who installs the blocking** — almost always the drywall sub, but it needs to be written.
3. **Who coordinates blocking locations** — the drywall sub must receive a blocking schedule from the GC or specialty subs before framing is closed.
4. **Which drawings govern** — reference the specific drawing sheets, not "as per plans."


A Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC put it plainly: *"It's descriptive — bread, put it on a plate, use the open jar. You have to get to that level of detail or else they'll just be like, 'you didn't tell us that.'"*


That's the standard. Blocking is no different. If you don't write it down at the peanut-butter level of detail, you'll pay for the omission.


## Gap 2: Gypcrete — Naming Collisions That Cost Real Money


### What the gap looks like


Gypcrete (gypsum concrete underlayment) is one of the most reliably misassigned items in commercial construction. The naming collision is structural: "gypcrete" is not a standard CSI spec section name. It lives in Section 03 54 00 (Cast Underlayments), not in the drywall section. But on drawings, it often appears in wall sections drawn by the architect — right next to drywall assemblies.


Drywall subs see it on the drawings and assume it's in their scope. Concrete subs see it in the spec and assume it's theirs. The GC's scope letter says neither — it just says "drywall as per spec" and "concrete work as per Division 3."


Now no one has explicitly covered it, or both subs have priced it. Either way, there's a dispute at buyout.


### The "gypcrete" naming problem specifically


"Gypcrete" is a brand name, not a spec term. Architects use it colloquially in drawing notes. But the spec may call the same product "gypsum underlayment," "cast underlayment," or "self-leveling topping." If your drywall sub searched for "gypcrete" in the spec and didn't find it, they may have excluded it from their bid entirely — without telling you.


This is not sub negligence. This is a documentation problem. It happens because project sets are inconsistent in how they name the same product.


### What the scope letter must say


The scope letter needs to:


- Name the product explicitly: "gypsum concrete underlayment / gypcrete / Section 03 54 00."
- Assign it to a specific trade — drywall or concrete flooring sub — not both.
- Reference the drawing sheets where gypcrete assemblies appear, not just the spec section.
- Confirm which sub is responsible for substrate prep, curing, and protection after placement.


If you're issuing the drywall scope letter and gypcrete is the drywall sub's responsibility, say so explicitly. If it's going to a specialty flooring sub, exclude it from the drywall scope by name.


Vague incorporation language — "all work shown on drawings and described in specifications" — will not hold up when both subs show up at buyout claiming they didn't include it. As one Estimating Manager at a Canadian ICI GC noted: *"We have less subs who just kind of do a gentleman's agreement — they've become more quick to clarify that we're not including that one piece of scope."*


You need to beat them to it.


## Gap 3: Hollow Metal Door Frames — Supply vs. Install vs. Both


### What the gap looks like


Hollow metal door frames live in Division 08 (Openings). But they are installed by the drywall sub — set in the stud framing, grouted, and prepped for the door hardware sub. The spec responsibility and the field responsibility are in different divisions. That split is where scope gaps form.


Drywall subs often price frame installation but exclude frame supply. The hollow metal supplier (Division 08) delivers frames to site but may not include installation in their scope. Unless the GC explicitly assigns both supply and install to a single trade in the respective scope letters, the frames sit on the loading dock and no one is on the hook to set them.


### The grouting question


Most hollow metal frames in masonry or CMU conditions require grouting — concrete or mortar poured into the frame to anchor it. In stud framing, grouting is less common but still specified. When it is specified, the scope letter needs to state explicitly who handles it: drywall sub, masonry sub, or concrete sub.


This is not a $5,000 line item. On a large institutional project with 150–300 door openings, frame supply and installation is a $200,000–$500,000 scope item. It cannot be left to inference.


### What the scope letter must say


For the drywall scope letter:


- Explicitly include or exclude hollow metal frame installation.
- If included: reference the Division 08 drawings, frame schedule, and required preparation scope.
- Confirm who coordinates frame delivery timing with the framing sequence.
- Confirm who is responsible for grouting (if specified).
- Note which frame types are in scope: standard, borrowed lite, sidelite, transom.


For the hollow metal supplier's scope letter:


- Explicitly include or exclude installation. Do not assume the drywall sub is covering it.
- Confirm delivery sequence requirements — frames must arrive before walls are closed.


The Scope Gap Playbook documents a recurring pattern here: GCs issue drywall scope letters that reference "doors and frames as per Division 08" without assigning installation. The drywall sub excludes it. The hollow metal supplier excludes it. The gap shows up at buyout — or worse, in the field when frames are missing from walls that are already drywalled.


## The Anti-Pattern Driving All Three Gaps


All three of these gaps share a root cause. It's the most-cited anti-pattern in the[Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook) : **"As per plans and specs."**


That phrase is not a scope assignment. It is a placeholder. It tells the sub that the GC has not done the work of reconciling the drawings with the spec with the scope letter — and that disputes are coming.


Subs know this. They price accordingly. Either they pad their bid to cover ambiguity, or they exclude the ambiguous items and wait to bill them as change orders. Either way, the GC pays.


The eight habits that prevent scope gaps — documented in the[playbook's trade chapter](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) — start with this principle: *drawings-first, not boilerplate-first.* Scope letters should be built from the actual project drawings, not copied from last quarter's similar job with the name swapped out.

## Practical Steps: Closing Drywall Scope Gaps Before They Become Change Orders


### Step 1: Start with drawings, not boilerplate


Pull the architectural reflected ceiling plans, wall sections, and door schedule before you open your scope letter template. List every item you see — blocking locations, gypcrete assemblies, frame types — before you write a single line of scope.


### Step 2: Reconcile spec section names against drawing notes


Search for "gypcrete," "gypsum underlayment," and "cast underlayment" in both the drawings and the spec. Confirm they refer to the same product. Note the spec section. Assign it to one sub — then put that section number in the scope letter.


### Step 3: Issue a blocking schedule before framing closes


Coordinate blocking locations from the architectural, specialty, and equipment drawings before the drywall sub starts framing. The scope letter should require the drywall sub to hold framing at blocking locations until the schedule is issued and confirmed.


### Step 4: Issue hollow metal scope to two trades simultaneously


Issue the drywall scope letter and the hollow metal supplier scope letter at the same time. Review both for supply and install assignments before you send either. Gaps are easier to catch when both documents are open side by side.


### Step 5: Run a pre-issue scope review


Before the scope letter goes to the sub, have a second set of eyes review it against the drawings and spec. It can prevent a $50,000 change order.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is a drywall scope letter in construction?


A drywall scope letter is the document a GC issues to a drywall subcontractor during buyout. It defines exactly what work the sub is responsible for — framing, boarding, taping, blocking, accessories, and related items. It is the binding reference for what is and isn't included in the subcontract.


### Who is responsible for wall blocking in a drywall scope?


Typically the drywall sub installs wall blocking, since they control the stud framing. But material supply is frequently unassigned. The scope letter must explicitly state who supplies blocking material, who installs it, and who coordinates blocking locations with the specialty trades before framing is closed.


### Why does gypcrete cause scope disputes?


Gypcrete is a brand name, not a standard CSI spec section term. It appears in drawing notes but is spec'd under Section 03 54 00 (Cast Underlayments), not in the drywall sections. Drywall subs and concrete subs both see it as potentially theirs. Without explicit assignment in the scope letter — by spec section and product name — it falls through the cracks.


### Who installs hollow metal door frames — the drywall sub or the door supplier?


In stud framing, hollow metal frames are almost always installed by the drywall sub. But the frames are supplied by a Division 08 hollow metal supplier. The scope letter must confirm installation is in the drywall scope and that the HM supplier's scope includes delivery to a sequence agreed with the drywall sub. Leaving this unassigned creates a gap at buyout or in the field.


### What does "as per plans and specs" mean in a drywall scope letter?


"As per plans and specs" is not a scope assignment. It is an invitation to dispute. Subs read it as GC shorthand for "we haven't reconciled the documents." They will price conservatively, exclude ambiguous items, or bill them as change orders. Scope letters need explicit, drawing-referenced item assignments — not boilerplate incorporation language.
