# RFI Volume Is a Symptom: How Scope Gaps at Bid Drive Field RFIs

## TL;DR


- Most field RFIs don't start in the field. They start during bid when scope is left vague or undefined.
- According to FMI, $31 billion in annual U.S. rework comes from miscommunication and bad project data — scope gaps are the primary driver.
- The Arcadis 2025 Global Construction Disputes Report puts the average U.S. construction dispute value at $60.1M. "Errors and omissions in contract documents" has been the #1 dispute cause for 6 of the last 9 years.
- Pre-construction teams that close scope gaps before award — not after — reduce RFI volume, change order exposure, and field conflict.
- Five habits separate low-RFI projects from high-RFI ones. They all live in pre-con.


## The RFI Problem Starts Earlier Than You Think


When RFI volume spikes on a project, the instinct is to look at the field. Maybe the super isn't coordinating well. Maybe the subs are fishing for change orders. Maybe the design team is slow to respond.


Those things happen. But they're rarely the root cause.


Most high-RFI projects trace back to a single failure point: scope that wasn't defined clearly enough at bid. The field is just where the gap surfaces. The gap itself was created weeks or months earlier — in the estimate room, in the scope package, in the subcontract.


This article breaks down how that happens and what pre-construction teams can do to stop it.


## What a Scope Gap Actually Is


A scope gap is any work required to complete the project that isn't clearly assigned to a responsible party. It's not always missing work. Sometimes it's work that two subs both think the other is doing. Sometimes it's work that the spec references but no scope package captures. Sometimes it's work that's "readily inferable" — and your subcontract language just handed it to you.


Scope gaps come in three forms:


- **Coverage gaps:** Work that nobody's scope includes. A trade falls through the cracks entirely.
- **Overlap gaps:** Two subs both exclude the same item, each assuming it's the other's responsibility. The RFI reveals the standoff.
- **Vagueness gaps:** The scope says "as per plans and specs." Nobody checked whether the plans and specs actually agree with each other.


All three end up as field RFIs. And all three were preventable at bid.


## How Scope Gaps Become Field RFIs


Here's the chain reaction most pre-con teams know but rarely map out explicitly:


1. **Bid-day pressure compresses review time.** Scope packages are written from templates, not drawings. Review happens five minutes before cutoff, if at all.
2. **Vague scopes get priced vaguely.** Subs exclude anything ambiguous to protect margin. Nobody surfaces the ambiguity at bid.
3. **The subcontract is written to match the scope package.** If the scope package has gaps, the subcontract does too.
4. **The gap hits the field.** Work needs to happen. Nobody's scope covers it. An RFI gets issued — sometimes to clarify responsibility, sometimes to set up a change order claim.
5. **The dispute starts.** The GC looks at the subcontract. The sub points to the scope. Nobody has a clear answer because the answer was never written down.


A Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC put it plainly: *"If you miss anything, they'll bill it."*


That billing starts with an RFI.

## Why "As Per Plans and Specs" Is the Most Expensive Phrase in Construction


It's the most common anti-pattern in scope writing. The scope package says "as per plans and specs." The sub prices it that way. Nobody checks whether the plans and specs actually align — or whether they contradict each other.


This phrase doesn't define scope. It defers it. And deferred scope becomes an RFI the moment the field needs an answer.


A Director of Pre-Construction at a Mid-Market Southeast GC described the disconnect this way: *"Pre-con is working in the scope sheet world and project management is working in the scopes of work."*


The gap between those two worlds is where RFIs live.


The Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC calls it the "peanut-butter test": *"It's descriptive — bread, put it on a plate, use the open jar… You have to get to that level of detail or else they'll just be like, 'you didn't tell us that.'"*


Most scope packages don't pass that test.


## The Anti-Patterns That Create RFI Volume


Based on the same 200+ GC interviews,[The Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook) identifies the habits that consistently produce high-RFI projects. These aren't edge cases — they show up across firm size, geography, and project type.


- **"As per plans and specs"** — assigns nothing, assumes everything.
- **Copy-paste from the previous similar job** — last job's scope doesn't match this job's drawings.
- **Junior estimators stuffing previous scopes verbatim** — no drawing review, no reconciliation.
- **The five-minutes-before-bid review** — no time to catch gaps, let alone close them.
- **Generic inferred-scope laundry lists** — broad language that sounds thorough but assigns nothing specifically.
- **Trusting subs on a gentleman's agreement** — works until it doesn't. An Estimating Manager at a Canadian ICI GC noted: *"We have less subs who just kind of a gentleman's agreement… they've become more quick to clarify that we're not including that one piece of scope."*


Each of these habits produces ambiguity. Ambiguity produces RFIs.


## Five Habits That Reduce RFI Volume — Before the Project Starts


The firms with the lowest RFI volume on delivery don't manage RFIs better in the field. They prevent them in pre-con. Here's what they do differently.


### 1. Start With Drawings, Not Boilerplate


Scope packages built from templates carry the previous project's assumptions. Scope packages built from the actual drawing set reflect what this project actually requires. The difference shows up in field RFI counts.


Review the drawings first. Build the scope to match. Then apply the template as a floor — not a starting point.


### 2. Use Specific Document References


Don't write "as per plans and specs." Write "per Structural Drawing S-301, Rev 2, and Spec Section 03300." Specific references leave no room for interpretation disputes. They also make RFI responses faster when questions do arise.


### 3. Front-Load the Buyout Conversations


Scope gaps between bid and buyout are a major RFI driver. When what the sub priced doesn't match what the GC's scope package says, the field pays for it. Front-loading buyout conversations — before award, not after — closes those gaps while there's still budget flexibility to address them.


### 4. Tailor Scopes to Sub Sophistication


A national mechanical contractor reads scope differently than a regional HVAC shop. The level of detail required in the scope package should match the sub's sophistication — and their history of RFI and change order behavior on past projects. Generic scope packages ignore this entirely.


### 5. Run a Pre-Issue Scope Review


Before the scope package goes out, someone who didn't write it should read it against the drawings. Not for grammar. For gaps. This is the checkpoint most teams skip — and it's the cheapest place to catch a $300K problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is the root cause of high RFI volume on construction projects?


Most high-RFI projects trace back to scope gaps created during bid. When scope packages are vague, built from templates rather than drawings, or use language like "as per plans and specs," ambiguity gets baked in. That ambiguity surfaces in the field as RFIs — often weeks or months after the gap was created.


### How does a scope gap become a field RFI?


A scope gap leaves work unassigned or ambiguously assigned. When that work needs to happen in the field, the team issues an RFI to get a direction or establish responsibility. If the subcontract mirrors the vague scope package, that RFI often turns into a change order dispute.


### Which trades produce the most RFIs from scope gaps?


MEP, concrete, steel, and envelope work consistently generate the most scope-gap-driven RFIs. Interface work — where two trades share a boundary — is especially high-risk. Generator field conditioning, base plate grouting, roof cover board, and masonry-storefront flashings are common examples where scope falls through the cracks.


### What does "readily inferable" language mean for GCs?


Many subcontracts include language requiring subs to perform work that is "readily inferable" from the contract documents — even if it isn't explicitly called out in the scope package. In practice, this means the GC absorbs cost for anything the documents imply but the scope doesn't assign. The $300K lead-lined glass example is a real case of this playing out on a hospital project.


### How can pre-construction teams reduce RFI volume before a project starts?


Five habits have the biggest impact: starting scope packages from drawings rather than templates, using specific document references instead of "as per plans and specs," front-loading buyout conversations, tailoring scope detail to sub sophistication, and running a pre-issue scope review before the package goes out.
