# Fire Protection Scope Review: The Trench, Fire-Rated Louvres, and Who Owns What

## TL;DR


- Fire protection scope gaps cluster around three items: the trench, fire-rated louvres, and conduit sleeves through fire-rated assemblies.
- On most ICI projects, no trade claims these items by default — so they land in change orders after bid day.
- The fix is explicit scope language tied to specific drawing and spec references, not blanket "as per plans and specs" inclusions.
- Healthcare construction projects in 2026 have made this worse. Fire-rated assembly requirements are denser, and spec books are longer.
- GCs who resolve ownership before issuing bid packages win buyout battles. GCs who don't fund them out of their own margin.


Fire protection is one of the most litigated scopes in ICI construction. Not because the work is complicated — most sprinkler contractors know their trade cold. The problem is the work that sits *between* trades.


The trench for the underground feed. The fire-rated louvres at mechanical penetrations. The conduit sleeves through fire-rated walls. Every one of these items is real work. Every one of them falls into a gap between the fire protection sub, the mechanical sub, and the general contractor's assumptions on bid day.


This article breaks down where fire protection scope falls apart on commercial and ICI projects — and what GCs can do before the bid goes out to stop funding those gaps after the fact.


---


## Why Fire Protection Scope Is Harder Than It Looks


Fire protection subs price what they see in the fire protection drawings. That's reasonable. But the related work — civil trenching, fire-rated penetration systems, louvre specifications — lives in other drawing sets and other spec divisions.


When a sub prices only Division 21, they miss everything that touches their work but belongs to another division. When the GC's scope template says "complete fire suppression system per plans and specs," it doesn't resolve those gaps. It just defers them.


According to the[Scope Gap Playbook's trade-specific chapter](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) , the fire protection and MEP categories consistently produce some of the highest per-project gap values on ICI work. These aren't rounding errors. They're budgeted items that no one budgets for.


The Arcadis 2025 Global Construction Disputes Report puts the average U.S. construction dispute value at $60.1 million. Errors and omissions in contract documents have been the top dispute cause in six of the last nine years. Fire protection scope — where the documents are routinely split across divisions — feeds directly into that pattern.


## The Three Scope Gaps That Show Up on Every Project


### 1. The Trench: Who Digs It?


The underground water service feeding the sprinkler system has to come from somewhere. On most projects, that means trenching from the site tie-in point to the building. Someone has to dig it, bed it, backfill it, and compact it.


The fire protection sub assumes civil or site covers it. The site sub's scope ends at the building line — or at their contract boundary, which may not match the drawing. The GC assumes someone priced it.


No one priced it.


This is the most common fire protection scope gap on ground-up ICI projects. On a mid-size commercial build, the trench alone can run $40K–$80K depending on depth, backfill requirements, and whether shoring is needed. On a hospital project with a 12-inch underground main, that number climbs fast.


The fix is not complex. In the fire protection scope package, specify:


- Who provides and installs the underground service from the site tie-in to the building entry point
- Who is responsible for trenching, bedding material, backfill, and compaction
- Which drawing sheet governs — civil, mechanical, or fire protection
- Who handles testing and any required inspections for the underground section


Referencing the specific drawing sheet number matters. "As per plans and specs" does not resolve a conflict between the civil plan and the fire protection riser diagram. A senior estimator at a Top-ENR Canadian GC put it bluntly: *"If you miss anything, they'll bill it."*


### 2. Fire-Rated Louvres: MEP or Envelope?


Fire-rated louvres sit at the intersection of three trades. The mechanical sub needs the penetration for airflow. The envelope or masonry sub built the wall the louvre goes into. The fire protection sub — or in some cases the mechanical sub — is responsible for the fire-rated assembly itself.


Who supplies the louvre? Who installs it? Who provides the fire-rated collar or intumescent wrap? Who submits the UL listing documentation?


On most project documents, the louvre shows up in the mechanical drawings with a note that reads something like "provide fire-rated louvre — see spec." The spec section for the louvre is in Division 08 (Openings) or Division 23 (HVAC). The fire-rated assembly requirement is in Division 07 (Fire and Smoke Protection). The structural opening is in the architectural drawings.


Four divisions. Three trades. Zero clear owner.


This gap shows up most often on healthcare and institutional projects — exactly the project types seeing the most activity in 2026. When you have a hospital with 200 rated penetrations, and each one carries an ambiguous supply-and-install question, the exposure adds up fast.


The $300K lead-lined glass omission documented in the Scope Gap Playbook came from a hospital imaging suite — the same project type, the same pattern. Specialty items at rated assemblies don't get priced because no trade claims them. The GC absorbs them under "readily inferable" language.


To close this gap, the scope package for both mechanical and fire protection subs needs to answer these questions explicitly:


- Who supplies fire-rated louvres — mechanical, envelope, or GC-furnished?
- Who installs the rated assembly, including any intumescent components?
- Who is responsible for the UL listing, submittal, and inspection?
- What spec section and drawing reference governs — and which controls in the event of conflict?


### 3. Conduit Sleeves Through Fire-Rated Assemblies


This one lives between electrical and fire protection — and it generates RFIs on almost every ICI project that doesn't resolve it in the scope package.


The electrical sub runs conduit. That conduit passes through a fire-rated wall, floor, or ceiling assembly. Someone has to install a listed fire stop sleeve or intumescent device at each penetration. Someone has to document the installation for the AHJ.


The electrical sub says it's a fire-stopping scope item — not electrical. The fire protection or drywall sub says they don't price penetration fire-stopping unless it's specifically included. The GC's scope template doesn't mention it.


On a mid-rise commercial project, you might have 600–800 rated penetrations. At an average installed cost of $150–$400 per penetration depending on assembly type, the unassigned scope runs $90K–$320K. That's not a rounding error. That's a change order the GC funds.


Resolve this in the pre-bid package. Assign fire-stop sleeves and intumescent devices to a single trade — typically the fire protection sub or a specialty fire-stopping contractor — and include the reference spec section and drawing sheets by number.

## The Anti-Pattern That Makes All of This Worse


The most common mistake GCs make in fire protection scope isn't missing a single item. It's relying on boilerplate scope language that was written for a different project type.


The Scope Gap Playbook identifies "as per plans and specs" as the most-cited anti-pattern in construction scope management. For fire protection, it's especially damaging. Because the fire protection work is documented across five or six divisions, "as per plans and specs" doesn't tell the sub which plans and which specs govern which work.


A second anti-pattern is copy-pasting the scope from the previous hospital project — or the previous office building — without checking whether the new project has the same trade breakdown, the same penetration count, or the same rated assembly requirements.


As one Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC described it, scope has to be descriptive enough that there's no ambiguity: *"It's descriptive — bread, put it on a plate, use the open jar… You have to get to that level of detail or else they'll just be like, 'you didn't tell us that.'"*


That standard applies directly to fire protection. If the scope sheet doesn't specify who supplies the fire-rated louvre and who installs the intumescent collar, expect a conversation on site — and a change order to follow it.

## How the 2026 Healthcare Construction Boom Is Making This Worse


Healthcare construction in North America is running at near-record volume in 2026. Hospital expansions, ambulatory care centers, and imaging suite additions are driving a significant share of ICI project starts.


Healthcare projects have stricter fire protection requirements than standard commercial work. NFPA 101 Life Safety Code compliance, compartmentalization requirements, and smoke control systems make the rated assembly count significantly higher per square foot.


A commercial office tower might have 400 rated penetrations. A same-size hospital wing might have 1,200. The louvre count triples. The conduit sleeve count triples. The buyout risk scales with it.


At the same time, spec books on healthcare projects have grown. Addenda are longer. Owner requirements for third-party commissioning, TAB, and fire protection testing have expanded. Fire protection subs are pricing more carefully — and excluding more items by default.


One Estimating Manager at a Canadian ICI GC described the shift: *"We have less subs who just kind of a gentleman's agreement… they've become more quick to clarify that we're not including that one piece of scope."*


That's not a complaint — it's a business reality. Subs learned to protect their margin. GCs need to match that discipline in how they write scope packages.

## What a Tight Fire Protection Scope Package Covers


A bid-ready fire protection scope package should resolve ownership before the sub prices the work. Here's what that looks like in practice:


### Underground Service and Trenching


- Confirm the contract boundary for the underground service line
- Assign trenching, bedding, backfill, and compaction to a named trade
- Reference the governing drawing sheet by number — civil or fire protection plan
- Specify who handles pressure testing and inspection of the underground section


### Fire-Rated Louvres at Mechanical Penetrations


- Name the trade responsible for supply and installation
- Identify the applicable spec division and UL assembly listing
- Confirm who provides the rated collar, sleeve, or intumescent device
- Assign the submittal and AHJ documentation responsibility


### Conduit and Pipe Sleeves Through Rated Assemblies


- Assign fire-stop devices to a single trade or specialty sub
- Reference the Division 07 spec section by number
- Define the documentation requirement — who maintains the penetration log for AHJ review
- Address coordination with the drywall and electrical subs on scheduling


### Sprinkler System Commissioning and Testing


- Confirm who provides the water for hydrostatic testing
- Assign the cost of disposal or drainage for test water
- Define who attends the AHJ inspection — and who pays for re-inspection if required


This isn't an exhaustive list — every project has its own wrinkles. But these items appear on nearly every ICI project, and they're almost never addressed in a boilerplate scope template. Use your templates as a floor, not a ceiling. That's Habit 4 from the Eight Habits in the Scope Gap Playbook.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is fire protection scope review for general contractors?


Fire protection scope review is the process of defining — before bid day — which trade is responsible for each item in the fire suppression and rated assembly scope. This includes underground service, trenching, fire-rated louvres, conduit sleeves, and commissioning. GCs conduct this review to prevent unpriced scope from becoming change orders during construction.


### Who is responsible for the trench for the underground sprinkler feed?


This depends on how the GC assigns it in the scope package. By default, no trade claims it. The fire protection sub prices the pipe and equipment. The site sub may stop at the building line. GCs must assign trenching, bedding, backfill, and compaction to a named trade in writing, with a specific drawing reference, before issuing bid documents.


### Who supplies and installs fire-rated louvres on ICI projects?


Fire-rated louvres typically fall between the mechanical sub, the envelope sub, and the fire protection sub. No trade claims them by default. The GC must name the responsible trade in the scope package and reference the applicable spec section — usually Division 07, 08, or 23 — along with the UL assembly listing and submittal requirements.


### What are conduit sleeve fire stops, and who installs them?


Conduit sleeves through fire-rated walls, floors, or ceilings require a listed fire stop device — typically an intumescent sleeve or collar — at each penetration. The electrical sub runs the conduit. The fire-stopping scope is separate. Unless explicitly assigned in the scope package, no trade prices it. On mid-rise ICI projects, unassigned penetration fire-stops can represent $90K–$320K in unpriced work.


### Why do fire protection scope gaps increase on healthcare projects?


Healthcare projects must comply with NFPA 101 and typically require significantly more rated assemblies per square foot than commercial office work. A hospital wing may have three times the penetration count of an equivalent office floor. More penetrations means more louvres, more conduit sleeves, and more rated assembly conflicts — all of which require explicit scope assignment before bid day.


### How does "as per plans and specs" create fire protection disputes?


Fire protection work is documented across multiple spec divisions — 07, 08, 21, 23, and 26 at minimum. When a scope sheet says "as per plans and specs" without specifying which division governs which item, subs price only what's in their trade division. Items that span multiple divisions — louvres, fire stops, underground service — fall through the gap and become change orders.
