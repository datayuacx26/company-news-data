## Why Bid Document Analysis Is a Separate Job From Takeoff


Takeoff answers one question: what's the quantity? Bid document analysis answers a different one: what are we actually agreeing to build?


Those are not the same question. And conflating them is how scope gaps get born.


A $300K lead-lined glass scope item was once missed on a hospital imaging suite because it appeared in a spec section nobody cross-referenced against the drawings. The GC absorbed it under "readily inferable" language. That's not a takeoff error — it's a review failure.


A $400K roof cover board was missed on a $50M project. The drawings showed the assembly. The spec called it out. But the scope package didn't capture it. The GC recovered it only through a relational concession from the sub.


These gaps don't show up in your quantity spreadsheet. They show up on your P&L at closeout.


FMI estimates that miscommunication and bad project data drive $31 billion in annual U.S. rework costs. Twenty-six percent of that rework traces directly to communication breakdowns in the early stages of a project — stages that start with how well you read the documents before you bid.

## Step 1: Orient to the Project Set Before You Review Anything


Before you open a single drawing or spec section, take 20 minutes to map what you've received.


### Project Set Intake Checklist


- Confirm the document list against the bid invitation — are all drawing disciplines included?
- Note the drawing issue dates — are civil, structural, architectural, and MEP all on the same revision?
- Check for addenda — has the owner issued any? Are they all in your set?
- Identify the spec book structure — CSI MasterFormat? Proprietary? Confirm Division 1 is present.
- Locate the bid form — this tells you what the owner expects you to price, which is not always what the drawings show.
- Find the substitution and alternates sections — these affect scope and pricing strategy.

## Step 2: Read Division 1 — All of It


Division 1 (General Requirements) is the most skipped section in the bid process. It's also the section that creates the most GC exposure.


This is where owners embed:


- Allowances and unit prices that affect your bid total
- Schedule requirements — liquidated damages, milestone dates, phasing
- Insurance and bonding thresholds
- Subcontractor substitution rights
- Owner-furnished equipment scope
- Project closeout and commissioning obligations
- Wage and labor compliance requirements


The anti-pattern that kills GCs here is treating Division 1 as background noise. It isn't. A Senior PM at a Canadian ICI GC described what happens when clients push scope boundaries: "Our construction management clients expect us to find the scope gaps in the design too now. They expect us to be designers and engineers."


Division 1 is where those expectations get written into your contract. Read it before you price anything.


### Division 1 Red Flags to Flag Immediately


- Liquidated damages above $5,000/day — flag for your legal team
- "Contractor shall be responsible for all work reasonably inferable" — this language shifts significant scope risk to you
- Indeterminate allowances — make sure your bid form handles them correctly
- Commissioning requirements that aren't in the MEP scope — common on institutional projects
- Submittal turnaround requirements that affect your schedule assumption


If you want a deeper breakdown of how "readily inferable" language creates GC exposure, the[Scope Gap Playbook chapter on subcontract language](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/subcontract-language-scope) covers it in detail with real operator examples.

## Step 3: Cross-Reference Drawings Across Disciplines


Drawing conflicts between disciplines are one of the most reliable sources of bid-day scope gaps. Civil and structural don't always agree with architectural. MEP coordination drawings get issued on a different timeline. Details contradict plan notes.


A $45K stone-depth mismatch between civil/structural and architectural drawings on a single slab showed up only when the field crew went to install. Nobody caught it at bid because nobody compared those two drawing sets directly.


### Cross-Discipline Review Checklist


- **Architectural vs. Structural:** Check slab thicknesses, opening dimensions, and column locations. Discrepancies in these areas are common and expensive.
- **Civil vs. Architectural:** Confirm finished floor elevations, site grades, and utility stub locations agree.
- **Structural vs. MEP:** Check penetration locations, beam depths vs. duct runs, and equipment pad locations.
- **Architectural vs. MEP:** Verify ceiling heights accommodate duct and pipe runs without conflicts.
- **All disciplines vs. Specs:** Cross-reference spec section numbers in drawing notes — confirm the spec section actually exists in the book.

## Step 4: Identify Trade Handoff Zones


The most expensive scope gaps don't live in the middle of a trade's scope. They live at the boundaries — where one sub ends and the next begins.


These are the handoff zones the[Scope Gap Playbook's trade-specific chapter](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) documents in detail. Here are the highest-risk zones by trade:


### Site / Earthwork


- The five-foot rule transition — who owns earthwork within five feet of the structure?
- Geotech compaction requirements — are they in the civil spec or the soils report?
- Excavator unit rates — confirm these won't be exploited on changed conditions


### Concrete


- Base plate grouting — drawings often show it; scope packages often miss it
- Concrete pumping — who pays if crane access isn't viable?
- Slab assembly conflicts between structural and architectural drawings


### Steel


- Embeds and anchor bolts — are they in the structural steel scope or the concrete scope?
- Hoisting and rigging — confirm who provides the crane for steel placement
- Miscellaneous metals — these scatter across multiple drawing sets and often fall between subs


### MEP


- Generator field conditioning — this is one of the most commonly disputed MEP items on institutional projects
- Motor starters — confirm whether they're in the electrical or mechanical scope
- Fire-rated louvres — mechanical or envelope sub?
- The trench — plumbing rough-in scope vs. concrete restoration scope


### Envelope


- Roof cover board — it's in the spec assembly; it disappears in scope packages
- Masonry-to-storefront flashings — who owns the transition?
- Fire stopping — this migrates between drywall, mechanical, and electrical subs depending on location


A Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC put it plainly: "If you miss anything, they'll bill it." The subs are watching these handoffs now more carefully than ever — the days of gentleman's agreements on ambiguous scope items are over.

## Step 5: Review All Addenda — In Order


Addenda are the section most likely to get skimmed. They're also the section that most often changes your scope, your schedule, and your price.


Review each addendum in the order it was issued. Changes in Addendum 2 sometimes supersede Addendum 1. Scope clarifications in Addendum 3 sometimes re-open scope questions you thought were closed.


### Addenda Review Checklist


- Log every addendum by number and date
- Note any scope changes — added or deleted work
- Check for revised drawings — confirm you have the latest revision of each sheet
- Flag any changes to the bid date or bid form
- Note any substitution approvals that affect your pricing
- Check for clarifications that resolve RFIs — these sometimes change your scope assumption


If an addendum revises a drawing sheet, make sure your takeoff is based on the revised sheet, not the original. This sounds obvious. It isn't, when you're under deadline pressure on three pursuits at once.

## Step 6: Audit Your Scope Package Against the Documents


Once you've completed your review, compare your scope package — or your subcontract scopes — against what the documents actually require.


The anti-pattern here is the boilerplate scope package. Copying last project's drywall scope onto this project's drywall scope works fine until the specs require wall blocking that wasn't in the last job. Or the hollow metal door frames are in a different sub's scope on this project.


As an Estimating Manager at a Canadian ICI GC described it: "Pre-con is working in the scope sheet world and project management is working in the scopes of work." The gap between those two worlds is where change orders get born.


### Scope Audit Checklist


- Every spec section referenced in the drawings has a home in your scope breakdown
- Every trade handoff zone is explicitly assigned — no shared assumptions
- Exclusions are stated in plain language, not just implied
- Owner-furnished items are documented and don't appear in your scope
- Alternates are priced separately and clearly identified in the bid form

## Step 7: Log Your RFIs Before Bid Day


Every gap, conflict, or ambiguity you find during your review is a potential RFI. Don't save these for bid day morning.


Log them as you go. Submit them as early as the bid schedule allows. If the owner doesn't respond before bid day, that ambiguity becomes your exposure — or your clarification and exclusion.


### RFI Log Structure


- Issue number and date submitted
- Drawing or spec reference
- Specific question (one question per RFI — don't bundle them)
- Impact on scope or price if unresolved
- Response received (and date)


A structured RFI log also gives you documentation. If a dispute arises at buyout or in the field, you have evidence that you flagged the ambiguity before you bid.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is the most important section of a bid document to review first?


Division 1 (General Requirements) carries the most contract risk and is most often skipped. It contains liquidated damages, inferred-scope language, schedule obligations, and owner-furnished equipment scope. Read it before you start takeoff. Errors here compound across every other section of the bid.


### How long should a thorough bid document review take?


A thorough manual review of a complex commercial project set takes 30–40 hours. Most estimating teams don't have that on every pursuit. Prioritizing Division 1, cross-discipline conflicts, addenda, and trade handoff zones gives you the highest return on review time. AI-assisted review can cut that time significantly without sacrificing coverage.


### What are the most common scope gaps found in bid documents?


The most common gaps are at trade handoff zones — where one sub's scope ends and another's begins. High-risk areas include MEP-to-envelope transitions, base plate grouting, roof cover board assemblies, and generator field conditioning. These gaps rarely appear in takeoff. They appear at buyout and in the field.


### What is "readily inferable" language and why does it matter?


"Readily inferable" language in Division 1 or supplementary conditions shifts scope risk to the GC. It means the contractor is responsible for work that a competent contractor should have anticipated, even if it isn't explicitly shown or specified. This language has caused six- and seven-figure losses on commercial projects when estimators missed scope that was "implied" by the documents.


### When should RFIs be submitted during the bid process?


Submit RFIs as early as the bid schedule allows — ideally in the first half of the bid period. Late RFIs often go unanswered before bid day. If you don't get a response, the ambiguity becomes your exposure. A structured RFI log also gives you documentation if disputes arise at buyout or during construction.


### How do addenda affect bid document review?


Addenda can change scope, revise drawings, modify the bid form, or alter schedule requirements. Review each addendum in the order it was issued. Confirm you're working from the latest drawing revision on every sheet. An addendum issued three days before bid day can invalidate hours of takeoff if you miss it.
