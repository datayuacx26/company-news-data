## Why Spec Risk Is Different From Contract Risk


Most GCs treat contract review and spec review as separate exercises. They're not — at least not from a risk standpoint.


The general conditions set the rules. But the specs — particularly Division 1 and the supplementary conditions — define the scope, shift responsibilities, and quietly expand your obligations. A requirement buried in a spec section can be just as binding as a clause in the contract itself.


The classic trap: a GC receives a 2,000-page project manual. Estimators pull the trade sections they need for takeoff. Nobody reads Section 01 50 00 in full. Nobody flags the sentence that makes the GC responsible for third-party commissioning services. By the time the project is awarded, that cost isn't in anyone's number.


That's how a $300K lead-lined glass package gets missed on a hospital imaging suite. The GC absorbed it under "readily inferable" language — language that was in the spec, not the contract. (Source: *The Scope Gap Playbook* , Provision, 2026.)

## Step 1: Read the Supplementary Conditions Before Anything Else


The supplementary conditions are the highest-risk document in almost any project set — and the most frequently skimmed.


They modify the general conditions. That means every deviation, addition, or deletion to the standard contract terms lives here. Owners and their lawyers use this document to shift risk. It's the section that:


- Expands the definition of "work" beyond what the drawings show
- Modifies indemnification, insurance, and warranty language
- Introduces concealed conditions clauses or waives them
- Changes the process for change orders and claims
- Adds liquidated damages rates or tightens cure periods


Read these line by line. Flag anything that adds scope, limits your rights, or introduces a financial penalty. Don't delegate this to a junior estimator — this document requires someone who knows what standard looks like so they can spot what isn't standard.


### What to flag in supplementary conditions


- **Expanded "work" definitions** — does the clause add obligations beyond what's shown in the drawings?
- **Modified notice requirements** — shortened timelines for RFIs, claims, or change requests kill your ability to recover costs later
- **Altered consequential damages language** — mutual waivers are standard; one-sided waivers are not
- **Non-standard insurance requirements** — excess limits, additional insured terms, or wrap-up exclusions that affect your cost
- **Dispute resolution changes** — arbitration clauses, venue selection, and governing law all affect your exposure

## Step 2: Read Division 1 as a Scope Document, Not an Admin Document


Division 1 is where owners hide operational requirements that have real cost implications. Most estimators treat it as boilerplate. That's the mistake.


A Pre-Construction Lead at a Top-ENR Canadian GC put it plainly: *"If you miss anything, they'll bill it."*


Division 1 sections that carry the most cost and scope risk include:


Section Common Risk What to check


01 10 00 – Summary Scope boundary ambiguity How is "work" defined? What's specifically excluded?


01 14 00 – Work Restrictions Sequencing and access constraints Phasing requirements, noise bylaws, restricted hours


01 25 00 – Substitution Procedures Specified-product lock-in Substitution deadlines, approval process, cost responsibility


01 31 00 – Project Management Meeting and reporting obligations BIM requirements, schedule of values format, software mandates


01 45 00 – Quality Control Third-party testing responsibility Who pays for testing? Who pays for re-testing on failure?


01 50 00 – Temporary Facilities Site services and protection costs Hoarding, power, water, dumpsters — who provides, who pays


01 77 00 – Closeout Commissioning and documentation Training, O&M manuals, as-built drawing requirements


01 78 39 – Record Documents As-built obligations Accuracy requirements, format, submission timeline


Don't just read these sections. Check whether they conflict with what the trade specs say. Conflicts between Division 1 and a trade section almost always resolve in favor of the more stringent requirement — and that's a cost you need to catch before bid day.

## Step 3: Map Scope Obligations Across Documents


Risk in construction specs rarely lives in one place. It's distributed — a requirement mentioned in the structural drawings, modified in the civil specs, and re-stated differently in Division 1.


This is where scope gaps form. The Scope Gap Playbook research found a **$45K stone-depth mismatch** between civil/structural and architectural drawings on a single slab. Neither document was wrong in isolation. The conflict only appeared when someone read them side by side.


A Director of Pre-Construction at a Mid-Market Southeast GC described the systemic problem: *"Pre-con is working in the scope sheet world and project management is working in the scopes of work."* The handoff is where the risk hides.


### How to map scope across the document set


1. **List every trade on the project.** Use the CSI divisions as your framework.
2. **For each trade, identify which documents define their scope.** Start with drawings, then specs, then Division 1, then any addenda.
3. **Flag every instance where two documents describe the same work differently.** Even minor differences in language can create disputes at buyout.
4. **Check addenda last.** Addenda modify everything that came before. A change in Addendum 3 can override a spec section and a drawing note simultaneously.

## Step 4: Flag "Readily Inferable" and "As Required" Language


These two phrases are the most expensive words in construction contracts. They shift scope onto the GC without specifying what that scope is.


"Readily inferable" language holds the GC responsible for work that a competent contractor should have anticipated — even if it's not explicitly shown or called out. Courts and arbitrators have consistently upheld this interpretation. The practical result: you own the gap.


A Senior PM at a Canadian ICI GC described the current environment: *"Our construction management clients expect us to find the scope gaps in the design too now. They expect us to be designers and engineers."*


The $300K lead-lined glass package mentioned earlier was absorbed under exactly this kind of language. The spec referenced the imaging suite. The drawings showed the room. Nobody connected the two until the sub's exclusion list came in after award.


### Language patterns to flag automatically


- "…and all work reasonably inferable therefrom"
- "…as required to complete the work"
- "…including all incidental work necessary"
- "…complete and in accordance with the intent of the contract documents"
- "…whether or not specifically shown or described"
- "…to the satisfaction of the owner/consultant"


When you find this language, stop and ask: what work could this clause pull in that isn't explicitly priced? If you can't answer that question, you have a risk item that needs a clarification or exclusion before you submit.

## Step 5: Build a Trade-by-Trade Risk Register


Generic risk reviews produce generic findings. The most useful spec reviews are organized by trade — because the risks cluster differently depending on what you're looking at.


Based on patterns identified across hundreds of project reviews, these are the trade sections that consistently hide contract risk:


### Site and Earthwork


Check who bears the risk for subsurface conditions. Look for compaction specs that conflict with geotech recommendations. Watch for the "five-foot transition" clause — the grey zone between the site contractor's scope and the structural backfill spec. That gap has cost GCs real money.


### Concrete


Who pays for concrete pumping? Is grouting of base plates explicitly included in the structural steel spec or the concrete spec — or neither? Slab assembly conflicts between the structural and architectural drawings are common and expensive.


### Mechanical, Electrical, and Plumbing


Generator field conditioning is one of the most consistently disputed MEP items. The spec often calls for it. The sub rarely includes it. The GC is usually stuck in the middle. Also check: motor starters, fire-rated louvres, lighting controls, and who owns the trench between trades.


These trade-specific patterns are documented in detail in[Chapter 5 of The Scope Gap Playbook](https://provision.com/ebooks/scope-gap-playbook/trade-specific-scope-gaps) .


### Envelope


Roof cover board is a recurring gap — a $400K miss on a $50M project is documented in the Scope Gap Playbook research. Fire-stopping at the envelope interface is another. Caulking scope — interior vs. exterior, who owns the masonry-to-storefront transition — creates disputes at buyout every time it's not explicitly assigned.

## Step 6: Run a Checklist Against Every Risk Category


A structured checklist beats a narrative review every time. Checklists are faster, auditable, and don't rely on one person's memory or expertise on a given day.


The categories your checklist should cover:


- **Scope definitions** — is the work boundary explicit or ambiguous?
- **Risk-shifting language** — indemnification, consequential damages, force majeure
- **Notice and claims requirements** — timelines, format, escalation path
- **Liquidated damages** — rate, cap, trigger conditions
- **Concealed conditions** — what's covered, what's excluded, differing site conditions language
- **Payment terms** — schedule of values, holdback, pay-when-paid vs. pay-if-paid
- **Termination rights** — for convenience vs. for cause, notice periods
- **Insurance and bonding** — limits, additional insured requirements, wrap-up programs
- **Warranty** — duration, scope, correction-of-work obligations
- **Dispute resolution** — arbitration vs. litigation, seat of arbitration, governing law

## Step 7: Document Every Risk Before You Bid or Execute


Finding a risk item is only useful if you do something with it. That means documenting every flagged item in a format that survives the handoff from pre-con to project management.


A risk log should include:


- The document, section, and page where the risk appears
- The nature of the risk (scope ambiguity, cost exposure, schedule risk, legal risk)
- The dollar exposure — estimated, even if rough
- The recommended action (clarification, exclusion, pricing allowance, escalation)
- Who owns the action item and by when


This log becomes your pre-bid risk summary for leadership review. It also becomes the starting point for your subcontract scope packages — so the risks you flagged at the spec review stage get addressed at buyout, not in a change order six months later.

## The Anti-Patterns That Kill Spec Reviews


Even experienced teams fall into habits that undermine the spec review process. The most common ones, drawn from 200+ GC interviews in The Scope Gap Playbook:


- **"As per plans and specs."** — The most-cited anti-pattern. It incorporates risk without identifying it.
- **The five-minutes-before-bid review.** — A rushed read produces false confidence, not a real risk picture.
- **Copy-paste from the last similar job.** — Every project set is different. The risk profile is different.
- **Junior estimators reviewing supplementary conditions.** — You need someone who knows what standard looks like to spot what isn't.
- **Trusting subs on a gentleman's agreement.** — As one Estimating Manager at a Canadian ICI GC noted: subs *"have become more quick to clarify that we're not including that one piece of scope."* The handshake era is over.

## FAQ


### What is construction contract risk in specs?


Construction contract risk in specs refers to clauses, language, or scope definitions that expose the GC to unpriced cost, legal liability, or schedule penalties. It appears most often in supplementary conditions, Division 1, and trade-specific spec sections that include ambiguous scope language or risk-shifting terms.


### What is the biggest source of contract disputes in construction?


According to the Arcadis 2025 Global Construction Disputes Report, errors and omissions in contract documents have been the #1 dispute cause for 6 of the last 9 years. The average U.S. dispute value in 2026 is $60.1 million. Most of these disputes originate in pre-construction document review failures.


### What does "readily inferable" mean in a construction contract?


"Readily inferable" language holds the GC responsible for work that a competent contractor should have anticipated — even if it's not shown on the drawings or called out in the specs. It's one of the most common sources of scope gaps and change order disputes. Any contract containing this language requires a detailed scope cross-check before bid.


### Which spec sections carry the most risk for a GC?


The supplementary conditions carry the highest risk overall. In the spec book, Division 1 — especially sections covering summary of work, temporary facilities, quality control, and closeout — is where owners embed operational requirements with real cost implications. Trade sections for MEP, envelope, and earthwork consistently produce the most costly scope gaps.


### How long should a contract and spec risk review take?


A thorough manual review of a mid-size commercial project spec book takes 30 to 40 hours per bid. That's the baseline. ### What's the difference between a contract review and a spec risk review?


A contract review focuses on the legal and commercial terms — indemnification, payment, termination, insurance. A spec risk review focuses on scope obligations and cost exposure embedded in the technical documents. Both are required. Doing only one leaves risk on the table. The two reviews are most effective when done in parallel and cross-referenced at the end.
