If you’ve been shopping for period pads in the last few years, you’ve probably noticed a shift. The aisle that used to be a wall of pastel plastic now has a bunch of beige, earthy-looking boxes promising “100% cotton,” “organic,” “natural,” and “skin-friendly.” Cotton pads are having a moment, and they’ve been positioned as the obvious upgrade over conventional pads.


But are cotton period pads actually better for your body? And if you’re not using cotton, what *should* you look for?


Let’s get into it.


## What are cotton period pads, exactly?


“Cotton period pads” is a bit of a catch-all term. Most cotton pads on the market today fall into one of three buckets:


1. **Conventional cotton pads:** Pads with some cotton in the construction, often blended with synthetic materials like rayon and a plastic backing. The “cotton” usually refers to the top sheet, not the full pad.
2. **Organic cotton period pads:** Pads made with cotton grown without synthetic pesticides, often certified by[GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard)](https://global-standard.org/) . These are what most “clean” period brands sell.
3. **100% cotton or “cotton core” pads:** Pads that try to use cotton throughout, including the absorbent core, often *without* superabsorbent polymers (SAP).


The pitch is the same across the board: cotton is natural, breathable, biodegradable, and gentler on sensitive skin. And cotton is real. It’s a plant fiber, it’s familiar, and it’s been used for absorbent products for centuries.


But cotton listed in the ingredients doesn’t tell you very much about whether a pad is actually safer, cleaner, or more comfortable. Here’s what the research says:


## Why people are turning away from conventional pads in the first place


Before we get to cotton specifically, it’s worth understanding why the ‘switch to cleaner pads’ conversation exists at all.


Here’s a fun fact: vulvar and vaginal tissue is *significantly* more absorbent than the skin on the rest of your body. Some research suggests that absorption rates from internal vaginal exposure may be[10 to 80 times higher](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9876534/) than through skin elsewhere on the body. That means whatever is in your period product gets a much faster, more direct route into your system than, say, the lotion you put on your arm.


What kind of stuff are we worried about? Frankly, a lot. Independent testing over the last decade has flagged:


- **PFAS (forever chemicals)** in pads, tampons, period underwear, and liners. A widely cited Mamavation/EHN investigation reported on by the[New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/wirecutter/blog/forever-chemicals-in-period-incontinence-products/) found PFAS in roughly 48% of sanitary pads tested.
- **VOCs (volatile organic compounds)** like toluene, xylene, and methylene chloride. A[2020 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0160412020303494) found that menstrual pads had some of the highest VOC-related hazard ratios among feminine hygiene products tested.
- **Phthalates and dioxins** linked to fragrance, plastic components, and chlorine-bleaching[residues](https://obgyn.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1471-0528.17668) .
- **Heavy metals** , such as[arsenic and lead](https://publichealth.berkeley.edu/articles/spotlight/research/first-study-to-measure-toxic-metals-in-tampons-shows-arsenic-and-lead) , including in some products labeled “organic.”


This is the backdrop against which cotton started to get trendy. But cotton in and of itself is not necessarily free of any of these contaminants, especially when you start to examine how it’s grown and processed en route from plant to wearable fiber. Let’s take a closer look.


## The case for cotton pads (and where it falls short)


### What cotton does well


Cotton is a natural fiber. It’s soft, breathable, biodegradable, and free from the synthetic-fiber concerns associated with materials like conventional rayon blends. GOTS-certified organic cotton, in particular, is grown without synthetic pesticides and is subject to residue testing through the supply chain. That’s a meaningful improvement over a lot of conventional period products.


For people with very sensitive skin, switching from a fragranced, plastic-coated conventional pad to an unscented organic cotton pad can absolutely make a noticeable difference. That part isn’t hype.


### Where the cotton story gets more complicated


Here’s what you won’t see in marketing materials for cotton pads:


**Cotton is the most chemically-intensive crop on the planet:** Conventional cotton occupies just 2.4–2.5% of the world’s arable land but accounts for roughly[16% of global insecticide use and 6% of all pesticide use](https://sustainfashion.info/the-worlds-dirtiest-crop-pesticide-use-in-cotton-production/) , according to the Environmental Justice Foundation.


The[UN Environment Programme](https://council.science/blog/the-environmental-impact-of-cotton-production/) estimates cotton cultivation consumes around 200,000 tons of pesticides and 8 million tons of fertilizers globally each year. That’s why conventional cotton pads aren’t automatically the earth-friendly option people assume.


**Pesticide residues can show up in finished cotton products:** A[2024 study](https://www.natracare.com/blog/tampon-study-shows-toxic-pesticides/) by the Women’s Environmental Network (WEN) and Pesticide Action Network UK, titled *Blood, Sweat, and Pesticides* , tested 15 boxes of mainstream non-organic tampons sold in UK supermarkets. Researchers found glyphosate (a weed killer the World Health Organization’s IARC classified as a[probable human carcinogen](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/MonographVolume112-1.pdf) in 2015) at levels 40 times higher than what’s legally permitted in UK drinking water.


A separate[preliminary study](https://detoxproject.org/argentinian-study-tampons-sanitary-pads-and-sterile-gauze-contaminated-with-probable-carcinogen-glyphosate/) from researchers at the University of La Plata in Argentina detected glyphosate and its breakdown product AMPA in 85% of tampon, pad, wipe, and gauze samples tested. So while organic cotton is meaningfully cleaner, “cotton” on its own isn’t a guarantee of anything. Unless a pad is certified organic (and ideally tested for residues), it could be carrying pesticide load.


**Cotton holds moisture against your skin:** This is the one that gets glossed over the most. Cotton is absorbent, but it isn’t *very* absorbent compared to modern superabsorbent polymers, and it doesn’t lock moisture away from the skin. Cotton tends to hold fluid in the fibers themselves, which means a pad with a cotton core can feel damp against your vulva even when it isn’t “full.”


That matters because heat plus moisture against vulvar skin is the recipe for irritation.[Dermatology research](https://www.healthline.com/health/rashes-from-pads) consistently identifies friction, moisture, and trapped heat as primary drivers of vulvar contact dermatitis from pads, separate from any specific chemical irritant. A pad that feels wet for hours is a pad that can cause rashes, even if its ingredient list is clean.


**“All cotton” pads often skip the structural materials that make pads actually work:** Pads need a waterproof backing to prevent leaks. They need adhesive to stay put. They need wings, channels, or shaped construction to fit a body that moves. Some 100%-cotton pads compromise on those, which is part of why so many “clean” pad reviews mention bunching, shifting, and leaks. (Sound familiar? Flex founder Lauren[wrote about exactly this experience](https://flexfits.com/blog/launching-bamboo-period-pads-and-liners/) before we developed our own line.)


### The honest bottom line on cotton


Organic cotton pads are a real upgrade over conventional ones, especially if your previous pad had fragrance, dyes, or wasn’t tested for PFAS. But cotton by itself is not a synonym for safer, more absorbent, or more comfortable. It just means cotton.


The actual question worth asking isn’t *“is this pad made of cotton?”*


It’s *“what’s in this pad, what isn’t, and does it perform the way I need it to?”*

## How to actually evaluate a pad (cotton or not)


If you’re trying to make a smarter pad choice, here’s what to look for instead of stopping at the word “cotton”:


- **Fragrance and herb-free.** Both are common irritants. Even “natural” herbs like aloe and chamomile can trigger reactions on sensitive vulvar skin.
- **PFAS-tested, with results published or available on request.** “Free from forever chemicals” should be backed by independent lab data.
- **Free from dyes, elemental chlorine, and added deodorants.**
- **A breathable, moisture-wicking top layer** , whether that’s organic cotton or another plant-derived material. The key is what happens to the moisture, not just what the cover is called.
- **Real absorbency claims with real numbers** (mL or g), not just “heavy” or “overnight.”
- **Hypoallergenic and dermatologist-tested** , especially if you have sensitive skin or a history of contact dermatitis.


And one more thing worth saying out loud: the regulatory gap around period products is real. In the US, pads and tampons are regulated as medical devices by the FDA, but[there are no mandatory tests for pesticide residues, PFAS, or many chemical contaminants](https://www.natracare.com/blog/tampon-study-shows-toxic-pesticides/) in finished products. That’s why independent testing and brand transparency matter so much. If a brand isn’t willing to publish what’s in their pad, that’s information too.
