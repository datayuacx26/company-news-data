# AI keyword research: How to use free chatbot tools

AI chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude can generate a list of keyword ideas in seconds. While it's tempting to treat that list as finished keyword research, a plausible-sounding keyword isn't the same as a validated one. And building content around unvalidated keywords wastes real time and budget.


Below, we test five free chatbots for AI keyword research, then show how to separate the useful suggestions from the noise using real search data.


## Can you use AI chatbots for keyword research?


Yes, you can use AI chatbots for keyword research. But it's important to understand the technology's strengths and weaknesses before getting started.


Generally, AI chatbots are good at identifying common terms found in written content because they learn from large datasets.


Chatbots are also good at understanding keyword meanings and relationships, thanks to their natural language processing and machine learning algorithms.


That means you can ask a free chatbot like ChatGPT to provide keyword ideas based on a particular topic and get a list of related keywords.


You can also ask AI chatbots to analyze search intent.


However, unlike[keyword research tools](https://www.semrush.com/blog/keyword-research-tools/) , chatbots don't have direct access to search engine data. That means chatbots can't reliably tell you how popular a keyword is or how difficult it is to rank for. And this can make it hard to prioritize keywords effectively.


That's why many marketers use AI tools in combination with keyword research tools — something we’ll discuss more later.


## 5 free chatbots for AI keyword research


You can use free chatbots for AI keyword research like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, and Copilot.


Here are some quick tips for success with any of those options:


- Write clear, conversational prompts and include any relevant context
- Upload supplementary files (like keywords you’re already ranking for) when possible and relevant
- If the tool has web access, consider asking the tool to refer to your and your competitors' domains for information
- Remember that AI chatbots can hallucinate (provide false information)
- Refine the output by providing clear follow-up prompts
- Experiment with different models, search settings, and other options to see what gives the best results


Below, we’ll show you how each tool responded when we tested it with two prompts:


1. Please provide keyword ideas for a blog post about AI keyword research
2. What is the intent behind someone searching "AI keyword research"?


Each prompt shows you the type of information you might get from different chatbots. Just bear in mind that your results will vary.


### ChatGPT


We asked[ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) to provide keyword ideas for a blog post about AI keyword research. The chatbot gave us roughly 70 unique keywords, split into lists like primary keywords, tool-focused keywords, and question keywords.


While some of the keyword suggestions were relevant, a beginner might find this list overwhelming. You might consider using a follow-up prompt to narrow down your list. (We’ll show you later how you can organize large lists of keywords like the above.)


And some of the[primary keyword](https://www.semrush.com/blog/primary-keywords/) suggestions deviated from the main topic (e.g., “AI SEO keyword research” and “AI keyword research” both have different[search intents](https://www.semrush.com/blog/search-intent/) — the reason behind a search).


Combining keywords with different intents means your content won't fully match what the reader is looking for, which can hurt conversions and potentially rankings.


We then followed up by asking ChatGPT to evaluate the intent behind someone searching "AI keyword research."


The tool explained that the keyword is mainly informational, but also highlighted how the query can have several search intents.


These outputs demonstrate that you need a good grasp of[keyword strategy](https://www.semrush.com/blog/keyword-strategy/) basics to get the most out of AI keyword research.


### Claude


When we asked[Claude](https://claude.ai/) to provide keyword ideas for a blog post about AI keyword research, the chatbot gave us a list of roughly 40 keywords. The primary keywords were very similar to the ones we got from ChatGPT.


Unlike the other chatbots we tested, Claude was the only tool that noted keyword data isn't something a chatbot can do.


When we asked Claude about the keyword’s intent, it gave us a similar answer to ChatGPT by stating that this keyword has mixed intent.


### Gemini


[Google's Gemini chatbot](https://gemini.google.com/app) listed around 20 keywords for AI keyword research, split into four sections:


- High-intent/primary keywords
- Informational and beginner queries
- Actionable “how-to” and workflow keywords
- Commercial and tool comparison terms


When we asked Gemini to analyze the intent behind "AI keyword research," the chatbot said it’s mostly commercial — a completely different answer than Claude and ChatGPT.


Getting intent wrong isn't a minor slip. If you write commercially-angled content for what's actually an informational query, you'll likely see high bounce rates even if you rank.


Gemini’s answer highlights why you can’t rely on chatbots for keyword research: These tools don't have the search data that keyword tools have, and their analysis can be wrong.


### Perplexity


[Perplexity](https://www.perplexity.ai/) gave us 20 keywords for “AI keyword research,” broken into three lists:


- Primary keywords
- Supporting keywords
- Topic-cluster ideas


One advantage of Perplexity is that it provides a list of web sources by default.


Viewing these sources can provide some useful insight into competing content. Although, it's better to review top-ranking results on Google's SERP if that's where you want to rank — the same principle behind the validation step below.


While Perplexity's list was more manageable than the lists from ChatGPT and Claude, fewer keywords means less raw material to work with. This is fine if you only want a few keyword ideas for a single blog post, but you'd want more before committing to a content plan.


Perplexity also gave a concise answer breaking down the likelihood of three different intents (informational, commercial, and transactional), plus the content that searchers likely want within an article.


### Copilot


[Microsoft's Copilot chatbot](https://copilot.microsoft.com/) generated roughly 20 keywords for AI keyword research, broken into the following lists:


- Core keywords
- Long‑tail keywords
- Semantic & related keywords
- Problem‑based keywords
- Content angle keywords


Unlike the other chatbots, Copilot didn’t give a specific intent (like informational), but still gave insight into search intent.


It's a starting point, not an answer. Real search data gives you the rest.
