# Local SEO keyword research: A step-by-step guide

Effective local SEO keyword research reveals how nearby customers search for your products or services, from city and neighborhood terms to “near me” queries.


AI search has expanded how customers discover and compare local services, so keyword research now needs to account for ranked results and AI-generated answers.


In this guide, you’ll learn how to find and prioritize high-intent local keywords, map them to the right pages and profile optimizations, and track their impact on local visibility and conversions.


## Local search now has two evaluators


A search for "best plumber in Denver" now gets judged twice.


The first evaluator is the one you know: Google's organic rankings, which match your pages to the query, and the Local Pack, which matches your business profile to it.


The second is AI-generated answers: ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity recommending nearby businesses, or an[AI Overview](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-overviews/) composing a response from a handful of cited sources.


Both are active discovery surfaces for local customers. But each evaluator assesses a brand differently.


Rankings reward how well your pages and profile match the words people search. AI answers weigh the details behind the decision: availability, service area, pricing, licensing, and recurring review themes.


[Google Business Profile (GBP)](https://www.semrush.com/blog/google-my-business/) data and your website content feed both, but ranking in one and appearing in the other are separate outcomes. One doesn't guarantee the other.


So local keyword research now has one list with two jobs: helping your pages rank, and surfacing the questions, service attributes, and location patterns AI systems draw on for answers like "best \[service\] near \[location\]." The steps below cover both.


## What is local SEO keyword research?


[Local SEO](https://www.semrush.com/blog/what-is-local-seo/) keyword research is the process of finding the search terms people use to discover businesses, products, or services in a specific area.


Optimizing your pages for these keywords increases your visibility in organic search results ([SERPs](https://www.semrush.com/blog/serp/) ), which drives more website traffic, inquiries, and sales. Maps visibility and AI-generated answers depend on more than page content, as the sections below explain.


### Where local keywords appear in Google


A search for "\[service\] in \[your location\]" can trigger multiple result types at the top of the page, including:


- **Local Services Ads** : A row of paid provider cards (often with photos and a "Google Guaranteed" badge) that can appear at the top for service queries, depending on the searcher's location
- **Businesses or Places** : A map view with text links listed below, also known as the[Google Local Pack](https://www.semrush.com/blog/google-local-pack/) . This section might be called "Businesses" or "Places," depending on the type of query used and the searcher's location.


- **Organic results:** Local businesses can also appear in the traditional organic results further down the page:


Local keywords can trigger both Local Pack and organic search results, but they rank differently.


Organic results rely on[SEO strategies](https://www.semrush.com/blog/seo-strategy/) like on-page optimization and backlinks. Local Pack rankings rely more on GBP optimization, proximity to the searcher, and review signals: the inputs behind what Google calls[relevance, distance, and prominence](https://www.semrush.com/blog/local-seo-ranking-factors/) , its three official local ranking factors.


Effective keyword research for local SEO identifies which terms trigger the Local Pack, and informs both your website optimization and GBP strategies.

## Implicit vs. explicit local keywords


Explicit local keywords include a location term like "plumber in boston" or "dentist[near me](https://www.semrush.com/blog/near-me-keywords/) ," making the searcher's local intent clear. Implicit local keywords don't specify a location but still trigger local results because the intent is inherently local.


A London resident who lost their keys might search "locksmith in london" or "locksmith near me." Both are explicit: the query itself tells Google where to look.


"Locksmith london" drops the "in" but still names the city, so it's explicit too, and Google returns near-identical results for both versions.


Someone searching just "locksmith" expects nearby options too, but the query never says so. That's an implicit local keyword: Google infers the local intent entirely from the searcher's location.


Where results vary most is with the searcher's position.


Google resolves every local query against where the searcher is standing, so two people searching from different neighborhoods, or even different postcodes, can see meaningfully different results, especially for implicit queries where location is the only signal Google has.


This matters for local SEO because it:


- **Affects**[ranking eligibility](https://www.semrush.com/blog/local-seo-ranking-factors/) : You can rank for implicit local queries even if your page doesn't use exact "in \[city\]" phrasing, as long as your GBP optimization, proximity, and review signals are strong.
- **Explains ranking volatility** : When rankings change, it's often just Google resolving location intent differently based on user location.
- **Changes how you write copy** : You don’t need to say “in London” everywhere on your website. Natural phrasing like “London locksmiths,” “trusted across London,” etc. still creates local relevance. The same logic applies to "near me": repeating the literal phrase in your titles and headers doesn't help you rank for "near me" searches. Google resolves that proximity signal from your Google Business Profile and location data, not from matching those two words on the page.
- **Reinforces the importance of proximity in search** : Implicit local searches depend more on distance and prominence, not just keyword matching. That’s why a small local shop can outrank a big brand two miles away.


Google uses algorithms to detect local intent and[multiple methods](https://support.google.com/websearch/answer/179386) to determine a user's location, and delivers local results whether the intent is explicit or implicit.


But implicit local keywords let Google decide how local the result should be at the time of the query, not just based on the words used. Because Google decides how local a result should be at query time, treating implicit terms like explicit ones leads you to misdiagnose ranking swings and over-optimize your copy.


## How to do local keyword research


Follow these six steps to perform local keyword research for SEO and AI visibility:


1. List the terms customers use when searching for your solutions in specific locations
2. Find relevant local keywords using research tools
3. Verify local intent in the SERP
4. Analyze competitors to find keyword gaps
5. Map keywords to existing or new pages
6. Research for AI visibility


Let's take a closer look at each step.


### Step 1: List terms for your solutions and locations


Before you open any keyword tool, gather the exact words customers use when they contact your business.


Call logs, staff notes, and chat transcripts show how real people describe your services, problems, and locations in everyday language.


Keyword tools measure demand for phrases people already know to search, but they can't suggest wording they've never seen enough people use.


For example, one person may search for "soda," while another looks for "pop": same[search intent](https://www.semrush.com/blog/search-intent/) , different regional wording. A tool has no way of surfacing a variant you didn't already think to enter.


Starting with your own customer language catches these variations before you ever open a tool to validate them.


Reviews and local community posts (Reddit, Quora, Facebook Marketplace) are worth mining the same way. People describe their needs in the language they'd use in a real conversation.


The vocabulary carries across both evaluators, but the query format changes. Someone might type “24-hour plumber South End” into Google and ask ChatGPT, “Can you recommend a 24-hour plumber in South End who can come tonight?” Add both the compact search term and the fuller conversational question to your seed list.


List the following types of solution-related terms:


- General terms for your business type
- Products and services people are likely to search for
- Problems or pain points you solve
- Questions and qualifiers customers use when asking for recommendations


Next, list location-related terms at multiple levels of specificity (these make local intent explicit):


- City
- Neighborhood
- ZIP or postcode
- Landmarks, such as "near Fenway Park," "near downtown," or "near the airport"

Use keyword research tools to expand your seed list, assess which terms are realistic to rank for, and see which queries surface local business results.

Search the keyword in Google and look at what shows up before you invest time targeting it.

Here's what each of these looks like in an actual search.


If someone searches for "emergency plumber near me," they need a plumber now. The "near me" is a proximity signal that implies they want to contact a business.


This type of keyword shows **transactional intent** . Google typically responds to a query like this with a Local Pack.


Now, if someone wants to hire a plumber but is comparing options first instead of calling the nearest one, they might search "best plumber in Boston."


This type of search signals **commercial intent** . Google still shows a Local Pack for this query, since the searcher is choosing between businesses.


The difference from transactional intent is timing: transactional means ready to take action now, commercial means deciding between businesses.


By contrast, if someone is looking for DIY or step-by-step instructions, they would search "how to fix a leaky pipe." This search query indicates **informational intent** .


In this case, the search results would include written content (blogs, landing pages, etc.) and video guides, and increasingly, an AI Overview.


If you've published a blog post that directly answers this question, that post has a real chance of being one of the sources the AI Overview cites.


Informational queries also open up two more paths to visibility: a[featured snippet](https://www.semrush.com/blog/featured-snippets/) or a[People Also Ask](https://www.semrush.com/blog/people-also-ask/) box. Both pull directly from an existing webpage, so if a page on your site clearly answers the question, Google can surface it there, even without a Local Pack.


If a keyword's results are only guides, articles, or videos with no Local Pack, treat that as weak local intent.

### Step 5: Map your keywords to URLs


Now that you have a comprehensive list of terms to target,[map each local keyword](https://www.semrush.com/blog/keyword-mapping/) to a specific page so every important search term is supported by a relevant, optimized URL.


The URL you choose is the one you'll optimize to rank, and the content should have a clear, retrievable structure to be cited in an AI-generated result.


For multi-location or service-area businesses, keyword mapping often means creating separate pages for city-level keywords (“plumber boston”) and neighborhood-level keywords (“plumber south end boston”).


[Keyword Strategy Builder](https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordmanager/) can group your keywords into pages automatically, based on relevancy and search intent, to make content planning easier.


Open the tool, and under “Keyword lists,” click to open the keyword list you created earlier.


Go to the “ **Pages** ” tab and click “ **Create list** .”


You’ll see your keywords grouped into pages. Each page focuses on a primary keyword.


Click the arrow next to a page topic to see:


- Secondary keywords to target for that page
- Keyword information like[search intent](https://www.semrush.com/blog/search-intent/) , volume, and[keyword difficulty](https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordoverview/)
- Top-ranking results for inspiration


Review the top-ranking results to get inspiration for the type of content you should create. Then, decide which pages you need to create and which pages you need to optimize.


If a page doesn’t exist yet, assign it a target URL and create a page that meets the keyword’s search intent.


For pages that already exist and need optimization, assign the existing URL.


A simple spreadsheet can help you assign URLs.


Download our free[keyword mapping template](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1ZbuxXHhVbPciTTdG-9wsZKhi4XsBBsU5AcgHEiks4hw/copy) , and use it to list all your pages, keywords, and target URLs.


### Step 6: Research for AI visibility


Google's AI generates an AI Overview from a small retrieval set: a handful of pages judged clear and specific enough to answer the query directly.

Structure your own service pages and Google Business Profile descriptions the same way. State the specific answer plainly, and pair your service with your location directly, for example, "Wheel Alignment Services in Philadelphia."

## Local keyword research FAQs


### Does local SEO still work?


Yes, local SEO still works because customers continue to discover nearby businesses through Google Search and Maps, while[AI-generated results add another visibility opportunity](https://www.theverge.com/news/712638/alphabet-google-earnings-q2-2025-ceo-sundar-pichai-ai) .


Google Maps has more than 2 billion monthly users, and Google reported that AI Overviews reached more than 2 billion monthly users across over 200 countries and territories by July 2025.


Local businesses now need visibility across organic results, the Local Pack, Maps, and AI-generated answers. Semrush's[AI Overviews study](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-overviews-study/) found that over 50% of AI Overviews on desktop and 60% on mobile didn't link to the top organic result. Most often our study saw less than 3 of the URLs from the organic top 10 repeated in the AI Overview. Organic rankings and AI citations are separate outcomes, so track them separately.


### Can I use ChatGPT for keyword research?


Yes, ChatGPT is useful for brainstorming local keyword ideas, generating variations, and clustering topics. However, it doesn’t have a native keyword database or built-in search-volume and difficulty metrics.


Use ChatGPT to build your initial seed list and explore subtopics, then validate and prioritize with a dedicated keyword research tool like Semrush’s Keyword Magic Tool, which provides search-volume estimates, keyword difficulty, and SERP feature data.

### How do local keywords affect AI Overview results?


Local keywords affect AI Overview results by revealing the service, location, and questions an AI Overview may need to answer. Pages most likely to get cited pair a specific service with a specific location, use structured content instead of dense paragraphs, and back that up with strong local signals like GBP data, consistent NAP information, and reviews.


Use[Keyword Overview](https://www.semrush.com/analytics/keywordoverview/) to find which of your local queries trigger an AI Overview, then check the cited sources for these same patterns. Ranking in the top 10 increases your odds of citation, but it isn't a guarantee, content structure and entity clarity matter on their own.


### Does Google Business Profile optimization help with AI search?


Yes, Google Business Profile optimization helps with AI search by giving AI systems a structured, machine-readable source to retrieve directly, unlike a paragraph of website copy an AI has to interpret.


Pair your services with your locations in the business description, keep your categories accurate, and maintain consistent NAP information across every listing. These are the same entity signals from Step 6, strengthening them gives AI systems clearer grounds to include your business in a generated answer.
