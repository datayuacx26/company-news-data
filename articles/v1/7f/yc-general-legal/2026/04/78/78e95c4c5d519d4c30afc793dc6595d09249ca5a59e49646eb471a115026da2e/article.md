# Why Do You Need a Commercial Contract? [11 Practical Reasons]

Every business relationship you enter raises the same open questions: who gets paid, who owns what, who takes the risk, and what happens when things don’t go according to plan.


A commercial contract **answers these questions.**


Some founders believe that these contracts only matter once you’re closing seven-figure deals. However, given that[12 million annual contract lawsuits](https://www.thezebra.com/resources/research/small-business-statistics/) are filed against small businesses in the US alone, it’s clear that **size doesn’t buy you an exemption from getting sued.**


To help you understand what’s at stake regardless of your size, growth stage, or industry, this guide breaks down **11 reasons why you need commercial contracts.**


## Key takeaways


- **Commercial contracts create clarity**
They define responsibilities, timelines, payment terms, and expectations so everyone has the same understanding of how things should work.
- **They protect your business from unnecessary risk**
Contracts allocate responsibility, limit liability, and establish safeguards before problems arise.
- **They help protect valuable business assets**
Well-drafted agreements can secure confidential information, intellectual property, and other assets that contribute to your competitive advantage.
- **They make growth easier to manage**
Standardized contracts create repeatable processes that help businesses onboard customers, vendors, and partners more efficiently.
- **Professional legal review can prevent costly mistakes**

## What are commercial contracts?


Commercial contracts are **legally binding agreements** that formalize the terms of a business arrangement, ensuring everyone involved is operating from the same playbook.


These contracts are typically signed when:


- Selling products or services to customers
- Purchasing goods or services from vendors and suppliers
- Hiring employees, freelancers, or independent contractors
- Sharing confidential information with third parties
- Licensing intellectual property, software, or proprietary technology
- Entering into a partnership or joint venture
- Bringing on advisors, consultants, or fractional executives
- Raising capital from investors
- Outsourcing operational functions like IT, customer support, or manufacturing
- Acquiring, merging with, or selling a business


Depending on the situation, you’ll likely need one or more of these commercial contract types:


**Contract type** **What it does**


[Master Services Agreement (MSA)](https://general.legal/library/master-services-agreement-msa) Establishes the framework for an ongoing services relationship


**Customer Agreement** Sets the terms under which you provide products or services to customers


**Vendor Agreement** Governs the purchase of goods or services from suppliers and vendors


[Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA)](https://general.legal/library/mutual-non-disclosure-agreement-nda) Protects confidential information shared between parties


**Employment Agreement** Defines compensation, responsibilities, and employment-related obligations


**Shareholders’ Agreement** Sets out the rights and responsibilities of founders and investors


[Advisor Agreement](https://general.legal/library/advisor-agreement) Formalizes an advisor’s role, compensation, and obligations


**Partnership or Joint Venture Agreement** Governs how two businesses will collaborate and share responsibilities


**SaaS Terms and Conditions** Establishes the rules governing access to and use of a software platform


‍


While the contract itself may change depending on the business scenario, the value it creates for your business remains consistent.


## Why you need commercial contracts: 11 reasons


From day-to-day operations to long-term growth, these 11 reasons illustrate why commercial contracts are a **fundamental part of running a successful business.**


### 1. They establish clear rules for the business relationship


A commercial contract gives every stakeholder the **same understanding of how the business relationship is supposed to work** . It outlines each party’s obligations, key deliverables, timelines, and performance standards, creating a shared framework from day one.


Without that framework, businesses are often forced to rely on assumptions, verbal conversations, and conflicting recollections of what was agreed upon.


### 2. They create guardrails around business risk


No matter how strong a business relationship is, **problems can still arise** . A supplier may miss a critical deadline, a customer may refuse to pay, a contractor may deliver substandard work, or a service outage may disrupt operations. When that happens, the fallout can affect cash flow, operations, and customer relationships.


Commercial contracts **keep those consequences under control.**


They assign responsibility for delays, defects, payment failures, data breaches, and other common risks before they become problematic. They also limit financial exposure, establish remedies, and outline how unforeseen events, such as supply chain disruptions or regulatory changes, should be handled.


Thanks to these provisions, commercial contracts **create predictability when circumstances are at their most uncertain** . Rather than negotiating solutions in the middle of a crisis, the parties can follow a framework they agreed to before problems emerged.


For startups looking to raise capital, there’s another benefit to proactively managing risk. Investors want confidence that the business can scale without preventable legal and operational issues, and robust commercial contracts provide that assurance.


### 3. They make business commitments enforceable


Businesses evolve quickly, and **agreements often outlast the people who negotiated them** . Team members leave, priorities change, and memories fade. A commercial contract preserves the terms of the deal in a form that doesn’t depend on anyone’s recollection of a meeting or Slack conversation.


This is particularly important for growing companies, where relationships with customers, vendors, partners, and contractors can span months or even years.


### 4. They reduce payment-related friction


Late payments are a persistent challenge for businesses. More than half of small businesses report being owed money on unpaid invoices, with the average business carrying[$17,500 in outstanding payments](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/r/small-business-data/small-business-late-payments-report-2025/) at any given time.


In many cases, the problem isn’t an unwillingness to pay; it’s **disagreement over what was owed, when it was due, or how it should be invoiced.**


Commercial contracts help eliminate that uncertainty by establishing:


- Pricing breakdown
- Payment schedules
- Accepted payment methods
- Invoicing requirements
- Refund terms
- Consequences for late payment


By documenting these details upfront, contracts make it easier for businesses to **get paid accurately and on time.**


### 5. They protect sensitive business information


Businesses regularly share confidential information with employees, contractors, vendors, partners, and prospective customers. While that information may be necessary to progress a deal, it can also **create significant risk** if it’s disclosed, misused, or shared with competitors.


Businesses typically protect sensitive information through **confidentiality clauses in commercial contracts or**[standalone NDAs](https://general.legal/blog/most-common-nda-pitfall) . Common examples of the type of information protected by these contracts include:


- Customer and prospect lists
- Pricing and commercial terms
- Financial data and forecasts
- Product roadmaps and business strategies
- Proprietary processes and trade secrets
- Technical documentation and source code


### 6. They establish ownership of intellectual property


For many businesses, intellectual property is among their **most valuable assets.** Software companies rely on source code, manufacturers depend on proprietary formulas and product designs, agencies create original creative assets, and consumer brands build value through trademarks and brand recognition.


Without ownership of these assets, much of the value created by the business can be placed at risk.


That’s why commercial contracts are necessary when engaging third parties who may create, access, license, or use intellectual property as part of the relationship. These parties include:


- Software developers
- Consultants
- Designers
- Manufacturers
- Distributors


By defining ownership rights, licensing permissions, and usage restrictions up front, contracts enable businesses to **retain control over the assets that drive their competitive advantage.**


### 7. They create accountability when agreements are breached


Disputes involving broken contractual obligations are one of the **most common sources of commercial litigation** , with breach-of-contract cases increasing[by 15% in 2023 alone](https://www.glennonlawfirm.com/resources/legalities-realities-the-blog/2025/february/navigating-the-top-trends-in-business-and-commer/) . Many originate from familiar situations: missed payments, service failures, delayed deliveries, or non-compliance with agreed terms.


Besides proving that something went wrong, businesses often struggle to determine what happens next because they have no prior agreement on how to handle the breach.


With a commercial contract in place, the breaching party may be required to:


- Fix the issue within a specified period
- Compensate the other party for resulting losses
- Pay predetermined damages
- Face termination of the agreement


For specific types of agreements, such as Service Level Agreements (SLAs), commercial contracts can include a[liquidated damages](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/liquidated_damages) **clause** , which establishes predetermined financial consequences for certain breaches or performance failures.


### 8. They provide a structured exit strategy


Not every business relationship is built to last. Priorities change, partnerships lose momentum, and sometimes, the relationship just stops delivering value.


Commercial contracts allow businesses to **exit these arrangements in an orderly manner** through termination provisions. These can specify:


- When an agreement can be ended
- How much notice must be given
- Which obligations survive termination
- What happens to ongoing work, payments, or confidential information


Rather than navigating a difficult separation without guidance, both parties can follow a predefined process that **minimizes disruption and helps protect their interests.**


### 9. They establish credibility with partners and customers


In many industries, a formal contract is the **standard way of doing business** . Customers, suppliers, investors, and strategic partners expect important commercial relationships to be documented.


For this reason, a well-drafted contract often signals that your business **operates professionally and takes its commitments seriously** . When both parties understand their responsibilities and commitments from the outset, there is less friction, fewer misunderstandings, and a more solid basis for long-term collaboration.


### 10. They support business growth and scalability


Informal agreements may be manageable when a business is small, but they become increasingly difficult to maintain as the company grows.


Standardized agreements make it easier to onboard new parties, negotiate terms more efficiently, and ensure key protections are applied across the business. Instead of reinventing the process for every new deal, companies can rely on a **repeatable framework that supports growth at scale.**


### 11. They embed compliance into business relationships


Many businesses operate in environments shaped by legal, regulatory, and industry-specific requirements. Depending on the industry, these obligations may relate to[data protection](https://general.legal/data-privacy) ,[employment practices](https://general.legal/employment) , cybersecurity,[financial regulations](https://general.legal/financial-regulatory) , and product safety.


Through commercial contracts, businesses can translate these requirements into **enforceable obligations** . They can require customers, vendors, contractors, and other third parties to do the following:


- Follow applicable laws
- Meet industry standards
- Maintain specific safeguards
- Comply with internal policies


By embedding compliance requirements directly into commercial relationships, businesses can **reduce regulatory risk and demonstrate that appropriate controls are in place.**

## FAQ


### Why is it important to have a contract?


A contract is important because it establishes expectations, allocates risk, protects business interests, and creates legally enforceable obligations between the parties.


### Can I create my own contract?


Yes, you can create your own contract using templates or precedent agreements. However, hiring a lawyer ensures the contract reflects your specific business needs and adequately protects your interests.


### What are some examples of commercial contracts?


Examples of commercial contracts include NDAs, MSAs, vendor agreements, employment agreements, software licensing agreements, and independent contractor agreements.


### What are the commercial terms of a contract?


Commercial terms are the business provisions of a contract, such as pricing, payment terms, deliverables, timelines, service levels, and termination rights.


### When do businesses need commercial law services?


Businesses typically need commercial law services when drafting, reviewing, negotiating, or managing contracts, as well as when entering new business relationships, resolving disputes, or navigating regulatory requirements.
