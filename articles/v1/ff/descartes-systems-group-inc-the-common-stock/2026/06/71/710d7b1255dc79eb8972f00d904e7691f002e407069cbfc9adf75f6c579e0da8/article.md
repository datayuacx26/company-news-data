# Why Many Companies Still Leave Millions in FTA Savings Untapped

## Free Trade Agreements Are Expanding — But Utilization Gaps Remain


For many organizations, the problem with Free Trade Agreements (FTAs) is execution. Most multinational companies operate across markets covered by multiple trade agreements, such as the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA), EU-Canada Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), and numerous Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) linked frameworks. According to the World Trade Organization (WTO), more than 380 regional and bilateral trade agreements are currently in force globally, creating significant opportunities to reduce duty exposure and improve landed cost efficiency.


Even with the scale of opportunity, many companies still fail to fully capture the benefits available to them. In most cases, they understand the agreements they operate under and recognize the potential savings available, but struggle when it comes to maintaining preferential eligibility. As sourcing decisions, supplier networks, manufacturing locations, and product structures continue to evolve, many FTA programs begin to break down operationally.


## Key Takeaways


- Most multinational organizations already operate within multiple FTA frameworks, but utilization remains inconsistent across products and trade lanes.
- Preferential eligibility is highly sensitive to sourcing changes, supplier shifts, and evolving bills of materials.
- Many unrealized tariff savings are the result of disconnected workflows between procurement, supplier management, trade compliance, and customs execution.
- Customs authorities are placing greater emphasis on proving country of origin, supplier evidence, and audit-ready documentation.
- Spreadsheet-driven and manually managed FTA programs become increasingly difficult to sustain as sourcing environments grow more dynamic.
- The next phase of FTA maturity will depend on continuous visibility across qualification status, supplier data, sourcing activity, and customs execution.


## Why Preferential Eligibility Becomes Difficult to Maintain


FTA qualification is often treated as a one-time exercise. Products are reviewed against rules of origin, supplier declarations are collected, and qualification status is established based on the sourcing environment at that time. Unfortunately, modern supply chains do not remain static long enough for that. Procurement teams are continuously adjusting sourcing strategies in response to increasing costs, geopolitical upheaval, lead-time constraints, supplier availability, and resiliency initiatives. Suppliers change production locations. Engineering adjustments affect bills of materials. Manufacturing moves between plants and regions. Even relatively minor sourcing changes can impact regional value content calculations or origin qualification requirements.


For example, a supplier substitution made during a China+1 sourcing initiative may improve supply continuity or reduce production costs while unintentionally affecting qualification. What may appear to be a routine sourcing adjustment upstream can quietly invalidate preferential treatment downstream. For many organizations, qualification analysis struggles to keep pace with the rate of operational change.


## Why Many FTA Programs Stall Operationally


One of the more common disconnects in global trade is that organizations often believe they are utilizing FTAs effectively because preference claims are being made in certain regions or product categories.


In practice, FTA use is frequently concentrated in mature trade lanes where documentation processes are already established and teams are comfortable with the qualification requirements. Outside those areas, execution is often inconsistent and heavily dependent on manual workflows.


This inconsistency matters more than many companies realize. To be effective, FTA strategy requires organizations to continuously determine which products qualify, under which agreements, based on up to date sourcing conditions supported by valid supplier documentation that arealigned with customs requirements.


Maintaining that level of consistency across procurement, supplier management, trade compliance, and customs filing processes becomes increasingly difficult when qualification analysis, supplier data, and customs execution sit in disconnected systems. That can lead to many areas where many organizations begin losing savings gradually over time, including:


- supplier declarations expiring or becoming outdated
- sourcing changes occuring without origin impact visibility
- qualification reviews failing to reflect current Bills of Materials structures
- customs teams lacking confidence in supporting documentation
- preference claims becoming inconsistent across trade lanes


Failures are rarely isolated. Frequently, they emerge through small operational gaps repeated across suppliers, products, and transactions over time.


### Customs Authorities Are Increasingly Focused on Origin Integrity


At the same time, customs authorities are placing greater emphasis on origin verification, supplier evidence, documentation integrity, and audit readiness.


Under agreements such as USMCA, proof of origin concerns have become increasingly common in recent years, placing greater pressure on organizations to demonstrate not only that products qualify, but that the underlying sourcing assumptions and supplier data remains current and defensible.


Organizations that lack confidence in their qualification data often avoid claiming preference altogether, paying the avoidable duty costs as a result. Others continue claiming preferential treatment while relying on fragmented supplier documentation, aging qualification analysis, or disconnected spreadsheets that may no longer reflect current sourcing realities. Neither position is sustainable in an environment where tariff exposure and enforcement scrutiny continue to increase simultaneously.


## Why Manual FTA Management No Longer Scales


Many FTA programs are managed through periodic supplier outreach campaigns, spreadsheet-based qualification analysis, disconnected workflows, and reactive documentation collection when customs requests arise. This approach has become increasingly difficult to sustain as supplier ecosystems expand, sourcing environments become more dynamic, agreements grow more complex, and customs scrutiny intensifies.


Managing preferential trade effectively requires far more than periodic qualification reviews. Organizations increasingly need continuous visibility into supplier data, sourcing changes, qualification status, and supporting documentation across products, agreements, and trade lanes. Without that visibility, maintaining qualification integrity at scale becomes extremely difficult.


### A Perspective on Modern FTA Management


FTA management is evolving from a periodic compliance activity into a continuously managed operational requirement. Organizations are under increasing pressure to improve landed cost efficiency while maintaining confidence in the integrity of their product data and customs documentation. At the same time, procurement, sourcing, and supply chain decisions are moving faster than many trade compliance processes have been designed to support. As a result, organizations increasingly need the ability to:


- continuously evaluate qualification as sourcing changes occur
- maintain current supplier qualification data
- improve visibility across products, agreements, and trade lanes
- support audit-ready origin documentation
- and connect qualification analysis more directly with customs execution processes


At scale, preferential trade management becomes as much a data governance and operational visibility challenge as it is a regulatory one. The organizations managing this effectively are often better positioned to reduce duty spend, improve sourcing agility, strengthen audit readiness, and respond more confidently to supply chain disruption.


## Why This Matters Now


Pressure surrounding preferential trade is unlikely to ease anytime soon. Supply chains continue to change, sourcing strategies are altered more frequently, and customs scrutiny around origin substantiation is increasing. Meanwhile, organizations remain under pressure to reduce landed costs while remaining compliant.


In that environment, underutilized FTAs are no longer simply missed efficiency opportunities. They directly affect sourcing flexibility, margin performance, pricing strategy, and competitiveness.


The companies creating long-term advantage will not necessarily be the ones with the largest trade teams or the most aggressive strategies. They will be the ones capable of keeping sourcing activity, supplier data, qualification analysis, and customs execution in synch as the business evolves.


## Final Thought


Free Trade Agreements continue to offer significant opportunities to reduce duty exposure and improve supply chain flexibility. But realizing those benefits consistently requires more than understanding agreement rules in theory.


Taking advantage of the benefits of FTAs requires the ability to maintain qualification integrity as sourcing environments evolve, supplier networks shift, and customs expectations become more demanding. For many organizations, the challenge is building the operational discipline required to execute them consistently at scale.
