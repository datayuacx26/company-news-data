# 3 major launches in 5 months – How we earned thousands of GitHub stars


### Lessons and tips for earning those coveted GitHub stars


[Daniel Farrell](https://substack.com/@danielfarrell)


Dec 02, 2024


One signal of a strong open-source project is GitHub stars – it’s a signal of both reach and interest from the developer community, and it’s something we periodically check on. If you don’t know what GitHub stars are, they’re equivalent to a “Liking” a project. When people star a project, they also subscribe to updates and announcements from the project in their GitHub feed.


For us, as owners of the project, a star history is a great way to visualize how well our marketing efforts reach developers over time, so I wanted to walk through some of the key moments that earned us stars over the past 5 months.

**Product Hunt**


## Launching on Hacker News


Just days after creating the repo,


[Kiet made a post on Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=40904862) , which immediately spiked our star-count.


We were fortunate to have a good amount of discussion from the community, and we wrote a lot about that experience in this post:


[How to absolutely crush your Hacker News launch](https://onlook.substack.com/p/launching-on-hacker-news)[Daniel Farrell](https://substack.com/profile/15503249-daniel-farrell) and[Kiet Ho](https://substack.com/profile/46760661-kiet-ho)


·


September 11, 2024


[Read full story](https://onlook.substack.com/p/launching-on-hacker-news)


That first post got us about


**+1,200 stars** from early to about the middle of July, and served as the initial spike for our userbase.


The good thing about this launch? The tool was usable, but still had a healthy amount of bugs.


The bad news? We didn’t have authentication in place, and wouldn’t for about a month, so while we captured emails via our website (“Sign up to get a link to download Onlook”) as well as some basic information, we had no idea who was actually using the product day-to-day.


Nevertheless, it spurred an initial wave of interest from the developer community and gave us a hot start to marketing Onlook as a visual editor for code.


### Launching as soon as possible


The biggest takeaway was to launch as early as you can.


Because star-charts are a great reference for the momentum and enthusiasm of a project, do a launch as soon as you create the repo. If you compare us to


[Supabase](https://github.com/supabase/supabase) , one of the most successful open-source projects, it looks like our star-count is quite low…


But, if you use the “Align timeline” button in the upper-right corner, you’ll see that we have a pretty comparable star trajectory.


[Starlet](https://star-history.com/) uses the first day the project was created on GitHub as a reference for the beginning of the chart, which is why you want to do a launch immediately after creating your repo – it may get an immediate boost in your star count.


### Support from Twitter


During the month of July, we also kept the fire going with some mockups of the experience we were going for.


[This post](https://x.com/D_R_Farrell/status/1814374745770492155) showcasing our frosted-glass editor was a huge hit on X/Twitter and earned


[our X account](https://x.com/onlookdev) hundreds of new followers.


Many of the followers weren’t developers, but it definitely got people curious about the project.


## Hacker News – Round 2


At the end of August, we were ready to share a more refined version of our product with the Hacker News community, so we decided to do another launch.


We really felt that waiting a bit before launching again would 1) respect the Hacker News rules on posting the same link again and 2) help reach a different crowd of users who hadn’t seen the project before.


Hacker News is possibly the best marketing channel for open-source tools, but you have to use it very


*intentionally* and


*authentically* .


We did our second launch in August, slightly changing the title to put a bit more emphasis on what we felt made our product special (local-first). This was something that a lot of developers expressed excitement about, so we used it in the newer title.


You’ll see that there is about 100 fewer points for the second post and about half as many comments, but this is expected for a repost roughly 2 months after the first post – the Hacker News crowd isn’t gigantic, and a lot of people likely saw the first link already.


This second post got us from about


**1,500 to 2,500 stars (+1,000)** .


As we think about when we’ll do another launch, we want to balance the technical side of what we’re building with how we self-promote. If you think about doing a second launch for your project, we can’t emphasize enough how posting on Hacker News should be in the spirit of educating or starting a discussion, rather than just trying to get stars.

## Product Hunt


We had been thinking about doing Product Hunt ever since the first Hacker News launch. The major difference between a Product Hunt launch and a Hacker News launch though, is that the Product Hunt audience is much broader – in other words, you’ll get designers, developers, indie-hackers, tech enthusiasts, and so much wider of a reach of people that you really should have a product people can use right away before posting.


We delayed our launch for months because the momentum from our first launches was already giving us enough signal to understand what worked and what definitely didn’t with our product.


Onboarding was a major problem with our product, and without addressing it, we felt that a premature Product Hunt launch would hurt us rather than help us. Also, Product Hunt encourages you to wait 6 months between launches, so it was important that we time the launch with a usable product.


The Product Hunt launch took us from about


**2,800 stars to 3,500 (+700 stars)** – a substantial boost to our star count, but not as effective as Hacker News.


We were #8 of the day, and if you’re not in the top 5 you’re not “above the fold” of the Product Hunt website, so we definitely learned the importance of building anticipation for our launch.


Besides building anticipation, having clearly branded graphics and a solid launch video also seemed to help drive people to discover the product.


One of the mistakes we made with the video was 1) not asking people to star the project and 2) not making it as engaging as we could. If you look at our video retention chart, we lost about 50% of users quite early, so definitely room for improvement there.


The overall viewer retention of our Product Hunt video


Some of the key takeaways from our Product Hunt launch:


1.


Do everything you can to get into the Top 5 of the day (get above-the-fold)


2.


Build anticipation before the launch


3.


Make your post rich with graphics and a good video


4.


Make the video engaging as much as possible (even experimenting on YouTube with other demos)


5.


Add Call-to-Actions for people to star the project in the video and in the launch description + comments.


### Refining our approach to earning stars


It’s clear that building momentum for an open-source project is as much about timing and strategy as it is about the product itself.


Each launch served as an opportunity to refine not just how we talk about Onlook but how we think about its value to users. The stars we’ve earned are more than just a vanity metric—they represent conversations, curiosity, and contributions from a growing community of potential users and supporters.


If there’s one overarching lesson here, it’s that marketing an open-source project is iterative.


You don’t need to have everything perfect from day one, but you do need to show up consistently and authentically. The journey from 0 to (now almost) 4k stars is just the beginning for us, and it’s shaped how we’ll approach our launches moving forward—with humility, experimentation, and a commitment to connecting with the people who believe in what we’re building.
