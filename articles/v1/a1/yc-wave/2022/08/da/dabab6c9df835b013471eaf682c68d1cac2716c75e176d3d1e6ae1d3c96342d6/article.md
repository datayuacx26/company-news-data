# Some tips for good one-on-ones


Ben Kuhn


on


August 1, 2022


culture


management


---


When I wrote[The unreasonable effectiveness of one-on-ones](https://www.benkuhn.net/11/) , I felt like my one-on-ones mostly involved doing generic stuff that anyone who cared a lot could do. So I was surprised when someone on Hacker News left this gem of a comment:


> I’ve had a thousand+ 1x1s with countless people (both as a manager and as a report), and the majority of them are just time sucking status updates, casual meet and greets, or some performance/process related discussion. Literally never have I walked out of a 1x1 and thought “wow that was unreasonably effective”.


(Thankfully, some wag beat me to the obvious retort: “There’s a common factor to all of your 1:1s, so that may be worth looking into.”)


Self-own aside, comments like that suggested that some people could use help making their one-on-ones more useful, so I’ve written up the basics of how I do mine. I don’t claim this way is the best, nor that any of it is original, but I do think it works pretty well, and at minimum it’ll prevent them from being “time sucking status updates.”


## One-on-ones are for the report, not for the manager


My main goal with 1:1s is to give the coachee whatever they need to have an awesome time at work. That could be many things. Sometimes it’s advice on a tricky problem. Sometimes it’s talking through concerns about company strategy or culture. Sometimes it’s career coaching. Sometimes it’s just listening, or hanging out.


In each case, 1:1s mostly achieve this goal by creating an[affordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affordance) for the coachee to talk about those needs and get help with them. In my experience, without a recurring meeting to prompt that kind of discussion, almost everyone waits way too long to ask their manager for time.


For example, I’m basically never worried *enough* about Wave’s strategy to schedule a random ad-hoc meeting with my manager to discuss it. But I often have some question in the back of my mind that I’ll bring up in 1:1s, get resolved, and then feel way better. Having that default time is important!


Some examples of frequent 1:1 topics:


- Getting to know each other better
- Giving feedback (in either direction)
- Postmorteming a recent project
- Figuring out how to do better next time
- Thinking about directions for career growth
- Voicing worries / uncertainties about company strategy, the future, etc.


Without 1:1s, this kind of[important, but not urgent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_management#The_Eisenhower_Method) conversation can get dropped, and people can end up ignoring feedback, not building strong relationships, or repressing concerns until they blow up messily later.


On the other hand, I’ve also found that there’s a different set of “attractors” topics that are easy to start talking about, but ultimately take time away from more valuable topics. I’ll try to steer 1:1s away from these. In particular:


- No status updates (these should be done in a separate meeting, or asynchronous)
- No solving urgent problems (if it’s urgent, it shouldn’t wait for our next 1:1!)


## Always have prep


Since 1:1s are for the coachee, it’s (by default) the coachee’s job to prep. The prep I ask for is a list of topics to talk about, in order of importance. (Ranking the topics is really important, in case we don’t get to the end of the list!) If there’s no prep, I usually offer to postpone (not cancel!) the meeting to give them time to prep.


(I will also occasionally add topics to our prep—usually important-but-not-urgent feedback or a periodic nudge for updates on a longer-running thread—e.g. “how is the adjustment from IC to manager going?”)


For my own 1:1s, I’ve found it useful to have a list of prompts to help me generate topics to discuss. When someone new joins Wave, I start them off with the following prompts:1


- How could my most important project have gone better this week?
- What could my lead do to make my work more effective and more fun?
- What am I trying to improve at right now? How’s that going? What are my next steps?
- What are my next career goals at Wave, what steps are needed to get there?
- Do I fully understand the reasoning behind Wave’s top goals and how my work fits in?
- Am I curious, uncertain, worried or stressed about anything?


But I strongly encourage people to iterate on the prompts over time, dropping ones that don’t work for them and adding new questions that they’ve found useful to periodically think about. I’ll also often end up suggesting that people do[weekly reviews](http://benkuhn.net/weekly/) as another way of generating topics.


For an example of a different prompt that I added for myself recently, right now my prep for my own 1:1s starts with an update on whether I’ve gotten enough sleep, exercise, and social time this week. I got pretty bad at that for a while and being an accountability buddy was legitimately one of the best ways Drew could help me!


During my first 1:1 with a new report, I ask for the following additional prep:


- Imagine your ideal role at Wave—if you could spend 100% of your time on the things you find most satisfying. What would it look like? (Your best guess at the fuzzy end state)
- Imagine your ideal work context—if your lead and teammates gave you the best possible support to excel. What would it look like? (Again, fuzzy / your best guess)
- Imagine your ideal self—if you became the best person in the world at your ideal role. What would that look like? How will you get there + how can Wave help?
- What are your preferred communication habits?
- What makes you grumpy? If you’re grumpy, how will your lead know and how can they help you?
- How do you prefer to receive feedback? Immediately, or later? In writing or face to face?


## Ask lots of questions


Often my coachees will ask me for my advice or thoughts on a problem. My natural instinct is to immediately give my best-guess answer, but unless I’m really confident in it, I’ve found it’s more productive to[get curious](https://www.benkuhn.net/listen/) and ask lots of questions first. Asking lots of questions feels slow, but it has two benefits:


- Often my snap answer is a bad idea because it’s based on a bad assumption. If I make that kind of suggestion, we might waste the next 10 minutes arguing about why it’s a bad idea before we get to the root of the disagreement and uncover my wrong assumption.
- It shows more of my thought process to the coachee and helps them figure out not just the answer, but the mental models that generated it. If I’m on top of my game, sometimes all I need to do is to ask the right questions and my coachee will discover a good answer themselves. (I’m not usually this on top of my game!)


## Logistics


Concretely, here’s what the mechanics of my 1:1s are:


- We meet for 30 minutes (or 1h for new senior people who need to download a lot of context quickly).
- We start out weekly, then switch to biweekly if/when we’re not using the full time.
- I schedule them in a batch on Tuesdays (Wave’s default “meeting day”).
- I try very hard not to cancel.

- “whys” / things you noticed (perhaps cultural) - why do it X way rather than Y?
- do you feel underprepared to solve any sorts of problems that come your way?
- are you on the job/career/self-improvement trajectory you want to be on?
- any design questions to talk through?
- any projects that went “interestingly”—well or poorly—let’s postmortem / talk it through.
