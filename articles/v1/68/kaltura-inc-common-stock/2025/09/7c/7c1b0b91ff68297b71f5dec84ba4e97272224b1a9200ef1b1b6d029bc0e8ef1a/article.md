# Day one doesn’t mean square one: lessons from a junior marketer

*Starting your first marketing role in a tech company can definitely feel overwhelming, especially when you don’t have much experience. Expectations are high and the challenge is real. But the thing is, you’re not the only one, and you don’t truly start from nothing.*


*This post is for junior marketers figuring things out as they go. By learning how to navigate the waters, you can turn early uncertainty into value.*


## **Here’s how I started thinking about that tension**


Some of the strongest contributions come from more than just checking off a to-do list. When you are just starting, it’s easy to focus on that list to keep up with the learning curve. But I quickly realized that wasn’t working for me, and it didn’t feel like I was truly contributing. I had to shift my mindset.


I started looking beyond that checklist. I read about what other marketers were doing, asked more questions, and showed interest in joining courses to improve or learn new skills. Within a week, my team offered and encouraged me to participate in several workshops, including one that taught me how to write this blog entry. These small steps helped me to slowly see a bigger picture, and spot gaps, patterns, and opportunities I hadn’t noticed before.


While the strong background of senior marketers brings proven success, it can also sometimes create habits or blind spots. As junior marketers, we may not have years of experience (yet), but we have something just as powerful: a blank slate. Since we aren’t comparing it to the old way, we aren’t thinking about “how things are usually done.” This means that we could discover new angles that others might have overlooked.


Sure, the learning curve is still steep. Many of my ideas weren’t groundbreaking and were rejected, and that’s okay, because I’m still learning. But the more I built context for myself and kept experimenting, eventually, some stuck. And if you are part of a team that values experimentation, it’s your chance to be curious, speak up, and learn from mistakes.


## **How I approach teamwork, idea generation, and cross-functional collaboration**


Everyone starts somewhere, and I’ve found that some of the biggest steps forward come through proactivity and collaboration. Knowledge and perspective don’t just come from doing, but also from observing, asking, and listening.


Even when I was sitting in meetings with people who seemed to know exactly what they were doing, I continued asking questions. They might have felt basic, but they were always appreciated and encouraged. In the end, there are no wrong questions; it’s just you learning.


While opportunities to grow are not always handed out, they will be available to those who show interest. Not only that, but seeing perspectives outside of your team can be incredibly valuable. I took the time to speak with different teams to establish a clearer picture of things, ranging from simple aspects like team structure and responsibilities to more complex ones, such as common goals. Over time, I began to connect the dots, understand how different teams function, and how my work contributes to the broader picture. Those insights made a real difference in how I approached everything.


## **Balancing creativity with practicality**


Exploring new angles and brainstorming fresh ideas is one of the most exciting parts of marketing. But it’s also when reality hits, and you realize that not every idea will be a fit. Creativity also needs to work within the limits of budget, timeline, and audience needs. Even the “best” idea, if not practical to scale, needs to evolve or it might remain just that: an idea.


At first, that felt frustrating. But you’re not choosing between creativity and practicality; you’re continuously adjusting. I’ve learned that most ideas evolve. They are shaped by feedback and team input, which are a natural part of the process. The key is figuring out how to work within those limits and to find the balance between what sounds good and what could actually work.


For an idea to land strongly, it doesn’t have to be the flashiest. Sometimes, the simplest ideas are the ones that resonate the most. It’s not always about being the loudest but about who listened the most. It’s about the fine-tuned insights that you have learned from talking to CSMs, listening in on sales calls, looking at data, and actively collaborating with different teams in your organization. It can feel like a lot, especially when you’re just getting started and still trying to navigate different changes. However, staying curious and taking the time to build that context will be worth it and help create something that actually translates into impact.


## **How I use AI in this process**


AI has become a natural part of how I work. It helps me explore ideas faster and test different directions more easily by giving me knowledge that otherwise would have taken much longer to gather on my own. It’s incredibly useful for finding inspiration and shaping early thoughts, especially when things are moving fast and time is tight.


But there is a fine line between using it to guide your thought process and letting it take over. I’ve found myself asking a quick question, only to find myself minutes later on a completely different track, disconnected from my original idea. We’ve all been there. That’s when I realized AI is only useful when you know how to approach it. I started creating more focused prompts, which really made a difference. I’m still experimenting with that and currently working on my own “master prompt”. When my inputs weren’t based on a clear purpose or a strong notion, the output was almost always underwhelming and generic.


But today’s audiences expect more than that. They expect relevance, authenticity, and value. To truly stand out, you need to go deeper than surface level. I’ve found that real impact comes from personalization that is grounded in a clear understanding of who your audience is, what they care about, and what they truly need right now.


That level of depth isn’t achieved by AI alone, but through an interplay between AI and human insight. And part of that process is asking yourself: If I were in the audience, would this genuinely resonate with me, or would it just feel like another generic experience?


## **You can bring value, even early in your career**


Lack of experience doesn’t have to be a disadvantage, but it does mean being aware of the opportunities you can create for yourself. I understood that asking questions, being curious, and not being scared of making mistakes to build that context is extremely important. At the end of the day, every marketer starts somewhere. What matters is how you show up and build on your strengths and (lack of) knowledge.


You’re not getting this from someone miles ahead. I’m a junior marketer too, learning these lessons as I go. And if there’s one thing I’ve learned, it’s this:
Yes, we’re new, but we never truly start from “nothing.” We are just getting started, and that’s a powerful place to be.
