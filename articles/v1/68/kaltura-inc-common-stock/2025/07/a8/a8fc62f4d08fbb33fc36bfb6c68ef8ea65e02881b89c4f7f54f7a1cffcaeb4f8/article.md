In 2017, after a 3.8 km swim in the Red Sea, I completed a 180 km bike ride through harsh desert winds. Now comes the final leg — a marathon I’ve never run. All are part of the Israman, one of the toughest Ironman-distance triathlons in the world.


My legs ache. My breathing is shallow. Each step feels heavier than the last.


You might be wondering how I ended up in this situation…


But it wasn’t the end — it was the beginning of the hardest part. Maybe the hardest physical challenge I’ve ever faced.


Eventually, I crossed the finish line after 12 hours and 5 minutes of extreme effort, to the sound of my family cheering me on.


That feeling? Almost impossible to describe.


This, for me, is the essence of stepping out of your comfort zone.


I didn’t just land there — I earned it.


It took a year of early mornings. Countless solo sessions — unnoticed, unglamorous, but relentlessly consistent. And yet, the reward was one of the most meaningful experiences of my life.


## But how does this athletic mindset apply to career decisions?


What happens when that same drive meets the uncertainty of a professional pivot?


That mindset — the choice to constantly pursue discomfort in the name of growth — is something I’ve carried with me far beyond sports.


Over the years, I’ve gone through several career shifts, each pushing me outside my comfort zone.


I began in my family’s business — a clear but limiting path. I felt the urge to carve out my own direction, so I enrolled in a B.A. in computer science. I kept working full-time during my studies, driven by a hunger to challenge myself intellectually.


During my final year, I entered the tech industry by joining a large corporation as a junior developer. I quickly joined the core development teams. Then came an offer from a promising startup with significant growth potential. I took the leap, only to find myself out of a job eight months later when the company began to fold.


It felt like a setback at the time, but it became a defining turning point in my career.


After several months of searching, I discovered what would become my next calling. I joined a seven-person team, building a startup from the ground up. Over the next 13 years, I grew from a solo developer to a team leader, eventually transitioning into a product role, where I learned a new language: understanding customers, stakeholders, and the business impact.


But even then, something was missing. My passion for triathlon, which initially started as a weekend hobby, took over. I studied, became certified, and left the tech industry to become a full-time coach. Coaching taught me to lead, stay calm, and focus on long-term growth — lessons that extend far beyond sports.


But reinvention doesn’t erase old passions — it reshapes them.


Still, I missed building things. I missed solving deep, systemic problems. So I returned to tech with a renewed mindset and joined an incredible team at Kaltura as a backend developer, bringing with me not only technical skills but everything I had learned from coaching athletes, leading teams, and navigating personal reinvention.


Real growth demands risk — and the courage to leave comfort behind.


So yes — I believe getting out of your comfort zone should be a habit. It’s essential for anyone who wants to live a meaningful life, grow, and evolve — not just professionally, but also personally. But how do we do it?


Let’s examine what makes staying comfortable so appealing in the first place.


## What we all learn early in our adult life


It’s easy (and very human) to stay in our comfort zone. Our instincts push us to conserve energy, seek safety, and avoid unnecessary risks. We all know the feeling — lying on the couch, eating chips, and binge-watching another show. We’re no longer saving energy to escape danger; we’re just avoiding effort, avoiding discomfort, and staying still.


But comfort quickly turns into dissatisfaction.


We lose drive. Our minds dull, our bodies stagnate, and we begin to drift without a clear sense of purpose.


I could’ve stayed in my family’s business — it was stable, familiar, and safe. I could have stuck with sprint, Olympic, or even half-Ironman-distance triathlons, which I did for years. But is “just okay” what we really want from our lives?


Growth doesn’t come from ease. It comes from discomfort — from doing the things that scare us, stretch us, and force us to become more.


## Steps to progress through obstacles


The first step is to clearly identify your dissatisfaction.


If you’re coming to work without enthusiasm or passion, spending your days on tasks that neither interest nor challenge you, you need to recalculate your path.


Sometimes, the path to growth lies within your current company. You might take initiatives, expand your expertise horizontally across different domains, or grow vertically by advancing into management roles. Large companies often support this kind of internal mobility, making it easier to explore new challenges without leaving your employer. However, don’t limit yourself to what’s familiar.


In smaller companies, such opportunities for role changes are usually limited, so personal growth may require stepping outside the organization entirely.


I spent 13 years at a startup, gradually transitioning from development to product roles. Eventually, stepping away from daily coding left me unfulfilled — I realized I’d lingered in a comfort zone misaligned with my core passion for building software.


I vividly remember staring blankly at the screen one morning, realizing I’d completely lost my passion and needed a change.


One of the best ways to stay open to change is to keep learning and exploring topics that genuinely interest you — even if they have nothing to do with your current job. Throughout my entire career in the tech industry, I maintained a deep curiosity about the world of triathlon. That ongoing interest, combined with years of self-driven research, eventually helped me become a triathlon coach with a solid foundation of knowledge — even as a beginner in the coaching field.


The key is to learn new skills and not be afraid to take calculated risks.


## Why stepping out of your comfort zone feels so hard


Stepping out of your comfort zone can bring on self-doubt and fear. I experienced this firsthand when I joined Kaltura after spending several years outside the tech industry. I had no prior experience with SaaS, video technologies, or the company’s specialized domains — the doubts were real.


I kept asking myself, *‘How will I fit in? Can I catch up? Will I be able to make a meaningful contribution?’*


Everything felt unfamiliar — the topics, the tools, the people, even the corporate culture. It was overwhelming.


Have you faced similar fears when approaching a new challenge?


## How can you overcome these obstacles?


Here’s what helped me stay grounded when doubt crept in.


I reminded myself of the skills I already had — and how they could help me move forward:


● **Mental fortitude, discipline, and consistency** developed through years of sports training became invaluable.


● **Self-directed learning** , a key trait of autodidacts, helped me navigate unfamiliar domains.


● **Social skills** , such as asking insightful questions, connecting with colleagues, and seeking out mentors, accelerated my growth.


● **Breaking down intimidating tasks** into daily micro-goals made the work more manageable and built confidence.


● **Creating a structured learning plan** with timelines and checkpoints reduced anxiety and provided clarity.


● **External resources** , including books, courses, videos, and documentation, helped fill knowledge gaps and restore a sense of control.


## What real growth actually feels like


Change is uncomfortable — there’s no way around that. But it’s also the most reliable path to real, lasting growth, both personally and professionally.


Every time I stepped into something unfamiliar — a new job, a new industry, a new athletic challenge — I discovered more about what I was capable of. I developed skills I didn’t know I needed and built confidence that couldn’t have come any other way.


It was in that messy in-between that I learned how to guide others — not just one-on-one as a coach, but also while standing in front of a crowd, helping people navigate their own doubts and setbacks.


If there’s one constant I’ve found across every chapter of my life, it’s this: the people who thrive are the ones who keep learning. Whether you’re shifting careers, picking up new technologies, or entering an entirely different world, adaptability is everything. The faster you accept discomfort, the faster you grow through it.


And for me, sports have been more than just a metaphor — they’ve been a mindset. Discipline, consistency, and celebrating small wins kept me moving forward. You don’t need to be an athlete to use this mindset. You just need to treat your own journey with the same level of commitment and care.


Standing at that Israman finish line taught me something invaluable: growth starts exactly where comfort ends. Take a small step today — your journey toward growth begins now.


So whatever stage you’re at, take a moment to reflect:


*Am I growing — or just coasting?*


Because on the other side of discomfort lies pride, purpose, and momentum — and that’s what makes it all worth it.


**What’s your next small step? Name it. Own it. Take it.**
