## Amazon connects with future data center talent in Hamlet, North Carolina


Amazon data center employees at a career networking event at Richmond Community College in Hamlet, North Carolina, on June 25.

Amazon's mobile data center training lab, equipped with servers and networking hardware, at a recruiting event in Hamlet, North Carolina.

"We want people in this community to see themselves in these careers," said Kelly Felix, data center cluster operations leader for North Carolina. ## How Amazon and Blue Mountain Community College are building a pipeline for data center careers in Oregon


About 10 years ago, Amazon came to


[Blue Mountain Community College (BMCC)](https://bluecc.edu/) with plans to build data centers in the region. The company needed trained technicians. The


[Data Center Technician program](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/teacher-works-with-aws-to-open-up-tech-careers-in-rural-community) , launched in 2015, helped make that possible.


When Jesus Casas was unsure of his next career step, he considered skipping school entirely. "But when funding became available," he said, "I knew it was meant for me."


Amazon has committed more than $700,000 in scholarships to the BMCC Foundation, helping students who might not otherwise pursue the program.


"Amazon has given a lifeline for students who need that financial support," said Leah Gadsden, BMCC Foundation engagement officer. "They have enabled so many students not just to get a job, but a real career they can be proud of."


Peter Hernberg, mathematics and computer science instructor at BMCC, remembers those early days. "I drafted a brief curriculum," he said. Getting students enrolled was tough at first. But word spread. "Most of our students have heard about the program from a friend of theirs, someone in their family, who've said, 'Hey, this is a great way to get a good job.'"


Louis Maxcy is one of those students. An Umatilla High School graduate directly hired by Amazon as a data center operations technician, he knows what these opportunities mean locally. "The stigma was always, if you want to become successful and have a good career, you need to leave this area. As the data centers came in, that provided great opportunities for people that live here."


"Helping the community is not just a partnership with BMCC. It's a commitment to the community,” said Casas.


On May 7, he shared his story at BMCC’s scholarship luncheon. Weeks later, 20 students graduated from Amazon’s Information Infrastructure Pre-Apprenticeship program after four weeks of paid training.

## Amazon retrofit of closed manufacturing plant in Clinton, Mississippi to create 100 full-time jobs


Clinton, Mississippi


When the Delphi manufacturing facility in Clinton, Mississippi closed in 2009, the loss of 280 jobs was felt across the community. Opened in the early 1970s to manufacture cable and wiring connectors for the automotive industry, the building sat vacant for nearly two decades. In April, Amazon announced its


[planned investment](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-25-billion-mississippi-data-centers) to refurbish the facility into a state-of-the-art data center. Today, Hinds County Economic Development Authority, the City of Clinton, and Alston Construction hosted Amazon for a ribbon-cutting ceremony to celebrate this investment and the creation of at least 100 full-time jobs.


For Hinds County, this investment is transformative. Mayor Will Purdie has called it "one of the largest economic development project in the history of the City and Hinds County," noting it will inject millions annually into Clinton's budget, schools, and county services. The project offers residents high-skilled careers, from data center engineers to network specialists, without leaving their community. Amazon has partnered with Hinds Community College and AccelerateMS on hands-on workforce training programs designed to prepare local residents for these jobs.


This is the first time Amazon has taken an existing industrial building and fully retrofitted it into a functioning data center. Rather than demolishing and rebuilding, Amazon chose an adaptive reuse strategy, repurposing what already exists rather than building from scratch. Reusing the building's existing materials and infrastructure means less waste, increased sustainability, and a faster path to completion.


The Delphi building's story isn't ending. Rather, it's beginning again, and it's the latest example of Amazon's continued


[long-term commitment to Mississippi](https://www.aboutamazon.com/impact/amazon-mississippi) .

Since announcing our


[$11 billion investment in Louisa County in 2023](https://www.louisacounty.gov/CivicAlerts.aspx?AID=363&ARC=2233) , we've been committed to being good neighbors. ## Understanding Our Water Infrastructure and Permit


As we progress our projects in Louisa, we're following the regulatory process, which involves applying for permits. Here's some context on our water discharge permit for the Lake Anna Technology campus and how we're working to be responsible stewards of local resources.


What is the permit for:


We design our data centers for efficiency and build sustainability into everything we do. In Louisa County, we rely on outside natural air cooling for about 96% of the year and only use water-based cooling during the hottest periods, which is about 4% of annual operations.


On only the hottest hours of the year, the facility uses a cooling system that evaporates water to safely cool air that in turn removes heat from the computers. As part of this process, cooling water needs to be periodically discharged; this cooling water is called ‘non-contact cooling water’. It never touches IT equipment, and it's treated before release in alignment with state environmental standards. This permit protects Lake Anna and local waterways while allowing us to discharge to Sedges Creek.


Where the water goes:


The permit allows discharge to Sedges Creek, which flows into Lake Anna.


Will this heat up Lake Anna?


No. On average, our daily discharge is approximately 0.0000882% of the volume of water in the Lake and will be discharged into Sedges Creek. The water will be at a cooler water temperature than


the warm side of Lake Anna where Sedges Creek eventually flows into.


Why metals are listed in the permit:


Very low concentrations of the metals listed in the permit are already naturally present in the water before being used by our data centers. As water evaporates during cooling, metal concentrations increase in the discharged non-contact cooling water—this is expected and is why monitoring will be required. The concentration of metals allowed in our discharge water is limited by the state and these requirements are determined by strict environmental and health standards. The limits for metals in our discharge permit are stricter than drinking water limits, meaning our discharge will be required to contain fewer metals than what is allowed in typical drinking water.


In summary, this permit exists to protect local water resources. Amazon’s system will minimize water use, treat water before discharge, avoid direct discharge into the lake, and operate under strict VDEQ oversight. Discharge would be intermittent, limited, treated, and monitored.

## Supporting conservation in Virginia


The Rappahannock River has shaped Virginia for centuries. Now, Amazon and STACK Infrastructure are investing in its future with a $1 million contribution to Friends of the Rappahannock—the largest corporate donation in the Fredericksburg-based nonprofit's 40-year history.


This contribution supports conservation and education programs protecting the Chesapeake Bay watershed and reflects our broader commitment to Virginia, where more than 43,000 Amazon employees live and work.


"This gift is a catalyst," said Daria Christian, executive director of Friends of the Rappahannock. "It allows us to expand our impact while maintaining our core values."


## Turning wastewater into a resource


Partnering with organizations like Friends of the Rappahannock that directly preserve and restore watersheds is just one way we're working to protect Virginia's natural resources.


We became the


[first data center operator](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/sustainability/amazon-water-conservation-replenishment-sustainability) in Virginia to use


[recycled water](https://sustainability.aboutamazon.com/stories/how-aws-uses-recycled-water-in-data-centers) in our cooling systems. Recycled water is wastewater that has been treated and purified so it can be safely reused for purposes like cooling and irrigation—helping us save energy while using 85% less water.


In partnership with STACK Infrastructure in Stafford County, we're investing more than $300 million to upgrade the Aquia Wastewater Treatment Plant, pump stations, and related infrastructure to support a new water reuse system. This project will transform wastewater into millions of gallons per day of recycled water at no cost to taxpayers, protecting the Rappahannock River and potable drinking water supplies for local communities.

## Amazon partners with Georgia technical colleges to offer fiber optic training courses


At two Georgia technical colleges, students aren't just learning what it takes to build data centers, they're building confidence.


"It's a very lucrative job. It's a very interesting job, and anybody can do it," said fiber optic fusion splicing instructor Dwayne Holst with Sumitomo Electric Lightwave.


To meet the increasing demand for skilled workers who can install and maintain the complex fiber optic networks that power data centers, AWS hosted dozens of local students in a free two-day training course of fiber optic installation and repair.


"It's basically an existential moment in technical history," said Holst. "There's a huge need for skilled labor and Amazon is trying to be proactive."


Kesha Cole is trying to be proactive too. She's been in the airline industry for more than 25 years and brought her nephew along.


"I definitely see tech as future," said Cole. After all, much like the airplanes she deals with, data centers are also taking off. "I'm seriously considering how I can build a career around it."


Cole knew nothing about fiber optic splicing before taking the courses. When she recently had fiber optic internet installed in her home, she thought, "I could never do that. That's too many wires."


But then, she spent two days learning all about them at Southern Crescent Technical College in Griffin, Georgia.


"If you had to ask me right now, 'Hey could you do a splice for me?' I could do it," Cole said confidently. "I think that had a lot to do with the professor being hands-on with us. He was right there with us the entire time."


Days later, the instructors were back helping other students build confidence at West Georgia Technical College in Douglasville, Georgia.


"There are three requirements that the contractors are looking for to get somebody into the job. The number one requirement they have is are you dependable?" explained Holst. "Two, are you willing to work? Three, are you willing to learn?"


"The Douglas County community really seems very excited that this project is here and is starting," said Kristen Buoy, the vice president of academic affairs at West Georgia Technical College. "We hope to build some programming around other needs with AWS as AWS continues to build out the facility."
