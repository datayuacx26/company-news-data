## What Is a Dunning Letter?


A dunning letter or notice, is a formal written communication sent to a customer or client with an overdue or[past due invoice](https://upflow.io/blog/ar-collections/past-due-invoices) . Its primary purpose is to remind the debtor of their pending payment, convey a sense of urgency, and clearly request that the balance be settled promptly. Unlike[email payment reminders](https://upflow.io/blog/payment-reminders/payment-reminders) , dunning letters mark a more serious phase in the[accounts receivable process](https://upflow.io/blog/ar-collections/accounts-receivable-process) , often issued when initial emails or phone reminders have not led to payment. This escalation highlights the importance of settling the debt to avoid potential consequences, such as penalties or legal action.


---


## A Typical Dunning Strategy


The dunning process typically begins a few days before the invoice due date with a friendly reminder, followed by another on the due date itself. After the due date has passed, initial payment reminder emails should be polite and gentle, assuming any delay might be unintentional. The goal at this stage is to encourage prompt payment while maintaining a positive[customer relationship](https://upflow.io/financial-relationship-management) .


If there’s no response after the first overdue reminder, subsequent emails should gradually become more assertive. By the third reminder, it may be appropriate to escalate to phone calls, which allow for direct communication and quicker resolution. Phone calls also add a personal touch, signaling both the importance of the payment and the value of the relationship.


When emails and calls do not secure payment, the next step is to send formal dunning letters, which signal a heightened level of urgency and convey that further delays may lead to more serious actions.


If these efforts are unsuccessful, the final escalation involves issuing a legal notice, indicating that the debt may be pursued through legal channels if left unpaid. This step is a last resort, to be taken only after all reasonable collection attempts have been made.

## Dunning Email vs Dunning Letter


Both are part of a structured[dunning strategy](https://upflow.io/blog/ar-collections/how-to-set-up-a-systematic-and-efficient-cash-collection) , but they serve different stages of the collections process and carry different weight with the recipient.


A dunning email is sent earlier in the escalation sequence, typically before or shortly after an invoice becomes overdue. The tone is lighter, the format is digital, and the intent is to prompt payment before the situation becomes formal.[Payment reminder emails](https://upflow.io/blog/payment-reminders/payment-reminders) land in a billing contact's inbox alongside dozens of other messages. They are easy to act on quickly, but also easy to deprioritise.


A dunning letter is a physical or formal written document sent when emails and phone calls have already gone unanswered. It signals that the matter has escalated beyond routine follow-up. Receiving a formal letter in the post carries more psychological weight than another email, which is precisely why it tends to prompt action when earlier reminders have not.


The practical difference for your AR process is timing and channel. Emails handle the early and mid-stage reminders. Letters come in when those efforts have been exhausted and the goal shifts from gentle follow-up to formal documentation of the debt and your attempts to collect it.


One thing both formats share: the more personalised they are, the better they work. Generic mass communications, whether email or letter, get treated as spam. A message that references the specific invoice number, the exact amount, and the customer's name and company performs significantly better across both channels.

## Things to Include in a Dunning Letter


While drafting an effective dunning letter, it’s essential to include specific details that encourage payment while maintaining a professional tone. Here’s a breakdown of key elements:


1.


**Recipient’s Information** : Address the letter to the appropriate individual by including their full name, title, and company name. Adding details about your provided services or products serves as a reminder of the relationship and reinforces the value you’ve delivered.


2.


**Your Contact Information** : Offer multiple ways for the recipient to get in touch. List your business address, email, and phone number to make it simple for them to resolve the payment issue or ask questions. The easier it is to contact you, the more likely they’ll address the payment promptly.


3.


**Overdue Invoice Attachments** : Attach copies of all overdue invoices to provide the recipient with clear visibility into what’s owed. This should include invoice numbers, amounts, and due dates, with a summary of these details in the body of the letter. A concise list helps avoid confusion, especially if multiple invoices are outstanding.


4.


**Invoice Details and Payment Deadlines** : Reference the specific invoice numbers, the total amount due, and the days past due to underscore the urgency. Reinforcing these details emphasizes missed deadlines and can motivate the client to act quickly.


5.


**Available Payment Options** : Clearly list payment methods such as credit card, bank transfer, or online payment options. Make payment as easy as possible by providing direct payment links if available. The more straightforward the process, the quicker you’re likely to receive payment.


6.


**Consequences of Continued Non-Payment** : Outline potential next steps if the debt remains unpaid, such as suspension of services, assignment to a collection agency, or legal action. Refer to any contractual clauses that back these actions, like[late fees](https://upflow.io/blog/ar-collections/late-fee) or penalties, while maintaining a respectful, professional tone. This demonstrates the importance of payment without damaging the client relationship.

## Our Dunning Letter Templates


Using a structured, phased approach to dunning letters can significantly improve payment recovery rates while maintaining strong client relationships. Below, we provide four dunning letter templates that escalate from a friendly reminder to a final legal notice, guiding the process and helping ensure timely collection of outstanding payments.


### **Template #1: Gentle Reminder for Payment**


The first dunning letter serves as a courteous reminder, typically sent after initial emails or phone calls haven’t resolved the overdue payment. It provides a summary of outstanding invoices, includes payment methods, and gently encourages the client to address the balance promptly.


### **Template #2: Notice of Impending Service Restriction**


This second dunning letter escalates the tone slightly, indicating that if payment is not made within a specified timeframe, access to services or products may be limited. The aim is to emphasize the importance of timely payment without yet taking restrictive action.


### **Template #3: Service Restriction and Legal Warning**


At this stage, the letter informs the client of restricted access due to non-payment and includes a clear warning of potential legal consequences if payment is not made within a certain period. The tone is more direct and signals the seriousness of the matter.


### **Template #4: Legal Notice Due to Non-Payment**


This final template is used once all previous reminders have been exhausted, and legal action has been initiated. It communicates that the debt is now in legal proceedings and urges the client to settle the outstanding balance to prevent further penalties or costs.

## How to Avoid the Need for a Dunning Letter


To prevent situations leading to the use of a dunning letter, let’s look at ways to reduce late payments for your business:


1.


**Establish Clear Payment Terms:**


Set precise payment terms in contracts, including due dates, payment methods, and late fees. Transparent terms help clients understand their obligations from the outset.


2.


**Use Proactive Payment Reminders:**


Schedule gentle reminders before invoices are due. Using tools like Upflow to send automated reminders can keep clients informed without the need for frequent follow-ups, keeping payments top of mind.


3.


**Offer Multiple Payment Options:**


Make it easy for clients by offering a range of payment methods, like bank transfers, credit cards, or online portals. Convenience can help reduce payment delays.


4.


**Incentivize Early Payments:**


Encourage timely payments by offering small discounts or incentives for early settlement. This motivates clients to prioritize your invoices.


5.


**Automate Invoicing and Payment Tracking:**


Implement automated systems to send invoices and track payments. Real-time tracking lets you take quick action if a payment is nearing overdue status.


6.


**Segment Clients Based on Payment Behavior:**


Identify and categorize clients by their payment history, allowing you to give extra attention to clients with frequent delays and streamline communications for others.

## FAQs


Q: What is a dunning letter?


A: A dunning notice (or dunning letter) is a formal written reminder sent to a customer with an overdue invoice. It highlights the amount owed, the invoice details, and the urgency of payment, often after initial reminders or calls have gone unanswered. Dunning letters mark a more serious stage in the collections process and may mention potential consequences if the payment is not resolved promptly.


Q: What is the difference between a dunning letter and a dunning notice?


A: The terms are used interchangeably in most business contexts. Both refer to a formal written communication sent to a customer with an overdue invoice, requesting payment and outlining potential consequences if the balance remains unpaid. Some businesses use "dunning notice" for earlier, less formal escalations and reserve "dunning letter" for more serious written communications sent by post, but there is no universal standard. What matters more than the label is the content: clear invoice details, a specific payment deadline, and an appropriate tone for the stage of the collections process.


Q: How is a dunning letter different from a payment reminder email?


A: A payment reminder email is typically informal and sent shortly before or just after an invoice is due. A dunning letter, on the other hand, is a more formal and escalated communication, sent when prior reminders have failed to secure payment. It usually implies potential consequences if the issue isn’t resolved.


Q: When should I send a dunning letter?


A: You should send a dunning letter after several polite payment reminder emails and a few phone calls have gone unanswered. This typically happens 20–30 days past the invoice due date, depending on your internal AR policy and the client’s payment behavior.


Q: How many dunning letters should I send?


A: A common approach involves sending 3 to 4 dunning letters at increasing levels of urgency: 1: Gentle reminder 2: Notice of potential service restriction 3: Service restriction in effect with legal warning 4: Final legal notice Each notice should be spaced 5–10 business days apart to give the client time to respond.


Q: What tone should a dunning letter use?


A: The tone should match the stage of the dunning process. Template 1 should be polite and professional, assuming the delay may be unintentional. By template 2, the tone becomes firmer, signaling that service restrictions are possible. Template 3 is direct, confirming restrictions are in place and warning of legal consequences. Template 4 is formal and factual, stating that legal proceedings have begun. Throughout all stages, keep the language respectful. The goal is to recover payment, not permanently damage a client relationship that may otherwise be valuable.


Q: Are dunning letters legally binding?


A: Dunning letter themselves are not legally binding documents, but they may reference contractual obligations, late fees, or legal consequences stated in your agreement. If escalated to legal action, well-documented dunning notices can support your case by showing a good-faith effort to collect payment.


Q: Can dunning letters be automated?


A: Yes, and for most B2B finance teams managing a high volume of invoices, automation is the only way to run a consistent dunning process. Manually tracking which customers have received which letters, at which stage, and when to escalate is error-prone and time-consuming. AR automation software like Upflow lets finance teams configure dunning workflows once, setting which communication goes out at each stage and triggering escalations automatically based on how many days past due an invoice is. Every interaction is logged, so if a case does escalate to legal action, there is a documented record of every attempt to collect.


Q: What happens if a dunning letter is ignored?


A: If a customer does not respond to any of your dunning letters, the next step is typically a formal legal notice, indicating that you intend to pursue the debt through legal channels. Before reaching that point, it is worth making one final attempt through a direct call from an account manager, since they may have context or a relationship that the finance team does not. If the debt remains unresolved after all dunning stages and direct outreach, options include referring the account to a collections agency, initiating small claims proceedings for smaller amounts, or instructing a lawyer to pursue formal legal action. Well-documented dunning letters strengthen your position in any of these scenarios by showing a clear, good-faith effort to collect before escalating.
