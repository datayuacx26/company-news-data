## It all started with a Slack message


It was a normal, busy day at Yassir. I prepared my coffee and was focusing on writing copy for a new flow, when a Slack message suddenly popped up:


*“Hey! Can you check here why the French word is breaking the button?”*


A screenshot followed. The French word ***“Remboursement”*** was spilling out of its container like a loose thread. At the same time, the English equivalent “Refund” sat perfectly, and the Arabic one, usually the trickiest of all three languages, looked just fine as well.


Another screenshot arrived moments later. Same word, same position, but smaller. An attempted fix that somehow made it worse.


It’s just one word! That’s not a big deal! You might think!


Yes. But it was enough to remind me that multilingual UX writing isn’t about swapping text. It’s about meaning, tone, and shaping words to fit the space they live in.

## The Invisible Work


Press enter or click to view image in full size


That moment wasn’t about fixing a label. It was a reminder of something we often overlook: working in three languages means constantly negotiating meaning, space, and language-specific constraints.


We tend to assume that an experience designed in English will naturally work in French and Arabic. But French expands. Arabic flows right-to-left. And sometimes, a word like **Remboursement** simply refuses to cooperate.


That little incident became a quiet reminder: words and design don’t exist separately. They move together, especially in products that ship in multiple languages.


## It takes a team


Stepping into a leadership role made one thing very clear to me: UX writing is never solo work.


Every feature, flow, and microcopy is shaped through collaboration with designers, researchers, and product managers. We debate tone. We negotiate character limits. We wrestle with right‑to‑left layouts. We question whether a French sentence feels too cold when mirrored in Arabic.


Day after day, the work proves the same point: this isn’t translation. It’s localization, shaping ideas so they make sense within the nuances of each language. And honestly? That complexity is what makes it exciting.


## The challenges are real!


Press enter or click to view image in full size


Below are some of the recurring challenges my team and I face as UX writers working in three languages at Yassir. They’re not edge cases or major incidents; just everyday realities that quietly shape how we design, write, and collaborate.


## 1- Space is not neutral


Words behave differently across languages. The “ **Remboursement”** button was a perfect example.


French tends to expand: one English word might turn into three in French, or a full sentence in Arabic! Add right‑to‑left directionality, and suddenly space becomes a UX constraint, not a design afterthought.


English layouts don’t automatically survive translation. They require early awareness of language specifications and close collaboration between designers and writers from the start.


**Key takeaway: Space is a content decision. Designing for multilingual products means designing with language in mind early, not after handoff.**


## 2- Tone doesn’t translate


While we aim for a cohesive brand voice, this becomes a challenge when our product speaks multiple languages across diverse cultures. What feels “Friendly” in English can often land as “too casual” in French. A polite phrase in French might feel unnaturally stiff in Arabic.


During our work as writers, we learned that literal translation is the enemy of nuance. Because a single phrase can shift from “acceptable” to “offensive” across borders.


That’s why we prioritize localization over simple word-swapping, which requires a lot of collaboration and constant debate with designers, product managers, and even among ourselves as writers.


**Key takeaway: Multilingual UX writing isn’t a literal translation, but more like crafting the user’s emotional experience in their own tongue.**


## 3- Process debt


Like many growing teams, copy doesn’t always arrive early enough.


Sometimes it comes in after the designs are finished, or even after a feature has shipped, which makes it feel like a last-minute addition rather than a core part of the process. Suddenly, we’re juggling multiple projects under tight deadlines, trying to survive a growing backlog of Jira tickets and endless lists of mismatched keys.


These misalignments add up quickly. They’re frustrating, but also instructive. They’ve taught us how to anticipate friction and advocate for copy to be part of the process much earlier.


**Key takeaway: Late copy is expensive. Involving writers early reduces rework and protects experience quality.**


## 4- Emotional labor


Another recurring challenge is seeing copy change in production without the writers’ input.


Even after alignment on tone and wording, we sometimes discover a different version live in the product. With many teams moving fast, changes get lost, or last‑minute requests never loop back to writers.


Those moments are draining. They can even make you question your impact.


But over time, we’ve realized these frustrations are actually a signal. Reminders showing us how important it is to make our work “stick” and to position ourselves as essential parts of the UX journey. It’s not that we’re against making changes; we just want to solidify a culture of collaboration throughout the entire product life cycle. It’s about advocating for clarity and ensuring we stay aligned with the brand’s tone of voice from start to finish.


**Key takeaway:** Emotional labor often points to process gaps. Fixing them strengthens trust and collaboration.


## Case Studies: when language meets design


To give you a better idea of what this looks like in practice, here are two moments where we had to move beyond simple translation to solve a real user problem.


## 1. The “Remboursement” ripple effect


Press enter or click to view image in full size


This is the button that first caught our attention in that Slack message earlier.


The French word Remboursement overflowed its container, while English and Arabic worked perfectly.


What started as a spacing issue turned into a deeper discussion: shorten the word? Truncate it? Adjust the layout? Or Rethink the label entirely?


There was no single right answer. But talking it through helped us balance clarity, usability, and space, a reminder that every character interacts with the interface and user expectations.


In the end, the solution was a mix of layout adjustments and word choice. We went with the infinitive verb **“Rembourser”** and slightly increased the button size to resolve the issue.


## 2. The “Got it!” challenge


Press enter or click to view image in full size


We often debate how to keep Yassir’s voice local, helpful, and bold, consistent, while allowing the tone to flex. Context is everything.


Take a simple confirmation message.


In English, **“Got it!”** feels casual and efficient.


In French: A literal translation feels abrupt, so we polish it for a better flow. Instead of one word “Compris” we use a full sentence: “J’ai compris !” (I understand).


In Arabic: A direct translation like Fahimt (فهمت) can come across as blunt or even dismissive. By using Hasanan (حسنًا), meaning “Okay,” we keep the voice consistent while respecting local social norms.


This isn’t about right vs. wrong. It’s about balancing brand consistency with cultural relevance, and it’s these moments that spark the liveliest debates in our team. Every word carries a weight of perception.


## What we’re learning


We’re still figuring out what works best. Early copy audits. Tone alignment sessions. Getting writers involved sooner. Clarifying Jira briefs.


These are experiments, not magic bullets. Each step teaches us something new. Helps our words as well as our intent travel more faithfully across languages.


**Learning as we go is part of the job. And that’s okay.**


Along the way, a few things have become clear:


- Multilingual UX isn’t translation; it’s language, culture, and psychology combined
- Copy shapes experience from the very beginning.
- Late‑stage edits cost more than early collaboration.
- Writers aren’t QA buffers — they’re part of the product.
- Tone matters as much as meaning, and adapting it locally is essential.
