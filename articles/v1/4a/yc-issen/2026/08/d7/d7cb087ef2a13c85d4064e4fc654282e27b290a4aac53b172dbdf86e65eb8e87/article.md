## What CEFR level does business English require?


Business English is less about a single fluency threshold and more about matching your level to the room you are in. Most professional contexts sit between B1 and C1, and the tasks inside each band are specific.


At **B1** (IELTS 4.5 to 5.0), you can follow routine meetings, give direct instructions, and handle predictable client emails. At **B2** (IELTS 5.5 to 6.5), you can lead a discussion, present quarterly results, and run a client call without a script. At **C1** (IELTS 7.0 to 8.0), you can chair a high-stakes meeting, negotiate a contract, and shift register on the fly.


If you can read a proposal but freeze when your manager asks for your view, you are likely B1 reaching for B2. That plateau is where[AI English fluency](https://www.issen.com/blog/english-fluency-with-ai) practice pays off fastest.


## What business English vocabulary should you learn first?


Grammar drills teach you to build sentences. Meetings ask you to steer them. The vocabulary that separates a B2 professional from a stalled one is functional: short phrases that buy time, soften disagreement, and confirm meaning without breaking flow.[AI English learning for non-native speakers](https://www.issen.com/blog/english-learning-non-native-speakers-ai) covers which of these patterns transfer fastest.


Learn these five function banks first. Rehearse them out loud before you touch industry jargon.


**Hedging**


- "I'd want to check that before we commit."
- "In principle, yes. The details we'd need to look at."
- "I'm inclined to agree, subject to timelines."


**Pushing back politely**


- "I take that point, though my concern is the runway."
- "That's fair. Can I offer another angle?"
- "I see it slightly differently, if I can explain."


**Buying time**


- "Let me make sure I understand what you're asking."
- "Give me a second to think that through."
- "Can I park that and circle back?"


**Interrupting**


- "If I could jump in for a second."
- "Sorry to cut across, but there's something worth flagging."
- "Just to build on what Sara said."


**Confirming understanding**


- "So what we're agreeing is that marketing owns Q2 delivery."
- "Just to play that back: two milestones, one owner, end of month."
- "So the ask is X by Friday. Have I got that right?"


The words for your field will come from the work itself. The scaffolding above is what holds a conversation together while you find them.

## How do you roleplay a job interview with an AI tutor?


Set the scene in one line, then hand the tutor the constraints. Something like: "You are a hiring manager at a Series B fintech interviewing me for a senior PM role. Respond at C1 level, use technical vocabulary, and interrupt me if my answer runs over 90 seconds. Ask two competency questions, then throw one unexpected follow-up."


The unpredictability is the point. Scripted prep tools let you rehearse polished answers to expected questions; the[best AI language tutors for conversation](https://www.issen.com/blog/best-ai-language-tutors-conversation-practice) force you to produce language you have not pre-loaded. That pressure surfaces the gap Merrill Swain's[Output Hypothesis](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1095572.pdf) names: knowing a phrase and retrieving it under stress are separate skills.


After each run, isolate one weak point (rambling, filler words, freezing on "tell me about a time") and rerun the same prompt with a tighter constraint on that behavior.


## How do you practice negotiating in English?


Negotiation English moves faster than meeting English and hides more. Speakers anchor with numbers, concede without conceding, reframe an offer as a question, and close by letting silence do the work. Speed, ambiguity, and face-saving indirection trip non-native speakers up, not vocabulary.


Set up the drill like this. Tell your tutor: "You are a procurement manager at a mid-market SaaS buyer. I am selling. Push back hard on price at B2 to C1 level. Use hedging and conditionals. Run three exchanges. Do not concede easily." Focus each exchange on one move type below.


**Anchoring**


- "Where we typically land for a team of your size is around X."
- "Before we get to numbers, what does success look like on your side?"


**Conceding**


- "I could see a path to that, provided we lock a two-year term."
- "I'd need to check, but in principle, yes."


**Reframing**


- "Instead of a discount, could we look at extended payment terms?"
- "If price is the blocker, what would the right number unlock?"


**Closing**


- "So if I can get you to X, we're signing this quarter?"
- "What would need to be true for procurement to move today?"


Run the same three exchanges twice. The second pass is where conditional grammar starts landing without conscious effort.


## Is AI roleplay better than a business English course?


A well-run business English course gives you frameworks: report structures, email registers, presentation openings, the grammar of formal writing. It also gives you a syllabus, which matters when you have a BEC exam or a certificate deadline. What it rarely offers is enough unscripted speaking time for retrieval to become automatic.


AI roleplay adds what courses skip: retrieval under real-time pressure, converting known forms into automatic speech. The[AI vs. human tutor](https://www.issen.com/blog/ai-vs-human-tutor-language-speaking) tradeoff maps onto Swain's distinction between knowing a form and producing it under real-time pressure, a gap most classroom hours cannot close because 12 learners cannot each get 40 minutes of open-ended speaking per session.


Use the course for structure. Use AI roleplay for volume. The combination beats either alone.
