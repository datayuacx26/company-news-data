# The best tinnitus apps of 2026, compared

There are dozens of tinnitus apps, and most reviews of them skip the three things that matter most:


1. Whether there is real research on the app itself.
2. What it actually costs.
3. How much of your day it needs.


This guide compares the tinnitus apps a US patient is most likely to come across, asking the same questions of every one, and showing our work.


One note first, in the interest of transparency.[Oto](https://joinoto.com/) , one of the apps below, is made by us. We have held our own app to the same standard as the rest, we show where other apps beat it, and every study and price is sourced at the bottom.

## How we compared them


We included the tinnitus apps a US patient is most likely to come across: ones you can actually get in the US, and ones people search for or get recommended. We left out apps you cannot use here, and tools with almost no footprint.


We asked the same five questions of each app:


1. What it actually does.
2. Whether there is published research on the app itself.
3. What it really costs in the US.
4. How you get it.
5. How much daily time it asks for.


One thing about evidence is worth understanding, because it is where a lot of tinnitus marketing gets slippery. There is plenty of research on the *techniques* , like[CBT for tinnitus](https://joinoto.com/articles/cbt-for-tinnitus) or sound therapy in general. That is not the same as research on a *specific app* . We only count a study as evidence for an app if that study actually tested that app.


## Two of these are programs you get through a clinician


Two options are not download-and-go apps. You get them through a clinician, as part of a program. They are also the two ends of the spectrum in this guide: one has the most research behind it, the other the least on its current form.


Clinician-led program


### Oto


CBT and sound-therapy program, used with a tinnitus specialist

Oto is the most clinical option here, used alongside care from a tinnitus specialist rather than on your own. You see an audiologist or doctor who assesses your tinnitus and hearing and builds a plan around you, and the app supports that plan with short CBT-based audio sessions, sound therapy and education.


Best for


People who want the most clinical, most personalized option and are happy to work with a tinnitus specialist. Not the pick if you want to sort it yourself without seeing anyone.


Because a professional sets it up and follows your progress, the plan is personalized and overseen by someone who knows tinnitus. Day to day, you do a five to ten minute guided session (a CBT exercise, breathing or a "skill of the day") and can dip into a sound library of white, pink and brown noise, nature sounds and layered soundscapes, including a background sound you set around your own tinnitus. There is a wind-down session for sleep, and your clinician can track your progress and adjust the plan.


**The research:** Oto has more[published clinical research](https://joinoto.com/research) than any other app in this guide. A randomized controlled trial run by Flinders University in 2025 found people using Oto reported about a 9-point greater drop in tinnitus-related distress than a wait-list group over six months, on the Tinnitus Functional Index (Mui et al., 2025). A larger University of Cambridge trial, DEFINE, has finished and is in peer review, so we are not quoting its numbers yet. An earlier feasibility study also reported a benefit, and for transparency, one of its co-authors is a co-founder of Oto.


**Cost and access:** Oto is not sold on its own and is not a separate charge. It is included in a tinnitus care plan from a participating clinic. If your clinic recommends a hearing device, Oto is included alongside it at no extra cost; if not, it is part of a basic care plan (these typically run $500 to $1,000, set by the clinic, with Oto included). Either way, you are paying for your care, not for the app. You can[find a provider](https://joinoto.com/providers) near you.


**Ratings and reviews:** 4.7★ on the App Store (845 reviews), around 4.0★ on Google Play. Reviewers most often credit the CBT-based sessions and breathing exercises with helping them feel calmer and more in control of the ringing, and like that the short daily format is easy to keep up.


**The catch, and the point:** Oto is not a download-and-go app. The full program comes through your clinic, not a self sign-up. You can[download a free demo](https://joinoto.com/download) to get a feel for it, but to unlock everything you go through a provider. That clinician-led setup is what makes it the most thorough and most personalized option here, and it is also the honest downside: if you would rather not see a clinician at all, one of the free apps below will suit you better.

Neuromonics is a supervised, six-month program. After an assessment with a certified specialist, the app plays music that has been acoustically modified and tuned to your hearing, which you listen to for two or more hours a day, alongside one-to-one coaching.


Best for


People set on a clinician-guided acoustic-therapy program who are comfortable with the high cost and the daily commitment, and who do not mind that the current app is unproven and lightly reviewed.


It used to be delivered on a dedicated device, and was rebuilt as an app after the company was acquired (it is now run by a firm called SoundVida).


**The research:** Neuromonics has a genuine history of controlled trials, but they were done on the original device version (Davis et al., 2007 and 2008), not the current app. As far as we can find, the app-and-telehealth version has not been studied on its own. The original device was cleared by the FDA back in 2005.


**Cost and access:** this is the most expensive and most demanding option here. You enroll through a clinician, the guided program is $199.99 a month, and it drops to $29.99 a month for maintenance afterwards. It asks for two or more hours of listening a day for six months.


**Ratings and reviews:** the current app has just 3.1★ from only 9 App Store ratings, so there is very little user feedback to go on, and what there is centers on a clunky sign-up (you have to get a clinician code before you can really start). Treat it as a small, early relaunch.

ReSound Tinnitus Relief is the best free place to start. You do not need a ReSound hearing aid; everything plays through your phone or any headphones. You build your own soundscape by layering up to five sounds (nature sounds, plus white and other noise), save your mixes, and set a timer.


Best for


Anyone who wants a solid, free sound-therapy toolkit, especially for sleep.


A "Relax" section adds guided meditations, breathing exercises and calming imagery, and there is a short "Learn" section on tinnitus. There is no set daily routine; most people use it at bedtime or in stressful moments.


**Cost:** the core app is genuinely free, with everything above included. There is an optional premium plan ($6.99 a month or $69.99 a year) that adds a personalized weekly plan, but you do not need it to get real value.


**Ratings and reviews:** one of the most-reviewed apps in the category, 4.7★ on the App Store from over 18,000 reviews, and roughly 4.4★ on Google Play with around 800,000 downloads. People call it a life-saver for sleep and nighttime tinnitus and like that the free version is genuinely full. The common complaint is reliability: the sound sometimes stops during a long overnight timer, or an incoming call interrupts it.


**Worth knowing:** Beltone Tinnitus Calmer is the same app from the same parent company, so if you wear Beltone hearing aids, that is the matching version. One catch: Beltone's app is currently only on iPhone, it has been pulled from the Google Play store, so Android users should use ReSound.

myNoise gives you the most control over your sound for almost nothing. It is a library of 300-plus adjustable soundscapes, built by one Belgian sound engineer, and several are made for tinnitus. Instead of one volume knob, each sound has up to ten sliders you move across the pitch range, so you can shape it precisely.


Best for


People who want to fine-tune their own masking sound and avoid subscriptions.


There is a tinnitus tool where you raise only the bands that match your own tinnitus, aiming for the quietest sound that blends with the ringing rather than drowning it. An optional calibration tunes everything to your headphones and hearing.


**Cost:** free to try (a 14-day full unlock), then a single one-time purchase of $19.99 to keep the whole library. No subscription, no ads. The same sounds are free to use on the myNoise website in a browser.


**Ratings and reviews:** 4.7★ on the App Store (949 reviews) and about 4.4★ on Google Play (500,000-plus downloads), and the website reaches far more people than the app does. People love the depth of control, the quality of the real recordings, and the one-time price. The recurring complaint is that saved custom mixes can disappear after a crash or an update, so back them up.


**The research:** none, and to its credit, myNoise does not claim any. It is a sound tool, not a medical program.

MindEar is the pick if you want structured CBT on your own, without seeing a clinician. It is a self-guided, roughly eight-week program built around a chat coach that is available any time and walks you through cognitive behavioral therapy techniques a little at a time, plus soundscapes, guided sleep sessions and a podcast.


Best for


People who want to try structured CBT on their own phone.


The chat coach covers education, coping strategies and exercises to change how your brain reacts to the sound. It comes out of tinnitus research at the University of Auckland, and offers a free 15-minute consultation with a tinnitus expert when you start. CBT is the approach with the strongest overall evidence for reducing tinnitus distress.


**Cost:** free to start, then a subscription for the full program, around $6 a month or $59 to $69 a year. (Their store listing shows several prices at once, so check what you are quoted before you buy.)


**Ratings and reviews:** 4.5★ on the App Store (464 reviews) and about 3.8★ on Google Play (10,000-plus installs). People like the breathing and relaxation exercises most, and that it is research-backed. The complaints are mostly technical: glitches, being asked to log in again after paying, and a hearing check that did not always show results.


**The research:** MindEar has a small pilot and a larger trial behind it (Jackson et al., 2026, found a meaningful drop in tinnitus distress over eight weeks). Both studies involve people connected to the company, so treat them as promising rather than fully independent.

### AudioCardio


Passive sound conditioning


4.2 ★


342 ratings


App Store


Cost in the US14-day free trial, then $9.99 to $99.99


TherapyPassive sound conditioning (near-inaudible tones)


Research on the appNone on the app for tinnitus


PlatformsiOS and Android


AudioCardio takes a different route from everything else here. Rather than masking the tinnitus, it plays personalized, almost-inaudible tones matched to your hearing test, in the background for about an hour a day, on the idea that gently stimulating your weaker hearing frequencies may help over time.


Best for


People curious about a hearing-conditioning approach who understand the evidence for this specific app is thin, and who will stop if their tinnitus feels worse.


You do a quick in-app hearing check, it builds a track targeting your weak spots, and you play it under music or work without paying attention to it. You retake the check every so often so it recalibrates.


**Cost:** a 14-day free trial, then a subscription: $9.99 a month for the basic tier, $12.99 a month (or $99.99 a year) for the higher tier that targets more frequencies.


**Ratings and reviews:** 4.2★ on the App Store (342 reviews) and around 3.1★ on Google Play (50,000-plus installs). Fans like that it is passive and fits into the day, and some report clearer hearing. The critics are blunter: several long-term users report no change after months and call it expensive for what it is, and a few report their tinnitus feeling worse after starting.


**The research:** AudioCardio's marketing points to a long list of studies, but those are about the general science of sound conditioning, not tests of this app, and much of it is not about tinnitus. We could not find an independent trial of the AudioCardio app for tinnitus. The app's own terms state it is not an FDA-reviewed medical device, and, notably, they warn that "rare" cases of worsened hearing conditions have been reported with intensive frequency stimulation, which lines up with those few negative reports.

## So which tinnitus app should you choose?


There is no single best tinnitus app, because the right one depends on where you are.


- **Want the most clinical, best-researched option, and happy to see a specialist?** Oto.
- **Want the best free place to start on your own?** ReSound Tinnitus Relief.
- **Want the most control over your own masking sound, for a one-off $20?** myNoise.
- **Want structured CBT without seeing a clinician?** MindEar.


Whichever you pick, an app tends to work best as part of a plan rather than on its own, and sticking with it matters more than which one you choose. And if your tinnitus is new, pulses in time with your heartbeat, is in one ear only, or came alongside dizziness or hearing loss, see a clinician first. Some tinnitus needs a medical check before you reach for an app.


## Frequently asked questions


Do tinnitus apps actually work?


For many people, the right approach can genuinely reduce how much tinnitus bothers them, though not by silencing the sound itself. The strongest evidence is for CBT-based techniques, and for some people, sound therapy helps too. Results vary a lot from person to person, and an app usually works best alongside support from a clinician.


What is the best tinnitus app?


There is no single best one. Oto has the most published research behind it, though you use it with a clinician. For a free start on your own, ReSound Tinnitus Relief is the fullest option, and myNoise gives you the most control for a one-off price. For self-guided CBT, MindEar.


Are there free tinnitus apps?


Yes. ReSound Tinnitus Relief and Widex Zen are free to use, and myNoise is free to try then a one-time $19.99. Some apps are free to download but charge for the full program, so check before you commit.


Is there a clinically proven tinnitus app?


"Proven" is a strong word for any tinnitus app. A few have been tested in clinical studies on the app itself, and most have not. Oto has the most, with university-led trials and a larger trial (DEFINE) awaiting publication. Many popular apps have no published research on the app specifically.


How much does the Oto app cost?


In the US, Oto is not sold on its own or charged separately. It is included in a tinnitus care plan from a participating clinic, either alongside a hearing device at no extra cost, or as part of a basic care plan (typically $500 to $1,000, set by the clinic, with Oto included). You can[find a provider](https://joinoto.com/providers) to get started.


Which tinnitus app is best for sleep?


Many people find a free sound-therapy app easiest for sleep, and ReSound Tinnitus Relief is the most-praised here for nighttime use. myNoise is a good choice if you want to shape the exact sound.


Can a tinnitus app cure tinnitus?


No. No app, and no treatment, can cure tinnitus today. What good tinnitus care can do is reduce how intrusive and distressing it feels, sometimes to the point where you rarely notice it. Be cautious of anything that promises a cure.
