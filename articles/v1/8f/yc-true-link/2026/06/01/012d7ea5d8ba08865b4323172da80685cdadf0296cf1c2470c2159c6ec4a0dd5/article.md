# When To Consider Legal Guardianship of an Aging Parent

*Learn what legal guardianship is, when it may be necessary for an aging parent, how it differs from powers of attorney and conservatorships, and what responsibilities guardians take on.*

When an aging parent starts making decisions that put them at risk, the question of what to do next becomes urgent. Legal guardianship often comes up as an option, and it can be the right one. But it's a significant legal step, and understanding what it involves, and what else is available, can make a real difference in how families move forward.


This guide covers the basics: what guardianship actually means, when it's typically used, and what other options are worth understanding first. Keep in mind that laws vary greatly state to state, but this article aims to give practical advice to consider (regardless of state) prior to making an application for guardianship.


### **What is Legal Guardianship?**


Legal guardianship is a court-authorized arrangement that gives one person (the “guardian”) the legal authority to make decisions on behalf of someone who can no longer make those decisions safely on their own.


For aging parents, this most often comes up when cognitive decline, dementia, a stroke, or serious illness has progressed to the point where the person can no longer manage their own care, finances, or daily needs.


Being named a guardian comes with legal responsibility. Depending on the authority granted by the court, the guardian may be responsible for medical decisions, managing finances, arranging housing, or all of the above.


**Power of Attorney vs. Guardianship**


A power of attorney (POA) and guardianship can accomplish similar goals — ensuring someone is authorized to act on your parent's behalf — but they work very differently.


A POA is a voluntary arrangement your parent approves and executes while they still have legal capacity. They choose someone they trust, sign a document, and no court is involved. A durable POA stays in effect after your parent loses capacity, which is what makes it[useful for aging parents](https://www.truelinkfinancial.com/blog/when-does-someone-need-help-with-managing-their-finances) . But a non-durable POA automatically terminates when your parent loses capacity, which is often when you need it most.


Guardianship, by contrast, is court-ordered and typically pursued when no POA is in place and a crisis has arrived, or when a parent has already lost the capacity to sign legal documents.


Many guardianship proceedings arise because a durable power of attorney was never established, is unavailable, or no longer adequately addresses the situation. If your parent still has legal capacity, a durable power of attorney is usually the faster, less expensive and easier path to achieve the same protections.


### **Guardianship vs. Conservatorship: What's the Difference?**


These terms are used interchangeably in some states and kept distinct in others, so it's worth understanding how your state handles the distinction early in the process.


In states that distinguish between the two:


- **Guardianship of the person** covers personal and medical decisions: where your parent lives, what healthcare they receive, and how their daily needs are met.
- **Conservatorship (also called “guardianship of the estate”)** covers financial decisions: managing bank accounts, paying bills, and handling property and assets.


A court may grant one or both, depending on what's needed. And in many cases, families pursuing guardianship of the person also seek conservatorship — but not always. If finances are being managed through an existing power of attorney or trust, a separate conservatorship may not be necessary.


### **Who Can Serve as Guardian?**


The individual petitioning for guardianship doesn't automatically get the role just by filing the request. Courts appoint the guardian and in most states look for someone who meets the following requirements:


- At least 18 years old;
- Without a disqualifying criminal history;
- Financially responsible (particularly relevant if conservatorship is also being sought); and
- Free of significant conflicts of interest.


Courts often give significant consideration to close family members, though state law and the specific circumstances of the case determine who is ultimately appointed. If no family member is willing, available, or suitable, the court may appoint a professional guardian or a public guardian instead.


Once appointed, guardians are subject to ongoing court oversight, which typically includes periodic reports or accountings to the court, often on an annual basis. It's an ongoing role, not a one-time legal action, and the court retains authority to modify or revoke the guardianship if circumstances change.


### **What Does a Guardian Actually Do?**


The scope of a guardian's responsibilities depends on what the court grants, but guardianship of the person usually involves:


- Deciding where your parent lives and what level of care they receive;
- Attending medical appointments and making treatment decisions; and
- Ensuring daily needs — food, safety, medication, social connection — are being met.


Conservatorship of the estate usually involves:


- Managing bank accounts and paying bills;
- Handling property, assets, and investments; and
- Maintaining detailed financial records and filing accountings with the court.


Across both roles, guardians are generally expected to act in the person's best interests while honoring their known wishes, values, and preferences whenever possible. Courts take this standard seriously, and guardians who don't meet the duties vested by the court can be removed.


Depending on state law and the scope of the appointment, certain major actions, such as selling real estate or making significant financial transactions, may require court approval.


**How the Guardianship Process Works**


The process varies by state but generally involves obtaining medical documentation of your parent's incapacity, filing a petition with the court, notifying your parent and other interested parties, attending a hearing, and receiving a court order defining the scope of your authority. Courts often limit guardianship to only what's necessary; full guardianship over both person and estate isn't automatic.


In situations where a parent is in immediate danger and there isn't time for the standard process, courts can grant emergency or temporary guardianship. This is a short-term arrangement with limited scope. In most states, a full hearing is still required before anything permanent is established.
