# Understanding the Process of Seeking Guardianship Over an Aging Parent

*Understand the key steps in seeking guardianship of an aging parent, from filing a petition and attending a hearing to taking on ongoing legal responsibilities.*

When a family decides to pursue guardianship of an aging parent, knowing what the process involves makes it easier to move through each stage. This guide walks through five common requirements for seeking a petition for guardianship and what to expect at each one.


Because guardianship can transfer important legal rights from the individual to another person, courts generally consider whether[less restrictive alternatives](https://www.truelinkfinancial.com/blog/when-does-someone-need-help-with-managing-their-finances) would adequately address the situation. If you're still weighing whether guardianship is the right path, this[overview of guardianship basics](https://www.truelinkfinancial.com/blog/%20guardianship-of-an-aging-parent) is a good place to start.


Laws vary greatly state to state, so this article aims to give good practical advice to consider prior to making an application in court but is not to be considered legal advice. Should you choose to file a petition, hiring a local attorney familiar with these complex proceedings is highly recommended.


**1. Obtain Medical Documentation of Incapacity**


The guardianship process begins with medical evidence. Most guardianship cases require medical or professional evidence demonstrating incapacity, although the timing and form of that evidence vary by state.


This typically takes the form of a physician's evaluation, resulting in a letter or certificate that attests to your parent's diagnosis and the ways it affects their decision-making capacity. The evaluating physician may be your parent's established doctor or an independent examiner, depending on your state's requirements.


What if your parent refuses the evaluation? This can happen, particularly when cognitive decline affects a parent's awareness of their own condition. If your parent won't cooperate, you can still file the guardianship petition, and as part of that process, ask the court to order an independent medical examination. If the court determines an independent evaluation is necessary, it may order one and appoint or approve a qualified professional to conduct it.


**2. File a Petition With the Court**


Guardianship begins with a formal legal filing, typically in probate court in the county where your parent lives. Filing the petition is what opens the process; it doesn't make you a guardian, and it doesn't obligate the court to grant one.


The petition generally includes:


- Your parent's name, age, and address
- Your relationship to them
- A description of the circumstances requiring guardianship
- The type of guardianship you're seeking (guardianship of the person, conservatorship of the estate, or both)
- Why other arrangements, such as a power of attorney, aren't sufficient or aren't in place


Filing fees vary by state and county. In most cases, you'll want[an elder law attorney](https://www.truelinkfinancial.com/blog/answering-common-questions-about-elder-law-attorneys) to prepare and file the petition, both to ensure it's complete and to avoid procedural delays.


**3. Notify Your Parent and Other Interested Parties**


Once the petition is filed, formal notice must be given to your parent and to others with a legal interest in the proceedings. This is a legal requirement, not a courtesy, and skipping or mishandling this step can delay or derail the process.


Your parent has the right to know what's being requested and to participate in the proceedings. In most states, notice must also be given to:


- Close relatives (spouses, adult children, siblings; the list varies by state)
- Anyone currently serving as a caregiver, healthcare proxy, or agent under a power of attorney
- In some cases, state agencies like Adult Protective Services


Family members who receive notice may have the right to object or to be heard by the court. This is by design; the notice requirement exists to protect your parent's rights and to give the court a fuller picture of the situation.


**4. Attend the Court Hearing**


The hearing is where the court evaluates the evidence and determines whether guardianship is warranted, and if so, who should serve as guardian. A judge will review the medical documentation, hear from the petitioner, and consider any objections from family members or other interested parties.


Many states provide for the appointment of an attorney or other representative for the proposed ward (your parent, in this case), often called an Attorney Ad Litem or court-appointed counsel, depending on the state. In many states, this attorney's job is to advocate for what your parent wants (their expressed wishes) rather than what you or the court believe is in their best interest. Other states instead direct appointed counsel toward the person's best interests, and a lawyer's obligations get more complicated when capacity is significantly diminished. The exact role depends on your state.


In some jurisdictions, the court may also appoint a Guardian Ad Litem — a separate person whose job is distinct from the Attorney Ad Litem's. Where the Attorney Ad Litem represents what your parent wants, the Guardian Ad Litem investigates the situation and reports to the court on what they believe is in your parent's best interest. These two roles can and sometimes do come into conflict.


The hearing is also where the court evaluates your suitability as guardian, reviewing your qualifications, any relevant background information, and potential conflicts of interest.


**5. Receive a Court Order Defining Your Authority**


If the court determines that guardianship is warranted and that you are a suitable guardian, it will issue a court order appointing you and defining the scope of your authority.


This order is not a blank check. Courts routinely limit guardianship to only what is necessary, which means you may be granted guardianship of the person without conservatorship of the estate or finances, or vice versa, depending on the situation. Full guardianship over both is not automatic.


What the order means in practice: Once appointed, you take on ongoing legal responsibilities. Depending on what the court grants, these typically include:


- Filing annual reports with the court on your parent's condition and living situation;
- Maintaining detailed financial records and submitting accountings if conservatorship is included;
- Seeking court approval for major decisions such as selling a home, significant financial moves, changes to care arrangements; and
- Acting in your parent's best interests while honoring their known wishes, values, and preferences whenever possible, with the court retaining authority to modify or revoke the guardianship if that standard isn't met.


Guardianship doesn't end with the court order. The court stays involved, and the responsibilities are ongoing.


**Emergency Guardianship: When There Isn't Time to Wait**


The five steps above describe the standard guardianship process. But in some situations, there isn't time for a full proceeding. If a parent is in immediate danger — facing an urgent medical decision, at risk of serious financial harm, or in a situation where waiting could cause real damage — courts can grant emergency or temporary guardianship.


This is a short-term arrangement that gives a guardian limited authority to act quickly while the standard process plays out. The scope is narrow, the duration is fixed, and a full hearing is still required before anything permanent is established. Emergency guardianship exists for genuine crises, not as a shortcut around the standard process. Courts are cautious about granting it and will want clear evidence that the situation can't wait.


**Retaining Legal Counsel**


The guardianship process has enough moving parts that trying to navigate it alone is rarely the right call. An elder law attorney can help you understand what's required in your state, prepare the petition, and represent you at the hearing.
