# How a Solo Contractor Wins Jobs by Showing Up With Two Estimates Already Done

**The client stood on his inherited roof, holding two estimates he never asked for.** One was $245,000: the full tear-off fully scoped. The other was $120,000: the approach Forrest Tlustos actually recommended, pending a structural engineer's sign-off. Forrest had built both before ever setting foot on the property, from a scope of work and a dozen photos the client sent the day before.


"He just looked at me and didn't know what to say," Forrest recalls. "He said, 'I couldn't even get anybody to show up. You showed up on time and you already had two estimates for me.'" The client called his brother that afternoon: no need to call anyone else.

## The problem: Waiting two-weeks for estimates


Forrest never planned on becoming a contractor. He came from sales and marketing, learned the field as a commercial superintendent, and got licensed in 2019 after a Cornell real estate development course convinced him contracting was his path into building and holding small multifamily properties. He started in Tucson knowing nobody.


As a solo operator, his bottleneck was the one every one-person shop knows: the bid process itself.


"Somebody wants me to come do a bid…do the dog and pony show, measure a bunch of stuff, bring my messy notes home, and I leave them on the counter," he says. "Then two or three days later I'm going through notes that don't make half as much sense,[I've got a bunch of pictures to sort through,](https://handoff.ai/creating-estimates-from-files) and by the time I get all that done, now I've got to pull out my estimate form."


Bigger jobs took a week or two to turn around. Some opportunities weren't worth the monotony at all.


## The new bid ritual: scope and photos before the site visit


Now[the estimate starts before the walkthrough](https://handoff.ai/instant-ai-estimates) . On the roof job, Forrest asked the client two things on the first phone call: send over the scope of work, and[take a dozen photos of the property from all sides.](https://handoff.ai/ai-site-walkthrough) He fed both into Handoff and ran the estimate two ways: the full tear-off as scoped, and the lighter re-roof he suspected the structure could support.


By the time he climbed the roof for the first walk, the only thing his preliminary numbers had missed was the pack-out of the original house. He handed the client both documents on the spot, and recommended the cheaper one.


The family hired him without calling another bidder.


The same play works mid-relationship. When a commercial client mentioned a smaller side project he wasn't ready to deal with yet, Forrest visited the site on a Friday and sent the estimate that afternoon. The client's reaction: "How come you can turn an estimate and be ready to go when we've been dealing with this other thing for nine months?"

## How to win a construction bid in 3 steps, according to Forrest


Forrest's pre-walkthrough ritual, in three steps any contractor can copy:


1. **Get the scope and photos before the visit.** Ask the client for whatever documents exist and a dozen photos of the property from all sides. Most will happily do it once you explain it helps you help them.
2. **Run the estimate both ways.** If there's a cheaper path the client hasn't considered, price it alongside what they asked for. Options build trust, and trust closes.
3. **Show up with paper in hand.** A preliminary estimate at the first meeting, clearly labeled as preliminary, beats a polished one delivered two weeks after the lead went cold.
