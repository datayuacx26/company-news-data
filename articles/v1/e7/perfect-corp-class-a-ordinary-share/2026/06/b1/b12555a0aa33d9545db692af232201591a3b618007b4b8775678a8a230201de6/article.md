## Why Knowing Customers' Skin Type Matters?


### The link between skin type and product effectiveness


Skincare products are formulated with specific skin types in mind. A rich, occlusive moisturizer that works beautifully for dry skin can clog pores and trigger breakouts on oily skin. A lightweight gel cleanser designed for oily skin may strip dry skin of the moisture it needs to stay healthy. When your products match your skin type, they work the way they're supposed to — and your skin responds.


### Common mistakes people make when they skip this step


The most common mistake is guessing. Many people assume they have oily skin because their face looks shiny by midday, when in reality they have combination or even dehydrated skin. Others assume sensitivity when they're actually reacting to a specific ingredient, not their skin type at all. These misidentifications lead to routines that address the wrong problem — and frustration when results don't come.


## The 5 Basic Skin Types Explained


### Normal


Balanced oil and moisture levels. Pores are small, the texture is even, and the skin rarely reacts to new products.


### Oily


Excess sebum production across the face, especially the T-zone. Prone to enlarged pores, shine, and breakouts.


### Dry


Insufficient sebum production. Skin feels tight, may flake or look dull, and is prone to fine lines appearing earlier.


### Combination


Oily in the T-zone (forehead, nose, chin) and normal to dry on the cheeks. The most common skin type.


### Sensitive / Redness-prone


Skin reacts easily to products, environmental triggers, or temperature changes. Often accompanied by redness, flushing, or irritation.


It's worth noting that skin type and skin condition are different things. Dehydration, acne, and hyperpigmentation are conditions that can affect any skin type and are influenced by environment, diet, and lifestyle. Your skin type is largely genetic and more stable over time, though it does shift with age, hormones, and seasons.


## How to Identify Your Skin Type at Home


### The Bare-Face Test (wash and wait method)


This is the most widely recommended DIY method. Wash your face with a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser, pat it dry, and don't apply anything afterward. Wait 30–60 minutes and observe


- Feels tight or looks flaky → likely dry


- Looks shiny all over → likely oily


- Shiny only on the forehead, nose, and chin → likely combination


- Looks comfortable, no shine or tightness → likely normal


- Looks red or feels irritated after washing → may be sensitive


### The Blotting Sheet Method


Press a clean blotting sheet against different areas of your face in the early afternoon (a few hours after your morning routin). Hold the sheet up to the light and check how much oil it absorbed.


- Oil on all areas → oily
- Oil only on the T-zone sheet → combination
- Little to no oil anywhere → dry or normal


### What to look for in the T-zone vs. U-zone


The T-zone (forehead, nose, chin) and U-zone (cheeks and jaw) behave differently and are key to distinguishing combination skin from oily or dry. If your T-zone is oily and your U-zone feels normal or slightly dry, that's the classic combination pattern. Pay attention to both zones separately rather than generalizing across your whole face.

## Why Home Methods Have Limits


### Skin changes with seasons, age, and hormones


Your skin in January may behave very differently from your skin in July. Cold, dry air pulls moisture from the skin, while heat and humidity increase sebum production. Hormonal fluctuations — from menstrual cycles, pregnancy, or menopause — also shift oil levels and sensitivity. A skin type assessment done once a year, or only in one season, may not reflect how your skin actually behaves year-round.


### Combination and redness types are often misidentified


Combination skin is the most commonly misidentified type because people tend to treat it as oily (since the T-zone is the most visible). This leads to over-stripping the skin, which can trigger even more oil production as a compensatory response. Similarly, skin that shows redness is often labeled "sensitive" when the redness may actually indicate a subtype of oily or combination skin rather than a fragility issue.


These nuances are hard to catch with a blotting sheet or a mirror. They require a more systematic analysis of the entire face, including areas that are easy to overlook.
