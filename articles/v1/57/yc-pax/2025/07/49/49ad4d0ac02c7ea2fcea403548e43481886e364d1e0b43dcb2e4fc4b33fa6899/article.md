# How to Save on Tariffs in 2025: Duty Drawback, Tariff Engineering, and Other Smart Import Strategies

## Other Effective Mitigation Strategies


Duty drawback, the First Sale Rule, and tariff engineering may get most of the attention—but they’re not the only tools available to companies trying to control landed costs. In today’s global trade environment, where tariffs fluctuate and supply chains shift constantly, the most resilient importers are deploying a combination of mitigation strategies across the entire sourcing and logistics lifecycle.


These strategies don’t just work in isolation. When combined, they form a layered defense against margin erosion—and create flexibility for companies navigating uncertainty.


### Inverted Duty Structures and Domestic Manufacturing Credits


In some cases, companies face an inverted duty structure—where the raw materials they import are taxed at a higher rate than the finished product they manufacture and sell. That makes local manufacturing not just patriotic, but financially advantageous.


Some importers take advantage of this by shifting final assembly to the U.S., then claiming drawback on the high-duty inputs exported as part of a finished product. Others invest in qualifying for domestic manufacturing incentives or seek out suppliers that can help rebalance their cost structure.


When paired with manufacturing drawback (or mixed provision claims that include both manufacturing and unused exports), this approach can both reduce duties paid and increase refunds recovered.


### Inventory Planning: Frontloading, Staging, and Tariff Timing


Timing can be a tariff strategy in itself. When companies anticipate new duties or know existing ones are set to expire, they often frontload inventory—bringing goods in early to avoid future rate increases. This tactic was widely used in 2024 and 2025 ahead of anticipated tariff increases.


But frontloading isn’t without risk. Overstocked inventory ties up cash and warehouse space, and unexpected demand shifts can lead to markdowns or write-offs. The most effective importers combine tariff forecasting with inventory staging strategies, pulling from bonded warehouses or foreign trade zones (FTZs) only when needed. This keeps goods “off the books” from a duty standpoint until timing is right.


### Supplier and HTS Code Review


Even outside of formal engineering changes, many companies find savings simply by reviewing the HTS classifications their brokers have been using. Often, codes were selected years ago and haven’t been revisited—even if the product has changed.


In some cases, the same item might qualify for multiple tariff codes, depending on how it’s packaged, marketed, or presented. A fresh review of classifications, ideally backed by binding rulings or legal opinion letters, can uncover lower-duty options that are entirely compliant.


On the supplier side, companies are increasingly working with vendors who understand tariff optimization—and who can provide documentation to support strategies like First Sale, FTA eligibility, or substitution drawback. In an era of automated enforcement, a supply chain that’s “compliance-ready” is becoming a competitive advantage in itself.


### Using Foreign Trade Zones or Bonded Warehouses


Foreign Trade Zones (FTZs) and bonded warehouses are designated spaces—usually near ports or airports—where imported goods can be stored, processed, or even manufactured *without immediately triggering customs duties* . These zones are legally considered “outside” U.S. customs territory for duty purposes, which means you don’t have to pay import taxes until the goods leave the zone and officially enter the U.S. market.


This setup gives importers more control over when and whether duties are paid. If the goods are later re-exported instead of sold domestically, no duties are owed at all. And if the product changes while in the zone—say it’s assembled into a different item or repackaged—you may be able to declare the new product at a lower duty rate upon entry.


Bonded warehouses function similarly, but are generally used for longer-term storage. Goods can remain in a bonded warehouse for up to five years without duties being assessed, giving companies more flexibility to manage timing, cash flow, or regulatory uncertainty.


For importers dealing with unpredictable demand, seasonality, or re-export operations, FTZs and bonded warehouses can be a powerful way to defer, reduce, or completely avoid duties—all while staying fully compliant with U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) regulations.


### Leveraging Chapter 98 to Reduce Duties on Returned or Assembled Goods


Chapter 98 of the U.S. Harmonized Tariff Schedule provides special duty exemptions for certain types of imports—especially when the goods involve U.S. components, returns, or re-imports. These provisions can be particularly valuable for companies that manufacture across borders or operate circular supply chains.


One of the most commonly used entries under Chapter 98 is HTS 9801, which allows goods that were originally exported from the U.S. to return duty-free—even if they’ve passed through or been used in other countries. For example, if you export diagnostic equipment for international servicing and then re-import it, you can use 9801 to avoid paying duty again on the same product.


Another valuable provision is HTS 9802, which applies when a product is assembled abroad using U.S.-origin components. In that case, importers can deduct the value of those U.S. parts from the total declared customs value—meaning duties are only assessed on the foreign value-added portion.


Chapter 98 isn’t a fit for every importer, but when used correctly, it can generate significant duty savings on returned goods, repairs, or cross-border manufacturing programs. Like all mitigation tools, it requires strong documentation and advance planning—but the payoff can be substantial.


### Technology and Data-Driven Compliance


None of these strategies work without clean, organized data. That’s why more companies are investing in systems that integrate with ACE, centralize entry records, and track eligibility across FTAs, Chapter 98, and drawback. When customs enforcement becomes algorithmic, documentation gaps don’t just delay refunds—they become financial liabilities.


Importers that build compliance infrastructure now are better positioned to pivot quickly when new duties hit, new exemptions emerge, or a key supplier changes their practices.


### Stacking Strategies Means Stronger Margins


There's no single answer to duty savings in 2025. Some companies benefit most from drawback. Others rely on FTAs or First Sale. And for many, the real advantage comes from stacking strategies together—claiming drawback on exports, using First Sale at entry, optimizing classifications, and staging goods in[FTZs](https://www.trade.gov/about-ftzs) until timing is right.


The more options a company builds into its supply chain, the more it can adapt to an uncertain trade environment—and the more margin it protects when tariffs move fast.
