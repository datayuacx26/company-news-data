The Defender is what a Land Rover should be.


As the rest of the lineup has trended toward luxury, the Land Rover Defender has stayed true to the brand’s roots of tough off-roaders ideal for the military and farmers alike. That’s mainly because Land Rover put off updates for decades, but a redesigned Defender that’s more passenger car than farm implement arrived for 2020, bringing the nameplate back to the U.S. Changes for 2026 include subtle styling changes, a larger touchscreen, and new color and accessory options.


**Verdict:** The Defender offers an unmatched combination of off-road capability and everyday civility.


**10/10**


The Defender embodies the ruggedness of its predecessors without being retro. The boxy shape, the squared-off wheel arches, and the flat hood bring to mind Land Rovers of the past, but the curved edges and minimalist lighting give it a modern touch. Land Rover made some changes for 2026, including a new headlight design and revised front and rear bumpers, but they don’t make much of an impression. That’s OK, though, because the Defender still looks impressively fresh for a six-year-old design.


Like the original Defender, buyers can get two-door 90 and four-door 110 versions, although the numbers no longer correspond to wheelbase length as they did on the old-school models. There’s also a stretched Defender 130 body style, and the 110-derived Octa high-performance model has massive fender flares that make room for enormous all-terrain tires, as well as optional “chopped” carbon fiber exterior trim with a marbled appearance instead of the neat cross hatching of regular carbon.


Those who want a more traditional look can equip a Defender with a white contrasting roof, white-painted 20-inch wheels meant to look like the steel wheels on older models (19-inch wheels are standard and various other 20-inch and 22-inch wheel designs are available), or exterior storage boxes and roof racks. The Trophy Edition takes things even further with Sandglow Yellow paint that recalls the Camel Trophy (a famously grueling off-road event) Land Rovers. The Defender X adds more black trim, including a blackout treatment for the front fascia and hood that looks like an oversized racing stripe.


The interior is an excellent blend of rugged style and upscale quality. From the concave dashboard with embossed “Defender” lettering, to the purposely exposed hardware on the doors, the design is distinctive while helping to sell the Defender as an off-road adventure vehicle. It may not be as posh as a fully optioned Range Rover cabin, but drivers don’t have to be as precious with it, either. And while there are screens, this is one of the few new-car cabins that isn’t designed around them, keeping distractions to a minimum.


Windsor leather or textile upholstery options are available, along with a massive glass roof that is thankfully equipped with a power sunshade. Fit and finish and material quality were also excellent, justifying the Defender’s premium pricing compared to similar off-roaders such as the Ford Bronco, the Jeep Wrangler, and the Toyota Land Cruiser.


**10/10**


Land Rover offers several powertrain options for the 2026 Defender, but that’s about to change. Slated for the axe in 2027 are the base turbocharged 2.0-liter four-cylinder engine and the supercharged 5.0-liter V8. The inline-four is good for 296 horsepower and 295 pound-feet of torque, while the V8 produces 518 hp and 461 lb-ft of torque. All Defender models have an eight-speed automatic transmission and standard four-wheel drive (4WD).


The most widely available powertrain is a turbocharged 3.0-liter inline-six that produces 395 hp and 406 lb-ft of torque. It’s teamed with a 48-volt mild hybrid system that provides more robust engine start/stop operation to help improve fuel economy. Land Rover’s spec sheet says it should get a Defender from 0–60 mph in just under or just over six seconds, depending on the body style. The top speed of six-cylinder models is 119 mph.


Naturally for Land Rover, off-road capability is standard. The standard coil-spring suspension affords 8.9 inches of ground clearance, rising to 11.5 inches with the optional air suspension and 12.7 inches for the Defender Octa. Land Rover’s Terrain Response 2 system groups settings into easy-to-understand drive modes to take the guesswork out of off-roading. For 2026, the All Terrain Progress Control system evolves into Adaptive Off-Road Cruise Control, allowing the driver to set a certain speed much like cruise control used on highways.


For this test drive, though, we grabbed the key fob to a Defender Octa, the high-performance version of the Defender 110 introduced for the 2025 model year. The Octa is powered by a twin-turbo 4.4-liter V8 sourced from BMW that produces 626 horsepower and 553 pound-feet of torque. That gets it from 0–60 mph in 3.8 seconds and on to a top speed of 155 mph on the standard 22-inch wheels and all-season tires, according to Land Rover. Enhancing off-road capability are optional 33-inch all-terrain tires on 20-inch wheels. Ride height and track width are wider, and the Octa swaps out the standard suspension for 6D Dynamics system from the Range Rover Sport SV.


This complex suspension system—which adds hydraulic interlinked dampers with air springs—actively counteracts body roll, achieving the wheel articulation and travel needed for high-speed running on rough terrain along with the compliance and mechanical grip that come into play on pavement. So while it was designed for off-roading, the Octa was surprisingly fun on ordinary roads, and we could toss it into corners like an oversized sports car without the chassis getting out of sorts.


A major advantage of the Defender over other hardcore off-roaders is refinement. Aside from the expected high level of noise from our test car’s all-terrain tires, the interior was as quiet as those of less rugged luxury crossovers. The optional air suspension provides an equivalent level of ride smoothness, and the extra compliance afforded by the Octa’s lack of anti-roll bars enhanced that further. The only blemish was what seemed like an artificial engine soundtrack that started out as a natural V8 rumble but transitioned to what sounded like a tinny recording of a V6 at higher rpm.


**8/10**


Depending on the body style, a Defender can seat five (90), seven (110), or eight (130). The two-door 90 is obviously the least practical, but still has decent rear-seat space. The back seats are just harder to access than in the four-door models, and carving out enough legroom to make them habitable eats into cargo space.


Available in both two-row and three-row configurations, the Defender 110 has interior space closer to current midsize SUVs. The 34 cubic feet of cargo space behind the second row and 78.8 cubic feet behind the first row in two-row models are both more than in a BMW X5, for example. Entry and exit are more of a process than less-rugged crossovers, though. The driver and front passengers do get grab handles cleverly integrated with the dashboard, and the air suspension has a lower access height.


The Defender 130 is only available with three rows of seats, but with either seven-seat or eight-seat configurations. It’s also 13.5 inches longer than the 110, but rides on the same 119-inch wheelbase. The extra length is all in the back, negatively affecting the 130’s departure angle when off-roading but providing more cargo space with a full complement of passengers. The 14.8 cubic feet of cargo space in a seven-seat 130—without folding any seats—is 4.1 more cubes than in a seven-seat 110.


The rear seats lack some of the amenities of current luxury crossovers, but upfront, the driver and front passenger get plenty of storage space, including small channels in the dashboard perfect for holding loose change. We also appreciate Land Rover’s continued use of analog controls, with several knobs and buttons grouped with the shifter in a compact and easy-to-reach pod jutting out of the dashboard.


Certain Defender models also have an 8,200-pound towing capacity, rivalling that of full-size SUVs such as the Cadillac Escalade.


**8/10**


For 2026, the Defender receives a larger 13.1-inch touchscreen that slots into the same place on the dashboard as the previous 11.4-inch screen and continues to integrate wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto. A 12.3-inch digital instrument cluster is also standard on most trim levels.


The three-pane touchscreen main menu remains one of the most stylish of its kind, thanks to a well-chosen typeface and a muted color palette. It’s as easy to use as it is appealing to look at, although it sometimes took a while to boot after starting the car, and required fairly deliberate finger pokes. The digital instrument cluster also impressed with its crisply rendered graphics and sensible layout.


Land Rover offers multiple Meridian-branded audio systems, from a 10-speaker version on lower trim levels to the 14-speaker, 700-watt system in our Defender Octa test car. Audio quality was reasonable considering that some competitors offer way more speakers and power, but what really set the experience apart were Land Rover’s “Body and Soul” front seats, which vibrate in time with the music. It’s a silly feature, but also kind of fun.


More useful is the array of available cameras, including one that offers a low-angle front view, that will definitely come in handy when off-roading or negotiating a tight parking lot.


**7/10**


When it comes to standard driver-assistance tech, Land Rover covers the bases. Expected features such as adaptive cruise control, lane-keep assist, blind-spot monitors, rear cross-traffic alert, forward-collision warning, and automatic emergency braking are all standard, along with a surround-view camera system. A driver-attention monitor is new for 2026, giving the Defender a feature that’s been available on certain non-luxury vehicles for some time.


The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) and National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) hadn’t released safety ratings for the current-generation Defender at the time of writing.


**6/10**


Even with the mild-hybrid turbo-four and turbo-six powertrains, the Defender isn’t very efficient. The Defender 110 is EPA-rated at 20 mpg combined (18 mpg city, 22 mpg highway) with either powertrain, while the two-door Defender 90 is actually a little less fuel efficient with the smaller engine, according to the EPA.


Models with the supercharged 5.0-liter V8 are rated at 16 mpg combined, which doesn’t seem like much of a penalty compared to the underperforming inline-four and inline-six. With its BMW-sourced, twin-turbo 4.4-liter V8, our Defender Octa test car was rated at 17 mpg combined (15 mpg city, 19 mpg highway). In mixed driving conditions, we came reasonably close to that with an indicated 16.3 mpg average.


Pricing ranges from just over $60,000 for a base Defender 90 S to around $160,000 for an Octa. The number of available powertrain, body style, and trim options mean buyers should be able to calibrate a Defender to pretty much any price point in between. But this SUV always errs on the side of ruggedness regardless of trim level; for sheer luxury, a Range Rover or Range Rover Sport is the way to go.


Considering how expensive a Ford Bronco or Jeep Wrangler can get with a few options, a base Defender is comparatively pretty good value and much more pleasant to drive, thanks in part to a fixed roof. And while the Toyota Land Cruiser offers that as well, its interior feels cheap compared to the Land Rover’s. The Ineos Grenadier was inspired by the original Defender, but as a result it may be too old school for the average buyer. Its base price is also about $10,000 higher than the Defender’s.


For about half the price of a Defender Octa, shoppers could have a Ford Bronco Raptor that’s also built for high-speed off-roading. Its interior and road manners are definitely a step down, but if buyers just want an off-road toy, it’s much better value. The Mercedes-Benz G-Class offers a luxurious interior, an all-electric option, and its own high-performance variant, but even the least-expensive version costs about as much as a Defender Octa. In other words, Land Rover has done a good job of carving out a niche for the Defender. There are many SUVs out there, but nothing quite like it.
