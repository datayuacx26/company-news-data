# The Best Used Electric Cars in 2026

The number of electric vehicles (EVs) on American roads has expanded enormously over the last decade, yet only the most capable, well-rounded models — those offering strong real-world range and genuinely low running costs — have left a lasting mark. Because technology advances so rapidly in this corner of the new car market, used examples can shed value at a striking pace, sometimes dropping to a small fraction of their original sticker prices.


That's good news for savvy shoppers. Used[EV tax credits](https://www.cargurus.com/Cars/articles/new-ev-tax-credits) from the federal government, combined with a range of state-level rebates and incentives, sweeten the deal further for budget-minded buyers. As long as the used[EV](https://www.cargurus.com/lpo/electric-cars) predates the 2023 model year and is priced under $25,000 at the point of sale, it may qualify for a $4,000 federal tax credit (subject to change).


If you can live without the very latest connectivity features and cutting-edge technology found in brand-new models, and you're comfortable with somewhat shorter range figures, a used EV can provide reliable, ultra-affordable transportation for years to come. It's also worth remembering that installing a home charger does a great deal to ease range anxiety and reduces your reliance on the public charging network.


Here's a closer look at some of the best used electric cars available today — models that deliver outstanding value for the money.

## Chevrolet Bolt EV


**Years built:** 2017-2023


The original[Chevy Bolt EV](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2023-Chevrolet-Bolt-EV-c32380) , produced through the 2023 model year, may well be the single greatest bargain on this entire list. Though it went through a turbulent period involving a widespread stop-sale order and a significant battery recall, the remedied Bolt EV is a genuinely excellent small car by any standard — electric or otherwise. Older examples are plentiful at under $20,000 with sensible mileage, and they're nearly as capable as later versions, with the caveat that only 2022 and 2023 models received GM's Super Cruise hands-free driver assistance system.


At its 2017 launch, the Bolt EV's 238-mile EPA-rated range turned heads, and Chevrolet stretched that figure to 259 miles for 2020. That's more than adequate for virtually any daily commute, and the Bolt is notably efficient to run. It supports DC fast charging at up to 55 kW and can move from 10% to 80% in roughly an hour at a common 50-kW charger. On a 240-volt home outlet, a full recharge takes around ten hours — not the quickest, but entirely manageable overnight.


Long-term battery durability is solid across the board. The Bolt EV is a genuinely rewarding car to drive, powered by a 66-kWh battery and a single electric motor making 200 horsepower and 266 pound-feet of torque. Its nimble dimensions and capable handling make it an entertaining city car, and there's enough cabin space for four adults to travel in reasonable comfort. Some buyers may still have reservations about the LG-sourced batteries given their history, but those who commit will find a practical hatchback with an eager powertrain, accessible technology, and a surprisingly roomy interior. If you're also considering other used electric city cars, the BMW i3 is worth a look — available as either a pure EV or in what BMW calls a 'range extender' configuration with a supplemental plug-in hybrid drivetrain.

## Ford Mustang Mach-E

The[Mustang Mach-E](https://www.cargurus.com/Cars/articles/2025-ford-mustang-mach-e-pricing-specs-release-date) generated no shortage of debate when Ford attached its legendary muscle car nameplate to an all-electric crossover. The GT and GT Performance editions lead the range for outright pace, but even entry-level models acquit themselves well on a twisting road.

## Hyundai Kona Electric


**Years built:** 2019-present


Before the Hyundai Ioniq 5 arrived and stole the spotlight, the original[Hyundai Kona EV](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2024-Hyundai-Kona-Electric-c32965) was the default choice for shoppers seeking a practical electric crossover with genuinely competitive range. Its 150-kW electric motor delivers 201 horsepower and 291 pound-feet of torque to the front wheels — not quite Lucid Air territory, but more than brisk enough for everyday use. The Kona EV handles city streets and highway stints with equal ease, helped along by the instant torque delivery that defines the EV driving experience.

A thorough suite of driver assistance features — including automatic emergency braking, forward-collision warning, and blind-spot monitoring — comes standard. The Kona Electric's most notable limitation mirrors that of its gas-powered counterpart: rear-seat space is tight. And while the conventional Kona offers available all-wheel drive, the electric version is front-wheel drive only.

## Jaguar I-Pace


**Years built:** 2018-2024


Just how quickly does EV technology move? Not long ago, the[Jaguar I-Pace](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2023-Jaguar-I-PACE-c32264) was among the most coveted electric vehicles on the planet, collecting prestigious honors including World Car of the Year and European Car of the Year. Today, Jaguar no longer builds the I-Pace. Its exit is partly attributable to its premium pricing and Jaguar's wider brand challenges, but the I-Pace also found it increasingly difficult to keep up with a fast-evolving competitive set — despite price reductions and progressively more generous standard equipment. The outcome? Sharp depreciation, as is typical of Jaguar products.


For used car shoppers, however, that depreciation translates into a genuine opportunity. A fully loaded top-trim I-Pace that carried a sticker price approaching $83,000 when new in 2020 can now be tracked down in the $20,000s with some diligent searching. Lower S and SE trims are available for less still. The I-Pace continues to reward behind the wheel and retains that unmistakable Jaguar character, even at bargain prices. Every model is equipped with a 90-kWh battery pack offering up to 246 miles of range, and a standard dual-motor all-wheel drive system producing 394 horsepower and 512 pound-feet of torque. It's swift, polished, and whisper-quiet — precisely what you'd expect from a Jaguar.


The cabin is comfortable, luxurious, and hushed, with enough space for four adults to travel in genuine style on longer journeys. The hatchback crossover body style also yields a useful amount of cargo room. There are a few considerations worth flagging, however. Jaguar's dealer network has contracted considerably, which can complicate servicing. And while the I-Pace can fast charge from 10% to 80% in as little as 40 minutes, Level 2 home charging times are on the longer side.

## Kia Soul EV


**Years built:** 2015-2019


The[Kia Soul EV](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2019-Kia-Soul-EV-c28166) was a solid new electric car, and it makes an even more compelling used purchase — provided it fits your needs. Early models were equipped with a 27-kWh battery pack and an EPA-rated range of 93 miles, while 2018 and 2019 versions received a larger 30-kWh pack and a 111-mile range. Even accounting for some battery degradation over time, that's sufficient for most short commutes and daily errands. The Soul EV supports charging at up to 100 kW, though it uses an older CHAdeMO connector, so an adapter may be required at contemporary charging stations.


The Soul EV also happens to be more entertaining to drive than the non-turbocharged gas-powered Soul. Despite producing only 109 horsepower versus 130 in the standard model, its 210 pound-feet of torque dwarfs the gas version's 118, and the response when you press the accelerator is immediate. In any configuration, the Soul is an exceptionally versatile vehicle. Its boxy profile, tall roofline, and clever packaging make it a smart alternative to bulkier, more expensive crossovers and SUVs. It can comfortably carry four tall adults, or two adults and three children, though cargo space is limited.


The Soul EV commanded a premium price when new, but used examples with respectable mileage can now be found for under $10,000. It wasn't sold in all 50 states, and supply remains more concentrated on the West Coast and in California, though examples do turn up across the country.

## Nissan Leaf


**Years built:** 2011-Present


The first truly modern, mass-produced electric car, the[Nissan Leaf](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2024-Nissan-LEAF-c33034) debuted in 2009 and entered production the following year as a 2011 model. Its original 24-kWh battery pack, 107-horsepower motor, and 73-mile range were modest by today's standards, but genuinely groundbreaking at the time. Over the years, the battery grew to 30 kWh and range extended to 107 miles, though fast-charging capability was reserved for the top SL trim. Nissan initially cautioned against frequent fast charging, and models without the fast-charge port require four to eight hours on a 240-volt outlet. The earliest examples tend to show meaningful battery degradation, so it's wise to focus on models newer than 2013 when shopping for a first-generation Leaf. That said, excellent examples can still be found for under $10,000.


The second-generation Leaf arrived in 2017 as a 2018 model, bringing refreshed styling, improved technology, and — finally — standard fast-charging capability. Like the Soul EV and the first-gen Leaf, it uses a CHAdeMO connector, making it the last EV to do so. In most other respects, the second-gen Leaf represents a meaningful step forward: 40- or 62-kWh battery choices and 147 or 214 horsepower options, with the more powerful variant offering a noticeably more satisfying driving experience. Range improves accordingly, reaching up to 149 miles with the standard pack and up to 226 miles with the larger one.


The Leaf won't win any styling awards, and it's not the most thrilling car to drive, but its purchase price and running costs are genuinely hard to argue with. Later models look and feel like proper contemporary small hatchbacks, arrive well-equipped with safety technology, and serve admirably as affordable everyday commuters.

## Tesla Model 3


**Years built:** 2017-Present


Tesla may never have fully delivered on the promise of a $30,000 brand-new[Model 3](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2024-Tesla-Model-3-c33224) , but eight years after its debut, the used market is well stocked with Model 3s available for less than that figure. Thanks in part to rental fleets cycling through lease returns, it's even possible to find examples under $20,000 — though those heavily used units come with caveats, and early production cars had their share of build quality concerns. The same applies to the Tesla Model Y, but the Model 3 tends to offer stronger overall value in the used market.


The Model 3's standing as one of America's best-selling EVs is thoroughly deserved. Key reasons include its impressive range figures — no variant has ever been EPA-rated below 220 miles, and Long Range and Performance models have consistently achieved 300 miles or more — along with sharp handling, strong acceleration, and genuine everyday practicality. The rear seat is a little cramped for taller occupants, but cargo capacity rivals vehicles from much larger segments. Tesla's Supercharger network also makes public charging faster and more dependable than most alternatives.


When evaluating a used Model 3, pay close attention to range differences between trim levels and be aware that some models fall short of their EPA estimates in real-world driving. In 2023, Tesla revised the Long Range Model 3's rated range from 348 miles down to a more realistic 333. Battery health is especially critical if you're considering a discounted former rideshare vehicle, which may have accumulated a high number of fast-charging cycles. A diagnostic test mode can assess battery condition, but it can take up to 24 hours to complete — something not every seller is willing to accommodate. Most Model 3s with fewer than 100,000 miles remain under warranty.


Pricing varies considerably across the lineup, with the Model 3 Performance commanding the largest premiums. The main drawbacks of the 3 are its spartan interior — which doesn't quite deliver a premium feel — and its heavy dependence on the central touchscreen for nearly all vehicle controls. The interface has a learning curve, and neither Apple CarPlay nor Android Auto is supported. There's also no traditional instrument cluster.

## Tesla Model S


**Years built:** 2012-Present


The car that made electric vehicles genuinely desirable to mainstream buyers and the long-standing benchmark for EV range, the[Tesla Model S](https://www.cargurus.com/Cars/articles/2024-tesla-model-s-pricing-specs-release-date) was a landmark when it launched in 2012 — and it's still in production today. Over the years, Tesla has offered a wide array of battery and powertrain configurations spanning rear- and all-wheel drive, beginning with a somewhat confusing numbered naming structure. Since a comprehensive overhaul in 2021, Tesla has offered standard and high-performance "Plaid" variants, alongside long- and standard-range options.


The Model S has always been quick. The early 60 produced 362 horsepower from a 60-kWh battery with around 210 miles of range, but successive variants — including the P85D, 90D, and Long Range — progressively pushed output beyond 400 horsepower and range past 370 miles. Since 2020, the standard (formerly Long Range) Model S has consistently delivered around 400 miles of range, and many versions sprint from zero to 60 mph in under four seconds. The Plaid, available since 2021, occupies a different performance tier entirely — offering straight-line pace comparable to a Dodge Charger Hellcat but with dramatically superior efficiency. It commands a significant price premium, however.


Inside, the Model S provides comfortable accommodation for four adults and a generous complement of screens, including a conventional instrument cluster absent from Tesla's more affordable models. The center display switched from portrait to landscape orientation in 2021, improving usability considerably, though the cabin still relies heavily on digital controls. The interior is minimalist but materially superior to the Model 3, even if it doesn't quite reach the opulence of a Mercedes.


Part of the Model S's enduring appeal lies in its comprehensive standard driver assistance suite — features that often carry hefty option prices on European rivals — and access to Tesla's extensive Supercharger network, which remains the gold standard for public EV charging. Battery degradation is worth monitoring, particularly in vehicles that have seen frequent fast charging, but Tesla offers a diagnostic tool to evaluate battery health, and the hardware generally holds up well over the long term. The Model S has a great deal to recommend it, though it's worth noting that some individual examples may have been modified by previous owners in ways that don't transfer to new ownership. Reviewing any available service history before committing to a purchase is strongly advised.

## Volkswagen e-Golf


**Years built:** 2015-2019


Like many mainstream automakers, Volkswagen made its first serious move into EVs by fitting an electric powertrain into an existing model. The[e-Golf](https://www.cargurus.com/research/2019-Volkswagen-e-Golf-c28852) arrived in 2015 as a premium-positioned EV with a 24.2-kWh battery pack, 115 horsepower, and an 83-mile EPA-rated range. In 2017, Volkswagen upgraded the package with a 35.8-kWh pack, 134 horsepower, and a 125-mile range. New, the e-Golf carried a steep price — top-trim examples approached an MSRP of $40,000 in 2019 — but used versions across all trim levels can now be found for under $15,000 with patient searching. Older, less sought-after trims tend to be the most accessible.


Of course, the e-Golf's appeal extends well beyond its electric credentials. One of Volkswagen's primary objectives was to make the e-Golf drive and feel as close to a standard Golf as possible — and they largely pulled it off. ## The Best Used Electric Cars 2026 FAQs


**What is the best used electric car to buy on a budget?**


The Chevrolet Bolt EV is the standout bargain in the used EV market, with older examples widely available for under $20,000 and EPA-rated range of up to 259 miles. If you need to spend even less, the Kia Soul EV and Nissan Leaf can both be found in good condition for under $10,000, though their shorter ranges make them better suited to daily commutes and local errands than long road trips.


**Can you get a federal tax credit on a used electric car?**


Yes — the federal government offers a $4,000 tax credit on qualifying used EVs, as long as the vehicle predates the 2023 model year and is priced under $25,000 at the point of sale. Many of the models covered here, including the Chevrolet Bolt EV and Volkswagen e-Golf, can realistically be found within that price threshold, making the credit a genuinely useful benefit for budget-conscious shoppers.


**How much range should I expect from a used electric car?**


It depends on the model and its age, but most used EVs on the market today offer somewhere between 100 and 300 miles per charge. Models like the Tesla Model 3 and Hyundai Kona Electric deliver over 250 miles, while more affordable or older options such as the MINI Cooper SE and first-generation Nissan Leaf offer closer to 110 miles — still enough for most daily commutes, especially if you can charge at home overnight.


**What should I watch out for when buying a used Tesla Model 3?**


The used Tesla Model 3 offers strong range, sharp handling, and access to Tesla's Supercharger network, but it's worth being cautious with heavily discounted examples that may have come from rental or rideshare fleets. A high volume of fast-charging cycles can affect battery health, and Tesla's battery diagnostic test can take up to 24 hours to complete — something not every seller will be willing to accommodate before a sale.
