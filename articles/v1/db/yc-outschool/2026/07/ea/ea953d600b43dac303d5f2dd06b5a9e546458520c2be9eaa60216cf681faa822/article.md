# Age-appropriate chores for kids: a chore chart that builds executive function skills

## The chore chart by age


Match the job to where your child's executive function actually is, not where you wish it were. A two-year-old can't plan a sequence yet; a ten-year-old is bored by a job with no ownership in it. Use these as a starting menu, then let your child help choose.


### Ages 2 to 3: one-step jobs, side by side


- Put toys in a bin
- Drop dirty clothes in the hamper
- Hand you groceries to put away
- Wipe a spill with a cloth


At this age the win is simply following a single instruction to completion, alongside you. You're planting the idea that they're a contributor.


### Ages 4 to 5: two-step jobs, with a picture chart


- Set the table (napkins, then forks)
- Feed a pet with a measured scoop
- Make the bed, roughly
- Water plants


This is where a picture chart earns its keep. Two steps in the right order is real planning for a preschooler.


### Ages 6 to 7: multi-step jobs they can start solo


- Pack their own snack and water bottle
- Sort laundry into lights and darks
- Clear and wipe the table after dinner
- Get the backpack ready the night before


Task initiation is the focus now. The goal is starting on one cue instead of five reminders.


### Ages 8 to 9: jobs with a quality bar


- Load or unload the dishwasher
- Take out trash and recycling on the right day
- Vacuum a room
- Help make a simple meal from a recipe card


Add a self-check step. "Recycling on the right day" quietly builds the calendar-awareness that homework planners will later demand.


### Ages 10 to 11: jobs they manage over time


- Do their own laundry start to finish
- Plan and prep one family meal a week
- Care for a pet's full routine
- Manage a weekly tidy of their room without prompting


These stretch across days, so kids practice holding a goal in mind and monitoring their own progress, the core of long-project stamina.


### Ages 12 and up: jobs that mirror adult systems


- Run a grocery sub-list on a budget
- Manage their own schedule of jobs across the week
- Do yard work or deeper cleaning tasks
- Track and complete a recurring responsibility without a chart at all


By now the chart itself should be fading. A teen who can run their responsibilities without an external tracker has internalized the executive-function skills you spent a decade building.


## Four mistakes that turn a chore chart into a battle


- **Doing the planning for them.** If you sequence every step, you're exercising your executive function, not theirs. Hand back the thinking.
- **Too many jobs at once.** Three consistent jobs beat ten sporadic ones. Overloading the chart guarantees it gets abandoned.
- **Redoing their work in front of them.** A wobbly bed made by a five-year-old is a win. Silently remaking it teaches them their effort doesn't count.
- **Never fading the reminders.** If you're still cueing every step months in, the chart has become your job. Shrink the prompt on purpose.


## Frequently asked questions


**Should chores be tied to allowance?** Many families separate the two: some jobs are simply part of belonging to the household, while a few extra paid jobs can teach money skills. If you want to build financial habits alongside responsibility, see our guide to[life skills to teach kids at home](https://outschool.com/articles/life-skills-to-teach-kids) .


**What if my child has ADHD or struggles with getting started?** Lean harder on the visual chart, shorten the jobs, and make the first cue playful rather than nagging. Our roundup of[executive function activities for kids](https://outschool.com/neurodivergent/executive-function-activities-for-kids) has more that pairs well with a chore routine.


**My kid just refuses. Now what?** Drop the count to one job, do it together for a week, and give them a real choice in which job it is. Ownership beats enforcement almost every time.
