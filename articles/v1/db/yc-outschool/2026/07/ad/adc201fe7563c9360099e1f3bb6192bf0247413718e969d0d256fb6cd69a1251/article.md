# Group classes vs. 1:1 tutoring: which format fits your child?

## A quick decision framework


When you are unsure, run through these four questions:


1. **Is this remediation or enrichment?** A specific gap points toward one-on-one. Exploring or deepening an interest points toward a group.
2. **How does my child respond to peers?** Energized and motivated, lean group. Self-conscious or easily distracted by others, lean one-on-one, at least to start.
3. **Does the subject reward discussion?** Writing, reading, and the humanities gain from many voices. A single stubborn math concept usually does not.
4. **What is the budget and timeline?** One-on-one costs more per hour but can work faster on a narrow goal. Groups stretch a budget and suit ongoing, lower-pressure learning.


Many families end up using both, and that is often the smartest play: a group class for a subject your child enjoys and wants to go deeper in, plus a few one-on-one sessions to shore up a weak spot before it snowballs.


## How ESA funds change the math


If you have an Education Savings Account, format matters less to your wallet than it used to. Most ESA programs cover both group classes and private tutoring as approved expenses, which means you can match the format to your child's need rather than your budget. That is a genuine shift, and it is worth revisiting a plan you set before your funds arrived.

## Frequently asked questions


**Is one-on-one tutoring always more effective?** For closing a specific, well-defined gap, usually yes. For discussion-based subjects, motivation, and collaborative skills, a group can produce better outcomes and stick better over time.


**What counts as a "small" group?** There is no official number, but the benefits of personalization start to fade quickly above roughly eight to ten kids. Ask about class size before you enroll.


**Can shy kids do well in group classes?** Many do, especially in small, warmly run groups where the teacher draws everyone in. If your child is very anxious about peers, starting one-on-one and transitioning to a group later is a reasonable path.


**How do I know if it is working?** Look for two signals within a few weeks: your child is less resistant to showing up, and they can explain something they could not before. If neither is moving, revisit the format.


Start with the problem you are trying to solve, not the format everyone recommends. Match the setup to your child, and stay willing to switch when the need changes. The best format is the one your child will keep showing up for.
