# AI tutoring for kids: what parents need to know before the 2026-27 school year

A year ago, most parents hadn't heard of AI tutoring. Now it's a line item in Deloitte's 2026 back-to-school survey, alongside backpacks and school supplies \[2\]. The tools have arrived fast: Khanmigo, Duolingo Max, Photomath, and a growing roster of apps that promise personalized instruction at a fraction of the cost of a human tutor.


Some of these tools deliver real value. Others over-promise. And for parents trying to figure out whether AI tutoring belongs in their kid's learning plan this fall, the landscape is hard to navigate without a clear look at what these tools can and can't do.


This guide breaks down what AI tutoring looks like in practice, where the research says it helps, and where live, human instruction still has an edge that no algorithm can match.


## What AI tutoring looks like right now


AI tutoring tools fall into a few broad categories, and knowing the differences matters when you're evaluating which (if any) might work for your kid.


**Practice-and-feedback engines** like Khan Academy's Khanmigo and IXL's SmartScore adapt problem sets based on your kid's answers. Get a question wrong, and the system adjusts difficulty or offers a hint. These are strongest in math and grammar, where answers are clearly right or wrong and the path to understanding follows a predictable sequence.


**Conversational tutors** powered by large language models can answer questions, explain concepts, and walk through problems step by step. Some are embedded in apps designed for kids; others are general-purpose chatbots that parents repurpose. The conversational format feels personal, but the quality of explanations varies widely, and younger kids can drift into off-topic exchanges without realizing it.


**Language learning apps** like Duolingo Max use AI to simulate conversations in a target language. These work well for building vocabulary and basic fluency, though they can't replicate the cultural nuance and real-time correction of a[live language class](https://outschool.com/online-classes/spanish) .


**Homework helpers** like Photomath and Socratic let kids photograph a problem and get an instant solution with steps. Useful for checking work, but risky when kids use them to bypass the thinking process entirely.


## Where AI tutoring helps


The Stanford Human-Centered AI Institute's 2025 review of AI in education found measurable gains in specific, narrow contexts \[1\]. AI tutoring tools tend to work best when:


- **The subject has clear right-and-wrong answers.** Math fact fluency, grammar rules, and vocabulary drills are well-suited to adaptive practice engines. The system knows when your kid gets it right and adjusts accordingly.
- **Your kid needs repetition without judgment.** Some kids freeze up when they get a math problem wrong in front of a teacher or parent. An AI tutor that adjusts and moves on can reduce that performance anxiety.
- **You need a bridge between live sessions.** For families using[live one-on-one tutoring](https://outschool.com/online-classes/1-on-1-tutoring) once or twice a week, an AI practice tool can keep skills sharp between sessions.
- **Your kid is self-motivated and old enough to stay on task.** The research suggests gains are strongest among older kids (ages 12 and up) who can use tools independently without constant redirection \[1\].


## Where AI tutoring falls short


The same research that highlights AI tutoring's strengths also reveals its limits. For parents, these limits matter more than the marketing copy suggests.


**It can't read the room.** A human tutor notices when a kid's eyes glaze over, when frustration is building, when the real barrier isn't the math but something that happened at school that morning. AI tutors respond to inputs, not to the whole child.


**It struggles with open-ended thinking.** Writing, critical analysis, scientific reasoning, historical interpretation: these subjects require a teacher who can push back on weak arguments, ask follow-up questions, and model the messy process of working through a problem. AI can generate a passable essay outline, but it can't teach your kid to develop their own voice or defend an idea under questioning. Research on[screen time and learning](https://outschool.com/articles/screen-time-and-learning) reinforces that passive digital interactions don't produce the same cognitive gains as active, dialogic instruction.


**It doesn't build relationships.** The Deloitte 2026 BTS survey found that a majority of parents considering AI tutoring still ranked "connection with a real teacher" as a top priority for their kid's learning \[2\]. Kids learn differently from someone who knows them, remembers what they struggled with last week, and celebrates genuine breakthroughs.


**Younger kids need more guardrails than most tools provide.** For kids under 10, the attention management, comprehension checking, and redirection that a live teacher provides is hard to replicate. Most AI tutoring tools are designed for middle school and up, and younger kids often need a parent co-piloting the session to stay productive.
