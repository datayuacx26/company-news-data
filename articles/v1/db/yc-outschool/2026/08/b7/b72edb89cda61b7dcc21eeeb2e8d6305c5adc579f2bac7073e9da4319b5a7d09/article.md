# Best educational apps for kids in 2026: what teaches (and what just entertains)

Most "educational" apps for kids fall into one of two categories: the ones that build real skills, and the ones that dress up screen time in bright colors and call it learning. The difference matters more than most product pages let on.


A 2025 study in JAMA Network Open found that interactive, skill-building screen use produced measurably different cognitive outcomes than passive consumption, even when total screen time was identical \[1\]. The format of the app, not just the hours logged, shaped whether kids retained anything.


So which apps hold up to scrutiny? We sorted through dozens of options, organized them by age, and flagged what to watch for before you hand over the tablet.


## Ages 3 to 5: building blocks, not busy work


At this age, the goal isn't academic acceleration. It's pattern recognition, letter-sound connections, and the kind of curiosity that makes a kid want to keep exploring. Three apps stand out.


**Khan Academy Kids** covers early literacy, math, and social-emotional learning through stories and interactive exercises. It's free, ad-free, and doesn't push in-app purchases. The content follows a developmental progression rather than letting kids skip randomly between activities.


**PBS Kids Games** bundles mini-games tied to shows like Curious George and Daniel Tiger. Each game targets a specific skill (counting, rhyming, sequencing), and the difficulty adjusts as kids improve. Also free and ad-free.


**Endless Alphabet** teaches vocabulary through word puzzles where kids drag letters into place while animated monsters act out definitions. It's playful without being mindless, and the visual approach works well for kids who aren't ready for phonics drills yet.


**Watch for:** Apps in this age range that reward tapping and swiping with animations but don't ask kids to think. If your kid can "play" the app without learning what any of the letters or numbers mean, it's entertainment.


## Ages 6 to 8: when practice needs a purpose


Kids in this range are building fluency in reading and math. The best apps here give structured practice with enough variety to prevent the worksheet-fatigue spiral.


**Prodigy Math** wraps grade-aligned math problems inside an RPG-style game. Kids answer questions to progress through quests. The free tier covers the core math content; the paid version adds cosmetic rewards but doesn't gate learning behind a paywall.


**Epic** is a digital library with 40,000+ books, audiobooks, and videos. Kids can browse by reading level, interest, or series. The read-to-me feature helps struggling readers follow along, and the quiz feature checks comprehension on select titles. Useful alongside a[summer reading list](https://outschool.com/articles/summer-reading-list-for-kids-by-grade) or structured reading practice.


**Duolingo** works for early language exposure, especially Spanish, French, and Mandarin. The gamified streaks keep kids coming back, and the speech recognition feature pushes them beyond passive reading. Fair warning: the free version includes ads between exercises.


**Watch for:** Math apps that let kids guess repeatedly without consequence. If wrong answers don't trigger re-teaching or adjusted difficulty, the app is training speed, not understanding.


## Ages 9 to 12: tools that build, not just drill


Older kids benefit from apps that shift from consumption to creation. The best options at this level ask kids to make something, solve open-ended problems, or build skills they can transfer outside the app.


**Scratch** (MIT Media Lab) is a free visual programming platform where kids create animations, games, and interactive stories by snapping code blocks together. It teaches computational thinking, sequencing, and debugging in a way that translates directly to text-based coding later. A natural complement to[live coding classes](https://outschool.com/online-classes/coding) .


**BrainPOP** covers science, social studies, math, and English through short animated videos followed by quizzes, concept maps, and creative challenges. The content aligns with common standards, and the "Make-a-Map" tool lets kids build visual summaries of what they've learned.


**Google Arts and Culture** isn't marketed as an educational app, but it's one of the most underused tools for this age group. Kids can take virtual tours of museums worldwide, zoom into artworks at brushstroke level, and explore historical timelines. Strong for curiosity-driven learning and homeschool enrichment.


**Typing.com** teaches touch typing through structured exercises and games. Typing speed matters more than most parents realize, especially as kids start writing longer assignments. The school version is free.


**Watch for:** Apps that claim to teach "coding" but only offer pre-built templates with no real logic or problem-solving. If a kid can't explain what their code does, they're assembling, not programming.
