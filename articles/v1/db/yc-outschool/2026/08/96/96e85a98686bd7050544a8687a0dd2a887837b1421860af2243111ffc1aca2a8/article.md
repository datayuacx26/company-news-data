# Reading level chart by grade: Lexile, F&P, DRA, and grade equivalents in one place

## How to figure out your kid's reading level at home


If your child attends a school that uses F&P or DRA, you likely already have a level from their last assessment. If you homeschool or want an independent check, here are practical options:


- **Lexile:** many standardized tests (MAP, STAR, state assessments) report a Lexile score. You can also use the free Lexile tool at[lexile.com](https://lexile.com/) to find books at your child's level
- **Informal running record:** pick a book you think is close to your kid's level. Have them read a page aloud. If they miss more than 1 in 10 words, it's too hard for independent reading. If they read every word effortlessly, it's too easy for growth. The right level is where they stumble occasionally but can still follow the story
- **Interest-led assessment:** sometimes the best "level" is whatever book your kid won't put down. A child reading below grade level who devours *Dog Man* is building fluency faster than a child reading at grade level who avoids books entirely


For a more structured at-home approach, our[back-to-school reading assessment guide](https://outschool.com/articles/back-to-school-reading-assessment) walks through how to check your kid's level before the school year starts.


## What to do when the numbers don't match


It's common for a child to test at one level in Lexile and a different level in F&P. A few reasons this happens:


- **The systems measure different things.** Lexile is algorithmic (word frequency and sentence length). F&P is observational (accuracy, fluency, comprehension, self-correction). A child who decodes well but struggles with comprehension may score higher on Lexile than F&P
- **Assessment conditions vary.** A formal F&P running record administered by a trained teacher in a quiet room will produce different results than a Lexile score derived from a 30-minute computer-adaptive test taken in a noisy classroom
- **Growth isn't linear.** Kids develop reading skills in bursts. A child might jump two F&P levels over a summer of enthusiastic reading and then plateau for months. Both patterns are normal


When numbers conflict, trust the system that captures the most information about your specific child. For most homeschool families, that means a combination of the informal running record (which you can do yourself) and your daily observation of what your kid reads independently, how they handle unfamiliar words, and whether they can retell what they've read.


## Reading levels and book selection: the 80% rule


Research on reading development consistently points to one principle: kids grow as readers when they spend most of their time reading texts they can handle with moderate success \[2\]. Too easy and there's no growth. Too hard and they shut down.


A practical framework:


- **Independent reading (about 70% of reading time):** books your child reads with 95%+ accuracy. These build fluency, stamina, and love of reading
- **Instructional reading (about 20%):** books at the edge of their ability (90-94% accuracy), read with support from a parent, teacher, or tutor. This is where growth happens
- **Read-alouds and audiobooks (about 10%):** books above their reading level but within their listening comprehension. These build vocabulary, background knowledge, and excitement about what's possible


This is why a child's "level" should never become a ceiling. A fifth grader reading at a third grade Lexile level can still listen to and discuss *The Lightning Thief* (680L) as an audiobook. And a second grader reading at a fifth grade level still benefits from picture books that build inference and visual literacy skills.


## When to worry (and when not to)


Reading level charts make it easy to spiral. If your kid is "behind," it feels urgent. If they're "ahead," you wonder if they're being challenged enough. A few things worth keeping in perspective:


- **Below grade level doesn't always mean behind.** Reading development has enormous natural variation, especially in kindergarten through second grade. Some kids don't click with reading until age 7 or 8 and then catch up rapidly. If your child is making consistent progress, even if it's slower than the chart suggests, that's often enough
- **Persistent struggles deserve investigation.** If your child is consistently reading two or more grade levels below peers by third grade, has difficulty with[high-frequency words](https://outschool.com/homeschool/high-frequency-words) , or avoids reading despite plenty of access to books, it's worth exploring whether a learning difference like dyslexia might be involved. Our[reading comprehension guide](https://outschool.com/how-to-teach/reading-comprehension) covers strategies, and[teaching phonics at home](https://outschool.com/how-to-teach/phonics) can help with decoding gaps
- **"Advanced" readers still need support.** A child with a high Lexile score can decode complex text, but that doesn't mean they're emotionally ready for mature themes. Use the reading level to guide text complexity, but use your judgment as a parent to guide content

## Frequently asked questions


### What Lexile level should my kid be at?


Lexile ranges by grade are guidelines, not benchmarks. A typical third grader falls between 520L and 820L, but many strong readers fall outside that range in either direction. Focus on whether your child is making progress and enjoying reading rather than hitting a specific number.


### How do I convert F&P levels to Lexile?


There's no exact conversion because the systems measure different skills. The chart above shows approximate correlations. For example, F&P level M (late second to early third grade) roughly corresponds to Lexile 420L-650L. Use these as starting points for book selection, not as precise equivalents.


### Do homeschool families need to track reading levels?


Not formally, unless your state requires standardized testing. Many homeschool families find that observing their child's reading habits, tracking books completed, and doing occasional informal assessments gives them enough information to guide instruction. That said, knowing your child's approximate Lexile range makes it easier to find books at the right challenge level.


### My child's Lexile score went down. Should I worry?


Lexile scores can fluctuate between tests due to test conditions, the specific passages used, and normal variation in performance. A single dip is not cause for concern. If scores decline over two or more consecutive assessments, look at whether your child's reading volume has dropped or whether they might be struggling with a specific skill (fluency vs. comprehension vs. vocabulary).


### What reading level is Harry Potter?


*Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone* has a Lexile of 880L, which falls in the fifth to sixth grade range. The later books in the series increase in complexity, with *Order of the Phoenix* at 950L and *Deathly Hallows* at 980L. Many fourth graders can read and enjoy the first book, especially if they're motivated by the story, even if the Lexile is technically above their assessed level.
