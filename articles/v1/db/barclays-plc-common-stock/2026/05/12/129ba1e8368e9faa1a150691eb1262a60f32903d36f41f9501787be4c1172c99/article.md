# Agentic AI: In conversation with Professor Michael Wooldridge

Artificial intelligence (AI) is moving beyond prompt-based tools and AI assistants towards systems that can act independently. But what does this mean in practice – and where are the real risks and opportunities?


Professor Michael Wooldridge is one of the UK’s leading AI experts. He is the Ashall Professor of the Foundations of Artificial Intelligence in the Department of Computer Science at the University of Oxford, and a Senior Research Fellow at Hertford College. Working extensively on multi-agent systems, Professor Wooldridge has published more than 450 articles and recently entertained and informed clients during thought-provoking evenings at our exclusive ‘Inspired Dinner’ events at the Dakota Hotel, Manchester, and the Four Seasons Hotel, Park Lane, London.


In this Q&A, Professor Wooldridge explores how agentic AI is emerging, where it falls short, and what organisations – and individuals – should keep in mind.


## What is the difference between generative AI and agentic AI?


**Professor Wooldridge:** Generative AI is fundamentally conversational. It produces text, images or summaries, but it relies on humans to guide every step of the process through prompts.


Agentic AI goes further. Rather than waiting to be instructed, it is designed to act on a user’s behalf. These systems can take a goal, decide what needs to be done, and carry out actions in the real world – such as booking services, moving files, or routing tasks within an organisation.


In simple terms, generative AI advises; agentic AI does.


## Why has agentic AI become viable now?


**Professor Wooldridge:** The key breakthrough has been tool use. Modern AI systems can now connect to external systems – including databases, email, booking platforms and internal software – allowing them to interact with the world beyond the model itself.


Crucially, new standards are emerging that allow AI agents to discover and use tools dynamically, rather than relying on fixed, pre‑programmed capabilities. This flexibility is what has turned agentic AI from a theoretical idea into something organisations can now experiment with.


## Where have AI agents gone wrong in the real world?


**Professor Wooldridge:** The main risk is that agentic AI inherits the weaknesses of generative AI – especially its tendency to be confidently wrong.


When an AI system is allowed to take action rather than simply offer suggestions, mistakes can have serious consequences. Real‑world examples include agents deleting files while ‘tidying’ systems, or being manipulated into making irrational commercial offers.


The lesson is clear: autonomy magnifies error. What might be a minor issue in a chatbot can become a material problem when an AI system is empowered to act.


## What guardrails should organisations put in place?


**Professor Wooldridge:** While technology providers have a responsibility to test and secure their systems, a great deal of responsibility currently falls on users.


Practical safeguards include testing agents in controlled or sandboxed environments, running trial actions before allowing real‑world execution, limiting access to live systems and original data, and treating AI output as provisional, not authoritative.


The guiding principle is to assume that AI systems will make mistakes – and to design their deployment accordingly.


## How can businesses avoid ‘permission creep’?


**Professor Wooldridge:** Permission creep happens gradually. As AI systems appear to perform well, users become more comfortable granting them broader access.


The risk is behavioural as much as technical. To counter it, organisations need to regularly review what agents can access and change, keep permissions tightly scoped to specific tasks and set clear rules or ‘mission statements’ defining what an agent is allowed to do.


Excitement about productivity gains should never override basic controls.


## Which jobs are most exposed to agentic AI – and which are more resilient?


**Professor Wooldridge:** Roles based on routine, scripted tasks are most exposed. Call‑centre work is often cited as an example, as it involves predictable inputs and standard responses.


More resilient roles tend to involve human judgement and contextual understanding, emotional intelligence and interpersonal skills, and physical dexterity in complex environments.


Importantly, AI is far better at replacing individual tasks than entire jobs. For most people, the impact is more likely to be augmentation than replacement.


## How will we know if AI progress is slowing down?


**Professor Wooldridge:** Researchers now use a growing number of benchmarks to assess whether AI systems can genuinely replace knowledge‑based work.


A sustained plateau in these benchmarks would suggest progress is slowing. However, at present, there is little evidence of this. Recent performance improvements suggest momentum is continuing rather than fading.


## Where is agentic AI heading over the next few years?


**Professor Wooldridge:** One emerging concept is the agentic organisation – where human employees and AI agents work alongside one another.


In this model, AI agents take ownership of defined workflows, such as handling customer enquiries, routing requests between departments, or managing end‑to‑end processes under human supervision.


Further ahead, it is plausible that AI systems could create and run stripped‑down organisations around specific opportunities. What once sounded like science fiction is now being discussed as a realistic medium‑term possibility.


## What are the biggest concerns looking ahead?


**Professor Wooldridge:** The most significant risk is over‑empowerment.


Granting AI systems access to sensitive data or critical systems before they are reliable enough creates obvious dangers. There are also broader concerns around misuse, such as coordinated misinformation campaigns driven by large numbers of AI agents.


Caution, restraint and governance will matter just as much as capability.


## Are we mistaking automation for intelligence?


**Professor Wooldridge:** In many cases, yes.


Much of today’s progress comes from automating routine white‑collar tasks rather than creating genuinely intelligent systems. Routing queries, categorising information or handling standard interactions often requires limited reasoning.


What is new is the extension of automation into knowledge work – a shift that mirrors what manufacturing experienced several decades earlier.


## What advice would you give to students planning their careers?


**Professor Wooldridge:** Rather than attempting to outguess AI, students should focus on studying subjects they find genuinely engaging.


Fields like computing are valuable when pursued out of intellectual interest, not simply as a defensive response to automation. Understanding how systems work – and where their limits lie – will remain important regardless of how AI evolves.
