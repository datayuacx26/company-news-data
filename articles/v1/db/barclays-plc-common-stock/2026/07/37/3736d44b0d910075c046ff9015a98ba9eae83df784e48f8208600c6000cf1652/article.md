Gold has long been a strategic holding in multi-asset class portfolios, not for its stellar returns, but for its diversification benefits. After a year where gold returned 64.6%(2) , driving the 30-year returns for the asset class higher than that of US and global equities(3) , and a bout of heightened volatility in the first months of 2026, some investors may be reassessing their exposure to the precious metal.


While strategic gold holdings have played a valuable role in diversified portfolios historically, investments in gold are not without limitations and investment risk. By example, gold’s returns can be volatile over shorter horizons, it generates no income, and its effectiveness as a hedge can vary materially depending on the macroeconomic backdrop.


Here, we consider some of the drivers, risks and historical observations relevant to assessing gold in a diversified portfolio context.

The charts above highlight that gold’s historical performance has not been linear. There have been extended periods in which gold underperformed equities, particularly during strong growth environments or when real interest rates were rising. For this reason, gold tends to be held as a strategic portfolio diversifier rather than a consistent return generator.


## A scarce and elusive resource


Gold is notoriously scarce. Approximately 220,000 metric tonnes of gold have been mined throughout history(4) . This is equivalent to a single cube of just 22 metres on each side, or roughly four Olympic-sized swimming pools. Estimates suggest that just 60,000 tonnes of known economically extractable reserves remain in the Earth's crust(5) , implying we may have already extracted nearly 80% of the world’s gold supply.


Not only is gold rare, but its mining is also arduous, meaning it is difficult and costly to increase supply. The easiest gold deposits have already been mined, so future extraction becomes ever more complex and expensive. Unlike traditional fiat currency, you can’t simply print more gold. The global stock of gold only increases by less than 2% per annum(6) . This restricted supply gives gold its renowned long-term inflation hedge.


## Diversity of demand


Demand however is diverse, and not so constrained. Gold has a unique dual-purpose, as both an investment and a consumer good. The largest source of gold demand (39%) comes from investors, with central banks comprising a further 21%(7) . Gold’s industrial utility comes in the form of jewellery, which accounts for roughly a third of global demand, and technological applications (7%), such as AI, electronics, and medical usage(8) .


These diverse sources of demand have historically allowed gold to deliver strong returns in different economic and market conditions, alongside reducing its volatility. Investment demand is typically counter-cyclical, supporting prices in times of economic or market weakness. Jewellery and technology demand is pro-cyclical, which also gives gold upside exposure to favourable economic conditions.


However, shifts in any one source of demand can at times dominate, leading to heightened volatility. For example, rapid outflows from investment products or changes in central bank buying patterns have, in the past, contributed to sharp price corrections.


Demand from central banks and investors was the primary driver behind gold’s strong rally in 2025, and for good reason. In a world of fast-changing international trends, be that escalating geopolitical tensions, war, tariffs, or deglobalisation, gold is a rare apolitical asset.


Central banks have sought to ‘de-dollarise’ their reserve exposures in 2025. President Trump’s tariff announcements in April brought the dollar’s position as the global reserve currency into question. Many investors no longer see US Treasuries as a risk-free asset, given the weakening fiscal position of the US government, and unpredictable government policy action.

## A US dollar hedge


Gold offers a natural hedge to the US dollar due to its global demand base and resilience to inflation. Reallocations away from dollar assets towards gold have marked a significant pivot in reserve management(9) and contributed to volatile long-dated Treasury yields alongside a weakening dollar(10) .


Equally, with inflation in many corners of the world remaining above central bank targets, and heavily indebted governments continuing to run budget deficits, currency debasement is suddenly a very real risk, against which gold can hedge. That said, gold’s relationship with inflation is not mechanical, and periods of rising real interest rates or tighter monetary policy have historically weighed on gold prices, even when inflation remains elevated.

Sovereign buyers have viewed gold has a means of some protection against unwanted exposure to another government’s fiscal and monetary policies, economic performance, or currency fluctuations. Similarly, increasing nationalism and deglobalisation could be viewed as another driver for investing in gold.


## ‘Paper’ gold exceeds physical supply


With 2025 being witness to rising geopolitical tensions, trade policy uncertainty, falling interest rates, and equity market volatility, many investors sought gold’s safe-haven qualities. Sharp increases in ETF inflows and record investment demand for physical bars and coins drove the gold price higher(11) . The surging gold price enticed further inflows from momentum-favouring investors, which supported a positive feedback loop of higher prices and greater investment(12) .

This points to another underappreciated dynamic of the gold market. ‘Paper’ gold exposure is multiples higher than global physical gold stocks. Paper gold includes investment products such as exchange-traded funds (ETFs) or gold futures(13) , which provide exposure to gold price changes without being backed by physical gold bars.


Estimates suggests that there could be 100-200 ounces of paper gold for each ounce of physical gold(14) . If demand shifts toward physical delivery, a supply shock could occur, sending prices sharply upward due to the limited physical stock.


## Risk of volatility


Despite its enduring appeal as a relative ‘safe-haven’, gold isn’t risk-free and, just like any other asset, it can be subject to excessive speculation at times. For example, gold experienced sharp price swings in early 2026, recording its biggest ever one-day sell-off only two days after its biggest one-day gain on record in January(15) . Given that gold does not offer periodic cash flows like fixed income or equity securities, there is no universally accepted valuation framework, which can lead to periods of elevated price volatility.


## Navigating supply chain challenges


Investors in gold may also be exposed to social and environmental risks. The gold supply chain is a source of numerous social challenges, including human rights violations, unsafe working conditions, conflict financing, corruption and economic inequality.


To contribute to the mitigation of some of these issues, investors may opt to hold gold that has been responsibly sourced and processed according to standards such as the London Bullion Market Association’s (LBMA) Good Delivery Standard, which aims to ensure that all gold traded internationally meets consistent quality and integrity specifications. The Good Delivery Standard mandates compliance with enhanced environmental, social and governance due diligence guidelines covering issues such as sourcing, financing, risk assessments and third-party assurance.


Gold mining can also cause significant environmental damage. Open mining techniques and large-scale excavation can lead to deforestation and loss of biodiversity. Ecosystems are permanently altered, affecting wildlife and plant species. The use of mercury and cyanide in the extraction process risks contaminating rivers and groundwater. Energy and water requirements are also sizeable. Metals Focus estimates that the gold industry produced nearly as 30 million metric tons of CO2e in 2024(16) , equivalent to driving over 76 billion miles, more than 3 million times around the earth, in an average petrol-powered passenger vehicle(17) .


With supply heavily constrained, a solution to matching ever-increasing demand is recycling. Recycled gold is responsible for >90% fewer GHG emissions than traditionally mined gold(18) , as recycling uses far less energy than mining. Furthermore, recycled gold supports the circular economy, which keeps valuable materials in use, reducing demand for virgin resources and supporting sustainable supply chains.


Recycled gold also has lower social and governance risks, due to reduced reliance on mining in regions with poor labour practices or conflict minerals. In theory at least, almost the entire stock of gold ever mined in human history can be recycled.


## A resilient, versatile asset


While not without risk, and subject to periods of volatility and underperformance, gold serves diverse use cases across different sources of demand, offering investors the possibility of positive and uncorrelated long-term real returns, and materially enhanced risk-adjusted returns. Furthermore, through recycling and responsible sourcing, investors can support positive environmental and social change within the gold supply chain.
