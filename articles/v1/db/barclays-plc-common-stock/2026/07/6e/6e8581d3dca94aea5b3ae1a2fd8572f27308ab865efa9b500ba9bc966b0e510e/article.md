# A nation that adapts: What 60 years of Barclaycard, the UK’s first credit card, tells us about how Britain pays

Britain has always had a quiet talent for adapting to change, and no story tells that better than how we pay.


As Head of the Barclays Archive, I spend my days immersed in the materials that trace this evolution, from handwritten records and customer correspondence to the various wonderful marketing campaigns, internal strategy papers and the artefacts of everyday banking life.


Payments may seem routine, even invisible, but in the archive, they tell a richer story: one of shifting expectations, changing social norms, and, crucially, growing independence.


2026 marks the 60th anniversary of Barclaycard, the UK’s first credit card, which launched on 29 June 1966. At the time, the idea of paying with a piece of plastic rather than cash or cheque challenged so many long-held assumptions about money.


And what began as a modest initiative, a six-page strategy, £20,000 of investment and a team of just 200 people working from a converted shoe factory in Northampton, quickly became something larger. Within 12 months, more than one million people had signed up.


That early adoption reflects something enduring about Britain: when a new way of doing things proves useful, people are willing to adapt and to do so quickly.


## A key driver of financial autonomy


For many people, the credit card was about far more than convenience. It marked a subtle but significant shift in financial independence.


The launch of the credit card marked the moment when people could quickly and easily access credit. No more uncomfortable interviews with the Bank Manager.


This was a huge change for everyone, but especially for women, who had often had to provide a male guarantor before credit was agreed.


Women were no longer just seen as household purchasers, but as individuals managing their own credit and financial decisions. It is a reminder that innovation in payments does not simply alter transactions; it reshapes who is able to participate fully in economic life.


The practicalities of this shift are equally striking. “How to use your Barclaycard” guides carefully walk customers through a system that was entirely new.


Today, payments innovation is often intuitive; in 1966, it had to be explained step by step. These documents speak to the effort required to build trust and to a public willing to learn.


## A slow burn


Adoption was not immediate. As Patricia Lancaster, an original Barclaycard employee recalled to me, many recipients initially returned their cards, unsure what they would use them for.


In the archive, traces of that uncertainty remain, letters, forms and queries from customers encountering something unfamiliar. Yet what followed is a pattern repeated over decades: scepticism gives way to understanding and understanding to widespread adoption.


Behind the scenes, the systems powering these early transactions were equally revealing. Patricia described a workplace driven by paper, punch cards and a single “giant” computer – - far removed from today’s real-time digital environment.


Yet even then, the foundations of modern payments were being laid: fraud prevention processes, transaction tracking and the operational discipline needed to grow securely.


Over time, novelty became habit. Through the 1970s and 1980s, credit cards moved from occasional tools to everyday essentials, supported by expanding merchant networks and rising confidence. By the time electronic payments, Chip & PIN and contactless technology arrived, the behavioural groundwork had already been established.


Barclaycard also played a pivotal role in accelerating that technological shift. In 1986, it installed the UK’s first electronic point-of-sale terminal at a Miss Selfridge store in Brent Cross, processing the country’s first in-store electronic card payment, a transaction that marked the beginning of faster, more efficient retail payments.


From there, it continued to pioneer new ways to pay, leading the rollout of contactless technology in the UK in 2007 and later introducing mobile and wearable payments, helping to move transactions ever closer to being seamless and invisible within everyday life.


Today, contactless is the UK consumer’s most popular way to pay, accounting for 66% of all credit card transactions.


## A total transformation


Today, that transformation is unmistakable. There are now 59 million credit cards in circulation in the UK, and, in March of 2026, the most recent month on record with UK finance, there were 419 million credit card transactions with a total spend of £23.8 billion.


But the innovation has not stopped. In 2025, for the second consecutive year, Barclaycard onboarded more than one million new customers. While forms have evolved, from plastic cards to phones, wearables and increasingly invisible payments, the demand for convenience, control and security remains constant.


From an archival perspective, that is perhaps the most powerful insight: payments innovation is never only about technology. It is about people, who they are, what they value, and how their roles evolve.


Moments of pressure have often accelerated that evolution. Economic uncertainty, shifting consumer confidence and, more recently, the pandemic have all driven rapid behavioural change - from the rise of contactless to the growth of digital-first transactions.


Each time, the same pattern emerges: necessity drives experimentation, and experimentation becomes habit.


## A time of change


Looking back to 1966, the launch of Barclaycard formed part of a wider moment of national change. The BBC was preparing to introduce colour television, England would go on to win the World Cup, and new consumer experiences were reshaping everyday life. It was a period defined by optimism and innovation and by a willingness to embrace the new.


That instinct endures. As payments become more seamless, more embedded and often less visible from biometric authentication to AI-driven systems - the pace of change is only accelerating. Yet the lesson of the past 60 years is clear: while the technology evolves, Britain’s capacity to adapt remains constant.


From a single card issued from a converted factory to a sophisticated digital ecosystem, Barclaycard’s history is not just a story of innovation. It is a story of a society learning, adjusting, and democratising payments to make them accessible for all.


And if the archive tells us anything, it is this: how Britain pays will keep changing, and it will be shaped not just by technology, but by the people who choose to adopt it.
