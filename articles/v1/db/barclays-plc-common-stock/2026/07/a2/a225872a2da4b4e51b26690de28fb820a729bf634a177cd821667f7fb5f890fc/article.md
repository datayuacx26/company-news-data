# Are you prepared for the pension cliff-edge?

# Are you prepared for the pension cliff-edge?

Pensions are not just about funding retirement; they can also be an important part of passing wealth on to loved ones. Until now, unused pension savings have often sat outside a person’s estate for inheritance tax purposes, which has made them especially valuable in later-life planning.


From 6 April 2027, most unused pension savings will be added to the value of your estate for inheritance tax purposes. If your estate, including your pension, is above the available tax-free allowances, inheritance tax may be payable.


A key point for families is that age 75 will matter. Whether someone dies before or after 75 can make a significant difference to how beneficiaries are taxed when they inherit a pension.


The main takeaway is simple: pensions may no longer be the automatic “last pot to touch” when thinking about what to leave behind. They should be reviewed alongside ISAs, investments, property, cash and life assurance.


According to Nick Bearne, Wealth Planner at Barclays Private Bank, “For many families, pensions have become an important part of inheritance planning. The April 2027 changes do not remove that role, but they do make it more important to understand how your pension fits with the rest of your estate.”


## If you die before age 75


If you die before 75, an inherited pension can still offer valuable flexibility for your beneficiaries. Although the pension may be counted for inheritance tax from April 2027, beneficiaries may be able to take withdrawals without paying income tax.


This can make a meaningful difference. Inheritance tax may reduce the amount available to loved ones, but income-tax-free withdrawals can help beneficiaries decide how and when to use the money without increasing their own taxable income.


They may not need to take everything at once. In some cases, inherited pension funds can remain invested to grow in a tax-efficient manner, giving families more flexibility for the future. As always, this will depend on the pension scheme rules, investment risk and the beneficiary’s own needs.


## If you die after age 75


If you die after 75, beneficiaries will generally pay income tax at their own rate when they take money from the inherited pension. From April 2027, the pension may also be included in your estate for inheritance tax.


This could lead to a higher overall tax burden, especially if the beneficiary is already a higher- or additional-rate taxpayer. The final position will depend on their own income, how much they withdraw and when they withdraw it.


That does not mean everyone over 75 should rush to draw down their pension. Taking too much too soon could create income tax during your lifetime or leave more wealth sitting elsewhere in your estate. But it does mean your pension should be reviewed carefully, rather than left on autopilot.


## Planning around age 75


Age 75 can be an important planning milestone. For those approaching 75, it may be worth reviewing whether their pension is still being used in the best way for their retirement and their family.


This review might include whether to take tax-free cash, whether to draw income from the pension or other assets, how much pension wealth to keep invested, and whether surplus income could be used for gifting or funding a life policy.


One point to consider is tax-free cash. If someone dies after age 75 without having taken their available tax-free cash, that opportunity may be lost, and beneficiaries may instead pay income tax when they draw from the inherited pension. This does not mean taking tax-free cash is right for everyone, but it is worth discussing before reaching 75.


“This is not about rushing into decisions. Drawing more from a pension may help in some cases, but it can also create tax consequences during your lifetime”, Nick continues. “The right answer depends on the individual, the family and the wider plan.”


## Spouses, civil partners and beneficiary nominations


The spouse and civil partner exemption remains important. If pension wealth passes to a surviving spouse or civil partner, inheritance tax may not be due at that point. However, the income tax treatment of future withdrawals can still depend on whether the original pension holder died before or after age 75.


There can also be a second stage to think about. A pension may pass to a spouse without inheritance tax on the first death, but if it is still largely intact when the surviving spouse dies, the next generation may face a different mix of inheritance tax and income tax.


That is why it is important to keep beneficiary nominations up to date. The right approach will depend on your family, your pension, your wider estate, your beneficiaries’ tax positions and who you want to support.


## Rethinking pensions within your wider plan


A useful way to think about wealth is through three lenses: liquidity, lifestyle and legacy. Liquidity is the money you may need readily available. Lifestyle is the wealth used to support the life you want to lead. Legacy is what you hope to pass on.


In the past, many people have thought of pensions as a legacy asset to preserve for the next generation. After April 2027, that may still be right for some families, but not for everyone. For others, it may make sense for the pension to play a greater role in supporting retirement income, gifting or wider estate planning.


The important point is to look at the whole picture. Drawing more from a pension could reduce a future inheritance tax issue, but it may increase income tax now. Leaving a pension invested could support long-term growth, but it may increase the value of the taxable estate. The right balance will be different for every family.


## What can you do now?


The April 2027 changes are a prompt to review your plans, not a reason to panic. Pensions remain a valuable part of long-term financial planning, but it is worth checking they are still working in the right way for you and your family.


Pensions, inheritance tax and income tax can be complex, particularly around age 75. A Wealth Planner can help you understand what the April 2027 changes could mean for you, your estate and the people you want to support.


Good planning is about balancing your needs today with the legacy you want to leave tomorrow. Reviewing your pension now can help make sure it continues to support both.


Nick explains, “The best planning starts with the whole picture: what you need for retirement, who you want to support, and how your assets are likely to be taxed. Reviewing pension nominations and wider estate plans now can help families make more confident decisions.”
