The human eye evolved for the environment that it faced, with distinguishing fruit on trees(2) and detecting subtle movements in grasslands(3) among the top priorities. Whilst our evolution set us up for survival, it also established natural limits on what we can perceive. Our resolution caps out at around the width of a human hair(4) , and beyond that lies a world of both danger and opportunity. Viruses, for example, threaten entire populations, but are invisible to us, and until the 17th century could only be inferred through symptom patterns(5) .


Over the centuries that followed, innovations in microscopy, the science of going beyond human resolution, accelerated at an extraordinary pace and opened up new scientific frontiers. Multiple Nobel Prizes were awarded throughout the 20th century to the pioneers, a testament to the significance of a field which underpins modern biology, drug discovery and materials science – even down to the chip in your phone.


## Foundational uses of magnification


The earliest evidence of humanity going beyond the eye’s capability is the Nimrud Lens, an ancient piece of quartz which achieved 3x(6) magnification and dates back around 2,700 years. The practical use of this lens is debated, and the more agreeable first application of magnification is the reading stone – hemispherical glass lenses used by European Christian monasteries around the 9th century to help elder monks read(7) .


Next came spectacles, as Venetian artisans began grinding glass into thinner, more precisely curved shapes. High-grade glass from Murano met Florence’s scholarly demand in the late 13th century, and wearable lenses were under way.


Over time, lens makers combined multiple lenses and adjusted focal lengths to enhance magnification and create compound microscopes, the first model of which is attributed to Zacharias Janssen(8) , a Dutch spectacle maker, around 1590. The leap in magnification (9x) was extensive, as demonstrated by the work it enabled, in Robert Hooke’s groundbreaking illustrations. Hooke’s work revealed natural structures with unprecedented precision, most famously the intricate compound eye of a tabanid fly(9) , offering readers a glimpse into complexities of nature that were previously invisible.


Proceeding breakthroughs came in the 1670s through Antonie van Leeuwenhoek(10) perfecting the art of single-lens microscopes. He was a fabric merchant looking to assess materials more closely, and achieved 200-300x magnification, a level at which red blood cells are visible. By correcting for aberrations, blurs caused by wavelength of light not focusing on the same point, the lenses created by Joseph Jackson Lister(11) in 1830 gave us the technological principles that are still used in modern optical instruments.


## Limitations of light


At their core, microscopes direct a projectile at a specimen, and a sensor picks up the way the specimen reacts with the projectile and creates an image. Critically, the projectiles need to be smaller than any objects you want to distinguish. In optical microscopy, the projectiles are photons of light, the wavelengths of which mean the smallest resolvable detail is limited to 200 nanometres(12) . This means structures smaller than that, such as the DNA double helix, cannot be resolved and magnification only enlarges a blur.


Overcoming this resolution barrier required a projectile with a much shorter wavelength. In 1929, Louis de Broglie’s Nobel-winning(13) work showed that electrons behave like waves, but with wavelengths up to 100,000x(14) smaller than visible light. This opened the door for German physicist Ernst Ruska to propose using electrons instead of light in microscopy. In 1931, Ruska built the first electron microscope, using magnetic coils to focus electrons through a specimen. By 1933, iterative prototypes were achieving magnification of 12,000x(15) , well beyond optical microscopy at 2000x(16) .


Despite this leap in capability, commercialisation wasn’t easy, and Ruska sought help from his brother’s former medical professor(17) , who released an advisory opinion describing how the electron microscope could be of immense value in advancing research into the causes of infectious disease that couldn’t be seen previously. This was a necessary carrot for the economic risk to be taken, and in 1939 laboratories could buy an electron microscope. In 1986, Ernst Ruska received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry(18) for his ingenious idea.


## Fresh frozen


Ruska’s models were transmission electron microscopes (TEMs), which send electrons through ultra-thin specimens, and dominate cutting-edge workloads today. Much like an X-ray revealing bones, a TEM images structures within materials. The most powerful TEM today has a resolution of 0.05 nanometres(19) – at half a ten-billionth of a metre, this extensive magnification is as if you were watching a game of tennis from the moon but were still able to follow the ball.


Source: Getty images. A picture of blood taken with a transmission electron microscope


TEMs need samples to be sliced extremely thin, often by diamonds, and cannot reveal surface details. This gap was filled by scanning electron microscopes (SEMs), which focus a beam of electrons to scan a surface and produce topographic images. SEM specimen preparation is less onerous, and resolutions can still reach 0.6 nanometres(20) .


Source: Getty images. Scanning electron micrograph (SEM) of tendon


The most recent leap in microscopy added to the list of Nobel prizes awarded in this space, as the 2017 Chemistry prize(21) went to three engineers who contributed to cryo-electron microscopy. This was transformational for life sciences, in enabling imaging of biomolecules at high resolution. The breakthroughs included ultra-rapid freezing of water into a glass-like, vitrified state. Freezing rapidly prevents crystal formation, and solidifies water molecules in the specimen, so they don’t evaporate in the vacuum under which these microscopes operate.


## Our reliance is broad-based


Since Ruska’s breakthrough, the frontiers of innovation in microscopy have been continuously expanding, with each type of EM offering unique pathways for discovery and technological advancement. Firstly, **cryo-electron microscopy** is essential for **drug discovery** , as designing effective drugs requires knowledge of the 3D structure of the proteins you’re targeting. Cryo-EM enables clear imaging of biomolecules in near-native states. This level of detail reveals every pocket and crevice in a molecule, allowing chemists to design drugs that fit precisely. Cryo-EM’s resolution even distinguishes different strains of a virus, informing public health and vaccine strategies, which saved lives in mapping the SARS-CoV-2(22) spike protein and its variants during the pandemic.


Source: ThermoFisher website. Structure of a glycoprotein determined using cryo-EM


**Scanning electron microscopes** are used in **forensics**(23) to provide investigators with definitive views of trace evidence. Atomic clues can be left by residue from gunshots, fragments of paint from hit-and-run vehicles or fibres from clothing. Particles of any size can now reliably inform justice.


Source: ThermoFisher website. Gunshot residue particle


**Transmission electron microscopy** is indispensable for **semiconductor designers** for whom reviewing device structures at atomic resolution is essential. Transistors, the building blocks of modern electronics, shrank below the wavelength of light two decades ago(24) , rendering optical microscopy obsolete. Developers rely on TEMs to see the nanoscale architectures and detect defects in manufacturing that are critical to the performance breakthroughs that have attracted enormous volumes of capital investment worldwide since 2022.


Source: ThermoFisher website. Cross-sectional milling showing transistor structures


## Where we are today


Research in advanced microscopy is increasingly supporting greater accessibility to the technology through automation, AI‑driven analysis, and intuitive instrument design. At the same time, new compact ‘desktop’ systems are bringing electron microscopy out of elite labs and into a far broader range of facilities. Both of these trends are widening the base from which microscopy can contribute to addressing sustainability challenges through advancements in medicine and materials science. As such, microscopy can be seen as a core building block for a more sustainable and resource-efficient future.
