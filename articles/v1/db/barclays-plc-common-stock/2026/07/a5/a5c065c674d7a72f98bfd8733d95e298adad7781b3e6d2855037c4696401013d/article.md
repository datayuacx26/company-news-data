> “The brain, the masterpiece of creation, is almost unknown to us.” – Nicolaus Steno, 1669


For most of human history, the brain has largely remained a black box. Paul Broca identified a region of the brain responsible for speech in 1861, and scientists began mapping broad cortical areas after Korbinian Brodmann published his landmark cortical atlas in 1909. Major breakthroughs came with the advent of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the 1970s and functional MRI (fMRI) in the early 1990s. These technologies allowed researchers to directly observe large-scale patterns of brain activity. Monumental international efforts, such as the Human Connectome Project, used MRI to map large-scale human brain networks, but the detailed underlying circuitry and molecular interactions remained largely hidden.


The human brain is an incredibly complex network of over 80 billion neurons, trillions of synapses and an entire ecosystem of supporting structures. Until a few years ago, mapping just 1mm³ of the human brain was impossible due to the sheer amount of data that would need to be analysed and processed. Today, artificial intelligence (AI) and broader advancements in technology, as well as electron microscopy, are making detailed brain mapping a reality, leading to exciting opportunities and advancements in neuroscience.


> “The connectome is the beginning of the digital transformation of brain science” – Professor H. Sebastian Seung (Princeton University)


## Mapping the brain


### Neural networks


Our brain makes us who we are, serving as the biological foundation of cognition, personality, emotion, and behaviour. Complex, distributed interactions between neurons give rise to our thoughts, memories, and actions. When the connectivity of neural networks is altered, brain function is affected. Modern neurology uses the term connectopathies to describe conditions believed to be rooted in the atypical wiring of the brain’s neural networks.


Advancements in AI, electron microscopy, and large‑scale data integration are enabling researchers to map brain circuits in organisms ranging from fruit flies to mammals(2) . These maps could become the foundation for understanding a range of conditions, such as dementia(3) and schizophrenia(4) , assisting with early detection and, over time, developing more targeted treatments. In 2024, an international collaboration called FlyWire published their research on the first full connectome (map of neural connections within the brain) of an adult fruit fly(5) . Scientists mapped over 50 million connections across nearly 140,000 neurons(6) .


Just one year later, in 2025, the MICrONS (Machine Intelligence from Cortical Networks) consortium(7) published a precise map of the vision centres of a mouse brain. Covering more than half a billion connections across 1mm³ of brain tissue(8) , this tiny sample is about 20 times bigger than the complete fruit fly brain and more complex(9) , showcasing the massive leaps in research capabilities.


Researchers used AI to trace every contour of every neuron, and the output was proofread by humans. The project created 1.6 petabytes of data, equivalent to storing 320,000 HD movies. Creating a map like this seemed impossible just a few decades ago, but a combination of new scientific techniques and advancements in technology have “opened the door to a new era of realistic brain simulations”(10) . The dataset is publicly available and has already led to interesting discoveries(11) .


While creating a full human brain connectome will take massive computational resources, years of coordinated neuroscience research and further advancements in imaging, automation, and data analysis, recent breakthroughs act as a proof-of-concept. In 2021, Google Research released the H01 database, in collaboration with the Lichtman Laboratory at Harvard University. The database includes imaging data that covers roughly 1mm³ of the human cerebral cortex, and includes tens of thousands of reconstructed neurons, millions of neuron fragments, 130 million annotated synapses and 104 proofread cells(12) . H01 remains the largest and most detailed synapse-level reconstruction of human brain tissue and the first large‑scale study of synaptic connectivity.


Connectomics can be revolutionary in helping us understand neural circuit function and revealing altered patterns of connectivity that may support the early diagnosis of neurological conditions. However, it does not explain why specific neurons behave differently or undergo progressive functional decline. To add these critical pieces to the puzzle, understanding cell biology is equally important.


> “If you want to understand function, study structure.” – Francis Crick


### Cell function


Inside every brain cell, there are tiny components that determine how that cell behaves – proteins, ions, neurotransmitters, and gene-regulating and signalling molecules. Some neurological disorders, such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Huntington’s, may be driven by changes in protein structure or by faulty interactions between proteins and other molecules inside the cell.


Proteins are made from long chains of amino acids, each with a unique complex 3D structure. Understanding just one structure used to take years of slow and expensive lab work costing hundreds of thousands of dollars. In 2020, Google’s DeepMind released AlphaFold 2, which was able to predict single protein structures with near-experimental accuracy in minutes, outperforming the next best system by a wide margin(13) . So far, AlphaFold has predicted over 200 million protein structures (nearly all catalogued proteins known to science). In 2024, John Jumper and Demis Hassabis (Google DeepMind) received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing this AI-based tool(14) .


The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database is freely available and over 2 million researchers in over 190 countries use it to inform their work(15) . AlphaFold 3, released in 2024, predicts not only the shape of a single protein, but how these interact with other essential biological components, such as DNA, RNA, drug‑like molecules, and ions. This has major implications for our understanding of neurological conditions and can accelerate drug discovery by shortening parts of the process from years to just a few months.


### Early detection and diagnostics


Neurological conditions are a leading cause of ill health and disability worldwide, affecting more than 40% of the global population(16) . Some of these conditions can be prevented or treated, and early diagnosis can be critical to improved patient outcomes.


Early neurological symptoms are often subtle and non‑specific, but AI models can detect weak, multivariate patterns in imaging and bio-signals that humans may struggle to recognise(17) . Across a range of neurological conditions, AI systems have demonstrated performance that matches or, in specific tasks, exceeds that of human experts(18) .


For example, studies show that AI models analysing MRI scans can identify Alzheimer’s disease with accuracies exceeding 90%, in some cases detecting structural brain changes years before clinical symptoms become apparent(19) . AI tools can also analyse brainwave recordings (EEG(20) data) to help anticipate seizures, achieving prediction accuracies above 85%. Similarly, speech and movement-based models can identify early changes linked to Parkinson’s disease(21) .


AI-based MRI image analysis has shown great promise when it comes to monitoring MS (multiple sclerosis) lesion activity. Research shows AI correctly detected new or enlarging lesions, key indicators of disease progression, in approximately 9 out of 10 cases, compared with around 6 out of 10 using routine radiology reports(22) . AI systems have also demonstrated their effectiveness in identifying early signs of ischemic stroke, detecting blocked brain blood vessels in around 94% of cases, compared with about 82% using standard scans alone(23) . Hospitals in the US(24) and the UK(25) are already using AI to help flag early signs of disease.


### Understanding the risks


While AI tools can enhance our understanding of the human brain, they should supplement, rather than replace, a comprehensive medical assessment performed by an experienced physician.


AI models can underperform and provide erroneous outputs for populations that were underrepresented during model training, resulting in delayed or incorrect treatment protocols. A number of lawsuits are also under way in relation to the denial of insurance claims as a result of AI-based claims analysis. In addition, overreliance on AI could contribute to skill degradation over time, resulting in a generation of physicians who are less proficient at performing assessments and interpreting results independently, and therefore less able to verify or challenge AI-based outputs.


Data privacy and data integrity are key considerations. Large-scale brain mapping is complex and error prone. At the same time, AI models can generate realistic but fabricated data, and databases can become corrupted, either through deliberate manipulation or unintended model “hallucinations”, which would lead to misdiagnoses.


Health-related data is personal and incredibly sensitive, carrying far greater implications than most other categories of information. Breaches of medical data can have profound consequences – from discrimination and financial harm to long-term psychological distress. As AI becomes more deeply integrated into diagnostics, personalised care, and large-scale brain-mapping initiatives, ensuring the confidentiality, integrity, and responsible use of health data is essential.


### Looking ahead


Technology is reshaping our understanding of what is scientifically and clinically possible. In the decades ahead, sophisticated AI systems have the potential to move us closer to identifying the causes of a variety of neurological conditions and potentially developing personalised treatments.


Multi-modal AI systems are already combining different types of information, from brain scans to speech and movement data, to provide a more comprehensive picture of brain function(26) . Digital brain twins are being developed to simulate disease progression, test interventions, and optimise therapies before they are applied in the real world(27) . Neuro‑adaptive therapies, which adjust to a patient’s brain activity in real time, are already used to help manage Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and chronic pain, with wider applications across psychiatry and rehabilitation still under development(28) .


Progress is never without risk, however, and early benefits are often not uniformly distributed or accessible. Data integrity, privacy, model bias, and the risk of overreliance all underscore the need for strong governance and human oversight. As we continue to push the boundaries of brain science, progress must be paired with safeguards to ensure technologies are deployed ethically, transparently, and in ways that strengthen, rather than compromise, patient care.
