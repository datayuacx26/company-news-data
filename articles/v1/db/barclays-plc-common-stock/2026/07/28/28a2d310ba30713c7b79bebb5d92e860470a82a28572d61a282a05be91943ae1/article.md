# The pulse of fashion: How the growth of the resale market has changed the game for retailers

Fashion and clothing, by its very nature, is an industry with only one constant. Change.


The advent of fast fashion saw[global clothing production double between 2000 and 2015](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/fashion-business-models/overview) , while in the same period the average number of wears each piece of clothing received before being discarded fell by 36%. That change has revolutionised the industry and the way retailers have set up their business models.


And now they’re being forced to do it all over again.


Why? There are a number of factors at play. Discretionary spend is under constant pressure. Consumers are savvier, far more vigilant about their outgoings and actively seek out savings wherever they can. Our[Consumer Spend Report](https://home.barclays/news/spend-reports/) data shows that when people are trying to spend less, new clothing and accessories are consistently among the top cutbacks.


We’ve tracked the proportion of consumers trying to cut discretionary spending since[January 2023](https://home.barclays/news/press-releases/2023/01/festive-gatherings-and-holiday-bookings-boost-hospitality-and-tr/) , and for this group, the share avoiding new clothes/accessory purchases has averaged 55% - higher than any other subcategory, and ahead of takeaways and meals out (both 54%).


That doesn’t mean consumers have stopped buying new clothes and footwear; the sector recorded steady year-on-year growth of 1.7% in 2025, but it is forcing retailers to work harder, show up on an ever-increasing number of marketing channels, and, as we’ve seen, come to terms with the changing nature of competition, not from new arrivals, but from resale platforms.


## The rapid growth of resale


### Vinted now has more than 17 million users in the UK alone, which puts it just behind Primark and Next in terms of customer reach


Previously, when fashion sector retailers were setting targets and benchmarks they knew precisely who to judge themselves against. Every business from the fast fashion behemoths, to the mid-market players, and the luxury houses all knew where to look to assess their competition.


But, in recent years, the huge growth of the resale market has completely changed the game. Based on our discussions with clients across the sector, it has changed brands’ thinking not only in terms of how they view their competitors, but how they continue to develop their own resale strategies.


Over the course of 2025 and into 2026, the pre-loved economy has accelerated, with platforms such as Vinted gaining popularity, notably across kidswear and affordable luxury.


In[February](https://home.barclays/insights/2026/03/February-2026-Spend-Trends/) , Barclays research shows that 38% of consumers said they had purchased something from a resale platform in the last year.


According to research from[OC&C Strategy Consultants](https://www.occstrategy.com/en/) , the UK second-hand fashion market is now worth more than £7bn, with nearly one in four fashion transactions involving resale.


Vinted now has more than 17 million users in the UK alone, which puts it just behind Primark and Next in terms of customer reach.


## Changing how we buy clothes


### Our consumer research shows one in five (19%) are using platforms like Vinted and Depop to keep their costs down.


Increasingly, clothing might have two, three or more owners. If you’re in the market for a big purchase, like a new winter coat or a leather jacket, then that plays into your thinking; is it worth spending more money on a high-quality garment that can then be sold on and retain some of its value?


Most consumers still want to buy new products, but the added competition from marketplaces will force businesses to up their game and focus on producing high-quality products at an affordable price.


Our Consumer Spend data shows that for those aged 65+, spending on clothing and footwear grew by 12.3% year-on-year in Q4 2025. Young shoppers, aged 16-24, cut spending on the category by -6.2%.


That change isn’t all down to resale platforms. Cost pressures are far more acute for the 16-24 age group than they are for the 65+ age group, but our data shows that 16-24s are far more likely to look towards resale platforms to save money. One in four (25%) of 16-24 say they look to the platform to save money or even spend more on new items for special occasions. That compares to only 9% of the over-55s.


As well as that, our consumer research shows that one in five (19%) consumers use platforms like Vinted and Depop as a way to keep their costs down, and 20% see the platforms as a way of making extra money

## How existing retailers are adapting


### One in four consumers globally say they buy second‑hand goods specifically to reduce their environmental impact


The growth of resale has also made big established players sit up and take notice, with a number launching their own pre-owned offerings.


Zara launched Zara Pre-Owned for clothing resale, repair, and donation. IKEA is testing a peer-to-peer used furniture marketplace, and H&M has tried pre-loved initiatives with Sellpy, while Marks and Spencer, COS, Levi’s and Baukjen all have their pre-owned resale offerings.


This not only offers those retailers another revenue stream, but also a chance to improve their carbon footprint and their ability to recycle at scale.


According to their own statistics,[H&M has diverted over 172,000 tonnes of clothing since 2013](https://www2.hm.com/en_gb/sustainability-hm/services/garment-collecting.html?msockid=3c68483427dc60e335835ec0262561bc) , with over 65% deemed suitable for resale and reuse.


[According to Euromonitor](https://www.euromonitor.com/press/press-releases/march-2024/1-in-4-consumers-buy-secondhand-goods-to-help-environment-euromonitor-international) , one in four consumers globally say they buy second‑hand goods specifically to reduce their environmental impact, and many established brands are looking to meet these consumers with their own resale offerings. Our own data echoes this; 16% of say they use resale platforms because it feels more sustainable than buying new clothing.


## What this means for our clients


### Today’s resale market is valued at $210-$220 billion and expected to reach $320-$360 billion by 2030


Our[January Consumer Spend Report](https://home.barclays/insights/2026/02/January-2026-Spend-Trends/) showed retail spending grew 1.7%, but spending growth at general retailers and marketplaces far outstripped this with growth of 4.8%. With a desire to avoid buying new clothes and footwear now the top money-saving tactic, you can see where some of the excess spend is going.


According to the[Boston Consulting Group](https://web-assets.bcg.com/fa/dd/b5849ed54807b140a220f835487e/resales-next-chapter-report-oct-2025-1.pdf) , today’s resale market is valued at $210-$220 billion and expected to reach $320-$360 billion by 2030. With a 10% annual growth rate, the second-hand market is expanding three times faster than the firsthand market.


You can see why Vinted is now[valued at over £8 billion](https://www.drapersonline.com/companies/directory/vinted/vinted-eyes-e8bn-valuation-in-second-share-sale) and is attracting huge amounts of venture capital and investment. You can also see why[eBay is set to part with $1.2 billion later this year to acquire Depop](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cx240kme2k8o) .


The platform has seven million active buyers and over three million sellers, with nearly 90% under the age of 34. In a crowded market, every single one of those sellers is now competition for fashion houses.


The growth of the market is also putting a new regulatory requirement on retailers, with the launch of Digital Product Passports, which will become mandatory in the European Union within the next two years.


Digital Product Passports are digital records that store and share verified information about a product across its entire lifecycle and are designed to offer a consumer the chance to easily and transparently trace the history of a garment’s origin.


Digital Product Passports will be required for almost every good in time, but clothing, footwear, household textiles have been identified as a top priority sector by the European Commission.


We would advise retailers to invest in traceability and data driven reporting early, so they’re best positioned to respond.

## What’s next? An AI acceleration?


As you might expect, the next frontier for the resale market is being driven by the ever-increasing capabilities of[artificial intelligence](https://home.barclays/insights/2025/09/BPI-AI-Focus-For-Business/) .


All the major resale platforms are investing in capabilities to make listings faster and to enhance fraud detection, a bugbear that has dogged many of these platforms which ultimately rely on consumer-to-consumer selling.


Back in 2023,[McKinsey predicted that generative AI could add up to $275 billion to the apparel, fashion, and luxury sectors’ operating profits](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/retail/our-insights/generative-ai-unlocking-the-future-of-fashion) , partly though cost-saving and driving efficiencies in production, and we are seeing that now.


Our clients are using AI to help grow their businesses as well as drive efficiencies. It’s proving particularly useful for content creation, both for product descriptions and photography. Shoots that might have cost hundreds of thousands of pounds and involved substantial foreign travel can now be done at a fraction of the cost.


Agentic AI represents a huge opportunity for both traditional retailers and resale platforms. In the past, consumers might have had an eye on a garment for months, waiting for it to go into a sale or come down in price, or they might be searching for something that’s unique. Sooner than we think, agentic AI will be able to do that hard work for them.
