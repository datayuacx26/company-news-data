# UK politics: Volatility risk rises, but the equity story is nuanced

The resignation of Prime Minister Keir Starmer is not, in itself, a major shock to equity markets. The more important question is whether the next Labour leadership preserves fiscal credibility. Until that becomes clearer, we would expect large-cap UK equities to remain better insulated from the effects of the change in leadership than small- and mid-caps.


## Political uncertainty has returned to the UK


Keir Starmer announced on 22 June that he will step down as Labour leader and prime minister, less than two years after the party’s 2024 election victory. He is expected to remain in post until a successor is chosen, with nominations due to open on 9 July. Andy Burnham, who has returned to Westminster after winning the Makerfield by-election last week, has confirmed that he will stand, while Health Secretary Wes Streeting has backed him.


## The initial market reaction has been contained


The FTSE 100 finished higher on the day of the announcement, sterling recovered after an early dip and 10-year gilt yields ended lower. The FTSE 250, by contrast, underperformed the large-cap benchmark, having initially sold off before recovering to finish broadly flat. This is consistent with the former’s higher sensitivity to domestic demand, UK confidence, financing conditions and gilt yields. In other words, the immediate signal is not one of broad equity stress, but of renewed differentiation within UK equities.


The distinction matters. The FTSE 100 is not a clean proxy for the UK economy. It is heavily exposed to global earners in sectors such as energy, healthcare, consumer staples, materials and financials. A large share of the index’s revenues is generated outside the country, while the FTSE 250 is more domestically sensitive. This helps to explain why the FTSE 100 can remain relatively resilient during periods of UK political uncertainty, especially if sterling weakens, while the FTSE 250 is more exposed to changes in UK growth expectations and the cost of capital (see chart).

## Fiscal discipline is the key variable


For investors, the key variable is not simply who becomes the next prime minister. It is whether the next leadership team can reassure markets on fiscal discipline.


The market focus now turns to three issues: whether the leadership transition remains orderly, who becomes chancellor and how firmly the next government commits to existing fiscal rules. A credible chancellor and clear fiscal framework would help to contain the domestic risk premium. A more expansionary fiscal signal, by contrast, could put upward pressure on gilt yields, weigh on sterling and tighten financial conditions for domestically exposed equities.


## Gilts and sterling remain the key assets to watch


Historically, the performance of the FTSE 250 relative to the FTSE 100 has been closely linked to the direction of UK gilt yields (see chart). That relationship remains central today. If yields rise again on concerns about fiscal slippage, the more domestically exposed parts of the market are likely to remain under pressure.

## From an equity perspective, this argues for a nuanced stance


The FTSE 100 should remain relatively well supported by its international revenue base, defensive- and value-oriented sector composition, and potential sterling translation benefit. Global earners in healthcare, consumer staples, energy, materials and selected industrials are less directly exposed to the UK political cycle than the “UK equity” label suggests.


Small and mid-caps are more complicated. The FTSE 250 trades at a discount to its own history and to the FTSE 100 versus the 20-year average (see chart). However, cheap valuations alone are not enough. Small- and mid-caps remain a leveraged play on a better domestic growth, interest rate and confidence backdrop. That backdrop has not yet clearly materialised.

## The sector implications are also differentiated


Defence and strategic autonomy-related industrials should remain supported by the broader policy focus on resilience, security and domestic capability. Selected infrastructure, construction materials, transport and industrial names could benefit from a stronger focus on regional investment and domestic capability, provided the policy package is credible and does not unsettle gilt markets.


By contrast, rate-sensitive domestic sectors remain more vulnerable. Housebuilders, real estate and highly-leveraged domestic cyclicals would be exposed if political uncertainty pushes gilt yields higher or delays rate relief. Consumer discretionary names are also more exposed to household confidence, import costs and domestic demand. Regulated utilities and other politically exposed sectors may attract defensive flows in a risk-off environment, but could also face a higher regulatory or affordability-related risk premium if policy becomes more interventionist.


## The bottom line


The UK market remains inexpensive, income-generative and diversified away from the US technology cycle. But within UK equities, the near-term risk-reward still favours large caps over small and mid-caps. The FTSE 100 has greater international earnings exposure and should be more resilient to domestic political risk. The FTSE 250 and smaller companies could perform well if the transition is orderly, fiscal credibility is preserved, gilt yields stabilise and investors regain confidence in the UK domestic recovery. Until then, the small-cap opportunity remains more medium-term than immediate.
