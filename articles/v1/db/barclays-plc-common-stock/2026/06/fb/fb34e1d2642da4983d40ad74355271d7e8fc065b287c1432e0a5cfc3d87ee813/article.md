# Women and Wealth: navigating transition with confidence and clarity

# Women and Wealth: navigating transition with confidence and clarity


June 2026

The transfer of wealth across generations is one of the most significant financial and personal events a family can face. While it is often approached through the lens of structures, tax and investment, those elements alone rarely determine whether a transition feels successful. In practice, wealth transfer is a deeply human process, shaped by relationships, expectations and the emotional readiness of those stepping into responsibility.


These insights emerged from a recent roundtable hosted by Isabelle Hayhoe, Senior Philanthropy Advisor, Barclays Private Bank, bringing together banking and advisory teams from across the UK, Europe, the Middle East and India. The discussion explored how women experience wealth transition and what most effectively supports them before, during and after wealth changes hands. Across regions, one theme stood out clearly: the challenge women face at transition is rarely about capability. More often, it reflects a lack of early involvement.


Across global markets, women are playing an increasingly central role in wealth creation, stewardship and succession, yet many are still brought into financial decision‑making only at the point responsibility shifts. When this happens, confidence has little time to develop, and even highly capable individuals can feel unprepared.


## Key themes from the discussion


- **Involvement often comes too late.** Many women are introduced to wealth decisions only when responsibility transfers, rather than earlier
- **Confidence develops through exposure, not complexity.** Familiarity over time builds confidence more effectively than technical sophistication
- **Wealth transition is as emotional as it is financial.** Family dynamics, communication and expectations play a central role
- **Decision‑making often becomes more inclusive.** When women take the lead, a broader, family‑oriented approach frequently emerges
- **Early engagement of the next generation is increasing.** Without structure, however, this can create uncertainty rather than clarity.


## The preparation gap: confidence meets first exposure


For many women, meaningful engagement with wealth begins at the moment of transition rather than before it. This is particularly common in family structures where financial decision‑making has historically sat with one individual or generation. The challenge is rarely technical. Instead, it lies in knowing where to start and how to translate broad intentions into structured decisions, often under time pressure.


As Urvi Shah, Director, Barclays Private Bank, observes:


> “There’s a sense of being overwhelmed… not really knowing where to start or how to break that journey into something manageable”


This feeling is often intensified by the breadth of responsibility women assume as they step into wealth decision‑making. Many approach the role with a strong sense of accountability to a wide range of stakeholders – family members, future generations and, in some cases, wider societal interests. That breadth of consideration can lead to more thoughtful outcomes over time, but it can also bring hesitation and self‑doubt in the early stages, particularly when decisions need to be made quickly.


What emerges is not a capability gap, but an exposure gap. Responsibility arrives before familiarity has had time to develop.


## A global shift, shaped by regional nuance


While this pattern is widely observed, it plays out differently across regions. In markets where wealth creation is more recent, such as parts of India and Africa, women may encounter formal wealth structures for the first time, often without long‑standing family frameworks to draw on. In India, advisors already see a clear generational shift underway.


> “The shift is visible…women are starting to take a much stronger stand in the family wealth”
> Pallavi Srivastava, Director, Barclays Private Bank


In more established wealth markets, including Europe and the Middle East, women may inherit sophisticated planning structures that have been in place for decades, without necessarily having been involved in their design. This can leave individuals responsible for managing arrangements whose purpose and boundaries are not fully understood.


As Victoria Nahmany, Director, Barclays Private Bank, reflects:


> “It’s hard to generalise…different cultures and jurisdictions shape whether individuals feel prepared and involved”


Despite these differences, the direction of travel is consistent across regions: earlier involvement, greater female participation and a broader understanding of wealth that extends beyond financial assets alone.


## The moment of transition: when preparation is tested


When wealth begins to move – through succession planning, a business exit or bereavement – the experience often changes abruptly. For women who were not closely involved beforehand, this may be the first time they are required to make significant decisions.


From the perspective of Birgit Snow, Director at Barclays Private Bank, this moment often brings fear and anxiety, simply because individuals have never previously been part of these conversations. At this stage, even robust financial structures can fall short if they are not understood.


What ultimately matters is not the sophistication of the planning, but the degree to which the individual is prepared to engage with it – understanding why structures exist, what decisions they require and where flexibility sits.


## Family dynamics: where complexity becomes visible


Wealth transfer rarely happens in isolation. It reshapes family relationships and can bring long‑standing, unspoken dynamics to the surface. Urvi Shah notes that individuals can feel “overlooked”, with resentment, competitiveness and ego emerging if these issues are left unmanaged.


Questions of fairness, control and responsibility often become more acute where conversations have been delayed or avoided. At the same time, advisors consistently observe a shift in decision‑making when women step into leadership roles, with a greater emphasis on consensus and broader family involvement, including children and other stakeholders.


This inclusive approach often reflects a broader perspective, considering the impact of wealth across the family rather than focusing solely on financial outcomes. However, without clear roles and governance, inclusivity can also introduce complexity, slowing decision‑making and creating ambiguity.


## After the transfer: from event to ongoing responsibility


Once wealth has transferred, the nature of the challenge changes, but it does not disappear. For women who step into stewardship unexpectedly, the responsibility can feel both significant and isolating.


> “When that falls on one person…there is a feeling of loneliness and overwhelm”
> Urvi Shah, Director, Barclays Private Bank


Confidence rarely arrives immediately. It develops over time, shaped by experience, support and the opportunity to engage without pressure.


Early priorities often centre on stability and security, with a preference for liquidity and simplicity as women take on new responsibilities. As confidence grows, attention increasingly turns to what the wealth is for, not just how it is preserved.


> “Many women feel a profound responsibility to use their wealth purposefully – not just to secure their family’s future, but to create a meaningful, long-term benefit well beyond their immediate circle”
> Isabelle Hayhoe, Senior Philanthropy Advisor, Barclays Private Bank


Balancing security, impact and long‑term sustainability becomes one of the defining challenges of stewardship.


## A different approach to the next generation


One of the most notable shifts discussed was how women approach involving the next generation. Reflecting a move towards transparency and shared responsibility, advisors increasingly see women bringing children and younger family members into conversations much earlier.


Early inclusion can be powerful, but it is not sufficient on its own. Families that navigate this well tend to combine openness with structure: clear roles, ongoing dialogue and opportunities for younger family members to build understanding gradually. Without this, early involvement can create uncertainty rather than confidence.


## Redefining what preparedness means


Across every stage of wealth transition, a consistent insight emerged: preparedness is not defined by access to expertise alone, but by readiness to make decisions.


For many women, this readiness is shaped by three factors:


1. Being included early enough to understand the purpose and structure of wealth
2. Having clarity on priorities and the decisions that will need to be made
3. Feeling confident to ask questions and engage fully.


Where these elements are in place, decision‑making tends to be more considered, resilient and sustainable over time. Where they are not, transitions become significantly more complex.


## Looking ahead


As more women move from wealth creation into stewardship, how effectively they are prepared before transition will increasingly shape outcomes. Three principles consistently stand out:


- **Start early:** Confidence is built well before wealth changes hands
- **Prioritise clarity:** Understanding roles and decisions matters more than complexity
- **Create space to engage:** Confidence grows through involvement, not expectation


Wealth transition is not simply a technical exercise. When approached with early inclusion, clear structure and an appreciation of the human dynamics involved, it can become a process that strengthens confidence, relationships and long‑term stewardship.
