# GLP-1 Peptide Programs & Pharmacy Integrations: August 2026

## What Your Intake Process Has to Handle Before the First Dose


Before a patient receives a GLP-1 or peptide prescription through your med spa, several intake requirements must be in place. Skipping any of these steps creates liability exposure and can put your compounding pharmacy integration at risk.


A compliant intake process typically covers:


- A documented medical history review that captures contraindications relevant to GLP-1 therapy, including personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2 syndrome.
- A prescriber-signed attestation confirming the patient was assessed and meets clinical criteria, which most compounding pharmacies require before dispensing.
- Informed consent specific to compounded medications, since patients must understand these products are not FDA-approved finished drugs.
- Baseline vitals and weight documentation to support dosing decisions and ongoing monitoring.


Getting this intake infrastructure right before you launch a peptide program is what separates practices that scale cleanly from those that face audit risk later.


## The Two Ways Med Spas Are Running These Programs Today


Med spas running GLP-1 and peptide programs generally fall into one of two practice models.


The first is the in-house prescribing model, where a supervising physician or nurse practitioner writes prescriptions directly, and the practice sources compounded product through a 503A or 503B pharmacy relationship. This gives the practice direct control over formulary and patient communication, but places the compliance and coordination burden squarely on the clinical team.


The second is a third-party telehealth overlay, where the med spa refers patients to an external prescribing service. This reduces internal liability exposure but also reduces revenue retention and limits how deeply the program integrates with the rest of the patient experience.


Most practices land somewhere between these two models, which is exactly where compounding pharmacy integration becomes a deciding factor in how well the program actually runs.

## Scheduling and Automated Follow-Up for Titration-Based Programs


Titration-based GLP-1 programs require precise scheduling over weeks and months, and manual follow-up processes break down fast at scale. Patients need reminders for dose adjustments, labs, and check-ins at specific intervals, and missing those touchpoints creates both[safety gaps and dropout risk](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12194299/) .


[Automated appointment scheduling](https://decodahealth.com/products/scheduling) tied to titration schedules lets your team stay ahead of patient needs without adding headcount. When a patient starts a new dose, the system should trigger[automated patient check-in messages](https://decodahealth.com/products/communications) at day three, day seven, and before the next appointment, flagging anyone who hasn't responded for staff review.


Key scheduling considerations for titration programs include:


- Appointment cadence should reflect dose escalation windows, not generic monthly slots, so patients are seen at the right clinical moment.
- Follow-up workflows need to account for side effect check-ins early in each titration phase, when tolerability issues are most likely to surface.
- Patients who pause or discontinue need a separate re-engagement track through[structured patient care plans](https://decodahealth.com/products/care-plans) instead of falling into a generic lapsed-patient queue.


Getting this right reduces dropout and keeps your compounding pharmacy integration running on predictable refill cycles.

## The Hidden Administrative Weight of a Recurring Medication Program


Running a recurring medication program sounds straightforward until the weekly practice reality sets in. Each patient cycle brings a new round of tasks: verifying that the compounding pharmacy has the formulation in stock, confirming the prescription is still valid, coordinating shipping timelines, and fielding patient questions about dosing adjustments or side effects.


For most independent med spas, none of this flows through a single system. Staff toggle between an EHR, a pharmacy portal, a texting app, and a spreadsheet to track who is due for a refill. That fragmentation costs time and creates gaps where patients fall through unnoticed.


The compounding pharmacy integration problem is less about finding the right pharmacy and more about connecting prescription data, inventory status, and patient communication into one coherent workflow your team can actually manage.
