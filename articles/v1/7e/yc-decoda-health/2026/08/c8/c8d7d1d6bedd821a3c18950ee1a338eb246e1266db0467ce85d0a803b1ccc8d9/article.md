# FDA GLP-1 Rules: What It Means for Weight Loss Practices (August 2026)

## Why GLP-1 Became Med Spas' Fastest-Growing Service


Between 2021 and 2023 (the most recent full-period comparison available), GLP-1 prescriptions in the U.S.[grew by over 300%](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11297375/) , with weight loss becoming the primary driver of that demand. Med spas were uniquely positioned to capture it: existing patient relationships, aesthetic-minded clientele already motivated by body composition goals, and the ability to offer[compounded semaglutide weight loss](https://decodahealth.com/who-we-serve/weight-loss) at a fraction of brand-name cost.


For many practices, GLP-1 services went from a pilot offering to their top revenue line within months.


## The FDA Crackdown: A Timeline Every Practice Should Know


The FDA's actions on compounded semaglutide unfolded in stages, and the timing matters for how you plan your practice's next move.


- In October 2023, the FDA added semaglutide to its drug shortage list, which opened the legal door for compounding pharmacies to produce copies of Ozempic and Wegovy.
- By early 2024, compounded GLP-1s had become a primary revenue driver for thousands of med spas and weight loss clinics across the country.
- In March 2025, the[FDA declared the semaglutide shortage officially resolved](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-clarifies-policies-compounders-national-glp-1-supply-begins-stabilize) and gave compounders a wind-down deadline to stop production.
- That deadline was extended under legal pressure, but by mid-2025, most 503A pharmacies were required to cease compounding semaglutide entirely.


## 503A vs. 503B Pharmacies: What the Difference Actually Means for Your Practice


Under FDA rules, compounding pharmacies fall into two distinct categories, and which one your supplier operates under has real consequences for your practice.


503A pharmacies compound medications for individual patients with a valid prescription. They operate under state board oversight and can produce semaglutide compounds, but cannot manufacture in bulk or distribute across state lines without a patient-specific order.


503B outsourcing facilities face stricter federal oversight directly from the FDA. They can produce larger batches, but must meet current Good Manufacturing Practice standards and are subject to regular inspections.

## The Warning Letter Campaigns: What FDA Is Actually Targeting


The FDA's warning letters have followed a clear pattern worth understanding. Most actions target facilities that make claims that semaglutide injections are "equivalent" to Ozempic or Wegovy, or use misleading language about purity and sterility that compounding regulations don't actually support. Facilities adding unauthorized additives like B12 or NAD+ to semaglutide formulations have also drawn scrutiny. The agency has been especially focused on outsourcing facilities operating under 503B rules, where manufacturing standards are stricter, and violations carry heavier consequences.


## The Salt-Form and Counterfeit Problem


The FDA's crackdown wasn't purely about the semaglutide shortage. A secondary concern was the widespread use of semaglutide salt forms, particularly semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate, which compounding pharmacies had been producing at scale. These are chemically distinct from the base semaglutide used in FDA-approved drugs, and the FDA has made clear they are not acceptable substitutes. Practices sourcing from pharmacies still using salt forms face both legal exposure and patient safety liability.


## State-Level Enforcement Is Accelerating


Even without federal enforcement, state medical boards and pharmacy regulators are stepping up. Several states have issued cease-and-desist orders to compounding pharmacies and the practices sourcing from them, a trend covered in detail under[new med spa laws in 2026](https://decodahealth.com/blog/new-med-spa-laws) . Boards are auditing prescription practices, questioning whether documented medical necessity requirements were met. For med spas operating under physician oversight arrangements, the liability question gets complicated fast. If your supervising physician's license is on the line, your practice is too.


## What a Compliant GLP-1 Program Looks Like in 2026


Running a compliant GLP-1 program in 2026 means sourcing from FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facilities or 503A pharmacies with valid patient-specific prescriptions, documenting medical necessity for every patient, and maintaining thorough prescribing records. Practices also need a clear informed consent process that covers the differences between compounded and brand-name medications, as well as a reliable way to[track medication inventory](https://decodahealth.com/products/medications) . Patient monitoring protocols and[treatment plan follow-up](https://decodahealth.com/products/care-plans) matter too, since regulators and state boards are paying closer attention to how weight-loss programs are supervised over time.


## Marketing Compliance for GLP-1 Programs


The FDA applies a "net impression" standard to GLP-1 marketing. Individual statements can be technically accurate and still render a product misbranded if the overall message implies FDA approval or equivalence to Ozempic or Wegovy. Regulators review your website, service pages, paid ads, social media, and intake forms, well beyond formal advertising campaigns, which is another reason thorough documentation and compliance records matter.


Specific claims to avoid across all of those channels:


- Describing a compounded product as a "generic" version of Ozempic or Wegovy
- Using "FDA-approved" language in any context that implies the compounded formulation has been reviewed
- Displaying Ozempic or Wegovy brand logos or product images near compounded product claims
- Guaranteeing weight loss outcomes tied to the compounded formulation


## What Investigators Look for in a GLP-1 Audit


FDA and state medical board investigators follow a consistent playbook. Knowing what they check first lets you close gaps before an audit becomes a formal complaint.


### Common Audit Triggers


- Patient records lacking documented clinical rationale for GLP-1 prescriptions raise immediate flags, especially when a supervising physician signed off on visits they never conducted, pointing to the key need for[GFE compliance](https://decodahealth.com/products/gfe) .
- Dispensing logs that don't align with purchase orders suggest diversion or unlicensed compounding activity.
- Complaints from patients or competitors about "online-only" prescribing without a valid patient-provider relationship.

## Frequently Asked Questions


What does the compounded semaglutide crackdown mean for med spas still offering GLP-1 programs?


The crackdown ends the era of bulk-compounded semaglutide at low cost, but compliant programs remain viable. Practices that source exclusively from FDA-registered 503A or 503B pharmacies, document medical necessity for every patient, and maintain clear informed consent processes can continue to operate legally; they simply can't lean on price parity with brand-name drugs as their competitive edge anymore.


How do I know if my compounding pharmacy is putting my med spa at legal risk?


Check whether your supplier is using semaglutide salt forms (semaglutide sodium or acetate) instead of the base semaglutide used in FDA-approved drugs. The FDA has explicitly stated these are not acceptable substitutes. Also verify that your pharmacy is FDA-registered and not solely state-licensed, and that it does not add unauthorized additives like B12 or NAD+ to formulations, which has been a direct trigger for warning letters.


What do FDA and state board investigators actually check first in a GLP-1 audit?


Investigators typically start with patient records to confirm documented clinical rationale for each prescription, then cross-reference dispensing logs against purchase orders to rule out diversion. Complaints about "online-only" prescribing without a valid patient-provider relationship are a common audit trigger, and for med spas operating under physician oversight arrangements, any sign that the supervising physician signed off on visits they never conducted draws immediate scrutiny.


503A vs. 503B pharmacy for a weight-loss practice: which should I be sourcing from?


503A pharmacies can compound semaglutide for individual patients with a valid prescription under state board oversight, making them the more accessible option for most independent practices. 503B outsourcing facilities operate under stricter federal FDA oversight, can produce larger batches, and face regular inspections, and violations there carry heavier consequences. For a single-location weight-loss or med spa practice, a compliant 503A pharmacy is the practical starting point, but verify they are not using salt forms or adding unauthorized compounds.


What GLP-1 marketing claims are most likely to trigger FDA action against a med spa?


The FDA applies a "net impression" standard, so the full picture your marketing creates matters as much as individual word choices. The highest-risk claims are describing a compounded product as a "generic" version of Ozempic or Wegovy, using "FDA-approved" language near compounded formulation descriptions, and displaying brand logos or product imagery in ways that imply equivalence to approved drugs. Audit your website, service pages, paid ads, and intake forms; regulators review all of them, including formal ad campaigns.


Can a med spa still legally offer semaglutide to patients after the 503A deadline passed?


Yes, but the path is narrower. Practices can source from compliant 503A pharmacies using patient-specific prescriptions or from FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facilities that meet federal manufacturing standards. The days of bulk-compounded, low-cost semaglutide are over, but a structured, medically supervised program built on compliant sourcing remains a legal and viable service offering.


What is a Good Faith Exam and why does it matter for a GLP-1 prescribing program?


A Good Faith Exam (GFE) is the documented patient-provider encounter that establishes a valid clinical basis for prescribing a controlled or high-risk medication. For GLP-1 programs, it is one of the first things a state board investigator will look for, and it must reflect an actual clinical interaction, not a rubber-stamp approval. Practices operating under physician oversight arrangements must confirm their supervising physician is conducting or reviewing these exams, not just co-signing them after the fact.


Should I shut down my compounded GLP-1 program or try to rebuild it around branded medications?


Shutting down is not the only option. Practices that shift to FDA-approved branded GLP-1 medications or rebuild around 503B-sourced compounded options with full documentation can continue serving this patient population. The competitive edge changes from price to clinical structure, follow-up quality, and outcomes tracking, which tends to attract and retain higher-value patients anyway.


What records should my practice keep for every GLP-1 patient to survive an audit?


At minimum, you need a documented clinical rationale for each prescription, signed informed consent that covers the distinction between compounded and brand-name medications, dispensing logs that match purchase orders, and records of ongoing patient monitoring. Any gap between what was prescribed, what was dispensed, and what the patient was told creates audit risk.


How do I transition patients currently on compounded semaglutide to a compliant program without losing them?


Communicate proactively before the change, explain why the sourcing is shifting, and present the compliant alternative as an upgrade in clinical structure rather than a retreat. Patients who enrolled because of price will require the most management; patients who enrolled for outcomes tend to stay when the clinical experience remains strong. Documenting the transition clearly in each patient's chart protects you if questions arise later.


What does documented medical necessity actually mean for a GLP-1 prescription, and who decides?


Medical necessity means there is a recorded clinical basis for why this specific patient, with their specific health profile, is an appropriate candidate for a GLP-1 medication. The prescribing or supervising physician makes that determination, and it must be written into the patient record, not just assumed. A BMI threshold alone is often insufficient; the record should reflect the clinical reasoning, not just a number.


What is the difference between patient intake forms and clinical note templates, and does that distinction matter for GLP-1 compliance?


Intake forms capture what the patient self-reports before the visit; clinical note templates document what the provider assessed, decided, and prescribed during the encounter. For GLP-1 compliance, the clinical note is the legal record of medical necessity, and it needs to reflect provider judgment, not just patient-submitted information. Using the same document for both functions creates a gap that auditors look for.
