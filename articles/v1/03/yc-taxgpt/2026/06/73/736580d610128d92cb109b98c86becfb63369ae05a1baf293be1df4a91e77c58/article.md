### Where is my refund?


This is a common question that you will most likely be asking yourself. The recommendation we have is to use Where’s My Refund to check your refund status. It’s updated once per day and shows status about 24 hours after an e‑filed return, 3–4 days after an e‑filed prior-year return, or about 4 weeks after a paper return. You’ll need your SSN/ITIN, filing status, and exact refund amount from your return. The IRS Individual Online Account also lets you view refund and amended return status, digital notices, transcripts, and more. It’s helpful, but not required to track a refund; Where’s My Refund is the primary tool.

### Should I take the standard deduction or itemize?


This question is a really good one to ask[TaxGPT](https://www.taxgpt.com/) —your personal AI assistant specifically trained on the tax code and the hierarchy of tax laws, making it the most robust tax-focused model available. Some might opt to use ChatGPT instead, but ChatGPT is a general-purpose AI designed to handle a wide range of topics. Here is a clear breakdown of when you should itemize:


Compare your total allowable Schedule A deductions to the standard deduction which will be based on your filing status. The main Schedule A categories and limits are:


- Medical and dental expenses:


- Deduct only the unreimbursed amount that exceeds 7.5% of your adjusted gross income.


- State and local tax (SALT):


- Combined deduction for state/local income (or general sales) tax, real property tax, and personal property tax is capped at $40,000 ($20,000 MFS) for 2025 (with a modified AGI phase-down but not below $10,000), so high-tax filers may consider itemizing if they hit the cap.


- Home mortgage interest:


- Generally deductible on qualified residence acquisition debt, subject to limits. Interest is deductible on up to $750,000 of post‑12/15/2017 acquisition indebtedness ($375,000 MFS), with higher limits ($1,000,000/$500,000 MFS) for older debt; home equity interest is deductible only if the proceeds were used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home that secures the loan .


- Charitable contributions:


- Cash gifts require proper records; any single contribution of $250 or more needs a contemporaneous written acknowledgment. Quid pro quo contributions must be reduced by the value of benefits received and disclosed by the charity for payments over $75.


- Non‑deductible or suspended miscellaneous items:


- Most miscellaneous itemized deductions (the former “2% of AGI” items, e.g., investment fees, tax prep fees, unreimbursed employee expenses) are suspended for taxable years beginning after 2017.


Practical guidance to follow:


- You should itemize if your total Schedule A (medical over 7.5% AGI + SALT up to the cap + mortgage interest within limits + charitable gifts with required substantiation) is greater than your standard deduction for your filing status.
- Common situations favoring itemizing:


- High SALT payments (up to the $40,000 cap), substantial mortgage interest on a primary/secondary home, large charitable giving with proper acknowledgments, or significant unreimbursed medical expenses over 7.5% AGI.


- Special rules that may affect the choice:


- If you file Married Filing Separately and your spouse itemizes, you cannot take the standard deduction.
- Nonresident aliens generally cannot take the standard deduction


Documents to have handy:


- Medical/dental totals and insurance reimbursements (to compute the 7.5% AGI threshold).
- SALT: state/local income tax paid (or general sales tax election), real estate tax bills, and personal property tax receipts (value‑based).
- Mortgage interest statements (Form 1098) and how any home equity proceeds were used.
- Charitable receipts; written acknowledgments for any single $250+ contribution and disclosure for quid pro quo payments over $75.

### Can I claim this person as a dependent?


This is another great question where you can use[TaxGPT](https://www.taxgpt.com/) to help you answer. Simply go to the research feature and enter the details of your dependent to verify whether you can claim them. A more general answer is that it really just depends—there are different criteria that need to be met.


The dependent must pass the Qualifying Child Test and here is the information you need to know the Qualifying Child Test,


**Qualifying Child (all tests must be met)**


- Relationship: Son, daughter, stepchild, eligible foster child, brother/sister (including half/step), or a descendant of any of these (grandchild, niece/nephew).
- Age: Under 19 at year end; under 24 if a full‑time student; or any age if permanently and totally disabled.
- Residency: Lived with the taxpayer for more than half the year (temporary absences allowed).
- Support: The child did not provide over half of their own support for the year.
- Joint return: The child did not file a joint return, unless only to claim a refund.
- Citizenship/residency: Must be a U.S. citizen/national/resident or (for dependency generally) a resident of Canada or Mexico ; see also IRS summary rules.


There is also the Qualifying Relative test,


**Qualifying Relative (all tests must be met)**


- Not a qualifying child of any taxpayer.
- Member of household all year OR specific family relationship (parents, in‑laws, siblings, step‑relations, descendants, etc.).
- Gross income: Less than the exemption amount ($5,050 for 2025).
- Support: You provide over half of their total support.
- Special exception: If the person’s parent is not required to file and does not file (or files only to obtain a refund), the person is not treated as a qualifying child of “any other taxpayer,” allowing you to claim them as a qualifying relative if other tests are met.


**List of Information/Documents to have ready:**


- Relationship: Birth certificate, adoption decree/placement, court order (as applicable).
- Residency: School or medical records, lease/mortgage/tax/utility statements showing addresses and dates; custody calendars (nights).
- Age/student/disabled: Government ID; school records showing full‑time enrollment for at least 5 calendar months; medical documentation of permanent and total disability.
- Support: Totals you paid vs. amounts from others; evidence of payments (housing, food, medical, transportation); consider the Notice 2008‑5 situation for qualifying relatives.
- Form 8332: Signed release (or revocation) from custodial parent, attached to the noncustodial parent’s return.

### What documents do I need to file my taxes?


**What documents to gather before filing**


- Identity and filing info:


- Social Security numbers or ITINs for you, your spouse, and all dependents.
- Current address and legal name on file with SSA; bank routing and account numbers for direct deposit/refund or payments.
- Prior‑year Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) and/or your self‑select PIN (needed to e‑file).
- Any IRS Identity Protection PIN (IP PIN) issued to you, your spouse, or dependents; you must include these on e‑filed returns.


- Income statements (information returns):


- Forms W‑2 (and W‑2G for gambling winnings).
- Forms 1099‑NEC (freelance/contractor income) and 1099‑MISC (miscellaneous payments) ; see what goes on 1099‑MISC for reference.
- Form 1099‑K (payments via cards or online marketplaces).
- Forms 1099‑INT (interest) and 1099‑DIV (dividends).
- Form 1099‑R (retirement, pensions/annuities) and SSA‑1099 (Social Security benefits).
- Form 1099‑G (unemployment or other government payments).
- Digital assets: Form 1099‑DA (digital asset proceeds from broker transactions) if applicable.


- Health coverage:


- Form 1095‑A (Health Insurance Marketplace Statement) if you had Marketplace coverage; needed to reconcile the premium tax credit.


- Credits and deductions (supporting proof):


- Child/dependent care expenses (for Form 2441) and care provider’s name, address, and TIN; use Form W‑10 to request provider info.
- Homeowner records if itemizing: mortgage interest (Form 1098) and property tax receipts.
- Charitable contribution receipts (if itemizing).
- Medical and dental expenses (if itemizing; special rules apply).
- Education: Form 1098‑T and receipts for tuition/books for education credits.
- HSA/FSA: contribution and distribution records (for HSAs, see Forms 1099‑SA/5498‑SA instructions).


- Self‑employment and side jobs:


- Statements from banks/payment apps/card processors/online marketplaces; checks paid to you; mileage logs; receipts for business expenses; records of any estimated tax payments.


- If claiming dependents (to avoid delays):


- Proof of relationship and age (birth certificate, adoption decree, court order; government ID for age) and residency (school/medical records, lease/utility/tax statements showing address and dates) as needed to support dependency claims under Publication 501.
- For ITIN dependents, follow the ITIN supporting document rules (passport or two acceptable documents; U.S. residency proof for dependents where required).

### What deductions can I take?


There are some common deductions that you can take. You can take the standard deduction, which most taxpayers tend to claim, but if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction then you’d want to take an itemized deduction. Some of the deductions you can take vary, here is some general guidance on what you should have ready to present to TaxGPT to help you figure out which you can take.


Here is the list of deductions that you can take!


- Standard deduction


- Most filers claim the standard deduction. It’s determined by your filing status (and increased if you’re aged 65+ or blind). For 2025, amounts are shown in IRS Form 1040‑ES (Single/Married Filing Separately $15,000; Head of Household $22,500; Married Filing Jointly/Surviving Spouse $30,000).
- The statutory definition of taxable income and standard deduction is in the Internal Revenue Code.


- Itemized deductions (Schedule A)


- Medical and dental expenses: Deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income; gather receipts and statements (insurance reimbursements don’t count).
- State and local taxes (SALT): Deductible state and local income/sales taxes, real estate (property) taxes, and certain personal property taxes are subject to an overall limit. For 2018–2025, §164(b)(6) caps the aggregate; beginning in 2025, an “applicable limitation amount” applies with phased thresholds (see §164(b)(6) and (7)).
- Home mortgage interest and points: Deductible if the loan is secured by a qualified home, subject to acquisition‑debt limits ($750,000 generally; $1,000,000 for certain pre‑12/16/2017 indebtedness), and only if proceeds were used to buy/build/substantially improve the home; home equity interest isn’t deductible unless used for improvements. Gather Form 1098 and loan details.
- Charitable contributions: Cash and noncash gifts to qualified charities are deductible when you itemize, with substantiation requirements. Gather bank records, receipts, and charity acknowledgments (for noncash, keep valuation documentation). See the IRS volunteer guide’s Schedule A charitable section as a preparation reference.
- Other Schedule A categories (e.g., certain casualty and theft losses) apply only in limited circumstances. Use the IRS volunteer guide to confirm scope.


- Above‑the‑line (adjustments to income) that reduce AGI


- Self‑employed health insurance deduction: If you’re self‑employed and qualify, premiums may be deductible. Keep proof of premiums and business income.
- Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions: Deductible within annual limits if eligible; gather Form 5498‑SA and contribution records.
- Traditional IRA contributions: Potentially deductible depending on income and coverage by a plan at work; keep contribution records.
- Student loan interest: Deductible up to the annual limit, subject to income phaseouts; keep Form 1098‑E and payment records.


- Prior‑year carryforwards


- Net operating losses (NOL): Individuals generally carry forward post‑2020 NOLs (subject to the 80% taxable‑income limit). Keep prior‑year schedules and use Form 172 instructions to compute and apply carryovers.
