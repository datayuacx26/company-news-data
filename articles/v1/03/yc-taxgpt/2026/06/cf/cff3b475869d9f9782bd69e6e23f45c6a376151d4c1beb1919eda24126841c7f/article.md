# Real Estate Tax Guide 2025

#### 4.4 Millage Rates Explained – The Hidden Driver of Property Tax ROI


While purchase price and depreciation often get the spotlight, millage rates quietly shape your long-term real estate ROI. A millage rate (or "mill rate") determines how much property tax you owe for every $1,000 of assessed property value.


Here’s how it works and why it matters:


- **Millage Math:** A mill is one-tenth of a cent. So a 25 mill rate means you pay $25 per $1,000 of assessed value.


Formula: Annual Property Tax = (Assessed Value / 1,000) × Mill Rate


- **ROI Impact** :
Two identical properties with different mill rates can have drastically different operating expenses directly affecting cap rates, cash flow, and net income. Lower millage often means higher ROI.
- **Key to Property-Tax Planning** :
Millage rates become especially important when estimating holding costs or analyzing year-over-year tax increases, which are often overlooked in pro forma projections.


### 5. Rental Operations: Deductions, Property-Tax & Compliance


Managing rental property successfully requires more than collecting rent. To maximize after-tax profits and stay audit-ready, real estate investors must understand how to calculate property taxes, claim allowable deductions, and comply with IRS reporting rules.


#### 5.1 Step-by-Step Property-Tax Calculation (Assessed Value → Millage → Bill)


Knowing how to calculate your property tax bill gives you better control over projected expenses, cap rate forecasting, and return-on-investment analysis. Here's a six-step breakdown that applies across most U.S. jurisdictions:


**Step 1: Find the Local Market (Appraised) Value** Your local county assessor assigns a market value (often called “full cash value” or “fair market value”) based on comparable sales and appraisal models. This is the starting point. You’ll find it on your annual assessment notice or by using your county’s property lookup portal.


**Step 2: Apply the Assessment Ratio** Most states do not tax 100% of a property’s market value. Instead, they apply an assessment ratio to reduce the taxable base.


- **Example – Tennessee** : Residential property is assessed at 25% of market value. A $400,000 home becomes $100,000 assessed value.
- **Example – Texas** : Most commercial and residential property is assessed at 100%, so market value equals assessed value.


*Note: If your state uses a 100% assessment ratio, skip to Step 3.*


**Step 3: Subtract Exemptions** Apply any available exemptions, such as homestead, senior citizen, or veteran exemptions, to reduce the assessed value further. For example, if a homestead exemption of $25,000 applies to a $100,000 assessed value, your new taxable value is $75,000.


*Important: Many exemptions require annual applications. For instance, Florida’s Homestead Exemption must be filed using Form DR-501 by March 1 each year.*


**Step 4: Locate the Millage Rate** Your total property tax rate is expressed in mills, where 1 mill = $1 per $1,000 of taxable value. This rate includes contributions from multiple local authorities:


- City
- County
- School district
- Special districts (e.g., fire protection, flood control)


Your total millage rate appears on your local property tax bill or assessor’s website.


**Step 5: Convert Mills to a Decimal Rate** To calculate your tax, first convert the millage to a decimal:


- **Formula** : Millage ÷ 1,000 = Decimal Tax Rate


For example: 28.5 mills ÷ 1,000 = 0.0285 (or 2.85%)


**Step 6: Calculate the Property-Tax Bill** Multiply your taxable value by the decimal mill rate to get your annual property tax due.


Formula: Taxable Value × Decimal Rate = Property Tax Due


#### **5.2 State/Millage Comparison**


While many investors focus on federal deductions and depreciation schedules, your local millage rate can quietly eat into your ROI year after year. And since millage is set at the county or municipal level, it varies dramatically across the U.S., even between neighboring towns.


Understanding these variations is essential for:


- **Choosing the right market** : A property with a lower purchase price but a higher mill rate may be less profitable long term than one with a higher price and lower taxes.
- **Accurate pro forma modeling** : Knowing the mill rate upfront allows for better forecasting of annual property tax expenses.
- **Comparing state-level incentives and burdens** : States like New Jersey and Illinois have some of the highest property tax burdens in the country, while others like Hawaii and Colorado remain comparatively low.


Whether you're evaluating short-term cash flow or projecting long-term hold costs, incorporating millage rates into your underwriting process is non-negotiable. *For more information,*[explore Property Tax Rates by State and County – Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/property-taxes-by-state-county/)

The IRS allows landlords to deduct ordinary and necessary expenses incurred in operating rental property, but deductions must be categorized properly. Schedule E (Form 1040) is where these are reported, and keeping expenses aligned with its structure makes tax prep and audit defense easier.

**De Minimis Safe Harbor Rule**


Under IRS Notice 2015-82, landlords can elect the de minimis safe harbor, allowing them to deduct up to $2,500 per invoice or item as a repair, rather than capitalizing it.


*Tip: You must have an accounting policy in place and apply the rule consistently across your records. Elections are made annually with the return.*


#### 5.4 Safe-Harbor, De Minimis & Capitalization Regs (§1.263(a))


Real estate investors often struggle with one of the IRS’s toughest gray areas: when to capitalize vs. expense costs. To simplify this, the IRS issued a set of “[safe harbor](https://www.taxgpt.com/answer/what-is-the-safe-harbor-for-estimated-taxes-and-how-can-it-prevent-underpayment-penalties) ” rules under Treasury Regulation §1.263(a), often referred to using the BRA tests: Betterment, Restoration, or Adaptation.


**Capitalization Decision Flowchart (Reg. §1.263(a))**


1. **Is the item ≤ $2,500 (per invoice)?** Yes → Deduct under **De Minimis Safe Harbor** No → Proceed to next question
2. **Does it materially improve the property (Betterment)?** Yes → **Capitalize** No → Continue
3. **Does it restore damaged property or replace major components (Restoration)?** Yes → **Capitalize** No → Continue
4. **Is it adapting the property to a new or different use (Adaptation)?** Yes → **Capitalize** No → **Deduct as repair**


**Key Safe Harbors to Know:**


- Routine Maintenance Safe Harbor: Deduct recurring activities (like HVAC servicing) if done regularly and expected to be repeated.
- De Minimis Safe Harbor: Deduct tangible property costs ≤ $2,500/item.
- Small Taxpayer Safe Harbor: For buildings < $1 million, if gross annual rent is < $10 million, some improvement costs can be deducted (limited to lesser of $10,000 or 2% of unadjusted basis).


#### 5.5 Short-Term Rental (< 7 Days) & Self-Rental Traps


Short-term rentals (STRs) are subject to a completely different tax regime than long-term rentals and errors in classification can trigger major compliance issues.


**Short-Term Rental Trap (< 7 Days)** :


- If the average rental period is 7 days or less, the activity is generally not treated as a rental activity under IRS rules, it’s considered a business, subject to self-employment tax and reported on Schedule C, not Schedule E.
- If you provide substantial services (cleaning during stay, concierge, breakfast), this can also make the activity a business, even for longer stays.


**Self-Rental Rule** :


- If you rent a property to your own business (e.g., office leased to your own S-Corp), the income may be reclassified as non-passive even though it’s reported on Schedule E.
- This means rental losses may not offset other passive income unless you meet Real Estate Professional Status (REPS).


***Key Compliance Tip:*** *Track average stay durations and document services provided. Misclassifying rental activity could lead to both audit red flags and unexpected tax liabilities.*

### 6. Leveraging Exemptions, Credits & Specialty Rules


Savvy real estate investors do not just manage operating expenses; they also actively leverage available exemptions, credits, and specialty rules to reduce tax burdens at both the state and local levels.


#### 6.1 Homestead, Senior & Disabled-Owner Exemptions


Many states offer property tax relief to qualifying individuals, typically targeting owner-occupied residences rather than investment properties. Understanding eligibility requirements can lead to substantial annual savings, sometimes in the thousands of dollars.


Here’s a quick overview:

Senior Citizen Exemption Freezes assessed value or additional reductions Age 62+ (varies by state), income limits may apply

**State-by-State Examples** :


- **Florida** : Up to $50,000 Homestead Exemption; additional Senior Exemption for low-income seniors 65+.
- **Texas** : $100,000 standard homestead exemption; optional additional $10,000 senior or disabled exemption.
- **Illinois** : $8,000 exemption for seniors; special assessment freezes available.
- **Georgia** : Varies widely by county, homestead plus senior exemptions layered together.


**Quick Eligibility Checklist** :


- Property is your legal primary residence
- You filed any required applications (often by March–May)
- Age or disability criteria (where applicable) are met
- Income/resource limits (if applicable) are documented
- You timely renew applications where annual re-filing is required


***Important:*** *Exemptions do not apply to rental properties unless the owner is occupying part of a duplex/multifamily home.*


#### 6.2 Church & Charitable-Use Property Tax Exemptions (Including “Income-Producing” Caveat)


Religious institutions, nonprofits, and charitable organizations often benefit from some of the most generous property tax exemptions available, but these benefits come with strict usage restrictions.


**General Rule** :


Properties owned and operated exclusively for religious, charitable, educational, or similar nonprofit purposes are typically exempt from property tax.


As the IRS clarifies:


*"Churches pay no property tax on property used for ministry."*


( *IRS Publication 1828 – Tax Guide for Churches and Religious Organizations* )


**Critical Caveat – Income-Producing Activities** :


If a nonprofit rents out part of the property to a business or conducts unrelated business activities (UBI) on-site (e.g., running a café open to the public), that portion may lose its exemption and become taxable.


For example, a church leases a portion of its fellowship hall to a for-profit daycare Monday–Friday. The leased square footage is subject to property taxation under unrelated business income rules.


***Key Compliance Tip*** *: Nonprofits must carefully segregate ministry/mission-related use from commercial activities and maintain detailed records to support property tax exemptions during local assessor reviews.*


#### 6.3 Rental QBI 20% Deduction & Aggregation Rules


According to the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA), many real estate investors may qualify for the powerful[Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction](https://www.taxgpt.com/answer/what-is-the-qbi-deduction#:~:text=Calculation%3A,dividends%20and%20qualified%20PTP%20income.) , offering up to a 20% deduction on eligible rental income.


**How It Works** :


- [Rental income reported on Schedule E](https://www.taxgpt.com/answer/how-do-you-report-rental-income-on-schedule-e-form-1120s-for-s-corporations) can qualify as QBI if the rental activity rises to the level of a trade or business.
- This generally means regular, continuous, and substantial activities — not merely passive ownership.


**Key QBI Eligibility Requirements** :


- Maintain contemporaneous records of real estate activity (hours, services performed, dates).
- Perform at least 250 hours of rental services annually across all properties (for each aggregation group).
- Services include marketing, negotiating leases, collecting rent, maintenance oversight, and tenant communications (not investment activities like financing decisions).


**Aggregation Rules** :


Multiple properties can be aggregated for QBI purposes to meet the 250-hour requirement, if:


1. They are commonly owned (≥ 50% ownership)
2. Share similar facts (e.g., residential properties grouped together)
3. You consistently apply aggregation from year to year


*Note: If you aggregate properties, you must disclose aggregation elections on your tax return.*


**20% Deduction Math**


Step Calculation


Net rental income (after expenses) $80,000


Multiply by QBI deduction rate (20%) $80,000 × 0.20 = $16,000 QBI deduction


Taxable income reduction $16,000 less taxable income


‍


**Important** :


- The QBI deduction does not apply to income taxed at capital gains rates (e.g., sale profits).
- If taxable income exceeds certain thresholds ($383,900 MFJ / $191,950 Single for 2025), W-2 wage or UBIA (Unadjusted Basis Immediately After Acquisition) limitations may apply.


#### 6.4 Energy-Efficient Credits & Historic-Rehab Credits


Real estate investors can also tap into lucrative energy efficiency and rehabilitation tax credits to directly reduce tax liability, often with fewer complexities than deductions.


Here’s a high-level overview:


**Section 45L – Energy Efficient Home Credit** :


- Applies to developers or builders of new or substantially renovated homes.
- As of 2025, it provides up to $5,000 per qualifying dwelling unit for homes meeting Energy Star or Zero Energy Ready Home program standards.
- Rental properties qualify if:


1. The building is newly constructed or substantially renovated, and
2. Certification requirements (thermal envelope, energy systems) are met.


*Tip: Engage a certified energy rater early during construction or rehab to validate eligibility.*


**Section 47 – Historic Rehabilitation Credit** :


- Designed for certified historic structures and qualified rehabilitation projects.
- Offers a 20% tax credit for qualified rehabilitation expenditures (QREs) on income-producing properties (including rentals, offices, commercial real estate).
- Requirements:


1. Property must be a certified historic structure listed on the National Register or located in a registered historic district.
2. Substantial rehabilitation test must be met (rehab costs exceed the building’s adjusted basis).


**Credit Timing** :


Credits are claimed ratably over 5 years after the year the property is placed in service (per TCJA modifications).


***Key Insight*** *: Energy and historic credits directly offset taxes owed, making them extremely valuable for developers, renovators, and investors focused on long-term cash flow optimization.*


### 7. Advanced Strategies


Once the fundamentals are in place, experienced real estate investors often layer in advanced tax strategies to defer income, unlock greater loss deductions, or capitalize on development incentives.


#### 7.1 Real Estate Professional Status (REPS) Audit-Proofing


The Real Estate Professional Status (REPS) designation under IRC §469(c)(7) is one of the most valuable and heavily scrutinized tax positions in real estate.


REPS allows qualifying taxpayers to:


- Treat rental activity as non-passive, enabling full deduction of rental losses against other active income (like wages or business profits).
- Bypass passive loss limitations that typically apply to landlords.


**REPS Qualification Requirements** :


1. More than 50% of all personal services performed during the year must be in real property trades or businesses (e.g., development, construction, rental, leasing, brokerage).
2. 750+ hours annually must be spent in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate.
3. Material participation must be documented under one of the seven IRS tests.


*Important: Time spent as an employee doesn’t count unless you own* ***≥5%*** *of the business.*


**Audit-Proofing Tips** :


- Maintain a contemporaneous time log showing hours worked, tasks performed, dates, and property activities.
- Avoid generic descriptions (e.g., “property management”) — instead, record specific actions like “coordinated HVAC repair, 2 hours.”
- Consider time-tracking apps or spreadsheets built for REPS compliance.


#### 7.2 1031 Like-Kind Exchanges: Timeline & Qualified Intermediary Guide


A 1031 exchange, named after IRC §1031, allows investors to defer capital gains tax when swapping one investment property for another “like-kind” asset, as long as strict rules are followed.


**Key Requirements** :


- Both relinquished and replacement properties must be held for business or investment use.
- Exchanges must be facilitated by a Qualified Intermediary (QI), you cannot take possession of proceeds at any point.
- Properties must be of equal or greater value to fully defer gains.


**1031 Exchange Timeline**


Stage Time Limit


Identify replacement property Within 45 days of selling old property


Close on replacement property Within 180 days of sale (or due date of return, including extensions)


‍


**Qualified Intermediary (QI)** :


- Must be an independent third party (not your attorney, accountant, or agent).
- Holds proceeds from the sale in escrow and handles all documentation.
- Select your QI before the sale of the relinquished property.


***Pro tip*** *: Include cooperation clauses in sale contracts and alert all parties (title, lender, closing agents) about the 1031 exchange to avoid missteps.*


#### 7.3 Opportunity Zone Stacking with 1031


It’s possible to stack Opportunity Zone (OZ) investments with 1031 exchanges, deferring capital gains through the 1031, then reinvesting new gains into an OZ Fund to further defer or reduce future tax liability.


This strategy can enhance long-term compounding but comes with key caution points:


- **Basis Resets** : Unlike 1031s, Opportunity Zone investments eventually trigger recognition of deferred gain (generally in 2026). Investors must plan for the basis step-up limits and reinvestment holding period rules.
- **OZ Eligibility** : Funds must be structured to meet IRS requirements, and substantial improvement or original use rules apply to qualifying properties.


#### 7.4 Installment Sales & Seller-Financing Spread


An installment sale allows a property seller to defer capital gains tax by spreading gain recognition across multiple years, based on the receipt of principal payments. This can smooth tax liability and improve post-sale cash flow, especially when paired with seller financing.


**Key Tax Treatment** :


- Principal payments received over time are treated as capital gains, taxable at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income.
- Interest charged on the loan is taxed as ordinary income, typically at your marginal tax rate (up to 37%).


**Installment Sale Tax Breakdown**


Component Tax Treatment

#### 7.5 Conservation Easements & IRS Crackdown (Notice 2017-10)


IRS Notice 2017-10 designates certain syndicated conservation easement transactions as “listed transactions,” a category used for abusive tax shelters. These arrangements typically involve promoters syndicating investments that offer inflated charitable deductions under IRC §170(h), often far exceeding investors’ actual cash outlay.


**How the Abuse Works** :


- A conservation easement restricts the development use of land, allowing a charitable deduction.
- In syndicated versions, promoters overstate the value of that easement, sometimes 4–5× higher than the property’s purchase price, using inflated appraisals.


**Key Compliance Rules** :


- Participants must file Form 8886; material advisors must file Form 8918.
- Transactions going back to January 1, 2010, may be reportable.
- Failure to disclose can trigger penalties under IRC §§ 6707A, 6707, and 6708.


**Penalty Risk**


- Under IRC §6662(h), if a claimed easement value exceeds 200% of its fair market value, a gross valuation misstatement penalty of up to 250% of the tax underpayment can apply.
- Additional penalties may arise for lacking economic substance or substantial understatement.


### 8. Exit & Recapture Planning


Selling a property also means planning for the tax impact of prior depreciation, asset improvements, and potential casualty events. Without smart exit strategies, investors can lose significant gains to tax recapture or miss key offsets.


#### 8.1 Depreciation Recapture Calculations (IRC §1250)


When you sell a depreciated real estate asset, the IRS claws back some of the depreciation deductions you’ve claimed over the years through a process known as recapture.


Under IRC §1250, the portion of gain attributable to depreciation on real property is taxed at a maximum rate of 25%, rather than the more favorable long-term capital gains rates.


**How it works** :


- Calculate the total gain: Sales price – Adjusted basis (original cost minus depreciation).
- Determine the unrecaptured depreciation — the total depreciation taken on the building.
- The lesser of the total gain or depreciation taken is taxed at up to 25%.
- Any remaining gain is taxed at the standard 0/15/20% capital gains rate.


**Example** :


- Purchase price: $400,000
- Depreciation taken: $80,000
- Sale price: $500,000
- Adjusted basis: $320,000
- Gain: $180,000
- Recapture portion (taxed up to 25%): $80,000
- Remaining gain (taxed at LTCG rate): $100,000


#### 8.2 Capital-Gain Optimization (Harvesting, Home-Sale Exclusion §121)


Smart timing and strategy can help reduce or eliminate capital gains when disposing of real estate, especially for mixed-use owners or those transitioning from primary residence to rental.


**Option 1: Capital Gain Harvesting**


This involves intentionally realizing gains during low-income years to take advantage of the 0% or 15% long-term capital gains rate brackets.


Filing Status 0% LTCG Limit (2025) 15% LTCG Limit (2025)


Single Up to $48,350 $48,351 – $533,400


Married Filing Jointly Up to $96,700 $96,701 – $600,050


Head of household Up to $64,750 $64,751 – $566,700


‍


If your taxable income falls below these thresholds, you may be able to sell assets and pay no federal capital gains tax.


**Option 2: Section 121 Exclusion – Home Sale**


If a property was your primary residence for 2 of the last 5 years, you may exclude up to:


- $250,000 of gain (Single)
- $500,000 (Married Filing Jointly)


*Note* : The exclusion doesn’t apply to depreciation taken during any rental-use period; that portion is still subject to §1250 recapture.


**Planning Tip** : If you convert a primary home into a rental, track usage history carefully — partial exclusions may still be allowed if you meet modified use tests.


#### 8.3 Partial Asset Dispositions & Casualty-Loss Offsets


Partial Asset Dispositions (PADs) allow you to write off the remaining basis of replaced building components, like a roof or HVAC system, when new improvements are made.


**Why It Matters** :


- Without this election, you may continue depreciating assets you no longer own or use.
- Under Reg. §1.168(i)-8, you can elect a PAD in the year the original asset is disposed.


Look at the example below to understand more:


- Original roof installed in 2015, now replaced in 2025.
- Instead of capitalizing the new roof **on top** of the old one, you can:


1. Write off the undepreciated portion of the old roof, and
2. Start depreciation on the new asset from its own basis.


**Casualty-Loss Offsets (Form 4684)** :


- If property is damaged or destroyed in a federally declared disaster, you may be eligible to claim a casualty loss deduction.
- Losses are calculated as the lesser of the decrease in FMV or adjusted basis, reduced by any insurance reimbursement.
- Individuals report losses on Form 4684, and can choose to claim the deduction in the disaster year or prior year (to accelerate relief).


*Note* : Only sudden, unexpected events qualify; gradual deterioration, termite damage, or long-term neglect are excluded.


### 9. Estate & Wealth-Transfer Planning


Real estate is a cornerstone of generational wealth, but without proactive planning, heirs may face unexpected taxes, probate delays, or loss of step-up benefits.


#### 9.1 Step-Up in Basis vs. Portability Post-2025 Exemption Sunset


### Step-Up in Basis


A step-up in basis adjusts the cost basis of inherited property to its fair market value (FMV) as of the date of the decedent’s death. This eliminates unrealized capital gains and provides substantial income tax relief to heirs.


**Example** :


- Original cost basis: $200,000
- FMV at death: $600,000
- New basis for heir: $600,000
- If the heir sells at $600,000, no capital gains tax is due.


***Key Point*** *: The step-up in basis is not affected by the 2026 sunset. It remains a function of income tax rules, not estate tax thresholds making it a critical component of intergenerational tax planning regardless of net worth.*


### Portability of the Estate Tax Exemption


Portability allows a surviving spouse to inherit any unused portion of the deceased spouse’s estate tax exemption. To elect portability, the executor of the deceased’s estate must file Form 706 within 9 months of death (or extended deadline).


**Example** :


- Exemption in 2025: $13.99 million
- Deceased spouse uses: $5 million
- Remaining $8.99 million can be ported to the surviving spouse


**Post-2025 Impact:** The estate tax exemption is scheduled to revert to pre-2018 levels after 2025, significantly reducing the exemption amount. This means higher estate tax exposure for married couples who delay planning or fail to file for portability.


Category Single Married


**Estate Tax Exemption** Single exemption (e.g., $6,000,000) Combined exemption via portability (e.g., $6,000,000 + $6,000,000 = $12,000,000)


**Step-Up in Basis** Applies to inherited assets; no difference based on marital status Applies to inherited assets; no difference based on marital status


**Estate Tax Liability** Higher liability due to lack of portability Reduced liability due to portability; surviving spouse can use unused exemption


**Planning Complexity** Simpler planning; no portability election required Requires filing Form 706 to elect portability


#### 9.2 Family Limited Partnerships & Qualified Personal Residence Trusts


Two common estate tools for real estate owners are Family Limited Partnerships (FLPs) and Qualified Personal Residence Trusts (QPRTs). Each allows asset transfer at reduced tax value, but they come with structural trade-offs and IRS scrutiny.


**Family Limited Partnership (FLP)**


**Pros** :


- Centralized control of family-owned assets
- Valuation discounts (lack of marketability, minority interest)
- Creditor protection


**Cons** :


- Must respect formalities to avoid IRS challenges
- Ongoing admin cost and fiduciary duties
- IRC §2036 risk: Retained control may cause inclusion in decedent’s estate


**Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT)**


**Pros** :


- Transfers a home to heirs at reduced gift value
- Freezes future appreciation outside the estate


**Cons** :


- Must survive trust term or entire value is included in estate
- Limits on post-transfer occupancy/use
- Illiquid asset may not suit all families


***Planning Tip* :** Avoid retained-benefit traps under §2036 by ensuring proper separation of control, fair market rent payments after QPRT term ends, and no implied reversions.


#### 9.3 Charitable Remainder Trusts Funded with Real Estate


A Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT) can be a highly tax-efficient vehicle for donating appreciated real estate while preserving income.


**How it works** :


- You contribute appreciated property (e.g., rental or commercial real estate) to a CRT.
- The CRT sells the property without triggering capital gains, since the trust is tax-exempt.
- You receive an income stream (fixed or variable) for life or a set term.
- At the end of the trust, the remaining assets pass to a qualified charity.


**Key Benefits** :


- No immediate capital gains tax on sale
- Charitable income tax deduction in the year of contribution (subject to AGI limits)
- Removes property from taxable estate
- Can diversify concentrated holdings without a taxable event


**Planning Considerations** :


- Requires proper structuring and trust administration
- Real estate must be debt-free at the time of transfer
- Valuation and income projections must be IRS-compliant to secure deduction


*Best Fit: Investors nearing retirement or planning philanthropic legacy while unlocking income and tax deferral.*


### 10. Multistate & Local Nuances


While federal tax rules set the framework, state and local tax laws add layers of complexity that can significantly alter your after-tax returns. States vary widely in how they treat depreciation, exchanges, rental income, and even who collects what.


#### 10.1 State Depreciation & 1031 Conformity Map


Many states do not conform fully to the federal Internal Revenue Code, especially when it comes to bonus depreciation under IRC §168(k) and 1031 like-kind exchanges. These decoupling rules create “add-backs” that increase taxable income at the state level.


**Bonus Depreciation Decoupling** :


- States like California, New Jersey, and Massachusetts disallow federal bonus depreciation, requiring investors to add back 100% of bonus amounts and instead depreciate over longer schedules.
- Some states (e.g., Wisconsin) use their own fixed depreciation system, independent of federal MACRS or ADS timelines.


**1031 Exchange Non-Conformity** :


While most states follow §1031, a few, such as Pennsylvania, do not allow like-kind exchange deferral for state income tax purposes. That means you may owe state tax even if your federal gain is deferred.


***Action Item*** *: Check your state’s Department of Revenue or consult a multistate tax advisor to determine whether:*


- Bonus depreciation is allowed or added back
- 1031 exchanges are recognized
- You need to track two sets of basis for federal vs. state returns


#### 10.2 Franchise & Gross-Receipts Taxes on Rentals


Even if your real estate operation shows zero net income, you may still owe gross receipts or franchise taxes to certain states, especially if you’re organized as an LLC, partnership, or corporation.


**Key Examples:**


**Texas Margin Tax (Franchise Tax)** :


- Applies to all entities with gross receipts over $1,230,000 (2025 threshold).
- Rate: 0.375% for retail/wholesale; 0.75% for all others.
- Real estate rental entities must file annually, even if tax due is zero.
- No personal income tax, but this entity-level tax often surprises new investors.


**Washington Business & Occupation (B&O) Tax** :


- Applies to gross rental receipts, regardless of profitability.
- Rate for real estate leasing: 0.00484 of gross.
- No deductions allowed for expenses — this is a true gross receipts tax.
- Applies even to out-of-state owners with nexus in WA (e.g., property ownership).


*Tip* : If you're investing through entities, understand which activities trigger tax; profit is irrelevant in these regimes.


#### 10.3 FIRPTA Withholding for Foreign Owners


Foreign individuals or entities selling U.S. real estate are subject to mandatory withholding under the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act (FIRPTA) even if no gain is ultimately owed.


**FIRPTA Basics:**


- Buyer must withhold 15% of the gross sales price (not gain) when purchasing from a foreign seller.
- Applies to nonresident aliens, foreign corporations, partnerships, and trusts.


**Buyer Obligations:**


- Must file Form 8288 and 8288-A with the IRS within 20 days of closing.
- Withholding is required unless an exemption applies, such as:


1. Buyer intends to use the property as a residence AND sale price is ≤ $300,000 (reduced or no withholding).
2. Seller provides a withholding certificate from the IRS showing reduced gain or exemption.


*Penalties apply to the buyer* * for failure to withhold, not the seller. Proper due diligence and title company coordination is essential.


#### 10.4 Local vs. State Jurisdiction – Who Really Sets Your Rate?


While property taxes are typically collected at the county level, the rate you pay is often a composite of multiple jurisdictions, a concept known as tax-rate stacking. Understanding who sets each layer of your property tax rate is key to accurately forecasting long-term holding costs.


**Tax-Rate Stacking** : Your total millage rate is the sum of rates set by different local authorities, including:


- City or town government
- County government
- School districts
- Special taxing districts (e.g., flood control, libraries, fire)


This creates wide variations within the same state, depending on location.


Here are a few examples:


**New Mexico** :


- Statewide millage floor exists, but most of the effective rate is driven by local governments and school bonds.
- In Albuquerque, for example, city, county, and school millage layers bring the effective tax rate to ~0.74% — far above rural areas.


**Pennsylvania** :


- The state does not set property tax rates; instead, school districts are often the largest taxing authority.
- In Philadelphia, the city rate is 0.6159%, and school taxes add another 0.7839%, bringing the total current tax rate to 1.3998% — another factor for investor underwriting.


*Key Tip* : Before buying, check your target property's full tax rate breakdown, not just the average county rate. Local add-ons can materially impact NOI and cap rate performance.


#### 10.5 State Spotlights:


Let’s have a look at how three major real estate states shape property tax liability through state-specific caps, exemptions, and local practices:


**California – Proposition 13**


- Limits assessed value growth to 2% per year, unless the property is sold or substantially improved.
- Base year value is locked at purchase price, creating significant tax disparities between long-time owners and recent buyers.
- Property tax rate is capped at 1% of assessed value, plus local voter-approved bonds and special assessments.


*Impact* : Investors can forecast long-term tax costs more easily, but lose flexibility if they exchange or subdivide, which may trigger reassessment.


**Texas – High Effective Rates, No Income Tax**


- No state income tax, but among the highest property tax burdens in the U.S.
- Average effective property tax rate: 1.63% (can exceed 2% in urban counties like Harris and Dallas).
- Tax rates are set by local taxing units, including cities, counties, schools, and hospital districts, each independently levying millage.
- Annual reassessments at market value are required.


*Impact* : High and rising valuations can drive substantial tax increases, particularly for new construction and multifamily developments.


**Florida – Homestead Exemption & Save Our Homes Cap**


- Owner-occupants may claim a Homestead Exemption of up to $50,000 on their primary residence.
- The Save Our Homes amendment limits annual increases in assessed value to 3% or CPI, whichever is lower.
- The cap does not apply to rental or second homes, which are assessed at market annually.


*Impact* : Strong protections for homeowners, but investors face unbuffered exposure to market-value growth, especially in fast-appreciating areas.


### 11. Common Mistakes & Audit Triggers


Even experienced real estate investors make costly errors that can trigger IRS scrutiny or inflate tax liability. Below is a 10-item checklist of the most common filing mistakes and how to prevent them.


Avoid Why It Matters Fix


Failing to Report Rental Income Unreported rental income can trigger audits and penalties. Ensure all rental income is reported on Schedule E.


Improperly Allocating Personal Use Misclassifying personal use of a rental property can disqualify deductions. Maintain accurate records of personal vs. rental use.


Overstating Property Depreciation Overstating depreciation can lead to penalties and recapture issues. Use IRS guidelines for MACRS depreciation.


Claiming Non-Deductible Expenses Certain expenses, like improvements, must be capitalized, not deducted. Differentiate between repairs (deductible) and improvements (capitalized).


Incorrect Passive Activity Loss Reporting Misreporting passive losses can result in disallowed deductions. Use Form 8582 to calculate allowable passive losses.


Failing to Report Like-Kind Exchanges Omitting or misreporting Section 1031 exchanges can lead to tax liabilities. File Form 8824 for like-kind exchanges.


Improper FIRPTA Withholding Failing to withhold tax on foreign sellers of U.S. real property can result in transferee liability. Ensure 15% withholding on the amount realized.


Neglecting to File Form 1099 for Contractors Not issuing Form 1099 to contractors can trigger penalties. Issue Form 1099-NEC for payments over $600.


Misclassifying Rental Property Use Misclassifying a property as a rental when it is not primarily used for income can disallow deductions. Follow IRS rules for personal vs. rental use.


Failing to Adjust Basis for Improvements Not adjusting the basis for capital improvements can lead to incorrect gain/loss calculations. Keep detailed records of improvements and adjust the basis accordingly.
