# Logistics Tax Guide 2025

## Operating Taxes Unique to Logistics


Beyond federal income and excise obligations, logistics companies must navigate operational taxes that vary by jurisdiction and vehicle type. While often overlooked, these state-level and multistate filings can carry steep penalties for misreporting or, worse, noncompliance.


### IFTA & IRP


For carriers operating across state lines, the International Fuel Tax Agreement (IFTA) simplifies what could otherwise be a reporting nightmare. Rather than file separately in each state, carriers submit a single quarterly return to their base jurisdiction, which redistributes fuel tax payments across all traveled regions.


To stay compliant:


- You must hold an IFTA license and display two decals per qualified vehicle.
- Each quarterly return must detail total miles traveled, fuel purchased, and gallons consumed in each jurisdiction.
- Records—including mileage logs and fuel receipts—must be retained for at least four years.


The International Registration Plan (IRP) works in tandem with IFTA, allocating registration fees across states and provinces based on miles driven. Together, these frameworks reduce administrative overhead—if your data is audit-ready.


**Tip for 2025:** Leverage your Electronic Logging Devices (ELDs) and fleet management software to get ahead of audits. By syncing ELD APIs with IFTA/IRP reporting tools, carriers can:


- Automatically track mileage and fuel usage by jurisdiction
- Flag inconsistencies before filing
- Maintain airtight documentation for pre-audit checks


### State Weight-distance & Highway-use Taxes: Location-specific Obligations


Certain states impose specialized operating taxes based on vehicle weight and miles traveled, requiring separate filings even for IFTA-compliant fleets.


In New York, the Highway Use Tax (NY HUT) applies to motor carriers using public highways with vehicles above specific weight thresholds. As of May 20, 2025, mileage rates have been updated across various weight categories. These new rates reflect changes in infrastructure funding strategy and are now published on the New York State Department of Taxation and Finance website.


Meanwhile, in Oregon, the Weight-Mile Tax remains in effect for commercial vehicles over 26,000 pounds. For 2025, the state has introduced an automatic re-enrollment process:


- Carriers with active, compliant accounts will be automatically renewed for the new tax year.
- Credentials and any updated rate information will be issued electronically, streamlining what was once a manual task.


However, automatic doesn’t mean hands-off. Carriers should still verify weight classifications and ensure vehicle data is current to avoid miscalculations or rate mismatches.


## State & Local Income-tax Minefield


While federal taxes get the headlines, it’s often state and local income taxes that quietly create the most complexity, especially for interstate carriers. Different apportionment formulas, entity-level tax regimes, and workaround structures mean logistics businesses must stay vigilant to avoid double taxation and missed credits.


### Apportionment for Interstate Carriers


States take different approaches to dividing up income for tax purposes, and for logistics companies, how income is “apportioned” can have a dramatic impact on what you owe.


- Washington State uses a single-factor apportionment method based on receipts. Interstate carriers must calculate the percentage of total receipts that are sourced to Washington and multiply that by their apportionable income. This simplifies the math but may disadvantage high-volume, low-margin operators.
- Ohio, on the other hand, applies its Commercial Activity Tax (CAT) based on where goods are delivered. If your freight ends up in Ohio, that revenue is “sitused” to the state, even if the route began elsewhere. For 2025, the CAT exclusion threshold is $6 million, meaning only businesses above that must pay.


Adding to the complexity, both states also levy public utility gross-receipts taxes on certain transportation activities:


- In Washington, exemptions exist for interstate transport to comply with federal commerce protections.
- In Ohio, these taxes apply to gross receipts from utility services provided in-state, separate from CAT liability.


For carriers crossing multiple states daily, apportionment rules can swing tax bills by tens of thousands of dollars.


### PTET & SALT Cap Workaround


The federal $10,000 cap on state and local tax (SALT) deductions hit pass-through owners especially hard. But the Pass-Through Entity Tax (PTET), now available in 37 states, offers a legal workaround, and for logistics firms structured as partnerships or S-corporations, it can unlock significant savings.


Here’s how it works:


- The entity pays state income tax on business income.
- The owners then claim a dollar-for-dollar credit on their individual state returns.
- This shifts the tax burden from personal to entity level, allowing the full deduction on the federal return without hitting the $10K SALT cap.


In Ohio, for example, electing entities pay PTET at a flat 3% rate, and owners receive a refundable credit on their state filings. It’s a clean workaround that can materially reduce state and federal liabilities, especially for high-earning owner-operators.


However, not all states offer this benefit. Washington, which has no personal or corporate income tax, does not have a PTET framework. Instead, businesses in the state are subject to the Business & Occupation (B&O) tax, a gross receipts tax that cannot be offset in the same way.


## Payroll & Labor Compliance


Labor costs are already one of the largest expense lines in logistics, and missteps in classification, reimbursement, or tax credit claims can lead to costly penalties. With new federal rules and updated rates for 2025, payroll compliance requires strategic risk management.


### Driver Classification: W-2, 1099, or Lease-operator?


The debate over how to classify drivers isn’t new, but it’s taken on fresh urgency with the Department of Labor’s 2024 “economic reality” rule. This rule reframes classification by asking: *Is the driver truly in business for themselves, or economically dependent on the carrier?*


Key factors the DOL emphasizes include:


- **Control** : Does the company set routes, schedules, or dispatch rules?
- **Investment** : Has the driver invested in a truck—leased or owned—and assumed financial risk?
- **Opportunity for profit/loss** : Can the driver book loads independently or take on additional contracts?
- **Permanence** : Is the engagement ongoing or tied to individual jobs?
- **Nature of the work** : Is driving central to the company’s operations, or a peripheral service?
- **Additional factors include:**


(1) the extent to which the work performed is an integral part of the employer's business, and


(2) the worker's skill and initiative.


Misclassification—labeling a driver as a 1099 contractor when they meet the test for W-2 employee—can expose carriers to IRS audits, back taxes, and penalties under both federal and state law. Relief may be available through the Voluntary Classification Settlement Program (VCSP), but it requires proactive action.


Also for 2025, the IRS standard mileage rate has increased to 70¢ per mile—useful for companies reimbursing driver expenses based on actual miles driven for business purposes. This rate can support accurate and defensible reimbursements, especially in hybrid lease-op or mixed-status driver fleets.


### WOTC & Per-Diem Plan: Optimize Credits and Compliance


The Work Opportunity Tax Credit (WOTC) remains a valuable yet underused incentive for logistics employers. If you hire individuals from targeted groups—including veterans, ex-felons, and long-term unemployed individuals—you may qualify for:


- A 40% tax credit on up to $6,000 in first-year wages for most qualifying employees (up to $24,000 for eligible veterans)


For carriers managing long-haul fleets, per-diem reimbursement plans offer another way to support drivers while optimizing tax treatment.


To qualify as an accountable plan and avoid having payments treated as taxable wages, the IRS requires three things:


1. A **business connection** : Payments must cover business-related travel (e.g., meals and incidentals on the road)
2. **Substantiation** : Drivers must document the time, place, and purpose of travel
3. **Return of excess** : Drivers must repay any amount that exceeds actual business expenses


For 2025–2026, the allowable per-diem M&IE (Meals & Incidental Expenses) rates are:


- $80/day within the continental U.S. (CONUS)
- $86/day outside the continental U.S. (OCONUS)


When structured properly, these plans can reduce payroll tax liability for employers and simplify expense tracking for drivers. But without documentation, they risk being disqualified, turning a benefit into a liability.


## International & Cross-border Considerations


As global shipping volumes rise and compliance frameworks evolve, logistics operators with cross-border activity must think beyond domestic tax codes. From duty-free limits on imports to international tax reforms and foreign VAT obligations, staying compliant is critical to protecting margins and avoiding costly penalties.


### Section 321, CTPAT, and ACE: Key Enablers of Efficient U.S. Entry


For logistics firms managing cross-border e-commerce, Section 321 of the Tariff Act of 1930 is a major operational advantage. It allows for duty-free entry of goods valued at $800 or less, facilitating low-cost, expedited customs clearance.


To take full advantage:


- Ensure accurate valuation and classification of shipments to avoid rejection or fines.
- Align with the Customs Trade Partnership Against Terrorism (CTPAT) program, which offers benefits like reduced inspections and faster border processing for certified operators that follow stringent supply chain security standards.
- Integrate with the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE) system to submit customs documentation electronically in line with U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) requirements.


Together, Section 321, CTPAT certification, and ACE integration offer a streamlined and secure customs experience, but only for those who meet the criteria and maintain airtight records.


### Pillar Two QDMTT for MX and CA hubs: Global Minimum Tax Reaches North America


The OECD’s Pillar Two framework, designed to enforce a 15% global minimum tax, now affects logistics firms with international subsidiaries, especially those with operational hubs in Mexico (MX) and Canada (CA).


Here’s what’s changing:


- Canada has enacted the Global Minimum Tax Act, effective for fiscal years starting after December 31, 2023. It includes both the Income Inclusion Rule (IIR) and Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-Up Tax (QDMTT) provisions.
- Mexico has publicly stated its intentions to follow suit, aligning its domestic rules with the OECD’s Pillar Two standards.


If your logistics operation functions through an entity or subsidiary in either country, you’ll need to:


- Assess your effective tax rate in each jurisdiction
- File GloBE Information Returns (GIR) to confirm compliance and avoid top-up penalties


Failing to comply may result in double taxation and reputational risk among multinational clients who expect tax transparency across the value chain.


### EU VAT on Freight: Tax Compliance Beyond Borders


If you’re moving goods across or within the European Union, freight services are not automatically exempt from tax. Under the EU’s Value-Added Tax (VAT) system, freight and transportation charges are typically subject to VAT at the standard rate, unless a specific exemption applies, such as in certain cases of international transport.


To stay compliant:


- Ensure accurate invoicing that properly reflects freight VAT charges
- Maintain VAT registration in every member state where your business operates or provides services
- Keep up with changes under the EU’s Digital Single Market Strategy, which continues to modernize and harmonize cross-border VAT compliance rules


## Sustainability & ESG Credits


As logistics companies face growing expectations to reduce emissions and invest in cleaner operations, the tax code is evolving to reward that transition. The § 30C Alternative Fuel Vehicle Refueling Property Credit, enhanced by the Inflation Reduction Act, provides meaningful financial incentives for building out electric vehicle (EV) and clean-fuel infrastructure, especially in underserved and energy-transition communities.


### The § 30c Credit: Up to 30% for Charging and Clean Fuel Equipment


Businesses can now claim a 30% tax credit, up to $100,000 per item, for qualifying refueling property placed in service through 2032. This includes EV charging stations, hydrogen dispensers, and other alternative fuel infrastructure.


To qualify for the full 30%, projects must meet prevailing wage and apprenticeship (PWA) requirements. If those standards aren’t met, the credit drops to 6%, though the same $100,000 cap applies for depreciable business property. For non-depreciable property, like residential chargers, the credit remains 30% of costs, capped at $1,000 per item.


### Energy-community Bonus


Projects located in designated energy communities—such as areas near closed coal mines, brownfields, or shuttered power plants—may be eligible for additional bonus credits. This makes § 30C even more valuable for carriers expanding into rural or economically distressed regions.


It’s a win-win: businesses receive enhanced tax savings while helping revitalize areas impacted by industrial decline.


### ESG Alignment


Beyond direct savings, the § 30C credit helps logistics firms advance Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) commitments:


- Environmental: Encourages clean-fuel adoption and reduces carbon emissions
- Social: Drives investment and job creation in low-income or underserved communities
- Governance: Requires compliance with labor and apprenticeship standards, promoting transparent and equitable operations


With sustainability increasingly embedded in procurement and investor decisions, taking advantage of § 30C can support both tax strategy and brand positioning.


#### Claiming the credit: What you need to file


To claim the credit:


- File IRS Form 8911
- Maintain documentation for equipment costs, labor, PWA compliance, and location eligibility
- Use IRS mapping tools to confirm the site is in a qualified census tract or energy community


Eligible entities may also benefit from elective (direct) pay provisions, which allow them to monetize the credit even without taxable income, making this particularly helpful for newer operations investing heavily in infrastructure.


For example, if your fleet installs $120,000 worth of EV charging equipment at a qualified facility and meets PWA rules, your credit could total $36,000. If the site is in an energy community, that figure could be even higher with bonus incentives applied.


## Risk Management & Audit Defense


For logistics operators, the risk of triggering an audit doesn’t just come from major violations; it often starts with small inconsistencies: overstated fuel credits, missing HVUT records, or mileage mismatches in state filings. The stakes are high, and so is the scrutiny.


### Fuel Tax Credit Over-claims: A Top Audit Trigger


Fuel tax credits, while valuable, come with detailed substantiation requirements. The IRS has ramped up enforcement around biodiesel, alternative fuel, and off-highway usage claims, particularly where documentation is weak or eligibility rules are misunderstood.


#### Risk management best practices:


- Accurate recordkeeping is critical. Maintain detailed logs of fuel purchases, usage, and receipts. Ensure any fuel used for tax-exempt purposes (e.g., off-road equipment) is clearly documented.
- Verify eligibility regularly against IRS guidelines, especially since some credits, like the biodiesel mixture credit, have sunset dates or restrictive conditions.
- Conduct periodic internal audits using IRS worksheets to validate computations before filing.


**Audit defense essentials:**


- Keep a complete archive of invoices, logs, and proof of non-taxable use.
- If an error is discovered post-filing, submit an amended return quickly to reduce penalties and demonstrate good faith.


### HVUT Suspended-use Gaps: Small Mileage, Big Exposure


Heavy Vehicle Use Tax (HVUT) filings under Form 2290 are another frequent source of compliance risk, particularly around the suspended-use category for vehicles under 5,000 miles (or 7,500 for agricultural use).


**To stay compliant:**


- File Form 2290 on time—by the last day of the month after a vehicle first operates on public highways.
- Use IRS-approved e-file systems for faster, error-free submission.
- Deploy GPS or telematics tools to ensure real-time, accurate mileage tracking.


**For audit defense:**


- Maintain mileage logs with odometer readings, trip dates, and usage context.
- Keep copies of Schedule 1 with the IRS stamp as proof of proper filing.


Even unintentional overuse of a suspended vehicle can lead to tax due, interest, and retroactive filing requirements.


### NY HUT Mileage Understatement: A State-level Red Flag


New York’s Highway Use Tax (HUT) is particularly aggressive in flagging underreported mileage, especially as telematics and GPS data become more accessible to state auditors.


**Prevention starts with:**


- Filing accurate Form MT-903 reports using either the gross-weight or unloaded-weight method, consistently applied throughout the year.
- Staying current with your filing frequency, which may be quarterly, monthly, or annual, based on prior-year liability.
- Integrating telematics systems with your reporting process to reduce discrepancies.


**If audited:**


- Present detailed daily logs of miles traveled in New York, supported by fuel receipts and dispatch records.
- Reconcile GPS data against reported mileage and fuel usage proactively to identify gaps before the state does.
- Respond swiftly to audit notices with a full explanation and supporting documents to reduce the risk of penalties or interest.


## Compliance Calendar & Roles Matrix


Logistics tax compliance is a rolling obligation that spans monthly, quarterly, and annual filings across federal and state agencies. Missing a deadline can lead to penalties, interest, suspended registrations, and audit exposure.


### Monthly: New York HUT and Oregon WDT


**New York Highway Use Tax (NY HUT)** Motor carriers operating qualified vehicles on New York public highways must file based on their prior year’s liability:


- **Monthly:** If prior-year liability exceeds $12,000
- **Quarterly:** If liability falls between $1,200 and $12,000
- **Annually:** If liability is $1,200 or less and you were subject to NY HUT for the entire preceding year


**Deadlines:**


- Monthly returns are due by the last day of the following month
- Quarterly deadlines: April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31
- Annual returns are due by January 31 of the following year


**Oregon Weight-Distance Tax (OR WDT)** Carriers operating vehicles over 26,000 lbs. on Oregon highways are subject to OR WDT. Returns are filed based on miles traveled and vehicle weight.


**Deadlines:**


- Monthly: Due by the 20th of the following month
- Quarterly: Same dates as NY HUT: April 30, July 31, October 31, January 31


### Quarterly: IFTA and Excise Tax Filings


**International Fuel Tax Agreement (IFTA)** If your fleet operates across multiple jurisdictions, IFTA compliance is mandatory. Filings reconcile mileage and fuel consumption by state or province.


**Quarterly due dates:** April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31


**IRS Form 720 – Federal Excise Tax** Form 720 is used to report various federal excise taxes, including those related to fuel, tires, and heavy trucks.


### HVUT (Form 2290)


**Heavy Vehicle Use Tax (HVUT)** Applies to vehicles 55,000 pounds and above in taxable gross weight. This tax supports the maintenance of federal highways. The tax period for Form 2290 runs from July 1 to June 30 of the following year.


- If the vehicle is first used in July, the return is due August 31
- For vehicles placed in service in other months, the return is due by the last day of the month following the first use
