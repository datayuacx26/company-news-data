# Tax Controversy & Litigation: A Practitioner's Guide to the IRS Process

**What happens when the IRS disagrees with a tax return?**


Tax controversy is the formal process of resolving a dispute with a taxing authority—most often, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS). It begins when the IRS questions the accuracy of a return, and it can end in an administrative settlement or escalate into litigation in federal court.


In Fiscal Year 2024, the IRS closed approximately 505,000 examinations, underscoring how common these disputes are. Yet for many taxpayers and professionals alike, the IRS procedural process remains opaque and intimidating.


This guide breaks down each phase of a U.S. tax controversy, highlighting the strategic decisions practitioners must make at every stage. It also demonstrates how tools like TaxGPT assist with document review, audit defense, appeals strategy, and litigation preparation.


*While the*[Ultimate Guide to Tax Research](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/tax-research) *explains how to identify and interpret tax law, this guide focuses on what to do when those interpretations are challenged.*


## The First Stage — IRS Examination (Audit)


### What Happens During an IRS Examination?


An IRS examination, more commonly referred to as an audit, is the entry point of the formal tax controversy process. This is where the IRS reviews a taxpayer’s return to verify the accuracy of the information reported. Examinations can be triggered by discrepancies in reported income, suspicious deductions or credits, or simply through random selection and algorithmic risk scoring.


Depending on the complexity of the issue, an audit may be resolved quickly or it may open the door to a lengthy tax dispute that could escalate to the IRS Independent Office of Appeals or even litigation.


### Types of Audits: Correspondence, Office, and Field


There are three main forms of[IRS audits](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/irs-tax-audit) , each varying in complexity and level of taxpayer involvement:


- **Correspondence Audit:** The most common type, conducted entirely by mail or electronically. While typically limited in scope and intended for quick resolution, it can become complex, spanning multiple rounds of document requests or statute extensions. Mishandling can expand the scope or trigger escalation.
- **Office Audit:** The taxpayer (or their representative) is asked to appear at a local IRS office with specific records. These audits typically involve multiple issues or a higher risk profile. Having a prepared representative is crucial to control the narrative and avoid unnecessary expansion of the audit scope.
- **Field Audit:** The most comprehensive and intrusive form of audit. IRS agents visit the taxpayer’s place of business or residence to conduct an in-depth examination. Field audits are often reserved for high-income individuals, complex businesses, or issues involving large dollar amounts. Practitioners must carefully manage these audits to protect their client’s position and limit overreach.


*Pro Tip: An early and accurate framing of the taxpayer’s story (backed by documentation) can significantly reduce the risk of the audit expanding into unrelated areas.*


### The Examiner’s Tool: Form 4564 (Information Document Request)


A key procedural mechanism during audits is the Form 4564, Information Document Request (IDR). This form is how the IRS formally requests records, explanations, and data from the taxpayer.


Practitioners must treat each IDR seriously and strategically:


- **Respond completely and clearly:** Incomplete or vague responses can lead to additional IDRs or broader audit scopes.
- **Organize documentation effectively:** Poor organization delays resolution and increases scrutiny.
- **Clarify ambiguous requests:** IRS examiners sometimes issue broad or unclear IDRs. It's the practitioner’s role to narrow their scope where possible.


Well-managed IDR responses are a practitioner’s best defense against audit expansion and a key part of audit defense strategy.


### The Outcome: Understanding the 30-Day Letter


At the end of the audit, the IRS may either accept the return as filed or propose adjustments. If it proposes changes, the taxpayer will receive a 30-day letter, which is generally a Letter 525 or 915.


This document plays a pivotal role in the tax controversy timeline:


- **Purpose:** To inform the taxpayer of the IRS’s proposed changes and provide an opportunity to respond.
- **Origin:** It is issued only after the audit concludes with unresolved issues.
- **Response Options:**


1. Agree with the findings and pay any tax owed.
2. File a Formal Protest requesting a hearing with the IRS Independent Office of Appeals.
3. Do nothing, which will result in the issuance of a Statutory Notice of Deficiency (90-day letter).


Practitioners must treat the 30-day letter as a strategic fork in the road. Failing to act within the 30-day window can severely limit a taxpayer’s future rights.


If the taxpayer and the examiner cannot reach an agreement, the controversy moves from the examination phase to the administrative appeals process, or straight to litigation if the taxpayer desires.


## The IRS Independent Office of Appeals


### What Is the Role of the IRS Office of Appeals?


When a dispute remains unresolved after an IRS examination, the case may enter a critical second phase: administrative resolution through the IRS Independent Office of Appeals (Appeals). This office plays a unique and essential role in the tax controversy process.


Established to ensure due process and reduce unnecessary litigation, Appeals is an independent function within the IRS. Its mission is to resolve tax disputes fairly and impartially, without the need to go to court.


*A high percentage of cases handled by Appeals are resolved without advancing to litigation, saving time, money, and resources for both the IRS and the taxpayer.*


### Who Handles the Case?


Cases are reviewed by an Appeals Officer, whose authority extends beyond mere technical application of tax law. Appeals Officers are empowered to consider the hazards of litigation.


Hazards of litigation refers to the legal uncertainty that could arise if the dispute were brought before a judge. If both sides face litigation risk, Appeals may settle the case based on a probability-adjusted outcome. For example, if the IRS is only 60% confident that it would prevail in Tax Court, it may offer to settle for 60% of the proposed deficiency.


This risk-based settlement framework differentiates Appeals from examination and makes it a vital forum for skilled negotiation.


### Initiating the Appeals Process: Filing a Formal Protest


To enter this phase, a taxpayer must respond to the 30-day letter by filing a protest. The form of the protest depends on the amount in dispute. Cases involving amounts greater than $25,000 require a formal written protest. This formal protest must include:


- A statement of intention to appeal
- Detailed identification of disputed items
- A statement of facts and legal grounds supporting the taxpayer’s position
- Supporting documentation and relevant legal authorities


### The Appeals Conference


Once the protest is accepted, an appeals conference is scheduled. This is a non-adversarial meeting between the taxpayer (or their representative) and the Appeals Officer.


Key features of this stage:


- Less formal than court proceedings, but still highly procedural
- Opportunity to present evidence, clarify legal interpretations, and narrow issues
- Negotiation-centric, allowing for flexibility in settlement based on legal and factual analysis


Appeals are not required to adopt the examiner’s position. In fact, Appeals Officers review the case independently and may disagree with the examiner if the facts or law support the taxpayer’s argument.


### **Strategic Implications**


For practitioners, Appeals is often the best opportunity to resolve a tax dispute without incurring the cost, time, and uncertainty of court. It also allows for:


- A reset in tone from the often adversarial audit experience
- Introduction of new arguments or case law
- Application of broader judgment, including fairness and administrative efficiency


Note, however, that if the taxpayer introduces new facts that were not disclosed during the audit, the Appeals Officer may return the case to IRS Examination to reopen the audit.


## Collection Due Process & Rapid Appeals


### What Are the Options for Accelerated Dispute Resolution?


Not all tax disputes follow a linear path from examination to appeals to litigation. In some cases, especially when collection actions are imminent or when time is of the essence, alternative procedures offer taxpayers faster and more flexible resolution paths. Two key options are the Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing and the Rapid Appeals Process (RAP).


### Collection Due Process (CDP) Hearings


When the IRS initiates collection actions, such as a[federal tax](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/federal-tax-research) lien or levy, the taxpayer has a statutory right to request a Collection Due Process hearing before the action is finalized. This request is made by filing Form 12153.


#### Purpose of a CDP Hearing


- To challenge the appropriateness of the IRS’s collection action
- To propose alternative arrangements, such as:


- Installment agreements
- Offers in Compromise
- Currently Not Collectible status


- To raise issues such as innocent spouse relief, collection statute expiration, or prior bankruptcy discharges


A CDP hearing pauses enforcement and gives the taxpayer a chance to negotiate or resolve issues before assets are seized. The hearing is conducted by a Settlement Officer from the IRS Office of Appeals, reinforcing the importance of this office in multiple stages of the controversy process.


If the taxpayer disagrees with the outcome of the CDP hearing, they can petition the U.S. Tax Court, making it a powerful tool for both resolution and legal protection.


### The Rapid Appeals Process (RAP)


The Rapid Appeals Process (RAP) is a pilot program developed to expedite the resolution of large-dollar disputes. It allows taxpayers to enter into early-stage mediation with the IRS Office of Appeals and IRS Examination to improve efficiency and timeliness of Appeals resolution..


#### Key Attributes of RAP


- Initiated through an agreement between the taxpayer and the IRS
- Involves pre-conference meetings between IRS Examination, Appeals, and the taxpayer
- Focuses on identifying core issues and accelerating resolution before formal escalation


RAP is particularly valuable in high-stakes corporate cases where early clarity on exposure can influence broader financial or legal strategy.


### Choosing the Right Path


Whether through CDP or RAP, these procedures give practitioners additional tools to manage tax disputes outside the traditional path. Both procedures:


- Preserve taxpayer rights
- Create opportunities for resolution before litigation
- Require strategic preparation, including the presentation of facts, law, and procedural compliance


## Tax Litigation


### How Does a Tax Dispute Go to Court?


If a case remains unresolved after the IRS Appeals process, the final recourse is tax litigation. This phase begins when the IRS issues a Statutory Notice of Deficiency, commonly known as the 90-day letter.


### The Gateway to Litigation: The 90-Day Letter


The Statutory Notice of Deficiency is the IRS’s legal prerequisite to tax litigation. It is issued when:


- The taxpayer has not agreed to the adjustments proposed during the audit or appeals process.
- The IRS intends to assess and collect the additional tax despite the disagreement.


#### Key Attributes of the 90-Day Letter


- **Function:** Grants the taxpayer the right to petition the U.S. Tax Court without paying the tax first
- **Critical deadline:** The taxpayer has 90 days from the date of the letter to file a petition. If they fail to do so, the IRS may assess the tax, and the right to Tax Court is forfeited.


Missing the 90-day deadline usually results in the tax becoming immediately due and payable, even if the taxpayer still disagrees with it.


### Choice of Forum: U.S. Tax Court and Other Federal Courts


Once the 90-day letter is issued, taxpayers must decide where to litigate. The choice of forum is a significant strategic decision that depends on several factors, including the nature of the dispute, the need to pay the tax upfront, and desired legal precedent.


#### Options:


1. **U.S. Tax Court**


- Most common venue for tax litigation
- No payment required before filing
- Judges are tax law experts
- Generally more accessible for individual and small business taxpayers
- Petition must be filed within 90 days of the notice of deficiency


2. **U.S. District Court**


- Requires full payment of the disputed tax
- Taxpayer must first file a claim for refund
- More formal, with broader procedural rules
- Often chosen when a jury trial is desired


3. **U.S. Court of Federal Claims**


- Also requires prepayment and refund claim
- Often used in large-dollar cases or complex technical disputes
- No jury trial, but specialized jurisdiction over monetary claims against the government


Each forum carries different implications for legal standards, cost, timeline, and litigation strategy. Practitioners must assess the forum best suited to their client’s financial position and legal arguments.


### Small Tax Case Procedure (IRC § 7463)


For lower-dollar disputes, the Small Tax Case Procedure under IRC § 7463 offers a streamlined alternative within the U.S. Tax Court.


#### Features of § 7463 Cases:


- Applies to disputes involving $50,000 or less per tax year
- Less formal procedures and expedited hearings
- Typically heard by a single judge with no written opinion published
- No right to appeal the decision


While this procedure sacrifices some legal formality and precedent-setting value, it offers a cost-effective and faster route to resolution for qualifying taxpayers.
