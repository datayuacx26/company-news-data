# California State Tax Guide

California is widely regarded as the most complex state tax jurisdiction in the country and for good reason. Unlike most states, California operates on its own conformity timeline, layers entity-level taxes on top of federal pass-through regimes, applies aggressive sourcing and residency standards, and enforces compliance through one of the most active state tax agencies in the nation. For tax professionals, even small missteps can quickly turn into notices, penalties, or audits.

## California Tax Overview: What Every Practitioner Must Know


California’s personal income tax and business income and franchise tax system is administered by the Franchise Tax Board (FTB). The FTB is responsible for return processing, payment application, notices, audits, and enforcement across entity types. This includes administration of the $800 minimum franchise tax, LLC annual tax and fee, estimated payments, penalties, and interest.


For practitioners, this distinction is important because income tax, franchise tax, and many entity-level compliance obligations funnel through the FTB, not the CDTFA or IRS. When a California issue involves income characterization, entity classification, or owner-level reporting, the FTB is almost always the governing authority.


### California’s Conformity Framework: Why Federal Law Is Only the Starting Point


California does not follow rolling conformity to the Internal Revenue Code. Instead, it operates under a specified date conformity model. This means California conforms to the IRC as of a fixed date, and only to the extent the Legislature explicitly adopts federal changes.


Historically, that specified date lagged significantly behind federal law. For many years, For many years, California’s conformity date was January 1, 2015, accompanied by extensive lists of continuing differences. Practitioners were required to reconcile modern federal provisions against an outdated California baseline.


[Senate Bill (SB) 711](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/about-ftb/newsroom/tax-news/index.html#article2) fundamentally shifted that baseline by advancing California’s general conformity date to January 1, 2025, effective for both personal income tax and corporation tax purposes. However, this change does not convert California into a rolling-conformity state. Conformity applies only subject to enumerated exceptions and modifications, many of which remain high-impact.


### SB 711 Conformity Update: What Changed and What Did Not


SB 711 updated California’s conformity framework in three meaningful ways while leaving the state’s selective approach intact.


#### What changed


- **Conformity date advancement.** California now generally conforms to the IRC as of January 1, 2025, replacing the long-standing January 1, 2015 reference point.
- **Business e-file mandate alignment.** California now aligns its e-file requirements with federal thresholds under IRC section 6011(e) and (h), including the federal 10-return filing threshold.
- **Research credit modernization.** California repealed its Alternative Incremental Credit and adopted the federal Alternative Simplified Credit framework with California-specific rates and election rules.


#### What did not change


- California did **not** adopt rolling conformity.
- Existing California-specific adjustments, suspensions, exclusions, and industry-specific nonconformities remain in place unless explicitly repealed or modified.
- Federal relief provisions and disaster postponements still require separate California authorization.


FTB emphasized in its December 2025 Tax News that conformity applies only “with specified exceptions and modifications,” reinforcing that practitioners must continue to track California-specific rules annually.

### High-Impact California Nonconformity and State-Specific Rules


Even after SB 711, California maintains numerous departures from federal law that directly affect engagement scoping and review.


California currently suspends net operating loss deductions for tax years beginning in 2024 through 2026, subject to income-based exceptions and disaster loss rules. Suspended years extend carryover periods, creating long-tail tracking issues.


A[$5 million cap on total business credits](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2024/2024-541-booklet.html) applies during the same period, with limited exceptions. Credits disallowed due to the cap may be carried forward, and refundable credit elections follow California-specific mechanics.


California continues to diverge sharply in the oil and gas sector, disallowing deductions for intangible drilling costs, repealing percentage depletion for certain resources, and eliminating the enhanced oil recovery credit except for carryovers.


The CA PTE elective tax allows eligible passthroughs to pay an elective 9.3 percent entity-level tax, generating an owner-level credit (with detailed payment/election timing rules)..


Disaster and wildfire relief further illustrate California’s selective approach. Federal disaster relief does not automatically apply. The Director of Finance determines when federal postponements are recognized for California, and taxpayers may need to request relief through California-specific procedures.


California also applies its own grant exclusions, residency standards, and sourcing rules, which frequently produce results that differ from federal outcomes even when the underlying income is the same.


### Business E-File Mandate: What Changed for 2025 Returns


Beginning with taxable years starting on or after January 1, 2025, California aligns its e-file mandate with federal filing thresholds.


Corporations must e-file if federal rules require them to file at least 10 returns during the applicable calendar year. Partnerships must e-file if they meet the same federal threshold or if they have more than 100 partners during the tax year. Organizations filing unrelated business income returns are also subject to mandatory e-filing.


Waivers remain available on an annual basis for technology limitations, undue financial burden, or reasonable cause. However, the alignment with federal thresholds significantly expands the number of California filers subject to mandatory electronic filing.


## California Business Tax Filing Deadlines & Requirements


California business tax compliance is deadline-driven. While the filing calendar appears familiar to federal practitioners, California’s payment timing, entity-specific rules, and franchise tax mechanics routinely create exposure when assumed to “mirror” federal practice. Let’s discuss the core filing, payment, and e-file requirements practitioners must apply year after year.


### Entity-by-Entity Filing Calendar


California filing deadlines are tied to entity type and taxable year. While extensions are generally automatic, original due dates still control payment obligations.

Partnership Form 565 March 15 October 15


LLC taxed as partnership Form 568 March 15 October 15

For fiscal-year filers, due dates fall on the 15th day of the applicable month after year-end. If a deadline falls on a weekend or legal holiday, filing and payment are timely on the next business day.


### Extension Procedures and Payment Requirements


California grants an automatic extension of time to file for most business entities. No separate extension form is required. However, this extension applies only to the return filing, not to tax payments.


- S corporations must pay any tax due by the 15th day of the third month after year-end.
- C corporations must pay any tax due by the 15th day of the fourth month after year-end.
- Partnerships and LLCs may still owe California entity-level taxes, annual taxes, fees, or withholding by statutory deadlines, even if the return itself is extended.


**Common Pitfall: Extension ≠ Payment Extension** Filing late with an extension is generally acceptable. Paying late is not. Interest accrues from the original due date regardless of extension status.


### Estimated Tax Payment Schedule


Corporations and certain exempt organizations subject to California estimated tax requirements must follow a quarterly payment schedule that differs from federal installment percentages.


Estimated payments are due on the:


- 15th day of the 4th month of the tax year
- 15th day of the 6th month
- 15th day of the 9th month
- 15th day of the 12th month


Payments are made using Form 100-ES, and underpayment penalties apply if required installments are missed or insufficient.


### Minimum Franchise Tax and First-Year Exceptions


California imposes a $800 minimum franchise tax on most corporations and certain pass-through entities doing business in, or organized or registered in, California.


- Corporations are generally subject to the $800 minimum tax, but newly formed or newly qualified corporations are exempt in their first taxable year.
- LLCs owe an $800 annual tax each year they are registered or doing business in California. This tax is due by the 15th day of the fourth month of the LLC’s tax year, **subject to the first-year exemption and other limited statutory exceptions** .


For LLCs, this annual tax is separate from the LLC fee, which is based on total California income and must be estimated and paid by the 15th day of the sixth month of the tax year. Underpayment of the LLC fee can trigger a 10 percent penalty.


### E-File Mandates and Waiver Requests


California requires electronic filing for business entity returns prepared using tax preparation software, including original and amended returns, unless a waiver is granted.


A waiver may be requested if the entity cannot reasonably comply due to:


- Technology limitations
- Undue financial burden
- Other reasonable-cause circumstances


Waivers must be requested through the FTB’s established process and are evaluated annually. Absent an approved waiver, paper-filed returns prepared with software may be treated as noncompliant.


#### Practical Payment Reminders


To avoid unnecessary notices and penalties:


- Pay S corporation and C corporation taxes by the original due date, even if filing is extended.
- Pay the LLC $800 annual tax using FTB 3522 by the 15th day of the fourth month.
- Estimate and pay the LLC fee using FTB 3536 by the 15th day of the sixth month.
- Make corporate estimated payments on the quarterly schedule using Form 100-ES.


The FTB strongly encourages electronic payments through Web Pay, though paper vouchers remain available where required.


## California Tax Treatment by Entity Type


California’s tax system applies different filing, payment, and sourcing rules depending on how a business is structured. While federal entity classifications provide a starting point, California overlays entity-level taxes, fees, and sourcing concepts that materially change outcomes. Practitioners must evaluate entity type early in every engagement to avoid misapplied rates, missed payments, or incorrect owner reporting.


### Sole Proprietorships


California taxes sole proprietors at the individual level, but the computation diverges quickly from the federal return.


California begins with federal adjusted gross income and requires state-specific additions and subtractions on Schedule CA. Residents report adjustments on Schedule CA attached to Form 540, while nonresidents and part-year residents apply California modifications on Schedule CA (540NR) as part of Form 540NR.


For nonresidents, only California-source income is taxable. California does not default to federal sourcing rules. Instead, income is sourced under California law, which can differ for services, independent contractors, and business activities. Residency status and sourcing determinations drive whether income appears on Schedule CA (540NR) and how much is subject to California tax.


Sole proprietors are also subject to California estimated tax requirements. Individuals generally make four estimated payments during the year, and underpayment penalties may apply if withholding and estimates do not cover the California liability. Practitioners should coordinate federal and California estimates but calculate each independently.


### Partnerships and LLCs Taxed as Partnerships


California filing forms depend on entity type and whether the entity is registered in California or considered to be doing business in the state. Partnerships generally file Form 565, while LLCs classified as partnerships often file Form 568 when organized/registered in California or doing business in the state.


#### LLC Annual Fee and Total Income Tiers


In addition to the $800 annual LLC tax, California imposes an LLC fee based on the LLC’s total income attributable to California (as defined by California law).. The fee is due during the tax year and must be estimated and paid separately.

#### Owner Reporting and Apportionment


Income, deductions, and credits flow to owners through Schedule K-1 (568 or 565). For many entities with multistate operations, Schedule R is used to apportion business income to California. The apportionment outcome affects not only owner reporting but also the LLC fee base, making accurate sourcing critical.


### S Corporations


S corporations doing business in California or earning California-source income must file Form 100S, regardless of federal pass-through status.


California imposes an entity-level tax equal to 1.5 percent of net income apportioned to California, subject to a minimum franchise tax of $800. This tax applies even when the corporation has no federal taxable income.


California also conforms to the built-in gains tax regime for S corporations that were formerly C corporations. Built-in gains recognized during California’s 10-year recognition period are taxed at the corporate rate of 8.84 percent (10.84 percent for financial S corporations) at the entity level, with apportionment applied where appropriate.


At the owner level, shareholders report California-source flow-through items on their personal returns. While the federal and California S corporation models are conceptually similar, California’s entity-level tax and minimum franchise tax create a materially different compliance profile.


### C Corporations


C corporations file Form 100 and are taxed at the 8.84 percent corporate rate on income apportioned to California. Financial corporations are subject to a higher 10.84 percent rate.


California imposes a minimum franchise tax of $800 on corporations that are incorporated, registered, or doing business in the state, subject to limited first-year exceptions. The minimum applies regardless of profitability.


For multistate and multinational groups, California requires combined reporting and allows an election between:


- Worldwide combined reporting, or
- Water’s-edge reporting, which generally excludes most foreign affiliates.


The water’s-edge election is made with a timely original return and typically locks the taxpayer into the election for an 84-month period, subject to limited termination and re-election rules. This election can materially affect apportionment, income inclusion, and audit exposure.

## California Sales and Use Tax: What Income Tax Practitioners Must Spot


### When a California Seller’s Permit Is Required


A business must hold a California seller’s permit if it is[“engaged” in business in California](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/formspubs/pub73.pdf) and sells or leases tangible personal property ordinarily subject to sales tax.


Being “engaged in business” is broader than having a storefront. Common triggers include maintaining or using a California office, warehouse, sales room, or other place of business, having representatives or agents in California who make sales or installations, owning or leasing California real or tangible personal property, or deriving rental income from tangible personal property located in the state.


Out-of-state retailers may also be considered engaged in business through economic nexus, even without physical presence. Registration is completed through CDTFA’s online system and does not carry a registration fee.


### Physical Nexus Indicators Income Tax Professionals Should Flag


Physical nexus exists when a business has a tangible or operational footprint in California. Red flags include inventory stored in California, including at third-party fulfillment centers, California-based employees or agents who support sales or delivery activities, a California office or warehouse, or business equipment such as servers or leased assets located in the state.


Any of these facts typically trigger seller’s permit and collection obligations.


### Economic Nexus for Remote Sellers


Since April 1, 2019, a[remote retailer is engaged in business in California](https://cdtfa.ca.gov/lawguides/vol1/sutr/1684.html) if combined sales of tangible personal property delivered into California exceed $500,000 in the current or preceding calendar year. Sales by related entities are included in this threshold.


Remote sellers meeting this standard must register with CDTFA and collect California use tax, even if they have no physical presence in the state.


### Use Tax Exposure: Where Issues Are Commonly Missed


Use tax applies to the storage, use, or consumption in California of taxable goods purchased from out-of-state sellers when California sales tax was not paid. This frequently arises with equipment, furniture, fixtures, and supplies purchased online or from non-California vendors and later brought into the state.


Businesses engaged in business in California are required to collect use tax from customers on taxable sales. Separately, businesses must self-assess use tax on their own out-of-state purchases when the seller did not collect California tax. Reviewing capital expenditures and supply purchases is a critical income-tax-level control.


### Why FTB Income Tax Audits Uncover Sales and Use Tax Issues


FTB audits routinely evaluate whether a business is doing business in California using sales, property, and payroll thresholds. These same facts often indicate sales or use tax obligations under CDTFA rules.


When an FTB audit establishes California nexus or significant California sales activity, it frequently exposes missed seller’s permit registrations, unfiled returns, or unaccrued use tax. At that point, the issue escalates beyond income tax and into multi-agency exposure involving the CDTFA (and potentially local tax authorities).


### Marketplace Facilitators: Who Collects and When


When a business sells exclusively through a marketplace facilitator that is registered or required to be registered with CDTFA, the facilitator is treated as the retailer and is responsible for collecting and reporting tax on those marketplace sales, even though marketplace activity itself may create nexus..


However, if the seller also makes direct sales through its own website, in-person channels, or other non-marketplace platforms, the seller must register and report tax on those transactions. Marketplace collection does not cover non-facilitated sales, and CDTFA reporting schedules are designed to separate the two.


### Income Tax Crossover Checks Practitioners Should Perform


Income tax engagements provide multiple opportunities to surface sales and use tax exposure:


- Confirm CDTFA registration for clients with California property, employees, or more than $500,000 in California-destined sales.
- Reconcile gross receipts on Schedule C or entity returns to taxable sales reported to CDTFA, ensuring that sales tax reimbursement collected from customers has been properly remitted.
- Tie marketplace settlement statements and Forms 1099-K to income reporting and verify that marketplace-collected tax is not double-reported by the seller.
- Review general ledger accounts for out-of-state purchases used in California and confirm use tax accruals where applicable.
- Identify entities treated as doing business in California for income tax purposes but showing no CDTFA activity, a common indicator of missed obligations.


For pass-through entities, sales and use tax liabilities can also affect K-1 income, particularly when unaccrued tax becomes an expense or audit adjustment at the entity level.


### Red Flags That Warrant Immediate Follow-Up


- California inventory, equipment, servers, or payroll with no seller’s permit on file
- Remote sellers exceeding the $500,000 California sales threshold without registration
- Marketplace sellers making direct sales but not filing CDTFA returns
- Material out-of-state purchases with no use tax accruals
- FTB nexus findings with no corresponding CDTFA compliance history

## California PTE Elective Tax: Complete Guide to the SALT Workaround


California’s Pass-Through Entity (PTE) elective tax is one of the most technically demanding areas of state compliance and one of the most frequently misunderstood. Properly implemented, it can materially reduce the federal SALT cap impact for owners. Mishandled, it can invalidate the election or permanently reduce the available credit.


### Eligibility and Qualified Taxpayers


#### Qualifying Entities


Only certain pass-through entities may elect California’s PTE tax:


- Partnerships
- LLCs taxed as partnerships
- S corporations


The entity must not be a publicly traded partnership within the meaning of IRC § 7704 and must not be permitted or required to file as part of a combined reporting group.


General partnerships may qualify if they otherwise meet the statutory requirements.


#### Qualified Taxpayers


Not every owner of an electing entity is eligible to receive the PTE credit. A qualified taxpayer is limited to:


- Individuals
- Fiduciaries
- Estates
- Trusts


Each qualified taxpayer must consent to have their distributive or pro-rata share included in qualified net income.


Corporations and tax-exempt entities are not qualified taxpayers and cannot receive the credit. Disregarded entities generally do not qualify, with a narrow exception for a single-member LLC owned by an individual, fiduciary, estate, or trust.


#### Disqualified Entities


An entity is disqualified from making the election if it:


- Is a publicly traded partnership within the meaning of IRC § 7704, or
- Is permitted or required to be included in a combined reporting group


Having a partnership or disregarded entity as an owner does not automatically disqualify the electing entity, but it may limit which owners are eligible to receive the credit.


### Election Mechanics and Deadlines


The PTE election is procedural and unforgiving.


The election must be made on the original, timely filed return. Amended returns cannot be used to make the election unless the amended return qualifies as a superseding return filed by the original or extended due date. Once made, the election is irrevocable for that tax year.


#### Payment Timing


For an election to be effective, payment requirements must be met:


- June 15 prepayment is required during the tax year.
- The remaining balance is due by the original return due date, without regard to extensions.


Payments are made using Web Pay or FTB Form 3893 and must be kept separate from other entity-level tax payments.


#### SB 132 Extension


SB 132 extended California’s PTE elective tax for tax years beginning on or after 2026 and before 2031. The extension preserved the structure of the election but introduced a credit reduction mechanism if the June 15 payment is missed.


### Calculating Qualified Net Income (QNI)


Qualified net income is the foundation of the PTE election and must be computed carefully.


QNI equals the sum of the distributive or pro-rata share of income and guaranteed payments attributable only to consenting qualified taxpayers and subject to California personal income tax.


Owners with net negative income are excluded from QNI. Non-consenting owners are also excluded entirely and receive no credit, even if the entity otherwise qualifies and elects the tax.


Each qualified taxpayer’s consent must be obtained and documented before inclusion in QNI. An entity may still proceed with the election even if some owners do not consent, but only consenting owners are counted.


#### QNI Calculation Example


An LLC has three individual members with California-source income:


- Member A (consents): $200,000
- Member B (consents): $100,000
- Member C (does not consent): $150,000


Qualified Net Income = $300,000
PTE elective tax at 9.3% = $27,900

### Credit Computation and Ordering


The entity pays PTE elective tax at 9.3 percent of QNI. Each consenting qualified taxpayer receives a nonrefundable credit equal to their pro-rata share of the PTE tax paid on their behalf.


Excess credit may be carried forward for up to five years.


#### Credit Ordering Rules


The PTE credit interacts with other California credits and must be applied in the correct sequence:


- The Other State Tax Credit (OSTC) is applied before the PTE elective tax credit.
- The PTE credit may reduce tax below tentative minimum tax, subject to Schedule P ordering rules.
- Improper ordering can distort OSTC calculations and trigger notices.


#### SB 132 Credit Reduction Rule


For tax years beginning 2026 through 2030, failure to make or fully fund the June 15 prepayment does **not** invalidate the election. Instead, the credit is reduced.


Warning: Missed June 15 Payment = Credit Reduction
If the June 15 prepayment is missed or underpaid, each qualified taxpayer’s credit is reduced by 12.5 percent of their pro-rata share of the unpaid June 15 amount. The election remains valid, but part of the credit is permanently forfeited.


### Step-by-Step PTE Election Checklist


1. Confirm entity eligibility and confirm no combined reporting disqualification.
2. Identify all owners and determine which qualify for the credit.
3. Obtain written consent from each qualified taxpayer.
4. Compute qualified net income using consenting owners only.
5. Make the June 15 prepayment using FTB Form 3893.
6. Elect the PTE tax on the original, timely filed return.
7. Pay the remaining balance by the original due date.
8. Issue Forms 3804 and prepare Forms 3804-CR for each qualified taxpayer.

## California Apportionment Rules for Multistate Businesses


California apportionment is one of the most litigated and audit-intensive areas of state taxation. For multistate businesses, the correct sourcing and apportionment methodology often determines whether California captures a meaningful share of income or none at all.


Let’s talk about California’s default framework, when special rules apply, and how to evaluate whether alternative apportionment relief under CCR section 25137 is appropriate.


### Core Apportionment Framework


#### Single-Sales Factor Formula


California uses a single-sales factor apportionment formula for most apportioning trades or businesses, subject to industry-specific exceptions.. Property and payroll factors are excluded. This has been California’s default approach since tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2013.


Under this framework, California’s share of business income is determined solely by the ratio of California sales to total sales. For tangible personal property, California applies the Finnigan rule, meaning sales shipped from California may be excluded from the California numerator if another member of the combined reporting group is taxable in the destination state.


Certain industries do not use the single-sales factor and instead follow alternative statutory or regulatory formulas, discussed below.


### Market-Based Sourcing for Services and Intangibles


California assigns sales of services and intangibles using market-based sourcing, not cost-of-performance.


- Services are sourced to California to the extent the customer receives the benefit of the service in California.
- Intangible property is sourced to California to the extent the intangible is used in California. For marketable securities, sourcing generally follows the customer’s location.


The detailed rules governing market-based sourcing are set out in CCR section 25136-2, which establishes cascading assignment methods and reasonable-approximation standards when precise customer-location data is unavailable. These rules are frequently scrutinized on audit, particularly where revenue streams involve asset management fees, government contracts, or multi-location customers.


### Special Industry Apportionment Rules


California applies special apportionment formulas when the standard rules do not fairly represent activity for certain industries.


#### Financial Institutions and Qualified Activities


If more than 50 percent of a combined group’s gross business receipts are attributable to banking, financial business, savings and loan activities, or certain extractive or agricultural operations, the group uses a three-factor formula consisting of property, payroll, and sales. The 50 percent test is applied at the group level.


This exception is explicitly reflected in Schedule R instructions and overrides the default single-sales factor rule.


#### Broadcasters


Certain broadcasters and cable system operators apportion income under industry-specific provisions incorporated into Schedule R and the Revenue and Taxation Code. These rules modify how sales are assigned and can materially change the California numerator.


#### Airlines and Other Transportation Companies


Airlines and other transportation companies are subject to special formulas under CCR section 25137 and related guidance. The FTB’s Multistate Audit Technique Manual includes industry-specific chapters covering air transportation, trucking, railroads, and sea transportation. These rules often replace market-based sourcing with mileage, revenue ton-miles, or other industry measures.


When a client operates in a specialized industry, practitioners should confirm whether a specific regulation or statute displaces the general apportionment rules.


### Unitary Business Principle and Combined Reporting


California applies the unitary business principle to determine whether income must be combined and apportioned on a group basis.


A business is unitary when there is functional integration, centralized management, and economies of scale, often evaluated through the “three unities” test or contribution and dependency analysis. If unitary, California requires combined reporting, with intercompany transactions eliminated and apportionment applied to the combined business income.


Once unity is established, apportionment is applied at the group level rather than entity by entity.


### Partnership Apportionment: Unitary vs. Non-Unitary Treatment


California treats partnership income differently depending on whether the partnership and the owner are unitary.


- **Unitary relationship:** The partner’s distributive share of partnership business income and the partner’s pro-rata share of partnership apportionment factors are combined with the partner’s own factors, producing a single apportionment percentage.


- **Non-unitary relationship:** The partnership apportions its income at the partnership level, and the partner reports its distributive share of income already apportioned to California, with no roll-up of factors.


These distinctions are governed by CCR section 25137-1 and are a common source of audit adjustments when partnership investments span multiple states.


### CCR Section 25137: Alternative Apportionment Relief


If California’s standard allocation and apportionment provisions do not fairly represent the extent of a taxpayer’s business activity in the state, CCR section 25137 allows either the taxpayer to petition, or the FTB to require, an alternative method where the standard formula does not fairly represent in-state activity. Relief is granted sparingly and requires a clear showing of distortion.


Alternative methods may include separate accounting, exclusion or inclusion of specific factors, or another reasonable approach designed to achieve equitable apportionment.


Section 25137 is limited strictly to apportionment and allocation. It cannot be used to challenge unitary determinations, water’s-edge mechanics, tax base issues, or credit limitations.


When a special industry regulation applies, deviation requires clear and convincing evidence that even the prescribed method produces distortion.


### When to File a CCR 25137 Petition: Decision Checklist


Use this screening tool before pursuing alternative apportionment:


- Is the issue limited to allocation or apportionment, not unity, credits, or tax base?
- Are the standard single-sales factor or a special industry rule producing results that materially overstate or understate California activity?
- Do extraordinary receipts, such as treasury or investment income, dominate the sales factor relative to operating activity?
- Are timing mismatches causing income recognition to misalign with factor inclusion?
- Does the formula generate nowhere income not contemplated by the regulations?
- Are market-based sourcing rules yielding clearly unreasonable results despite good-faith application of the cascading methods?
- Can a specific, supportable alternative method be articulated and substantiated?


If the answer to several of these questions is yes, a section 25137 petition is generally appropriate. The burden rests with the petitioner to demonstrate distortion and propose a reasonable alternative.

## California Residency and Nonresident Sourcing Rules


Residency is one of the most audited and most consequential determinations in California taxation. Before sourcing income, selecting a return, or assessing withholding and estimated tax obligations, the FTB first answers one question: is the taxpayer a California resident?


Let’s discuss how California makes that determination and how residency status drives what income is taxable, how it is sourced, and which compliance obligations follow.


### Residency Classifications Under California Law


California recognizes three[residency statuses](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2024/2024-1031-publication.pdf) , each with different tax consequences.


- **Resident** A resident is taxed on all income from all sources, regardless of where it is earned.
- **Nonresident** A nonresident is taxed only on California-source income.
- **Part-year resident** A part-year resident is taxed on worldwide income during the resident portion of the year and only California-source income during the nonresident portion, based on facts and circumstances..


These classifications apply to individuals, not entities, but they directly affect owner-level taxation for pass-through income.


### The “Temporary or Transitory” Standard


California does not rely solely on domicile or physical presence. Instead, residency hinges on whether an individual’s presence in or absence from California is[temporary or transitory](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/misc/1100.html) .


The FTB evaluates intent supported by objective facts, not stated plans alone. Relevant factors include:


- Location of the individual’s principal residence
- Where family members live
- Location of employment or business activity
- Time spent inside versus outside California
- Where vehicles, voter registration, professional licenses, financial accounts, and other indicia of economic life are maintained


No single factor is determinative. A taxpayer can be physically absent from California yet remain a resident if the absence is considered temporary. Conversely, extended physical presence outside California does not automatically terminate residency.


### Why Physical Presence Alone Is Not Determinative


Many taxpayers assume that spending fewer than 183 days in California resolves residency. California law does not adopt a bright-line day-count test. Short stays tied to business, property ownership, or ongoing ties can sustain residency, while extended absences may not sever it.


This fact-intensive analysis explains why residency audits are common and often retrospective.


### California-Source Income Rules


Once residency status is established, sourcing rules determine what income is taxable.


#### Wages and Compensation


Wages are sourced using a workdays method. Compensation is California-source to the extent services are performed in California. For remote employees, this requires allocating wages based on California workdays versus total workdays, not employer location or payroll reporting alone.


#### Business Income


For pass-through entities, California-source business income depends on entity-level sourcing and apportionment, then flows to owners based on residency status. Nonresident owners are taxed only on the California-apportioned share, while residents report their full distributive share.


For sole proprietors, income is sourced based on where the business activity occurs under California law, not federal sourcing rules.


#### Equity Compensation


Equity compensation is an audit hotspot. Stock options, RSUs, and similar awards are subject to special California sourcing rules, often tied to vesting periods and service locations rather than grant or exercise dates. Nonresidents with California service periods frequently underestimate this exposure.


### Why Residency Drives Everything


Residency status determines:


- **Which return is filed** : Form 540 for residents, Form 540NR for nonresidents and part-year residents
- **What income is taxable** : worldwide versus California-source only
- **Withholding obligations** : including pass-through and wage withholding
- **Estimated tax exposure** : residents often face higher estimated tax requirements
- **Audit risk** : residency disputes routinely trigger multi-year examinations


An incorrect residency determination can cascade into missed withholding, underreported income, and compounded penalties.


#### How Residency Affects Compliance


**Residency determination → income sourcing → return selection → withholding and estimates**


Residency must be analyzed first. Sourcing and filing decisions follow from that conclusion, not the other way around.


### Top FTB Residency Audit Red Flags


- Claiming nonresidency while retaining a California home available for use
- Significant California-source business income with minimal reported California presence
- Remote work arrangements without wage allocation support
- Equity compensation tied to California service periods omitted from California income
- Inconsistent residency positions across income tax, withholding, and information returns


## California Individual Tax: Critical Issues for Practitioners


California individual income tax compliance continues to diverge from federal rules in ways that materially affect return preparation, refund opportunities, and penalty exposure. The issues below are among the most frequently missed in practice and are commonly raised in FTB correspondence and audits.


### Employee Retention Credit: California Nonconformity


California does not conform to the federal Employee Retention Credit. There is no California ERC and no requirement to mirror the federal wage disallowance or income inclusion mechanics.


For California income tax purposes:


- Employers are not required to reduce wage deductions for ERC amounts.
- ERC proceeds are not included in California gross income, including both the portion that offsets employment taxes and the refundable portion.


This disconnect often results in incorrect Schedule CA adjustments when federal treatment is carried through without modification. Practitioners should confirm that ERC-related federal adjustments are fully reversed for California.


### One-Time Timeliness Penalty Abatement (FTB 2918)


California provides a once-in-a-lifetime abatement of timeliness penalties for individuals, offering meaningful relief when reasonable cause is unavailable.


Key requirements include:


- Available for taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 2022
- Applies only to individuals
- The taxpayer must not have previously received a one-time abatement
- All required returns must be filed
- All taxes, penalties, and interest must be paid in full or the taxpayer must be current on an installment agreement
- The penalty must be a failure-to-file or failure-to-pay penalty


Requests are made using FTB Form 2918 or by contacting the FTB directly. If the penalty has already been paid, the form also functions as a claim for refund, subject to standard statutes of limitation.


This relief is elective and does not require reasonable cause. However, eligibility generally requires the balance to be paid in full or subject to a current installment agreement, and denials are typically not subject to administrative protest, making payment strategy an important planning consideration.


### Special California Income Exclusions


California continues to enact targeted exclusions that do not always align with federal treatment. These exclusions frequently support amended returns or refund claims.

### Group Nonresident Returns


California allows certain nonresidents to elect into a group nonresident return, filed by an authorized representative on behalf of participating individuals.


#### Requirements and Advantages


Eligibility and procedures are governed by FTB Publication 1067. Group returns can significantly reduce administrative burden by consolidating multiple nonresident filings into a single return. They are commonly used for investment partnerships and professional service firms with numerous nonresident owners.


Recent FTB guidance has streamlined identification and estimated tax requirements, improving usability for both practitioners and taxpayers.


### Nonresident Aliens in Group Filings


California has expanded relief for nonresident aliens participating in group nonresident returns.


- For taxable years 2021 through 2025, FTB has not required estimated tax payments from nonresident aliens included in a group return.
- Beginning with taxable years starting on or after January 1, 2026, permanent changes eliminate the estimated tax penalty for electing nonresident aliens, remove the SSN or ITIN requirement for group filings, and formally codify the group return election for nonresident aliens.


These changes reduce compliance friction and audit exposure for partnerships with international ownership.


## California Withholding Obligations for Nonresidents and Businesses


California’s withholding regime is one of the most common sources of notices, penalties, and delayed refunds. The rules operate independently from federal withholding and apply broadly to nonresident payments, pass-through income, real estate transactions, and backup withholding.


Practitioners who miss these requirements often discover the issue only after credits are denied or refunds are frozen.


### When California Withholding Applies


California generally requires withholding on California-source nonwage payments made to nonresidents once annual payments exceed $1,500. The default withholding rate is 7 percent, unless an exemption, waiver, or reduced rate applies.


This obligation falls on the withholding agent, not the payee, and applies regardless of whether federal withholding is required.


### Independent Contractors and Nonresident Service Providers


Payments to[nonresident independent contractors](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/pay/withholding/withholding-on-nonresidents.html) for services performed in California are subject to 7 percent withholding once California-source payments exceed $1,500 in the calendar year.


This applies to payments reported on Form 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC and is reported to the FTB using Form 592, with payment remitted using Form 592-V. Withholding agents must issue Form 592-B to the payee annually so the credit can be claimed.


Withholding may be reduced or waived if the payee submits the appropriate exemption or reduction forms, but absent approved documentation, full withholding is required.


### Pass-Through Entity Withholding on Nonresident Owners


Partnerships, LLCs, and S corporations must withhold 7% on California-source income allocated to nonresident owners.


The entity reports and remits withholding during the year and allocates it annually using Form 592-PTE. Each nonresident owner must receive Form 592-B to claim the credit on their return.


Upper-tier and lower-tier entities must[pass withholding through](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/pay/withholding/pass-through-entity-withholding.html) properly and on time. Failure to do so frequently prevents owners from claiming credits and triggers FTB mismatch notices.


### Real Estate Withholding and Nonresident Sellers


California requires withholding when title to California real property is transferred, regardless of the seller’s residency. Withholding is generally calculated as 3⅓ percent of the sales price, though an optional gain method may apply.


The transaction is reported using Form 593, with payment remitted via Form 593-V. Numerous exemptions exist, including principal residence sales, transactions with no gain, qualifying like-kind exchanges, and certain entity transfers. However, exemptions must be properly certified and documented.


California real estate withholding is separate from FIRPTA, which is a federal regime. Federal FIRPTA compliance does not satisfy California requirements.


### Backup Withholding: California Mechanics


When federal backup withholding applies, California generally requires parallel backup withholding on California-source income at a 7% rate.


Payers must report and remit backup withholding using Form 592 and issue Form 592-B to payees. To claim the credit, the payee must provide a valid taxpayer identification number to the FTB. If the TIN is missing or invalid, the credit may be denied or delayed until the issue is resolved, even if tax was withheld and paid.

### Claiming Withholding Credits on Form 540NR


Nonresident individuals claim California withholding credits by attaching the required payee statements to[Form 540NR](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/2024/2024-540nr-booklet.html) .


- Nonresident and backup withholding credits require Form 592-B.
- Real estate withholding credits require Form 593.
- Amounts must be entered on the correct lines as instructed in the Form 540NR booklet.


If the required forms are missing or amounts do not match FTB records, credits are delayed or denied. For backup withholding, credits are denied outright unless the payee has provided a valid TIN to the FTB before filing.


### Why Missing Filings Lead to Lost Refunds


Withholding credits are document-driven. Common causes of refund delays and denials include:


- Failure to attach Form 592-B or Form 593 to the return
- Invalid or missing TINs for backup withholding credits
- Claiming incorrect withholding amounts
- Pass-through entities failing to allocate or furnish withholding statements on time


When credits cannot be substantiated, the FTB adjusts the return, often issuing notices months later.


### Most Common Withholding Mistakes Seen by FTB


- Claiming withholding without attaching required payee statements
- Mixing wage withholding with nonresident or real estate withholding
- Backup withholding credits denied due to missing TINs
- Late or missing Forms 592-B issued by payers or entities
- Improper real estate exemption certifications or incorrect method selection


## California Business Tax Credits: What’s Current in 2025


California business tax credits continue to evolve independently of federal incentives. For 2025, the most significant change is the restructuring of the state research credit under SB 711.


Practitioners should also be aware of how legacy credits remain relevant through carryovers and which credits require advance application rather than return-based elections.


### Research Credit Overhaul Under SB 711


SB 711 significantly modified California’s research credit by eliminating the Alternative Incremental Credit and requiring taxpayers to transition to an ASC-style calculation beginning with taxable years starting on or after January 1, 2025.


#### Repeal of the Alternative Incremental Credit


California repealed the Alternative Incremental Credit (AIC) for 2025 and later tax years. Taxpayers who previously relied on AIC must affirmatively select a different method on a timely filed original return. Prior AIC elections do not automatically convert to another calculation method.


#### Adoption of the Alternative Simplified Credit


California adopted the Alternative Simplified Credit (ASC) framework, but with state-specific rates:


- 3% of qualified research expenses in most cases
- 1.3% if the taxpayer has no qualified research expenses in any of the three preceding tax years


While the methodology is conceptually similar federal ASC concepts, California’s rates are materially lower and must be computed separately.


#### Election Mechanics and Revocation Rules


The ASC is available only for taxable years beginning in 2025 and later. California did not adopt the federal automatic revocation provisions. Once elected, revoking the California ASC requires FTB consent, and the request must be made before filing the original timely return. This creates a higher procedural bar than under federal law and makes advance planning essential.
