# Investors Tax Guide 2025

### Wash Sale Rule: Applicable to Securities Not Yet Crypto


The IRS’s wash sale rule under IRC §1091 prohibits deducting a loss if the same or substantially identical security is repurchased within 30 days before or after the sale. This rule currently applies only to securities, not to digital assets such as cryptocurrencies or NFTs. That means investors can, for now:


- Sell a crypto asset at a loss
- Realize the tax deduction
- Immediately repurchase the same token


However, proposed regulations have repeatedly targeted this exemption, and there is bipartisan support to close this gap.


#### Best practice:


While this loophole remains available in 2025, document all transactions and prepare for the possibility that Congress may extend the wash sale rule to digital assets beginning in 2026.


### Crypto & Digital Assets: Major Reporting Shift Incoming


#### Draft Form 1099-DA Rules: Reporting Begins for 2025 Transactions


Under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, crypto brokers will be required to report digital asset transactions to the IRS. The IRS has released draft Form 1099-DA, and while final regulations are pending, the compliance timeline is clear:


- Transactions occurring in 2025 will trigger Form 1099-DA filings in 2026
- Cost basis reporting will become mandatory in 2027


These rules will apply to:


- Centralized crypto exchanges
- Custodial wallet providers
- Possibly some NFT and DeFi platforms, depending on how the final rules define "broker"


#### Investor action items:


- Reconcile past transactions (2022–2024) to avoid mismatches once automated IRS data matching begins
- Implement basis tracking tools—the IRS will begin matching your 8949 filings to broker-issued 1099-DAs


#### Enforcement note:


These forms will enable the IRS to systematize enforcement around crypto tax compliance, much like it already does for traditional brokerage accounts. Manual reporting gaps that went unnoticed in previous years will now be easily flagged.


## International & Alternative Assets


Investors holding foreign funds, offshore corporations, collectibles, or niche assets like art, wine, or digital tokens must navigate a thicket of specialized tax rules, many of which carry severe penalties for noncompliance and are tightening in 2025.


### Passive Foreign Investment Companies (PFICs)


Investors in foreign mutual funds, ETFs, offshore hedge funds, or certain crypto-adjacent structures may unknowingly hold PFICs (Passive Foreign Investment Companies), one of the most punitive areas of the tax code.


A PFIC is generally any foreign corporation that meets either:


- The income test: ≥75% of gross income is passive, or
- The asset test: ≥50% of assets produce passive income


Tax impact if not properly elected:


- Gains are treated as ordinary income, not capital gains
- Subject to interest charges on tax deferral going back to the original investment year
- No ability to harvest losses


### QEF and Mark-to-Market Elections


To avoid PFIC default treatment, investors must proactively file one of two elections:


1. Qualified Electing Fund (QEF):


- Requires annual reporting of your share of ordinary income and capital gains, regardless of distributions
- Only viable if the foreign fund cooperates and provides PFIC statements (rare in practice)


2. Mark-to-Market (MTM):


- Only available for marketable securities
- You report annual unrealized gains as ordinary income
- Can reduce back-year penalties but accelerates taxable income


If you invest in offshore funds through a U.S. feeder, confirm whether the master fund is a PFIC and whether the U.S. vehicle blocks PFIC exposure. Many investors discover PFIC status too late, after IRS Form 8621 is already required.


### Foreign Tax Credit (FTC)


The IRS finalized new foreign tax credit regulations that impact how U.S. investors claim credits for foreign taxes paid, particularly relevant for equity investors, dual-residents, and those earning royalties or dividends abroad.


Key change: The “net gain” requirement now demands that foreign tax regimes be reasonably similar to U.S. rules for credits to be allowed.


**Impact:**


- If a foreign jurisdiction taxes gross revenue rather than net income, the U.S. may deny the foreign tax credit.
- Many investors with foreign investment platforms or private equity funds may find prior FTC claims disallowed.
- Rules also scrutinize withholding taxes on dividends or interest if they aren’t imposed on a net-income basis.


**Planning move:** Review all FTC claims for 2023–2025. Reassess how your tax software or preparer classifies each jurisdiction and transaction type under the new rules. Improper FTC claims can result in audit adjustments and interest.


### Pillar Two & Global Minimum Tax


The OECD’s Pillar Two global minimum tax framework imposes a 15% effective minimum tax on large multinationals. But it’s starting to ripple through investment structures, especially those involving foreign-controlled corporations, holding companies, or cross-border SPVs.


#### Key concerns for investors:


- Top-up taxes may be triggered in countries that have adopted Pillar Two, affecting the after-tax yield of certain international investments.
- Private funds with foreign portfolio companies may see lower post-tax exit proceeds, even if U.S. tax impact is deferred.
- Reporting burdens may increase as fund managers push compliance obligations onto investors indirectly (via updated K-1s or side letters).


**Investor takeaway:** Review your exposure to non-U.S. corporate holdings or any structure involving foreign tax jurisdictions adopting Pillar Two. While individuals are not directly taxed under Pillar Two, they can bear the economic cost through reduced returns and greater fund compliance friction.


### Collectibles and Alternative Assets: The 28% Problem


Alternative assets such as art, antiques, coins, wine, and even physical NFTs are subject to collectibles taxation rules. Gains from the sale of these assets—if held more than a year—do not qualify for the standard 20% long-term capital gains rate.


Instead, they are taxed at a **maximum rate of 28%** , plus:


- 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) if applicable
- State-level capital gains or income tax, depending on jurisdiction


Asset categories taxed as collectibles under IRC §408(m):


- Artwork
- Antiques
- Gems and metals (excluding certain bullion)
- Stamps and coins (with exceptions)
- Alcoholic beverages
- Physical NFTs and certain tokenized real-world assets (gray area but increasingly scrutinized)


Consider pairing collectible gains with capital loss harvesting or using a charitable contribution strategy (e.g., donating appreciated art to a 501(c)(3)) to mitigate the high tax cost. Also, document holding periods and appraisals carefully, especially if preparing for a sale or estate inclusion.


## Real Estate & Passive-Loss Strategies


For investors in rental real estate, 2025 presents both challenges and planning windows, particularly around passive loss rules, accelerated depreciation, and the narrowing window on bonus depreciation.


### Real Estate Professional (REP) Status


Most rental activities are considered passive by default under §469, meaning losses can only offset passive income. However, qualifying as a Real Estate Professional (REP) allows active treatment of rental activities, making losses fully deductible against ordinary income.


To qualify for REP status:


- You must spend over 750 hours annually in real estate trades or businesses in which you materially participate.
- More than 50% of your total working time must be devoted to real estate activities (includes development, brokerage, leasing, operations, etc.).
- Time spent as an employee doesn’t count unless you own ≥5% of the employer.


**Planning tip:** Grouping elections (under Reg. §1.469-9(g)) can consolidate multiple rental activities for material participation purposes, helping borderline REP filers qualify.


### Bonus Depreciation: 40% in 2025 Then Gone


The sun is setting on one of real estate’s most powerful tax tools. Bonus depreciation, which allows immediate expensing of qualifying property, continues its phaseout:


- 2025: 40% first-year bonus depreciation (was 60% in 2024).
- 2026: Drops to 20%.
- 2027: Fully phased out unless extended by Congress.


This is especially relevant when paired with cost segregation studies, which break out shorter-life components (e.g., carpeting, lighting, landscaping) from a property’s structure. These short-life assets often qualify for bonus depreciation, allowing significant upfront deductions.


### 1031 Exchanges: Don’t Miss the Clock


§1031 like-kind exchanges remain a staple for deferring capital gains on real estate. However, the strict timing rules often trip up taxpayers:


- **45-Day Rule** : Identify replacement properties within 45 days of sale.
- **180-Day Rule** : Close on the replacement within 180 days of the sale.


These are calendar days, not business days, and no extensions are granted, even for weekends or holidays.


Also:


- Only investment or business-use real estate qualifies (no flips or primary residences).
- Exchanges must be property-for-property; cryptocurrency, partnership interests, or personal property do not qualify.


## Retirement & Tax-Deferred Accounts


Major policy changes from SECURE 2.0 and sunset-era tax strategy windows are reshaping how high earners and pre-retirees should think about retirement savings.


### SECURE 2.0


Several provisions from the SECURE 2.0 Act come into effect in 2025, with important implications for contribution strategies and catch-up planning:


- **Mandatory Roth Catch-Up for High Earners** : If your wages exceed $145,000 (indexed), all catch-up contributions to employer-sponsored plans (like 401(k)s) must be made on a Roth basis starting in 2025. This could increase current-year tax liabilities but lead to more tax-free income in retirement.
- **Increased Catch-Up Limits (Age 60–63)** : In 2025, workers aged 60 to 63 will benefit from enhanced catch-up contributions, up to the greater of $10,000 or 150% of the standard catch-up amount (indexed). Planning around this window can unlock last-minute deferrals before retirement.
- **Student Loan Matching** : Employers can start making matching contributions to retirement plans based on qualified student loan payments. This encourages retirement savings even for those focused on debt repayment.
- **Emergency Savings Linked to Roth Accounts** : Employees can contribute up to $2,500 into a designated Roth emergency account within employer plans, offering penalty-free access while staying under the retirement umbrella.


### Roth Conversions


2025 presents a limited window of opportunity for Roth conversions before the TCJA tax bracket sunsets in 2026, when marginal rates are set to rise across income tiers. Strategic considerations include:


- **Harvesting Low Brackets** : Converting traditional IRA or pre-tax 401(k) balances into Roth accounts at current lower rates can lock in tax-free growth. This is especially appealing for taxpayers currently in the 22%–24% brackets who expect to be in higher brackets post-2025.
- **State Tax Timing** : For taxpayers in states with variable tax regimes, coordinating Roth conversions in years with residency changes or anticipated lower income can amplify net savings.
- **Medicare & NIIT Planning** : Be mindful of income thresholds that trigger IRMAA surcharges or the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT). Partial conversions may optimize total tax exposure rather than maximizing the conversion amount.


## Estate & Gift Planning—Beat the 2026 Cliff


Time is running out on the historically high estate and gift tax exemption. Under current law, the lifetime exemption, estimated at $13.99 million per person in 2025—is scheduled to sunset after December 31, 2025, reverting to pre-TCJA levels (roughly $6–7 million per person). For high-net-worth individuals, that means millions in potential tax savings could vanish if action isn’t taken before the cliff.


### Key Strategies to Consider Before the Sunset


#### 1. Upfront Gifting vs. Portability


- Upfront gifting lets you lock in today’s high exemption before it disappears.


- Once used, the IRS won’t “claw back” gifts made under the elevated exemption, per final regulations (Treasury Decision 9884).
- Gifting removes appreciation from your estate, critical in high-growth asset cases.


- Portability, where a surviving spouse uses the deceased’s unused exemption, is helpful but:


- Doesn’t remove asset growth.
- Requires a timely-filed estate tax return even for non-taxable estates.
- Only applies to the federal exemption, not available in most states with estate taxes.


#### 2. SLATs (Spousal Lifetime Access Trusts)


- SLATs allow you to gift assets outside your estate while retaining indirect access through your spouse.
- Pros:


- Leverages current exemption.
- Maintains lifestyle flexibility.


- Considerations:


- Can’t name both spouses as beneficiaries of each other’s SLATs, watch for reciprocal trust doctrine risk.
- Divorce or premature death of the beneficiary spouse could cut off access.


#### 3. GRATs (Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts)


- GRATs are ideal for low-interest-rate environments or volatile assets.
- You retain an annuity stream, and excess appreciation passes to heirs gift-tax free.
- Benefit: No use of exemption if structured as a zeroed-out GRAT.
- Planning tip: Use rolling short-term GRATs to mitigate mortality risk.


#### 4. Strategic Use of Annual Exclusion Gifts


- For 2025, the annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient ($368,000 per couple).
- Consider using 529 plan frontloading, Irrevocable Life Insurance Trusts (ILITs), or Crummey trusts to maximize exclusions while keeping control.


### Planning Windows Are Narrowing


Even if Congress acts to extend or modify the exemption post-2025, there’s no guarantee and retroactive changes are politically unlikely but not impossible. The most prudent strategy is to:


- Use the exemption while it’s available.
- Pair gifts with valuation discounts (e.g., for minority or lack-of-marketability interests).
- Work closely with estate planners to navigate state estate taxes, which often have far lower thresholds.


## Risk Management & Audit Defense


The IRS is significantly ramping up enforcement activity targeting high-income individuals and complex tax structures. With $80 billion in additional funding allocated under the Inflation Reduction Act, the agency is deploying advanced analytics, AI models, and a wave of new auditors to focus on previously under-scrutinized taxpayers and transactions.


### IRS Targets To Watch In 2025 And Beyond:


- **High-Income Non-Filers** :
The IRS has explicitly prioritized enforcement against individuals with income over $400,000 who have failed to file returns. In 2024, over 125,000 such cases were flagged, and more automated notices and enforcement actions are expected in 2025.
- **Crypto Asset Tracing And Reporting** :
With final regulations for Form 1099-DA expected later in 2025, the IRS is sharpening its ability to match crypto transfers across wallets, exchanges, and blockchains. Transfers exceeding $10,000—especially between self-custodied wallets and offshore platforms—are under heavy scrutiny. Taxpayers should prepare for heightened documentation and potential amendments to prior-year filings.
- **Micro-Captive Insurance Schemes** :
These arrangements, often marketed to closely held businesses, remain on the IRS “Dirty Dozen” list. IRS Notice 2016-66 and subsequent litigation have narrowed the safe harbor for micro-captives. If your business relies on a §831(b) captive, review structure, premiums, and economic substance immediately.
- **Partnership and pass-through audit surge** :
Under the BBA centralized audit regime, the IRS is increasing exams of complex partnerships and multi-tiered SMLLC structures. The 2025 enforcement wave will emphasize:


- Inconsistent Schedule K-1 reporting
- Basis overstatements and capital account misstatements
- Improper allocations of losses and credits
- Misapplied PTET benefits across jurisdictions


### Defensive playbook for high-risk taxpayers:


- Run mock IRS audits internally or with advisors to assess vulnerabilities.
- Document positions clearly—keep memos, substantiation, and contemporaneous records.
- Use protective elections like BBA push-out elections to shift liability to reviewed-year partners.
- Consider amending returns proactively if risks are identified.
- Track IRS campaigns and court cases to anticipate shifting audit priorities.


## Compliance Calendar & Key Deadlines


Staying ahead of critical federal and state filing deadlines is non-negotiable in 2025. From estimated tax payments to FATCA and FinCEN reporting, here’s what tax teams must track to avoid penalties and preserve compliance.


### Estimated Tax Payments (Federal)


Quarterly estimated payments are required for individuals, corporations, and pass-through owners who expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax beyond withholding. The 2025 deadlines are:


- April 15, 2025: 1st quarter (Jan 1 – Mar 31)
- June 17, 2025: 2nd quarter (Apr 1 – May 31)*
- September 15, 2025: 3rd quarter (Jun 1 – Aug 31)
- January 15, 2026: 4th quarter (Sep 1 – Dec 31)


*Note: June 15, 2025, is a Sunday; payment due the next business day.


Underpayment penalties can apply if safe harbor thresholds (100–110% of prior year tax or 90% of current year liability) are not met.


### FATCA – Form 8938 (Specified Foreign Financial Assets)


U.S. taxpayers holding specified foreign financial assets must file Form 8938 if the value exceeds:


- Single filers (U.S.-resident): $50,000 at year-end or $75,000 any time during the year
- Married filing jointly: $100,000 at year-end or $150,000 any time


Attach to your federal income tax return (e.g., Form 1040) and file by:


- April 15, 2025 (or October 15 with a valid extension)


Failure to report can trigger substantial penalties, including a $10,000 minimum fine per violation.


### FinCEN BOI Reporting – Corporate Transparency Act (CTA)


Under the CTA, nearly all corporations, LLCs, and similar entities must file Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reports in 2025.


- Entities created before March 26, 2025:
Must file by April 25, 2025
- Entities created on or after March 26, 2025:
Must file within 30 calendar days of receiving confirmation of registration (revised from 90 days under new FinCEN rules effective Jan 1, 2025)


Exemptions apply for regulated entities (e.g., banks, large operating companies) but must be carefully reviewed. Penalties for noncompliance can reach $500 per day.


### State PTET (Pass-Through Entity Tax) Deadlines


State-specific PTET elections and estimated payments remain a planning lever and compliance obligation:


- **California:**


- PTET prepayment due June 17, 2025, to preserve the annual election
- Additional payment due by original return date (March 15, 2026)


- **New York:**


- Election due March 15, 2025
- Quarterly payments due March 15, June 17, September 16, and December 15, 2025


Other states (e.g., IL, NJ, CT) maintain varied schedules. Use a centralized compliance calendar to track each state’s deadline.


#### Additional Considerations


- **FBAR – FinCEN Form 114:** Required for U.S. persons with foreign financial accounts totaling over $10,000 at any time in 2024.


- Due: April 15, 2025
- Automatic extension: October 15, 2025 (no need to request)


- **Disaster Relief Extensions:** IRS often extends deadlines for affected regions. For example, taxpayers impacted by 2025 North Carolina storms may have until September 25, 2025, to file certain forms.


### FAQs


#### How do I know whether a single K-1 will force me to file state tax returns?


Not all K-1s trigger state filing requirements, but many do, especially when the underlying entity operates in multiple states. To find out whether your Schedule K-1 for 2024 requires filing a state return, follow these key steps:


**1. Identify the Type of K-1 You Received**


- **Partnership (Form 1065):** Reports your share of income from a partnership.
- **S Corporation (Form 1120-S):** Reports shareholder income and deductions.
- **Estate or Trust (Form 1041):** Reports beneficiary allocations from fiduciary income.


**2. Look for State-Specific Allocations on the K-1**


- Check for supplemental schedules or footnotes showing income sourced to specific states.
- On the federal K-1:


- Box 20 (Partnerships) and Box 17 (S Corporations) may contain state codes.
- Estates and trusts often note state-sourced income in Part III.


**3. Determine if the Income Creates a State Filing Obligation**


- States generally require a return if you earned income sourced to that state, even as a nonresident.
- Common triggers include rental income, passthrough business income, and gains from in-state activities.


**4. Check State Filing Thresholds** Some states have surprisingly low thresholds:


- **California:** $1,000 of California-sourced income may trigger a return.
- **New York:** Similar $1,000 threshold applies for nonresidents.


**5. If There Are Multiple States, You May Need Multiple Returns**


- A single K-1 may include income from several states.
- You may also be eligible for a credit for taxes paid to other states on your home state return.


**6. When in Doubt, Get Help** If your K-1 includes income from multiple jurisdictions or comes with complex attachments, consult a tax advisor. Mistakes in multi-state filings can lead to interest, penalties, or double taxation.


#### What exactly is a PTET credit, and how do I claim it?


The Pass-Through Entity Tax (PTET) credit is a workaround designed by states to help business owners bypass the federal $10,000 cap on state and local tax (SALT) deductions. It allows pass-through entities (PTEs), like partnerships and S corporations, to pay state taxes at the entity level. The business deducts these taxes on its federal return, and the individual owners receive a state tax credit for their share.


Here’s how it works:


**1. Who qualifies for PTET?**


- Eligible entities include partnerships and S corporations (as defined under state law).
- The entity must elect into the PTET program—this election is usually annual and irrevocable for that tax year.
- The owners must be subject to state personal income tax to claim the credit.


The IRS confirmed in Notice 2020-75 that PTET payments are deductible by the entity without being limited by the individual SALT cap.


**2. How do I claim the PTET credit?**


**Step 1: Make the PTET Election**


- This must be done through a state-specific form or portal.
- Deadlines vary—some states require the election by the original due date of the entity’s return (without extension).


**Step 2: Pay the PTET at the Entity Level**


- Payments may be quarterly or annual, depending on the state.
- The PTE deducts the PTET on its federal return (Form 1065 or 1120-S).


**Step 3: Claim the Credit as an Owner**


- Each owner reports their share of the PTET paid and claims a credit on their personal state return.


**3. Examples of PTET Programs by State**


- **California (AB 150):**


- Annual election required.
- Credit is claimed on Form 540.


- **New York:**


- Opt-in deadline is March 15.
- Owners use their NY personal return to claim the credit.


- **Connecticut:**


- PTET is mandatory, and the credit is automatically applied to owners.


Each state has different mechanics, so consult specific guidance to stay compliant.


**4. Required Forms**


- **Federal return** :


- Use Form 1065 (partnerships) or Form 1120-S (S corps) to report the PTET deduction.


- **State return** :


- Varies by jurisdiction (e.g., CA Form 540, NY IT-204-LL).


**5. Important Considerations**


- Estimated payments may be required during the year.
- Double taxation risks exist if you’re taxed in multiple states—coordinate credits carefully.
- Irrevocability: Once made, the PTET election can’t be undone for that tax year.


#### I hold a foreign fund—should I make a QEF election or rely on the PFIC high-tax exception?


It depends on your specific tax goals, access to information from the fund, and the foreign jurisdiction’s tax rate. Both options, Qualified Electing Fund (QEF) election and the PFIC high-tax exception, offer ways to reduce the punitive tax treatment under the Passive Foreign Investment Company (PFIC) rules. But each has its own tradeoffs in terms of tax timing, reporting burden, and eligibility.


Here’s a breakdown to help you choose the best option:


**1. Understanding PFIC status**


A foreign corporation is classified as a PFIC if it meets either of the following tests:


- Income Test: 75% or more of its gross income is passive (e.g., dividends, interest).
- Asset Test: 50% or more of its assets produce—or are held to produce—passive income.


If your fund meets either test, PFIC rules apply unless you qualify for an exception or make an election.


**2. What is a QEF election?**


A QEF election (under Section 1295) lets you avoid deferred taxation and interest penalties by reporting your pro rata share of the fund’s ordinary earnings and capital gains annually—similar to U.S. mutual funds.


**Pros:**


- Avoids excess distribution penalties under Section 1291.
- Allows for long-term capital gains treatment.


**Cons:**


- Requires cooperation from the fund to provide annual PFIC statements.
- You must file Form 8621 every year with detailed income and gain disclosures.


**3. What is the PFIC high-tax exception?**


The high-tax exception (under Section 1297(b)(2)(B)) lets you exclude certain passive income from PFIC classification if it’s taxed at a high rate abroad—specifically, if the effective foreign tax rate exceeds 90% of the highest U.S. corporate rate (i.e., above 18.9% as of 2025).


**Pros:**


- Avoids PFIC status entirely for qualifying income.
- May reduce annual reporting under Section 1298(f).


**Cons:**


- Requires careful tracking and proof of effective foreign tax rates.
- Less predictable if foreign taxes or the fund’s income mix changes.


**4. Key factors to consider**


**A. Tax timing & flexibility**


- **QEF** : Pay U.S. tax on earnings as they arise—good for long-term planning.
- **High-tax exception** : Defer income unless distributed, but only works if foreign tax rates remain high.


**B. Reporting requirements**


- **QEF** : More paperwork (Form 8621) but clear guidance.
- **High-tax exception** : Fewer forms, but higher audit risk if foreign tax details are missing or inconsistent.


**C. Fund cooperation**


- QEF only works if the foreign fund provides an annual PFIC information statement—this is often not available for hedge funds or pooled investments outside U.S. structures.


**5. When to choose which?**


- Use QEF if the fund cooperates and you’re in it for the long haul. It’s more transparent, avoids penalty taxes, and ensures clean exit taxation.
- Use the high-tax exception if the fund is in a jurisdiction like Germany or France with consistently high taxes, and you don’t want to rely on fund cooperation.


**6. Forms and references**


- Form 8621 – Required for both QEF elections and high-tax exception reporting.
- IRC Section 1295 – Details the QEF regime.
- IRC Section 1297 – Covers PFIC definitions and high-tax rules.


#### What happens to my estate plan if the lifetime exemption falls after 2025?


If the lifetime estate and gift tax exemption decreases after 2025 as scheduled, it could significantly affect your estate plan. The exemption—currently set at $13.99 million per individual—is expected to drop to roughly $6–7 million (adjusted for inflation) beginning in 2026, unless Congress acts to extend current levels.


This reduction means more of your estate could become subject to federal estate tax, which has a top rate of 40%. However, the IRS has clarified that gifts made before the exemption sunsets will not be retroactively taxed. For example, if you gift $10 million in 2025, that amount remains exempt even if the exemption later drops.


To mitigate potential exposure, consider making large gifts before 2026. Strategies like irrevocable trusts, spousal lifetime access trusts (SLATs), and family limited partnerships can help move appreciating assets out of your taxable estate.


Married couples can also preserve unused exemption through portability, which must be elected via a timely filed estate tax return. This can effectively double the exemption for joint estates.


Given the uncertainty around future tax law, reviewing your estate plan now is critical. Acting in advance allows you to lock in today’s higher exemption and implement tax-efficient structures tailored to your long-term goals.
