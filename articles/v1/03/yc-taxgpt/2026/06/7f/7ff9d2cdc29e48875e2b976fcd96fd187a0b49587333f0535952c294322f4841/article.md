# Employer’s Guide to Withholding & Depositing Payroll Taxes

Handling payroll taxes is one of the most critical responsibilities of any employer. Failure to withhold and deposit these taxes correctly can lead to penalties, interest charges, and compliance issues with the IRS. This guide breaks down everything employers need to know about withholding and depositing payroll taxes.


### Understanding FICA & Federal Withholding


Payroll taxes fall into two major categories: **FICA taxes** and **federal income tax withholding** .


Let’s discuss both of them in detail below.


#### FICA Taxes


**FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes** include **Social Security** and **Medicare** taxes, which are shared by both employers and employees. These contributions help fund benefits for retirees, disabled individuals, and those receiving medical care under Medicare.


#### Social Security Tax


The **Social Security tax rate** is **6.2% for employers and 6.2% for employees** , totaling **12.4%** of an employee’s wages. This tax **only applies to wages up to the annual wage base limit** , which is **$176,100 for 2025** . Any income beyond this amount is not subject to Social Security tax.


#### Medicare Tax


The **Medicare tax rate** is **1.45% for employers and 1.45% for employees** , totaling **2.9%** of an employee’s wages. Unlike Social Security, **there is no wage base limit for Medicare tax** , meaning all wages are subject to the tax. Employees earning over **$200,000 in a calendar year** are subject to an **Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9%** . Employers must **withhold** this extra tax but are **not required to match it** .


#### Federal Income Tax Withholding


Federal income tax withholding is another major component of payroll taxes. Unlike FICA taxes, which have fixed rates, **federal income tax withholding varies based on an employee’s earnings, tax filing status, and withholding allowances** .


#### How Federal Income Tax Withholding Works


- Employers must **withhold federal income tax from employee wages** based on IRS guidelines.
- The withholding amount is determined by the employee’s **Form W-4** , which specifies their filing status, dependents, and additional withholdings.
- The IRS provides **withholding tables and a Tax Withholding Estimator tool** to help employees ensure the correct amount is deducted from their paychecks.


### State & Local Withholding


Now that we've covered federal withholding, let's turn to state and local taxes. These taxes vary significantly based on where employees work and live, creating unique compliance challenges for employers.


#### State Withholding Taxes


State withholding taxes are deductions from an employee’s wages to cover their state income tax obligations. Unlike federal withholding, state tax laws vary widely, making it essential for employers to understand the specific rules in each state where they have employees.


**Key Variations in State Income Tax**


- **Flat vs. Progressive Tax Rates** : Some states, such as Colorado and Pennsylvania, impose a **flat tax rate** , meaning all income is taxed at the same percentage. Others, like California and New York, use **progressive tax rates** , where higher earners pay a higher percentage of their income in taxes.
- **No State Income Tax** : States like **Florida, Texas, Washington, Tennessee, and Nevada** do not levy state income tax, meaning employers in these states are not required to withhold state tax from employee wages.
- **Withholding Methods** : Some states use **federal Form W-4** for withholding calculations, while others require their own state-specific forms.


#### Local Withholding Taxes


In addition to state income taxes, some local governments impose their own withholding requirements. **Local payroll taxes can be levied by cities, counties, or school districts** , adding another layer of complexity to employer tax obligations.


**Key Considerations for Local Taxes**


- **Not All States Have Local Taxes** : States such as Ohio and Pennsylvania have numerous local tax jurisdictions, requiring employers to withhold taxes based on an employee’s residence or workplace. However, states like Florida, Texas, and Alaska do not impose local income taxes.
- **Different Local Tax Rates** : Within a single state, tax rates can vary significantly. For example, **Ohio has over 600 local tax jurisdictions** , each with different withholding rules. Employers must track which local taxes apply to their workforce.


#### City and Wage Taxes


Some cities impose **city-specific income or wage taxes** , which are separate from state taxes. These can apply to both residents and non-residents working within the city limits. For example, NYC imposes a **city income tax** in addition to New York State taxes, meaning employers must withhold both state and city taxes from employee wages. Another example is Philadelphia. The city has a **wage tax** , which applies to both residents and non-residents working in Philadelphia. Unlike most income taxes, this is a **flat tax on all wages earned** within the city.


#### Compliance & Reporting Obligations


Managing multi-state and local tax withholding requires **strict compliance and**[timely reporting](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/corporate-tax-filing-deadlines-extensions) to avoid penalties. Here is a list of reporting requirements:


- **Employer Registration** : Businesses must register with **state and local tax authorities** in every location where they have employees to withhold and remit the correct taxes.
- **Withholding & Deposits** : Employers must **calculate, withhold, and remit taxes** according to each state’s specific deadlines, which may be **monthly, quarterly, or annual** depending on payroll size.
- **Quarterly & Annual Reports** : Employers are typically required to file **quarterly payroll tax reports** with state and local agencies, detailing wages paid and taxes withheld.


#### Special Considerations


Certain **agreements and exemptions** may impact withholding requirements, especially for employees working across state lines or in government roles.


- **Reciprocal Agreements** : Some states have agreements that allow employees working across state borders to **only pay income tax to their home state** , avoiding double taxation. Employers must recognize these agreements and adjust withholding accordingly.
- **Section 218 Agreements** : State and local government employees may have unique **Social Security and Medicare withholding rules** under Section 218 Agreements. Employers must determine whether these agreements apply to their workforce.
