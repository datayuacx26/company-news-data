# Florida State Tax Guide

Florida is often described as a “simple” tax state. No personal income tax at the state level. Familiar federal starting points. Relatively low headline rates. For tax professionals, that reputation is misleading and costly.


Florida is not complex in the way California or New York can be. It is operationally strict. The Florida Department of Revenue enforces compliance through registrations, filing mechanics, electronic payment timing, and documentation standards that leave little margin for error. Many Florida assessments do not arise from aggressive tax positions. Many arise from procedural failures. A payment initiated too late in the ePay system. A missing resale certificate. A misclassified worker. An extension filed correctly but paid incorrectly.


This guide is for tax professionals who need to manage that reality. It focuses on the Florida taxes that create the most exposure in practice, including corporate income and franchise tax, sales and use tax, reemployment tax, and the nexus rules that pull out-of-state businesses into Florida compliance.

## Florida Tax Overview: What Every Practitioner Must Know


### Core Florida Tax Regimes Relevant to Practitioners


Florida’s business tax system is concentrated in three areas that routinely intersect with federal compliance work.


**Corporate income or franchise tax** applies to corporations, certain LLCs treated as corporations for federal purposes, financial institutions, and tax-exempt organizations with unrelated business taxable income. Florida generally uses federal taxable income as the starting point but applies its own exemption, apportionment, additions, and subtractions. Filing obligations are formal and unforgiving. Extension relief generally depends on whether a tentative tax is paid correctly and on time, and electronic payment rules are strictly enforced.


**Sales and use tax** represents Florida’s most active enforcement area. The state imposes a 6% sales tax, with many counties layering a discretionary surtax. Collection responsibility depends on delivery location, service performance, or customer location, and errors are common where businesses operate across county lines or sell remotely. Use tax exposure from untaxed out-of-state purchases is a frequent audit finding, even for businesses that otherwise file income tax returns correctly.


**Reemployment tax** , Florida’s unemployment tax regime, functions as the state’s primary payroll enforcement mechanism. Employer liability is triggered quickly, filing is quarterly, and worker classification issues are a consistent audit focus. From a risk perspective, reemployment tax in Florida plays a role similar to withholding tax in personal-income-tax states.


### Corporate Income and Entity Classification Reality


Florida’s corporate income tax framework pulls in more entities than many practitioners initially expect. Corporations doing business, earning income, or deriving income from Florida sources must file Form F-1120. LLCs require careful classification analysis. An LLC treated as a corporation for federal purposes files as a corporation in Florida. A partnership-filed LLC may still trigger Florida filings indirectly when corporate owners are involved. A disregarded single-member LLC does not file separately, but its activity must be reflected on the owner’s Florida corporate return if the owner is a corporation.


Florida allows a limited exemption on corporate income and requires estimated tax payments once liability thresholds are met. Multistate businesses must apportion income, and Florida sourcing rules for services, intangibles, and financial activity are often clarified through DOR Technical Assistance Advisements. Documentation supporting sourcing positions is not optional. It is expected.


### Sales and Use Tax Is Not a Side Issue


Sales and use tax compliance often sits outside the core income tax workflow, but Florida audits do not treat it that way. Registration is required once taxable activity begins, not when revenue becomes material. Dealers must collect both state and applicable county surtax, apply surtax caps correctly, and remit on time. Electronic filing and payment thresholds are low, and payment timing rules require action by 5:00 p.m. ET on the applicable eServices deadline date. Waiting until the due date frequently results in late payments due to transaction-date and processing constraints.. A payment initiated on the due date itself is late.


Remote sellers and marketplace activity have expanded Florida’s reach beyond its borders. Economic nexus rules bring out-of-state businesses into the Florida system even when no physical presence exists. Practitioners should assume that sales tax exposure will be examined whenever income tax or payroll issues surface.


### Reemployment Tax as an Enforcement Tool


Florida’s reemployment tax applies once modest wage or employment thresholds are met. Registration, quarterly wage reporting, and payment are mandatory, and penalties accrue quickly for late or incomplete filings. Worker classification is a major risk area, particularly where businesses rely on contractors or seasonal labor. Florida coordinates reemployment tax with federal FUTA reporting, and failures at the state level can increase federal exposure through lost credits.


### Florida’s Lack of Personal Income Tax Does Not Reduce Audit Risk


Florida does not impose a personal income tax, but that absence often creates a false sense of security. The DOR actively audits corporate income tax, sales and use tax, and reemployment tax. These audits follow structured timelines, apply statutory lookback periods, and result in assessments that include penalties and interest as a matter of course. The state’s enforcement posture is focused on compliance mechanics rather than aggressive tax theory, which makes procedural mistakes especially costly.


Remote seller rules, local surtax complexity, and electronic payment enforcement have expanded audit scope in recent years. Businesses with clean federal returns still face meaningful Florida exposure when state-specific rules are not followed precisely.


#### Practitioner Takeaway


Florida risk is procedural, not computational. The state aggressively enforces registration requirements, filing deadlines, payment timing rules, and documentation standards. Practitioners who treat Florida as a “low-tax” state rather than a “high-compliance” state expose their clients to avoidable penalties.


#### Florida vs High-Tax States: Where Practitioners Actually Get Burned


In high-tax states, exposure often comes from complex rate structures and aggressive tax positions. In Florida, exposure comes from missed registrations, voided extensions, late electronic payments, incorrect surtax application, and undocumented sourcing. The tax itself may be modest, but the compliance penalties are not.

## Florida Business Tax Filing Deadlines & Payment Requirements


### Corporate Income and Franchise Tax Filings


#### Form F-1120 (corporations)


Florida Form F-1120 is required for corporations subject to Florida corporate income or franchise tax, even if no tax is due for the year. A complete copy of the actual federal income tax return filed with the IRS must be attached to the Florida return.


The due date for Form F-1120 is the later of:


- For tax years ending June 30, the first day of the fourth month following the close of the tax year.
- For all other taxable year ends, the first day of the fifth month following the close of the tax year.
- The fifteenth day following the original due date, without extension, of the related federal return.


Florida does not waive filing obligations simply because liability is zero. Failure to file when required is treated as a compliance violation regardless of payment position.


### Extension Mechanics via Form F-7004


Florida does not recognize a federal extension by default. To extend the time to file Form F-1120, the taxpayer must file Florida Form F-7004 on or before the original Florida due date.


An extension is valid only if all tentative tax due is paid timely. The extension is void if tentative tax is underpaid by more than the greater of $2,000 or 30 percent of the tax ultimately shown on the filed return. When an extension is voided, penalties and interest are assessed as if no extension had been granted.


Extensions are generally granted for six months, or seven months for June 30 year-end filers. Only one extension may be granted per tax year.


### Estimated Tax Framework


#### Thresholds


Corporations that expect Florida corporate income or franchise tax liability to exceed $2,500 for the year must file a declaration of estimated tax and make installment payments using Florida Form F-1120ES.


#### F-1120ES cadence


Estimated tax is paid in four equal installments. For calendar-year filers, payments are due on May 31, June 30, September 30, and December 31. Florida generally expects estimated tax to be paid in four required installments. Uneven payments can trigger underpayment penalties.


### Critical Payment Mechanics Practitioners Cannot Miss


Florida’s electronic payment system is a frequent source of penalties. The state does not treat electronic payments as timely based on submission date alone.


#### Electronic payment deadlines


Electronic payments require processing time. To be considered timely, the payment must be completed and a confirmation number received no later than 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the applicable deadline listed in the Florida eServices calendar. Deadlines falling on weekends or holidays are adjusted according to state rules, not federal banking conventions.


#### Confirmation number requirement


A payment is not considered made until the transaction is completed and a confirmation number is issued. Payments completed after 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the deadline are processed the next business day and are late, even if initiated earlier.


#### 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time prior-day rule


For corporate income and estimated tax payments, Florida generally requires electronic payments to be completed and confirmed by 5:00 p.m. Eastern Time on the applicable e-Services deadline date. As a best practice, practitioners should treat the effective cutoff as the business day before the statutory due date to reduce timing and processing risk.


#### No automatic payment extension


An extension of time to file is never an extension of time to pay. Tentative tax must be paid by the original Florida due date, regardless of federal extensions or filing relief. Florida enforces this separation strictly.

#### Penalties and Interest Reminder


Florida penalties apply automatically. Late-filed returns are penalized at 10% per month, capped at 50 percent of tax due, or $50 per month up to $300 when no tax is due. Underpayment of tentative tax and estimated tax generally accrues penalties at an annualized rate of 12%. Interest accrues at a floating rate updated semiannually.


#### **Extension ≠ Payment Extension (Florida Enforces This Aggressively)**


Florida extensions protect only the filing deadline. If tentative tax is late or materially underpaid, the extension can be void. Payment timing and confirmation requirements are strictly enforced. In practice, most Florida penalties arise from payment timing failures, not incorrect tax calculations.


## Florida Tax Treatment by Entity Type


Florida’s entity-level tax rules are closely tied to federal classification, but state filing and registration obligations often arise in ways practitioners do not expect. The absence of a personal income tax does not eliminate Florida compliance for pass-through entities or their owners. Instead, exposure is driven by nexus, entity classification, ownership structure, and activity within the state.


### Sole Proprietorships


#### Federal filing backbone


A sole proprietor reports business income and expenses on Schedule C attached to Form 1040, with self-employment tax calculated on Schedule SE. From a Florida income tax perspective, there is no separate personal income tax filing. That simplicity, however, ends at the registration and indirect tax level.


#### Florida nexus considerations


A sole proprietor may have Florida tax obligations even if the business is organized or headquartered elsewhere. Florida nexus can be created through employees or agents operating in the state, maintaining an office or place of business, assembling or servicing property, owning or leasing property in Florida, or delivering goods using company-owned or leased vehicles.


#### Sales, use, and reemployment exposure


Sole proprietors selling taxable goods or services, or renting taxable property in Florida, may be required to register for and collect sales and use tax. Use tax exposure is common when inventory or equipment is purchased out of state and used in Florida without tax paid at purchase.


Reemployment tax applies once wage or employment thresholds are met. Paying at least $1,500 in wages in any calendar quarter or having at least one employee for any part of a day in 20 different weeks can trigger liability. Worker classification is a frequent audit issue, particularly where contractors are used.


### Partnerships and LLCs Taxed as Partnerships


#### Federal pass-through baseline


Partnerships are pass-through entities for federal income tax purposes and file an information return, with income reported at the owner level. A domestic LLC with two or more members is treated as a partnership by default unless it elects corporate status. Single-member LLCs are disregarded for income tax purposes but remain separate entities for employment and certain other taxes.


#### When Florida filings are triggered indirectly


Florida requires a Partnership Information Return, Form F-1065, when a partnership is doing business, earning income, or deriving income from Florida sources and at least one partner is subject to Florida corporate income tax. This rule frequently pulls partnerships into Florida compliance solely because of their ownership structure.


An LLC classified as a partnership must file Form F-1065 if one or more owners is a corporation. In those cases, the corporate owner is also required to file Florida Form F-1120 to report its share of the LLC’s income, even if the LLC itself does not owe Florida corporate tax.


#### Sales, use, and payroll touchpoints


Partnerships and LLCs may be required to register for Florida sales and use tax when selling taxable items or leasing property. Payroll activity can trigger reemployment tax obligations based on employee counts and wage thresholds, and misclassification risk applies equally to pass-through entities.


### S Corporations


#### Federal S corporation treatment


S corporations are pass-through entities for federal income tax purposes and file Form 1120-S. Income and losses flow through to shareholders, although certain items, such as built-in gains or excess passive income, may be taxed at the entity level under federal rules.


#### Florida corporate income tax filing triggers


S corporations generally do not pay Florida corporate income tax. However, Florida requires an S corporation to file and pay tax when it is subject to federal income tax at the corporate level. This can occur when built-in gains tax is reported on the federal return. In those cases, the S corporation must file Florida Form F-1120 or, if eligible, Form F-1120A.


#### When S corporations are pulled into Florida compliance


Even when no Florida income tax return is required, S corporations conducting business in Florida may have sales and use tax or reemployment tax obligations. Out-of-state S corporations frequently trigger Florida compliance through employees, property, offices, servicing activities, or in-state deliveries using company vehicles.


### C Corporations


#### Form F-1120 requirements


Corporations doing business, earning income, or deriving income from Florida sources are required to file Florida Form F-1120 and attach a complete copy of the federal return. Required schedules, including apportionment schedules, must be included. Late filing or underpayment results in automatic penalties and interest.


#### Corporate income and franchise tax


Florida imposes corporate income or franchise tax on corporations, including certain tax-exempt organizations with unrelated business income. Estimated tax payments are required once liability thresholds are met. Multistate corporations must apportion income using Florida’s property, payroll, and sales factors and apply Florida-specific exemptions and net operating loss rules.


#### Consolidated return elections


Florida permits consolidated filing for affiliated groups when the parent corporation is subject to the Florida Corporate Income Tax Code. The Florida consolidated group must mirror the federal consolidated affiliated group. Each member must consent by filing Florida Form F-1122, and the election must be made by the due date, including extensions. Once elected, consolidated filing generally applies in subsequent years.


#### Water’s-edge versus worldwide concepts


Florida does not impose a separate worldwide unitary combined reporting regime. Consolidated filing is tied directly to the federal affiliated group, and the membership of the Florida consolidated return must match the federal group. Income is apportioned using Florida’s statutory factors as reported on Schedule III, rather than through a standalone combined reporting framework.


## Florida Corporate Income / Franchise Tax


### Return Mechanics


Florida computes corporate income tax starting with federal taxable income and then applies Florida-specific additions, subtractions, and apportionment to arrive at Florida net income.


#### Additions and subtractions


Federal taxable income is adjusted to “adjusted federal income” under Chapter 220. Common additions include state income taxes deducted federally, tax-exempt interest net of disallowed expenses, and Florida decoupling adjustments tied to federal legislative changes.


Florida requires a full addback of federal bonus depreciation under IRC section 168(k) for assets placed in service before January 1, 2027. That addback is recovered through a seven-year subtraction schedule, beginning in the year of the addback. Qualified improvement property follows a separate adjustment framework and does not use the standard seven-year recovery. Florida also decouples from federal provisions allowing expanded deductions for restaurant business meals and expensing under IRC section 181 for film, television, and live theatrical productions. These differences must be tracked annually and supported with schedules.


Subtractions include allowable net operating loss deductions, capital loss carryovers, excess charitable contributions, certain foreign-source dividends and inclusions, and other items specifically permitted by statute. Florida does not permit net operating loss or capital loss carrybacks under any circumstances.


Form F-1120 reports additions and subtractions on Schedule I and Schedule II, with dedicated lines for major decoupling adjustments. Errors in these schedules are among the most common sources of DOR assessments.


#### Apportionment


Adjusted federal income is apportioned to Florida using a weighted three-factor formula: property at 25 percent, payroll at 25 percent, and sales at 50 percent. Apportionment is computed on Schedule III of Form F-1120. Certain industries are subject to modified apportionment rules, but most corporations must apply the standard formula consistently year to year.


After apportionment, nonbusiness income allocated to Florida is added, and the Florida exemption is applied to arrive at Florida net income subject to tax. Florida applies apportionment before net operating loss deductions, which directly affects the amount of NOL available for carryforward.


### Consolidated Filing


#### Eligibility and mechanics


Florida permits consolidated filing for affiliated groups when the parent corporation is subject to the Florida Corporate Income Tax Code and the group files a federal consolidated return. The Florida consolidated group must be identical to the federal consolidated affiliated group. Florida does not allow a separate state-only consolidation.


The consolidated election must be made by the return due date, including extensions. Once made, consolidated filing is mandatory for subsequent years and applies to new members as they enter the federal group.


#### Consent forms and schedules


Each subsidiary included in a Florida consolidated return must file Form F-1122 consenting to inclusion. This applies in the initial year and whenever a new member joins the group. An affiliations schedule identifying the group must also be attached, typically federal Form 851 or its Florida equivalent. Missing or incomplete consents are a frequent basis for DOR adjustments.


### Net Operating Losses


#### Carryforward only, no carryback


Florida conforms to federal timing rules for net operating losses but expressly prohibits carrybacks. Losses must be carried forward and used in future years. Losses generated in taxable years beginning before January 1, 2018 may be carried forward for up to 20 years. Losses generated in taxable years beginning after December 31, 2017 carry forward indefinitely.


For taxable years beginning after December 31, 2020, Florida applies a stacking approach. Pre-2018 carryovers are applied first and may offset 100 percent of Florida tentative apportioned adjusted federal income. Post-2017 carryovers are then applied, subject to an 80 percent limitation.


#### Apportionment interaction


Florida applies net operating loss deductions after apportionment. The Florida NOL carryover is generally limited to the federal NOL multiplied by the Florida apportionment fraction, adjusted for Florida-specific decoupling items. Other carryover deductions must be applied before the Florida NOL deduction. Failure to apply apportionment correctly to NOLs is a common audit issue.


### Penalties and Interest Posture


Florida penalties are automatic and statutory. Late-filed returns are penalized at 10 percent per month or fraction thereof, capped at 50 percent of the tax due. If no tax is due, the penalty is $50 per month, up to $300.


Corporations expecting more than $2,500 in annual tax must make estimated payments using Form F-1120ES. Underpayment of estimated tax is assessed at an annual rate of 12 percent. Underpayment of tentative tax during an extension period is also subject to a 12 percent annual penalty and may void the extension entirely. Interest accrues at a floating rate updated semiannually and applies independently of penalties.


### Amended Returns


Amended Florida corporate returns are filed on Form F-1120X. This form is commonly used to reflect IRS audit adjustments or federal amended returns. Supporting federal documentation, such as a signed IRS Revenue Agent Report, must be attached along with updated Florida schedules.


Florida generally requires refund claims to be filed within three years. When a federal change occurs, Florida imposes specific reporting timelines, including a 60-day reporting window and a two-year limitation period for certain adjustments. Amended returns must respect Florida’s prohibition on loss carrybacks.


### Practitioner Focus


#### What commonly triggers DOR adjustments


The most frequent Florida adjustments arise from missed or incorrect decoupling entries, particularly bonus depreciation, qualified improvement property, business meals, and IRC section 181 addbacks. Apportionment errors, including inconsistent factor computation and misclassification of business versus nonbusiness income, are also heavily scrutinized.


Consolidated filing defects are another recurring issue, especially when the Florida group does not exactly match the federal group or when required consent forms are missing. Net operating loss errors, such as improper carrybacks or failure to apply the post-2020 stacking and limitation rules, routinely generate assessments. Estimated tax underpayments trigger penalties even when the annual return is ultimately correct.


#### Documentation expectations


Florida expects complete and organized filings. A copy of the actual federal return filed with the IRS must be attached, along with all relevant federal schedules. Consolidated returns must include all Forms F-1122 and the appropriate affiliations schedule.


Taxpayers should maintain detailed schedules tracking net operating loss origination, apportionment fractions, usage, and remaining carryovers. Similar schedules should be retained for all decoupling adjustments, including bonus depreciation addbacks and multi-year subtractions. For amended returns, supporting federal audit or amendment documentation is required.


In Florida, incomplete documentation is treated as a substantive compliance failure. Proper schedules and attachments are often the difference between a routine processing review and a formal assessment.


## Florida Sales & Use Tax: What Income Tax Practitioners Must Spot


### When Registration Is Required


Florida requires registration before a business may collect, report, and remit sales and use tax. Registration is triggered by engaging in taxable activity, not by revenue size or profitability. Common registration triggers include:


- Selling taxable tangible personal property at retail or wholesale, including sales made through internet, catalog, or other remote channels.
- Preparing or selling food or drinks for immediate consumption, whether from a permanent location or a temporary or event-based setup.
- Renting or leasing taxable tangible personal property, or charging taxable admissions or memberships.
- Renting or leasing transient accommodations, parking spaces, boat or aircraft storage, and similar taxable rentals.
- Acting as a contractor that improves real property or fabricates components incorporated into real property, which may require self-accrual of tax on materials.


Nonprofit organizations are not exempt from registration merely because they hold exemption certificates for purchases. If a nonprofit sells taxable items or services, or leases or rents commercial real property or transient accommodations, Florida requires registration and ongoing sales tax compliance.


Once registered, the Department issues a Certificate of Registration and an Annual Resale Certificate for Sales Tax, both of which play a central role in ongoing compliance and audits.


### Use Tax Exposure From Out-of-State Purchases


Use tax is one of the most overlooked Florida exposure areas. When a business purchases taxable items from out-of-state vendors that do not charge Florida tax and then brings those items into Florida for use, storage, or consumption, Florida use tax is generally due.


Practitioner review should focus on accounts payable activity and fixed asset purchases from vendors that do not collect Florida tax. Common examples include equipment, supplies, and materials purchased online or from out-of-state suppliers. Failure to self-accrue use tax routinely results in audit assessments.


In some cases, businesses may apply for a direct pay permit, allowing them to self-accrue and remit use tax directly to the Department rather than paying tax to vendors. This can streamline compliance for contractors and businesses with frequent taxable purchases used in Florida projects.


### Marketplace Facilitator Rules


Florida’s marketplace laws assign collection responsibility based on how sales are structured, not simply on branding or platform use.


A marketplace is any physical or electronic medium through which tangible personal property is offered for sale. A marketplace provider is a party that both lists or advertises a seller’s products and collects payment from the customer, transmitting all or part of that payment to the seller.


This distinction is critical. If a platform collects customer payments and remits them to sellers, it is generally responsible for collecting and remitting Florida sales tax on those transactions. If the platform does not collect payment, and customers pay sellers directly or through a separate finance arrangement, the platform may not be treated as a marketplace provider. In those cases, the seller remains responsible for Florida registration and tax collection.


Practitioners should ask a simple but decisive question when clients sell through platforms: who collects the customer’s payment and transmits it to the seller? The answer usually determines who bears Florida sales tax responsibility.


### Resale Certificates and the Annual Renewal Cycle


Florida issues an Annual Resale Certificate for Sales Tax to registered dealers each calendar year. These certificates must be renewed annually and provided to vendors to support tax-free purchases for resale.


Practitioners should confirm that clients print and use the current year’s certificate and understand that prior-year certificates are not valid. Florida provides online tools for printing and verifying resale certificates, and vendors are expected to verify certificate validity.


Nonprofits follow a different cycle. Consumer’s Certificates of Exemption generally expire after five years and are renewed through a review process. These certificates apply to exempt purchases, not resale activity, and should not be confused with resale certificates.


### Substantiation Requirements Practitioners Must Enforce


Sales tax audits often turn on documentation rather than substantive tax law. Vendors must retain proof of valid resale certificates for untaxed sales made for resale. Florida provides verification systems, but the burden of substantiation remains with the taxpayer.


For nonprofits, exempt purchases should be paid directly with organization funds to preserve the exemption.. Purchases made with personal funds, even if later reimbursed, are taxable and routinely disallowed on audit.


Registered dealers must be able to produce current resale certificates, exemption documentation, and supporting sales records promptly when requested.


### Audit Failure Patterns


Florida’s sales and use tax audits follow a structured process, and procedural missteps can severely limit a taxpayer’s ability to contest an assessment.


Failure to provide requested records allows the Department to issue estimated assessments based on the best information available. These estimates are treated as correct unless the taxpayer can prove otherwise. Records withheld during the audit or protest phase may be excluded from evidence later, absent good cause.


When the Department issues administrative subpoenas for records and the taxpayer fails to comply, Florida law creates a rebuttable presumption that the Department’s proposed assessment is correct and that withheld records would be adverse to the taxpayer’s position.


Certain industries face heightened consequences. Dealers selling alcoholic beverages or tobacco who fail to produce required records risk suspension of their resale certificates, publication on Department lists, and potential suspension or revocation of related licenses.


#### Practitioner Takeaway


Income tax practitioners are often the first line of defense against Florida sales and use tax exposure. Confirm registration when taxable activity exists, identify use tax liabilities from out-of-state purchases, clarify marketplace roles, and enforce resale certificate discipline. In Florida, sales tax audits are documentation-driven, and failures in recordkeeping routinely result in assessments that are difficult to unwind.


## Florida Nexus & Out-of-State Business Triggers


Florida’s nexus standards are activity driven and applied broadly. Businesses do not need to be organized in Florida, generate significant Florida revenue, or maintain a permanent office to create filing and registration obligations. From the Department of Revenue’s perspective, in-state activity is a key driver of nexus and compliance responsibility.. For out-of-state businesses, nexus analysis is often the starting point for Florida exposure.


### Activities That Create a Florida Business Connection


A business based outside Florida establishes a Florida business connection when it engages in certain in-state activities. Common nexus-creating activities include:


#### Employees or agents in Florida


Having employees, agents, or independent contractors conducting sales or other business activities in Florida points to nexus. The Department does not distinguish between revenue-generating and support roles when evaluating in-state presence.


#### Installation or servicing activities


Assembling, installing, repairing, or servicing products in Florida establishes nexus, even when sales contracts are executed elsewhere. Short-term work can “trigger registration/collection obligations for the period of activity.”


#### Property ownership or leasing


Owning, renting, or leasing real property or tangible personal property in Florida creates nexus. This includes equipment, inventory, or other assets located in the state for business use.


#### In-state deliveries using company vehicles


Delivering goods into Florida using company-owned or leased trucks is a nexus-creating activity. Delivery method matters. Use of common carriers is treated differently from company-controlled transportation.


These activities are evaluated independently. A single qualifying activity is sufficient to establish a nexus.


### Registration Implications Across Florida Taxes


Once nexus is established, Florida generally expects registration and compliance across multiple tax regimes. Practitioners should evaluate each tax separately rather than assuming a single registration covers all obligations.


#### Corporate income and franchise tax


An out-of-state corporation doing business in Florida is generally required to file Florida Form F-1120. Florida specifically treats an out-of-state corporation that is a partner in a Florida partnership as subject to the Florida Corporate Income Tax Code, even if the corporation has no other Florida activity. Nexus created through employees, property, or servicing activities typically triggers corporate income tax filing obligations.


#### Sales and use tax


Out-of-state businesses with Florida nexus are required to register for sales and use tax when they sell taxable goods or services delivered into Florida. Employees or agents in Florida, installation or servicing activity, property ownership, and company-truck deliveries are all cited by the Department as common registration triggers. Remote sellers and marketplace facilitators are subject to Florida’s electronic filing and payment requirements and must comply with Florida’s administrative systems once nexus exists.


#### Reemployment tax


Reemployment tax liability is triggered by relatively low thresholds once employees are present. Paying at least $1,500 in wages in any calendar quarter or having one or more employees for any part of a day in 20 different weeks generally creates liability. Separate thresholds apply for agricultural, domestic, nonprofit, and governmental employers.


Worker classification is critical. Employees are subject to the employer’s direction and control, including corporate officers and individuals performing services for LLCs treated as corporations. Misclassifying workers as independent contractors can result in retroactive tax, penalties, and interest. Employers that meet liability thresholds must register and file quarterly Employer’s Reports using Form RT-6, with electronic filing required for certain employers.


#### Practitioner Takeaway


Florida nexus is established through activity, not intent. Out-of-state businesses frequently trigger Florida compliance through routine operational decisions such as sending employees into the state, installing equipment, or delivering goods with company vehicles. Once nexus exists, registration and filing obligations often span corporate income tax, sales and use tax, and reemployment tax. Early identification and prompt registration are the most effective ways to control Florida exposure and avoid cascading penalties.
