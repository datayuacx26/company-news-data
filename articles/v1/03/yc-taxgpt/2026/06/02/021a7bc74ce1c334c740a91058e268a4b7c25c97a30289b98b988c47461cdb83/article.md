# Construction Contractors Tax Guide 2025

A 5% bid margin feels safe until it isn’t.


In construction, the numbers are always tighter than they seem. You plan for material costs, labor, fuel, and overhead, but what about the timing of your deductions? Or the ripple effect of one small compliance misstep across multiple states?


Margins disappear not just because of errors, but because the rules change, deadlines shift, and critical decisions get siloed between departments. Too often, tax is treated as an afterthought when it should be part of the bid strategy itself.


This guide breaks down the critical tax exposures contractors face today and helps you how to get ahead of them. Let’s get started!


## Why Construction Taxes Can Wipe Out a 5% Bid Margin


In construction, a 5% bid margin might seem like a buffer until tax timing errors quietly erase it. Take bonus depreciation, for example. In 2023, contractors could write off 80% of qualifying equipment up front.


In 2025, that’s dropped to 40%. If you apply 2023 assumptions to a $500,000 excavator bought this year, you’re leaving $60,000 of immediate tax savings on the table. That’s a direct cash-flow hit that could have covered payroll, fuel, or supplier deposits.


Many firms still aren’t accounting for the bonus depreciation phase-down in their capital planning or tax forecasts. Section 179 offers some relief, but it comes with caps and phase-outs, and requires precise coordination with bonus depreciation rules to avoid misapplication.


When tax planning doesn’t align with job costing or capital schedules, the impact becomes real: *unexpected liabilities, strained cash reserves, and lost margin.* For firms competing on tight bids across multiple states, this is the kind of mistake that turns a profitable job into a breakeven one.


## 2025 Federal Tax Landscape for Contractors


### Contract-Accounting Method Rules


#### Small-Contractor Exception: Average Gross Receipts ≤ $30 Million for 2025


For 2025, the inflation-adjusted gross receipts threshold for the small-contractor exception under IRC §§ 460(e) and 448(c) is $30 million. Contractors under this limit can generally avoid the mandatory use of the percentage-of-completion method (PCM) for long-term contracts, instead electing to use the completed contract method (CCM).


CCM can significantly improve short-term cash flow by deferring revenue recognition until the job is done, making it ideal for contractors managing tight margins and variable project timing. However, firms hovering near the $30 million limit should tread carefully. A single large job or asset sale can tip you over the threshold, forcing a method switch and potentially triggering taxable income acceleration.


#### PCM vs. CCM Decision Tree; Rev. Proc. 2024- XX & ASC 606 Alignment


Once contractors exceed the $30 million average, they are locked into PCM for most long-term contracts. This requires income recognition as work progresses, even if cash hasn’t been collected, tightening cash flow just as costs ramp up.


Rev. Proc. 2024- XX (expected final guidance) provides a bridge between federal tax treatment and GAAP financial reporting under ASC 606. While ASC 606 emphasizes control-based revenue recognition, PCM focuses on cost-to-cost inputs. Misalignment between the two can result in book-tax timing differences, audit risk, and mismatched job-cost reports. Construction CPAs and controllers should be reviewing project-level revenue policies to avoid compliance gaps and system inconsistencies.


### UNICAP § 263A & Inventory Capitalization


Section 263A, the Uniform Capitalization (UNICAP) rule, generally requires businesses to capitalize both direct and certain indirect costs associated with the production or resale of real and tangible property. For contractors, this can mean applying overhead and indirect expenses to jobs, adding administrative burden, and affecting cost tracking.


*Contractors under the $30 million gross receipts threshold are exempt from UNICAP.* But that exemption disappears the moment a firm surpasses the threshold, even mid-project. If you're in the gray zone, flipping into UNICAP midstream creates a compliance headache: cost allocations must change, methods may need IRS approval, and the transition year often requires catch-up adjustments that increase taxable income.


Job-cost accounting systems should be reviewed annually to ensure they can toggle between capitalized and expensed treatments if needed. Firms with projected growth must plan proactively to avoid tax shocks from unexpected UNICAP exposure.


### Bonus Depreciation Phase-Down & § 179 Expensing


The once-generous 100% bonus depreciation under the TCJA is winding down. In 2025, the rate drops to 40%, then to 20% in 2026, and disappears entirely by 2027. For capital-intensive contractors, this phase-down means fewer immediate deductions for equipment purchases, turning what used to be a tax shield into a deferred benefit.


This hits especially hard for firms financing large purchases, expecting quick depreciation to offset tax liabilities. A $500,000 excavator now only nets a $200,000 deduction in Year 1, compared to $400,000 just two years ago. For firms managing dozens of pieces of yellow iron or fleet equipment, that delta adds up fast.


Section 179 remains available as a fallback, allowing for up to $1.29 million in deductions for 2025 (phase-out starts at $3.22 million). But Section 179 has limitations: it can’t create losses, must be elected, and caps out quickly for firms making heavy year-end purchases.


Tax teams should model both methods side-by-side to make sure they’re capturing maximum upfront value, particularly as bonus depreciation winds down and project cash needs rise.


### Interest-Expense § 163(j) & Real-Property Trade-or-Business Election


Under IRC § 163(j), businesses face limitations on the deductibility of business interest expenses, generally capped at 30% of adjusted taxable income (ATI). For construction firms with significant debt financing, this limitation can restrict interest deductions, impacting cash flow.


However, eligible real property trades or businesses (RPTBs) can elect out of these limitations by making an irrevocable election under § 163(j)(7)(B). This election requires the use of the Alternative Depreciation System (ADS) for certain property classes, such as nonresidential real property, residential rental property, and qualified improvement property, resulting in longer depreciation periods and the forfeiture of bonus depreciation benefits.


The decision to make this election involves a trade-off: opting for full interest deductibility at the expense of accelerated depreciation. Contractors must carefully model the long-term tax implications, considering factors like project timelines, financing structures, and capital expenditure plans.


### Energy-Efficiency & Apprenticeship Credits


The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 expanded energy-related tax incentives, notably enhancing the § 179D deduction and § 45L credit.


#### Section 179D Deduction:


For commercial building owners and designers, § 179D offers deductions for energy-efficient improvements. In 2025, the base deduction ranges from $0.58 to $1.16 per square foot, depending on the level of energy savings achieved. Meeting prevailing wage and apprenticeship requirements can increase the deduction up to $5.81 per square foot.


#### Section 45L Credit:


Residential builders can benefit from the § 45L credit, which provides up to $5,000 per home for constructing or substantially reconstructing energy-efficient homes. Eligibility depends on meeting specific energy-saving criteria, such as certification under the DOE's Zero Energy Ready Home (ZERH) program.


To maximize these incentives, contractors should integrate energy efficiency and labor compliance considerations early in project planning and maintain thorough documentation to substantiate claims.


### NEW » R&D Credit for Design-Build & BIM Development


The Research & Development (R&D) tax credit under IRC § 41 offers opportunities for construction firms engaged in innovative activities. Qualifying activities may include:


- Developing design-build solutions that address unique engineering challenges.
- Creating and testing prefabricated prototypes.
- Advancing Building Information Modeling (BIM) technologies to improve project outcomes.


However, recent changes to IRC § 174 now require capitalization and amortization of R&D expenditures over five years for domestic activities. This shift can impact cash flow and tax planning.


To effectively leverage the R&D credit while navigating § 174 requirements, construction firms should:


- Conduct thorough R&D studies to identify and document qualifying activities.
- Ensure proper accounting treatment of R&D expenses in compliance with § 174.
- Coordinate with tax professionals to optimize credit claims and manage amortization schedules.


## State & Local (SALT) Obligations


### Project-Based Nexus & Sales/Use Tax on Materials


Since the Supreme Court’s 2018 *South Dakota v. Wayfair* ruling, physical presence is no longer the threshold for state tax nexus. For contractors, this means you can trigger sales, use, or income tax obligations in a state just by performing services or shipping materials into that jurisdiction, even without a job trailer or in-state office.


On top of that, sales and use tax rules for construction materials vary significantly by state and even by transaction type. For example:


- Materials incorporated into real property (e.g., poured concrete, HVAC systems) may be exempt from sales tax when sold to the end customer as a real-property improvement.
- However, contractors can be exposed to use tax liability when purchasing these items, especially if bought out-of-state without proper resale or exemption documentation.


Failing to collect or remit the correct tax or missing exemption certificates can lead to penalties, interest, and retroactive assessments during audits. States like California and Texas aggressively enforce material-use compliance, often using job-cost records to reconstruct exposure. Your procurement and tax teams must collaborate on exemption certificate tracking, supplier compliance, and nexus reviews before mobilizing on multi-state jobs.


### Multi-State Income/Franchise Tax & Apportionment


Even if your business is domiciled in one state, operating across state lines often creates income or franchise tax exposure elsewhere. States apportion income using different formulas, and the rules are shifting.


- Cost-of-performance sourcing allocates income based on where the contractor performs the service (common in states like Colorado and Alabama).
- Market-based sourcing, now adopted by over 30 states, taxes income where the benefit of the service is received, often the project location.


This divergence can create double taxation if one state taxes you based on where you work and another taxes you based on where the client is located.


To complicate things further, cities like Portland (Clean Energy Surcharge - CET) and San Francisco (Gross Receipts Tax - GRT) impose their own local-level business taxes, sometimes without offsetting credits or deductions. These can add a significant margin drain on large projects and are often overlooked until a city business license audit uncovers them.


Construction CFOs and controllers should review state-by-state filing thresholds annually, monitor project-based receipts, and model apportionment exposure to avoid surprises. Where project value is concentrated in just a few high-tax jurisdictions, entity structuring or project-specific SPVs may offer relief.


### Managed Audit & VDA Options


Use tax is one of the most frequent SALT audit triggers, especially in construction, where material movement and purchase sourcing can be hard to trace across vendors, states, and job sites. But there are proactive tools available to manage risk.


Managed audits, offered by states like Texas, Tennessee, and Kansas, allow taxpayers to perform a self-review under state supervision. In exchange for transparent cooperation, states may waive penalties and reduce interest. This can be an effective option when you suspect exposure but want to maintain goodwill with the DOR.


Voluntary Disclosure Agreements (VDAs) go a step further. When a contractor realizes they’ve triggered nexus in a state but haven’t filed, a VDA lets them come forward anonymously (via a representative), limit the lookback period (often 3–4 years), and secure penalty relief in return for compliance going forward.


Firms expanding geographically or acquiring new entities should screen for unknown nexus or unfiled use tax via a SALT risk assessment before the state comes knocking. It's easier to negotiate from a position of proactivity than defense.


## Payroll, Labor & Subcontractor Issues


### Worker Classification & Certified Payroll


In March 2024, the U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) reinstated the "economic reality" test to determine whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). This six-factor test assesses:


1. Opportunity for profit or loss depending on managerial skill
2. Investments by the worker and the employer
3. Degree of permanence of the work relationship
4. Nature and degree of control over the work
5. Whether the work is integral to the employer's business
6. Skill and initiative of the worker


No single factor is determinative; instead, the totality of circumstances is considered.


For construction firms, misclassification can lead to significant liabilities, including back wages, taxes, and penalties. To proactively address classification uncertainties, businesses can file IRS Form SS-8, "Determination of Worker Status for Purposes of Federal Employment Taxes and Income Tax Withholding," to obtain an official determination.


Additionally, contractors working on federally funded projects must comply with certified payroll requirements under the Davis-Bacon Act, ensuring that workers are paid prevailing wages. Accurate classification directly impacts compliance with these requirements.


#### NEW » ERC Look-Back and Audit Risk


The Employee Retention Credit (ERC), introduced to support businesses during the COVID-19 pandemic, has become a focal point for IRS scrutiny. In 2024, the IRS announced that a significant percentage of ERC claims exhibited a high risk of noncompliance, leading to increased audits and enforcement actions.


Key risk indicators identified by the IRS include:


- Claims for periods when the business was fully operational without a significant revenue decline
- Inclusion of wages already used for Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) loan forgiveness
- Claims involving family members or related parties
- Overstated number of qualifying quarters


## Equipment & Asset-Management Strategies


### Cost Segregation for HQ & Yard Facilities


Cost segregation is a strategic tax planning tool that allows construction firms to accelerate depreciation deductions by reclassifying components of a building into shorter-lived asset classes. Instead of depreciating an entire facility over 39 years, certain elements, such as electrical systems, plumbing, and specialized finishes, can be depreciated over 5, 7, or 15 years.


Implementing a cost segregation study for headquarters, warehouses, or equipment yards can lead to substantial tax savings and improved cash flow. However, it's essential to ensure that the study is conducted by qualified professionals and adheres to IRS guidelines to withstand potential audits.


### Repair-vs-Capitalize Safe Harbors


Determining whether to expense or capitalize costs related to tangible property is a critical decision for construction firms. The IRS provides specific safe harbor rules to guide this determination:


- **De Minimis Safe Harbor** : Allows businesses to deduct amounts paid for tangible property up to $2,500 per invoice or item ($5,000 for businesses with applicable financial statements).
- **Routine Maintenance Safe Harbor** : Permits the deduction of costs for recurring activities that keep property in its ordinarily efficient operating condition, provided they are expected to be performed more than once during the property's life.


**Safe Harbor for Small Taxpayers** : Applicable to businesses with average annual gross receipts of $10 million or less, allowing them to deduct certain improvements to eligible buildings if the total amount paid during the year doesn't exceed the lesser of $10,000 or 2% of the building's unadjusted basis.
