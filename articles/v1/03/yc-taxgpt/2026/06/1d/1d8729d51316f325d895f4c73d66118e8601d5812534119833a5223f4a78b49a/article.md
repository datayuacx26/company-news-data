# Insurance Companies Tax Guide 2025

## State and Local Tax Obligations


### Premium Taxes vs. Corporate Income Tax


State taxation of insurance companies doesn’t follow a uniform model. While most traditional corporations are subject to income or franchise taxes, insurance companies are primarily taxed through premium taxes—a gross receipts-based levy that applies to written premiums, regardless of profitability.


However, the rules vary significantly across states:


- Some states levy only premium taxes (e.g., Florida, Texas).
- Others impose both premium and corporate income/franchise taxes (e.g., Illinois, Michigan).
- Certain hybrid states apply income taxes selectively based on insurer type or activities (e.g., D.C. and Tennessee may apply income tax to certain non-admitted insurers or affiliates).


### Retaliatory Tax Rules and Exemptions


Retaliatory tax is one of the most misunderstood elements of insurance taxation. Designed to “equalize” tax burdens across states, retaliatory tax provisions penalize insurers domiciled in high-tax states when they write business in lower-tax states.


#### How the Retaliatory Formula Works (Step-by-Step):


1. Determine what a foreign (out-of-state) insurer would owe in its home state if it were domiciled there but doing business in the host state.
2. Compare that amount to what a domestic (in-state) insurer pays under the host state's rules.
3. If the home-state burden is higher, the host state imposes a retaliatory tax to make up the difference.


For example, if your home state charges a 2.35% premium tax rate and the host state only charges 1.75%, the host state will require you to pay the difference (0.60%) as retaliatory tax.


#### Top 5 High-Retaliatory Impact States (2025 trend data):


1. California
2. Florida
3. New York
4. Minnesota
5. Michigan


Insurers domiciled in these states often trigger retaliatory taxes across a wide swath of other jurisdictions, especially when premium tax surcharges, assessments, or fees are included in the comparison.


#### Common Exemptions and Workarounds:


- Surplus lines are often exempt from retaliatory tax.
- Reciprocal state agreements may reduce liability in certain jurisdictions.
- Some captive insurers and HMOs qualify for special treatment depending on state legislation.
- Reinsurance premiums may be excluded from the retaliatory base in specific states.


#### Required Documentation and Filing


Many states now require detailed breakdowns of how the retaliatory tax was calculated, and some mandate the upload of third-party documents like:


- The NAIC “Schedule of Fees and Charges”, which details a company’s home-state assessments, taxes, and licenses.
- Annual statement supplements, showing premium breakdowns and company classification.
- Apportionment schedules are used when income-based taxes are involved.


Failure to include these documents can delay processing or trigger audit flags.


### Multistate Filing and Audit Risk


Unlike income tax filings, which can be centralized and somewhat standardized, premium tax filings are highly jurisdiction-specific, form-intensive, and prone to audit triggers.


#### The Complexity of Premium Tax Matrices


Each state applies its own tax rate by line of business, legal entity type, and premium class (e.g., direct, assumed, reinsured). What’s more, some states publish annual matrices or use blended rates that shift midyear.


Insurers must maintain a dynamic premium tax matrix, a constantly updated internal model mapping each state’s:


- Applicable rates
- Credit programs
- Filing thresholds and deadlines
- Assessment surcharges


Missing a rate update or misclassifying premium types can lead to underpayments, penalties, and multi-year audit exposure.


#### Refund Claims and Amended Returns


Premium tax is a gross receipts-based tax, easy to miscalculate, especially when refunds or reinsurance adjustments come in after initial filings. However, claiming overpayments or filing amended returns is complex and time-sensitive, with statutes of limitation as short as 12 months in some states.


Because amended filings often undergo heightened scrutiny, insurers must document:


- Original vs. revised premium totals
- Basis for revision (e.g., audit, policy rescission, cession correction)
- Revised retaliatory impact, if any


#### Premium-Tax Credit Offsets


Many states offer direct premium tax credits that generate immediate ROI, reducing payable tax on a dollar-for-dollar basis. These credits may apply to:


- Hiring initiatives
- Rural or underserved market expansion
- Historic preservation or housing investment
- State-sponsored guarantee funds


Proper identification and claiming of these credits can reduce tax liability by millions, but require advance registration, certified documentation, and coordination with external stakeholders.


#### Local GRT and Lease-Based Tax Filings


Remote operations have made city-level tax exposure harder to track. Several municipalities, including Philadelphia, Portland, and San Francisco, levy gross receipts taxes (GRTs) or commercial lease-based excise taxes even if insurers don’t maintain physical offices there.


Triggers may include:


- Remote staff presence
- Leasing coworking space, even short-term
- Marketing or sales activity attributed to city jurisdiction


Failure to register or file in these cities can result in interest-bearing assessments that stretch back multiple years.


#### ESG and Renewable-Based Incentives


A growing number of states are tying premium tax credits or abatements to ESG initiatives, particularly green bonds, solar investment, and climate resilience insurance offerings.


As of 2025, notable examples include:


- **Colorado** : Credits for wildfire and flood mitigation insurance products
- **New Jersey** : Abatements linked to green real estate investment portfolios
- **New York** : Renewable energy bond incentives applicable to premium tax liabilities


Insurers that align capital deployment or product strategy with these incentives can reduce state tax while advancing ESG mandates, but must prequalify and substantiate eligibility through designated agencies.


## International Tax Compliance Issues for Insurance Companies


In 2025, global tax reforms are directly reshaping how insurance companies structure reinsurance, capital flow, and risk across borders. From anti-base erosion rules to minimum tax regimes, insurers face heightened scrutiny and increased reporting obligations.


### BEAT on Reinsurance and the QDP Election


The Base Erosion and Anti-Abuse Tax (BEAT) remains a top concern for insurers making payments to offshore affiliates. Premium payments to non-U.S. reinsurance companies are frequently flagged as base erosion payments, which are subject to BEAT if they exceed the 3% threshold of total deductions for large U.S. taxpayers.


**Why it matters:** Even when reinsurance is fully risk-shifting and risk-distributing, the structure can trigger BEAT liability if the ceding company falls under the $500 million gross receipts threshold and makes significant deductible payments abroad.


*Key pressure point:*


*Premiums ceded to Bermuda, Cayman Islands, and Ireland continue to dominate BEAT assessments in the insurance sector.*


**Qualified Derivatives Payments (QDP) Election** For insurers with investment arms or capital desks engaged in derivative transactions with offshore affiliates, the QDP election provides some relief. When properly elected, QDPs are excluded from base erosion payments, reducing BEAT exposure.


However, QDP:


- Requires consistency across all similar transactions
- Must be clearly disclosed on Form 8991 and backed by detailed transactional records
- Demands careful coordination with treasury and asset management groups


Despite its complexity, the QDP election is becoming a standard defensive move for large insurers managing both risk and yield offshore.


### QDMTT Impact on Bermuda and Other Reinsurance Hubs


The introduction of the Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-Up Tax (QDMTT) under OECD Pillar Two has significant implications for global insurers, especially those with reinsurance hubs in low-tax jurisdictions.


Bermuda, a key domicile for reinsurance entities, enacted QDMTT legislation in 2024 to comply with global minimum tax standards, ensuring that companies subject to Pillar Two will be taxed at a minimum 15% rate locally, rather than being topped up elsewhere.


**Implications for insurance groups:**


- Global Minimum Tax Application: U.S.-parented insurance groups with Bermuda-based reinsurers must now account for Bermuda’s QDMTT when modeling effective tax rates (ETRs) under GloBE rules.
- Reduced benefit from base erosion: The arbitrage advantage of booking reinsurance profits in low-tax jurisdictions has eroded, making pricing, capital allocation, and risk transfer strategies more tax-neutral.
- Increased compliance burden: QDMTT requires granular entity-level income and deferred tax tracking, often diverging from traditional U.S. GAAP or statutory frameworks.


Other jurisdictions like Guernsey, the Isle of Man, and Singapore are following suit. As a result, reinsurance structuring now demands synchronized international tax modeling, integrating BEAT, GILTI, and QDMTT across jurisdictions.


## Aligning Accounting with Tax (GAAP, SAP, Tax)


### IFRS 17 / LDTI Convergence (for Groups with Foreign Subsidiaries)


For multinational insurance groups, the convergence of IFRS 17 and U.S. GAAP Long Duration Targeted Improvements (LDTI) is reshaping how long-duration contracts are valued and creating significant new timing differences between financial and tax reporting.


What changed:


- IFRS 17 (effective globally) and LDTI (effective for U.S. insurers beginning 2023) both replace static reserving approaches with updated current assumptions, requiring remeasurement of liabilities for future policy benefits each reporting period.
- These updated models are forward-looking and market-sensitive, introducing more volatility in reported earnings.


**Tax implication:** The remeasurement and deferral of profits under IFRS 17/LDTI do not impact taxable income in the same periods. As a result, insurers are now seeing:


- Increased deferred tax assets/liabilities from valuation differences
- Complexity in reconciling GAAP/IFRS results to Subchapter L tax rules, especially for reserve deductions under §807 and §816
- Need for enhanced disclosures in MD&A and investor communications, explaining why financial profits diverge from taxable income


Investor pressure is growing.
Institutional investors and rating agencies are requesting clarity on:


- Taxable earnings forecasts vs. financial earnings under new accounting standards
- How deferred tax positions will unwind
- Potential impacts on regulatory capital or dividend capacity


For groups with foreign subsidiaries reporting under IFRS 17, aligning local financial reporting with U.S. consolidated tax modeling is no longer optional. Tax departments must work closely with controllers, actuaries, and treasury to track and reconcile timing differences, especially for intercompany reinsurance, reserves, and DAC analogs under new valuation frameworks.


### Reserve & DAC Reconciliation


Few areas expose insurance companies to greater tax-accounting friction than reserves and DAC. While statutory accounting principles (SAP) drive regulatory reporting and capital adequacy, tax treatment follows the IRS’s Subchapter L, leading to significant timing and measurement differences.


#### Life Reserve Treatment: SAP vs. Tax


- Under SAP, life insurance reserves are determined based on conservative assumptions and statutory formulas, often leading to higher liabilities than under tax.
- Tax reserves under §807 are calculated using prescribed interest and mortality tables, which may result in lower deductions relative to statutory reserves, especially in rising rate environments.
- The discrepancy creates deferred tax liabilities (DTLs) that must be clearly disclosed in both GAAP financials and, where applicable, statutory footnotes.


#### DAC Amortization: Life vs. Non-Life Products


- Life insurance DAC is generally broader and amortized over a longer 15-year schedule under §848, while non-life DAC applies more narrowly and may qualify for shorter amortization periods or small-insurer exceptions.
- In SAP, acquisition costs are usually expensed as incurred, creating further divergence.
- These mismatches demand careful tracking to ensure accurate book-to-tax reconciliation, especially in consolidated financial statements and tax footnotes for public filers.


### Book-to-Tax Tracking Best Practices


With growing investor scrutiny and regulatory expectations, insurers must move beyond manual reconciliations. Accurate book-to-tax tracking is a strategic advantage for forecasting, provisioning, and audit defense.


#### Deferred Tax Inventory Schedules


- Maintain a granular inventory of deferred tax assets and liabilities, broken down by origin (e.g., reserves, DAC, impairments, lease adjustments).
- Include tax attributes like net operating losses (NOLs), foreign tax credits (FTCs), and valuation allowances.
- Automate rollforwards to tie beginning and ending balances with current-year activity across GAAP, SAP, and tax filings.


**Internal Controls and System Integration**


- Establish control points between actuarial systems, financial reporting platforms, and tax compliance tools, especially for reserve and DAC data inputs.
- Implement SOX-compliant review procedures to verify book-tax reconciliations prior to provision and return finalization.
- Conduct periodic variance analyses between projected and actual deferred balances to validate underlying assumptions.


Insurers that invest in scalable reconciliation frameworks not only reduce risk, they enable more confident financial planning and quicker audit responses.

## Sector-Specific Issues: Life, P&C, and Health Insurers


While federal and state tax principles apply broadly, each insurance sector carries its own tax rules, reporting quirks, and regulatory sensitivities. Life, property & casualty (P&C), and health insurers must address distinct tax challenges in 2025, many of which directly affect provision accuracy, IRS exam readiness, and investor transparency.


### Life Insurers


Separate accounts, DAC, and proration rules dominate the tax profile for life insurers.


- **Separate accounts** must be tracked and reported independently from general accounts. Under Subchapter L, income from these accounts—typically tied to variable annuities or life policies—is subject to unique reserve rules under §807 and may affect adjusted financial statement income (AFSI) for CAMT purposes.
- **DAC amortization** remains a top complexity. Life insurers are subject to a broader DAC base and a 15-year straight-line amortization under §848. Capitalized costs include commissions, underwriting, and policy issuance expenses, often requiring layered actuarial and accounting inputs to validate.
- **Proration under §805** introduces another layer: life insurers must disallow 25% of tax-exempt income and split the benefit 70/30 between the company and policyholders. This allocation, unchanged for 2025, directly impacts investment strategies and provision modeling.


### P&C Insurers


For P&C insurers, loss reserves and Schedule P are at the heart of both tax compliance and audit exposure.


- Loss reserve discounting is governed by §846, which requires use of IRS-published discount factors. These differ sharply from SAP reserves and must be applied at the line-of-business level.
- Schedule P reconciliation is essential for both exam defense and internal consistency. Discrepancies between tax-discounted reserves and Schedule P reported figures often trigger IRS exam questions or documentation requests.
- In Rev. Proc. 2025-15, the IRS released updated discount factors reflecting recent interest rate shifts. These factors affect 2024 tax returns filed in 2025 and must be updated in systems to avoid computational errors.


Accurate reconciliation between actuarial models and return positions is critical, especially for companies triggering CAMT due to reserve-related AFSI adjustments.


### Health Insurers


Health insurers operate under a hybrid model, and their tax obligations reflect a blend of life and P&C treatment, plus additional ACA-era compliance requirements.


- Under §833, qualifying Blue Cross/Blue Shield organizations receive special deductions for claims and reserves, provided they meet the 85% medical loss ratio (MLR) test. This benefit can materially reduce taxable income, but must be substantiated with actuarial and claims data.
- Forms 1095-B and 1095-C continue to present operational and reporting challenges. Penalties for late or incorrect filings remain high, and the IRS has increased enforcement for noncompliance, especially for insurers offering group or individual coverage.
- Many states impose premium tax surcharges to fund reinsurance pools or public health initiatives. These surcharges may not be offset by retaliatory tax credits and must be modeled distinctly from general premium tax liabilities.


In 2025, health insurers must tighten integration between tax, compliance, and operations to keep pace with layered reporting and eligibility requirements.


## Managing Risk and IRS/State Audits


For insurance companies, tax audits are not a matter of *if* , but *when* . The IRS has signaled heightened enforcement on Subchapter L positions, international structures, and reserve methodologies. At the state level, premium tax audits have become more frequent, with some states applying aggressive retaliatory tax recalculations and disallowing undocumented credits. In 2025, audit readiness must be built into the tax function, not bolted on.


### Red Flags and Documentation Readiness


Certain tax positions consistently draw scrutiny from both the IRS and state auditors. Insurers should treat these as red flag zones, requiring extra substantiation and real-time audit readiness.


**Key Red Flags:**


- **DAC substantiation** : The IRS increasingly requests detailed support for DAC capitalization bases, especially when involving affiliated commission structures or policy acquisition expense allocations across multiple lines.
- **Intercompany reinsurance** : Transactions with offshore affiliates or group captives often trigger BEAT exposure, transfer pricing reviews, or reserve arbitrage scrutiny.
- **Reserve modeling** : Aggressive or inconsistent discounting assumptions, particularly when not aligned with Rev. Proc. 2025-15 or §807 standards, can raise exam flags and delay return acceptance.


**Best Practice: Build “Audit-Ready Files” in Real Time** Tax departments should maintain continuously updated digital audit files, including:


- DAC calculation memos and actuarial allocation workpapers
- Reinsurance treaties, intercompany pricing documentation, and Schedule F reconciliations
- Reserve rollforwards and tie-outs to SAP and tax filings
- State-specific premium tax matrices and NAIC schedules


### FinCEN BOI Data Sharing as a Trigger


Beginning in 2024, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) implemented Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting requirements under the Corporate Transparency Act. While designed to combat money laundering and shell company abuse, this new data stream is now directly feeding into IRS risk modeling.


**Why it matters for insurers:**


- Groups with nested captives, offshore holding structures, or intercompany reinsurers may now face enhanced audit targeting if ownership chains are flagged as opaque or high-risk.
- BOI filings disclose beneficial owners, control persons, and entity relationships, which can be cross-referenced with Form 5471, 5472, and 8991 (BEAT) filings to identify inconsistencies.


Risk signals include:


- Unreported offshore affiliates holding reinsurance risk
- Rapid entity creation tied to premium income routing
- Discrepancies between BOI declarations and U.S. tax forms


Insurers must ensure that BOI filings submitted by legal/compliance teams are reconciled with tax disclosures to avoid mismatches that could prompt unnecessary audit activity.
