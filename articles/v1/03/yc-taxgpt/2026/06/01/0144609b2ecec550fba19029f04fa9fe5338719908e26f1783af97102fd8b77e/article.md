# 7 Types of Taxes That Actually Matter (And How Each One Affects Your Wallet)

"Wait, how many types of taxes am I paying?"


If you've ever looked at your paycheck, a receipt, or your property tax bill and felt confused about where your money's going, you're not alone. The average American pays 7 different types of taxes without even realizing it, and each one works differently.


Here's the frustrating part: Most people overpay simply because they don't understand which taxes apply to them, how rates work, or what deductions they qualify for.


In this guide, I'll break down every major tax type in the U.S., from the income tax on your paycheck to the property tax on your home, in a simple way. No IRS jargon. No confusing formulas. Just clear explanations that help you understand exactly where your money goes and how to keep more of it.

## Key Tax Definitions


**Progressive Tax:** A tax system where the rate increases as income rises, placing a greater burden on higher earners (e.g., U.S. federal income tax).


**Regressive Tax:** A tax that disproportionately impacts lower-income individuals because it takes a larger percentage of their income (e.g., sales tax).


**Excise Tax:** A tax on specific goods or services, such as gasoline, alcohol, or tobacco, often aimed at modifying behavior.


**Estate Tax:** A tax levied on the transfer of wealth from a deceased individual's estate before distribution to heirs.


**Payroll Tax:** Taxes withheld from wages to fund programs like Social Security and Medicare.


**Tariff:** A tax imposed on imported goods to protect domestic industries or generate government revenue.


## Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rates


Understanding the difference between these two rates is necessary for accurate tax planning.


### Marginal Tax Rate


The marginal tax rate is the rate at which your last dollar of income is taxed. It represents the highest tax bracket your income reaches. This rate determines how much tax you'll pay on additional income, bonuses, raises, side income, or investment gains.


When deciding whether to work overtime, take a side gig, or realize investment gains, your marginal rate shows how much of that additional income you'll keep after taxes.


### Effective Tax Rate


The effective tax rate is your average tax rate, the percentage of your total income that goes to taxes. Calculate it by dividing your total tax paid by your total taxable income.


**Formula:** Effective Tax Rate = Total Tax Paid ÷ Total Taxable Income


Your effective rate gives you the complete picture of your tax burden. It's always lower than your marginal rate because lower portions of your income are taxed at lower rates.


**Example:** Single filer with $60,000 of taxable income in 2026:


- Marginal rate: 22% (highest bracket reached)
- Effective rate: 13.2% (actual percentage paid on total income)


## 1. Income Taxes


Income taxes form the foundation of federal revenue and most state revenue systems. These taxes are levied on earnings from all sources: salaries, wages, business profits, investment income, rental income, and more.


The U.S. federal income tax system uses a progressive structure. Higher income levels face increasing tax rates, ensuring those with greater earnings contribute a larger share. This progressive design aims to distribute the tax burden equitably across income levels.


### How Federal Income Tax Works


Federal income tax operates on marginal tax rates. Your income is divided into brackets, with each bracket taxed at a specific rate. You don't pay your highest rate on all income, only on dollars that fall within each bracket.


### 2026 Federal Income Tax Brackets

### How Marginal Brackets Work: Real Example


- Single filer with $60,000 of taxable income in 2026:
- First $12,400 taxed at 10% = $1,240.00
- $12,401–$50,400 taxed at 12% = $4,560.00
- $50,401–$60,000 taxed at 22% = $2,112.00
- Total federal income tax: $7,912
- Effective tax rate: 13.2% | Marginal tax rate: 22%


This taxpayer is "in the 22% bracket" but pays an effective rate of only 13.2% because lower portions of income are taxed at 10% and 12%.


### 2026 Standard Deduction


Most taxpayers reduce their taxable income using the standard deduction:


- **Single or Married Filing Separately:** $16,100
- **Married Filing Jointly:** $32,200
- **Head of Household:** $24,150


These amounts are adjusted annually for inflation. Taxpayers can choose between the standard deduction or itemizing deductions (mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable contributions, medical expenses above a threshold). Most taxpayers find the standard deduction more beneficial.

### State and Local Income Taxes


Forty-one states levy income taxes, though rates and structures vary significantly:


**States with no income tax:**


- Alaska, Florida, Nevada, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, Wyoming


**Highest state income tax rates:**


- California: 13.3%
- Hawaii: 11%
- New York: 10.9%
- New Jersey: 10.75%


State income taxes reduce the benefit of earning additional income because they stack on top of federal taxes. A California resident in the 32% federal bracket effectively faces a 45.3% marginal rate on additional income.


### Capital Gains Tax


Capital gains tax is a subset of income taxes, applied to profits from selling assets like stocks, real estate, or business interests.


**Long-term capital gains** (assets held over one year) benefit from preferential tax rates:


- **0%** for taxpayers in the 10% or 12% ordinary income brackets
- **15%** for taxpayers in the 22%, 24%, 32%, or 35% brackets
- **20%** for taxpayers in the 37% bracket


**Short-term capital gains** (assets held one year or less) are taxed at ordinary income rates, the same as your salary or wages.


**Why it matters:** The preferential treatment of long-term gains incentivizes investment and rewards patient capital allocation. Holding an asset just one day longer, from 364 days to 365 days, can save thousands in taxes.


**Example:** $50,000 profit on stock sale:


- Short-term gain (held 11 months): Taxed at 24% = $12,000 tax
- Long-term gain (held 13 months): Taxed at 15% = $7,500 tax
- Savings from holding 2 extra months: $4,500

## 2. Payroll Taxes


Payroll taxes are mandatory contributions tied directly to employment. These taxes fund Social Security and Medicare, providing retirement income, disability benefits, and healthcare coverage for eligible Americans.


### How Payroll Taxes Work


**Employee portion:**


- **Social Security:** 6.2% on wages up to $184,500
- **Medicare:** 1.45% on all wages (no limit)
- **Additional Medicare Tax:** 0.9% on wages above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly)


**Employer portion:**


- Employers match the employee contribution: 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare
- Employers do not match the Additional Medicare Tax


**Total payroll tax rate:** 15.3% combined (7.65% employee + 7.65% employer)


### Social Security Wage Base


For 2026, Social Security tax applies only to the first $184,500 of wages. Any earnings above this threshold are not subject to Social Security tax, though they remain subject to Medicare tax.


**Example:** Employee earning $200,000:


- Social Security tax: $184,500 × 6.2% = $11,439
- Medicare tax: $200,000 × 1.45% = $2,900
- Total payroll tax (employee portion): $14,339


Once you earn above $184,500, each additional dollar is only taxed at 1.45% for Medicare (plus 0.9% if above $200,000), not the full 7.65% rate.


### Self-Employment Tax


Self-employed individuals, freelancers, independent contractors, sole proprietors, pay both the employee and employer portions of payroll taxes. This is called self-employment tax.


**Self-employment tax rate:** 15.3%


- 12.4% for Social Security (on first $184,500 of net self-employment income)
- 2.9% for Medicare (on all net self-employment income)
- Additional 0.9% Medicare tax applies above income thresholds


**Small benefit:** Self-employed individuals can deduct the employer-equivalent portion (half) of self-employment tax when calculating adjusted gross income, slightly reducing the overall burden.


**Example:** Freelancer with $80,000 net self-employment income:


- Net earnings subject to SE tax: $80,000 × 92.35% = $73,880
- Self-employment tax: $73,880 × 15.3% = $11,304
- Deductible employer-equivalent portion (half): $5,652


Self-employment tax is one reason many independent contractors earn less take-home pay than W-2 employees at the same gross income level.


## 3. Corporate Taxes


Corporate taxes are levied on business profits earned by corporations. These taxes apply to C-corporations but not pass-through entities like S-corporations, partnerships, or sole proprietorships.


### Federal Corporate Tax Rate


**2026 federal corporate tax rate:** 21% (flat rate)


This rate was established by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, which replaced a progressive corporate rate structure (ranging from 15% to 35%) with a single flat rate.


### State Corporate Taxes


Most states impose additional corporate income taxes, with rates varying significantly:


**Highest state corporate tax rates:**


- New Jersey: 11.5%
- Pennsylvania: 9.99%
- Minnesota: 9.8%


**States with no corporate income tax:**


- Nevada, Ohio (commercial activity tax instead), South Dakota, Texas (franchise tax instead), Washington (gross receipts tax instead), Wyoming


Combined federal and state rates determine where businesses choose to incorporate and locate operations. A corporation in New Jersey faces an effective rate of 32.5% (21% federal + 11.5% state), while a corporation in Nevada faces only the 21% federal rate.


### Pass-Through Entities Avoid Double Taxation


Pass-through entities, S-corporations, LLCs, partnerships, sole proprietorships, don't pay corporate income tax. Instead, profits "pass through" to owners' individual tax returns, taxed at personal income tax rates.


This avoids the double taxation that C-corporations face:


1. Corporation pays 21% on profits
2. Shareholders pay capital gains tax (0%, 15%, or 20%) on dividends


**Qualified Business Income (QBI) Deduction:** Pass-through entity owners may deduct up to 20% of qualified business income, effectively reducing their tax rate on business profits. OBBBA made this 20% deduction permanent (it was previously scheduled to expire after 2025). This deduction phases out at higher income levels and has limitations for specified service businesses.

## 4. Consumption Taxes


Consumption taxes are levied when you purchase goods or services. Unlike income taxes, which tax what you earn, consumption taxes apply to what you spend.


### Sales Tax


Sales tax is a percentage added to the purchase price of most goods and many services. Rates and rules vary by state and locality.


**State sales tax rates (examples):**


- **California:** 7.25% base + local taxes (up to 10.75% in some cities)
- **Texas:** 6.25% state + local taxes
- **New York:** 4% state + local taxes (8.875% in New York City)


**States with no sales tax:**


- Alaska (local jurisdictions may impose), Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire, Oregon


**What's typically exempt from sales tax:**


- Groceries (in most states)
- Prescription medications
- Medical services


**What's typically taxable:**


- Clothing (in most states)
- Electronics
- Prepared food
- Most services (varies by state)


### Why Sales Tax is Regressive


Everyone pays the same rate regardless of income, but lower-income households spend a larger percentage of their income on taxable goods. This makes sales tax regressive, it takes a larger share of income from low earners than high earners.


**Example:**


- Household earning $30,000 spends $25,000 on taxable goods
- Household earning $150,000 spends $50,000 on taxable goods
- At 8% sales tax:


- Low-income household pays $2,000 (6.7% of income)
- High-income household pays $4,000 (2.7% of income)


### Excise Taxes


Excise taxes target specific goods, often to discourage consumption or fund related programs.


**Common federal excise taxes:**


- **Gasoline:** $0.184 per gallon
- **Diesel fuel:** $0.244 per gallon
- **Cigarettes:** $1.01 per pack (plus state taxes ranging from $0.17 to $5.35)
- **Alcohol:** Varies by type (beer, wine, spirits)
- **Airline tickets:** 7.5% of ticket price plus fixed fees
- **Firearms and ammunition:** 10-11% (funds wildlife conservation)


**Purpose:**


1. Generate revenue for specific programs (e.g., gas tax funds highway maintenance)
2. Modify behavior (e.g., reduce smoking through higher cigarette prices)
3. Recoup societal costs (e.g., alcohol taxes offset healthcare costs)


Excise taxes are typically "hidden" in the product price rather than added at checkout, making them less visible than sales taxes.


## 5. Property Taxes


Property taxes are local taxes assessed on real estate, both residential and commercial. They're the primary funding source for local services: schools, police, fire departments, libraries, and road maintenance.


### How Property Tax Is Calculated


**Formula:** Property Tax = Assessed Value × Local Tax Rate


**Assessment process:**


1. Local assessor determines your property's fair market value
2. Assessment ratio is applied (varies by jurisdiction, typically 80-100% of market value)
3. Tax rate is set by local government based on budget needs
4. Property owners receive annual tax bill


**Example:**


Home with fair market value of $300,000:


- Assessment ratio: 100%
- Assessed value: $300,000
- Local tax rate: 1.2%
- Annual property tax: $3,600


### Property Tax Rates by State (2026 Average)


**Highest:**


- New Jersey: 2.13% (average $9,500/year)
- Illinois: 1.97%
- Connecticut: 1.79%
- New Hampshire: 1.77%


**Lowest:**


- Hawaii: 0.31%
- Alabama: 0.37%
- Louisiana: 0.52%
- Delaware: 0.59%


Location decisions significantly impact property tax burden. A $400,000 home in New Jersey costs $8,520 annually in property taxes, while the same home in Hawaii costs only $1,240.


### Property Tax Exemptions


Many jurisdictions offer exemptions that reduce your taxable property value:


**Common exemptions:**


- **Homestead exemption:** Reduces assessed value on primary residences (typically $25,000-$50,000)
- **Senior citizen exemption:** Additional reduction for homeowners 65+
- **Veteran exemption:** Benefits for military veterans
- **Disability exemption:** For homeowners with qualifying disabilities


Always apply for exemptions you qualify for, they can save hundreds to thousands annually.


### Property Tax Deduction (Federal Income Tax)


Homeowners who itemize deductions on their federal return can deduct property taxes paid, subject to the SALT cap. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act raised this cap from $10,000 to $40,000 for 2025 through 2029 (indexed up ~1%/year, so roughly $40,400 in 2026). The higher cap phases down for taxpayers with modified AGI above $500,000 and reverts to $10,000 in 2030.


SALT cap limitation:[State and local taxes](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/state-local-tax-research) (property tax + state income tax OR sales tax) are capped at $40,000 for 2025–2029 under OBBBA, up from the $10,000 limit originally set by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. High-tax states like California, New York, and New Jersey are most affected by this limitation **.**

## 6. Estate and Gift Taxes


Estate taxes are levied on wealth transfers when someone dies. Gift taxes apply to transfers made during a person's lifetime. These taxes work together to prevent wealthy individuals from avoiding estate taxes by gifting assets before death.


### Federal Estate Tax (2026)


The federal estate basic exclusion amount is $13,990,000 for 2025 and $15,000,000 for 2026.


**Tax rate:** 40% on amounts above the exemption


Only estates exceeding the exemption threshold owe federal estate tax. For married couples, the exemption is effectively doubled through "portability", allowing a surviving spouse to use any unused exemption from their deceased spouse.


**Example:**


Individual dies with $17 million estate in 2026:


- Exemption: $15 million
- Taxable estate: $2 million
- Estate tax: $2 million × 40% = $800,000


### Gift Tax


The gift tax applies to transfers exceeding the annual exclusion amount.


**2026 annual gift tax exclusion:** $19,000 per recipient


You can give up to $19,000 to as many people as you want each year without filing a gift tax return or using any lifetime exemption. Amounts above $19,000 per recipient require filing Form 709 and count against your lifetime estate and gift tax exemption.


**Example:**


Parent gives $25,000 to an adult child in 2026:


- Annual exclusion: $19,000
- Amount using lifetime exemption: $6,000
- Remaining lifetime exemption: $14,994,000 ($15M − $6K)


### State Estate Taxes


Twelve states and the District of Columbia impose their own estate taxes, often with much lower exemption amounts than the federal level:


**States with estate tax (examples):**


- Massachusetts: $2 million exemption
- Oregon: $1 million exemption
- New York: $7.35 million exemption


Wealthy individuals often consider state estate taxes when choosing retirement locations.


## 7. Tariffs and Import Taxes


Tariffs are taxes imposed on imported goods. While technically paid by importing businesses, the costs typically pass through to consumers via higher prices.


### Purpose of Tariffs


- **Revenue generation:** Historically a major revenue source, now less significant
- **Protect domestic industries:** Make imported goods more expensive, helping domestic producers compete
- **Trade negotiations:** Used as leverage in international trade agreements
- **National security:** Protect strategic industries deemed critical to national defense


### How Tariffs Affect Consumers


When the U.S. imposes a 25% tariff on imported steel, American companies buying that steel pay 25% more. They typically pass those costs to customers through higher prices for cars, appliances, and construction.


**Example:**


- Imported washing machine costs $400 before tariff
- 20% tariff imposed: $400 × 1.20 = $480
- Consumer pays $80 more due to tariff


Studies consistently show that tariffs function as a consumption tax, consumers bear most of the burden through higher prices.
