# Manufacturer's Tax Guide 2025

## State and Local (SALT) Landscape


### Economic Nexus & P.L. 86-272 Erosion


For years, P.L. 86-272 offered a shield to out-of-state manufacturers from state income taxes, as long as their in-state activities were limited to the solicitation of orders. In 2025, that protection is under heavy erosion.


States like California, New York, and New Jersey have issued definitive guidance: digital interaction with customers can void P.L. 86-272 protections, exposing companies to new filing obligations.


#### Digital Interactions Now Count as “Business Activity”


Interactive website features such as:


- Live chat support
- Downloadable product spec sheets
- Customer portals and warranty registrations
- Product configurators or guided selection tools


are now viewed by these states as active business conduct, not mere solicitation. As a result, these features disqualify a business from relying on P.L. 86-272.


For example, a New Jersey-based manufacturer with an online platform accessible in California, offering downloadable documents or chat support, may face California franchise tax obligations if it exceeds $500,000 in California sales or engages in these digital activities.


#### Filing Positions Must Account for Economic, Not Just Physical, Nexus


With economic nexus rules expanding across states, traditional metrics like physical presence or employee footprint are no longer sufficient. For example:


- New York enforces sales tax nexus for businesses with over $500,000 in gross receipts and 100+ transactions.
- New Jersey uses a $100,000 receipts or 200 transactions threshold.


Manufacturers must now reassess income tax exposure based on digital and economic presence, not just physical operations. Over-reliance on old nexus models may result in underreporting, penalties, and audit risk.


### Sales & Use Tax Exemptions


Manufacturers often assume that production machinery and raw material exemptions shield them from sales and use tax liability. But in practice, exemption rules vary widely across states, and minor oversights can trigger full-scale audits and retroactive assessments.


#### Exemptions Are Not Uniform, and Enforcement is Tightening


- In Texas, installation labor on otherwise exempt equipment is often taxable, depending on who performs the service and how it’s billed.
- In Ohio, testing equipment is exempt if it's used in the manufacturing operation, including when testing raw materials, in-process products, or the final finished product
- In California, manufacturers may need to provide documentation that the equipment is directly used in qualified production to claim the exemption.


These nuances demand detailed operational visibility and accurate application of exemptions on a per-transaction basis.


#### Even SST States Require Proactive Exemption Maintenance


Membership in the Streamlined Sales Tax (SST) agreement does not relieve manufacturers of documentation responsibilities. SST states like New Jersey and Ohio require:


- Accurate and current exemption certificates
- Monitoring of expiration dates
- Assurance that vendor systems reflect the correct exemption status


Auditors in states like California and New York are increasingly disallowing exemptions based on incomplete or outdated paperwork, even if the transaction itself was arguably exempt.


### Apportionment & Throwback Rules


Apportionment formulas determine how a multistate manufacturer’s income is divided among states for tax purposes. But what often flies under the radar is the “throwback rule”, a provision in some states that can significantly inflate tax exposure for businesses selling across state lines.


#### Understanding Throwback


In states that apply the throwback rule, sales of tangible personal property shipped to customers in states where the seller isn’t taxable must be “thrown back” to the origin state, usually the state of shipment or where the sale was negotiated.


For example, a manufacturer based in Illinois ships to a customer in Montana, a state where it has no nexus. If Montana doesn’t tax the sale, Illinois can claim that sale in its income tax base, even though the revenue never came from Illinois customers.


This creates a double exposure problem: manufacturers may pay tax on revenue in a state they barely operate in simply because another state doesn’t claim jurisdiction.


#### Apportionment Shifts


While most states have moved toward a single-sales factor formula, complexities remain:


- Market-based sourcing may apply to service revenue (e.g., engineering services, design consulting), requiring careful tracking of where the benefit is received.
- Cost-of-performance vs. destination sourcing distinctions can skew the apportionment factor, especially for vertically integrated manufacturers with R&D and production in different locations.


Manufacturers should regularly model state-by-state sales and sourcing logic, particularly for large B2B or OEM contracts, where misunderstanding delivery terms or customer use locations could result in unintentional over-apportionment.


### Credits & Incentives


R&D tax credits and related state-level incentives remain a high-potential but underclaimed opportunity for manufacturers. As of 2025, 37 states offer R&D tax credits, yet many companies fail to benefit fully due to compliance fatigue, inconsistent documentation, or misunderstanding of state-by-state differences.


#### R&D Tax Credits


While most manufacturers are familiar with the federal R&D credit under IRC §41, state credits introduce another layer of complexity:


- Base period calculations vary. Some states use fixed-base percentage models, while others adopt rolling averages or industry-specific benchmarks. These distinctions affect both eligibility and benefit calculations.
- Qualifying expenses may exclude third-party contractor costs or certain employee wages unless predefined thresholds are met. It’s crucial to align state rules with the federal four-part test while also accounting for local definitions and exceptions.
- Refundability and carry-forward provisions diverge significantly. For instance, California imposes strict limitations on how credits can be used but still offers substantial benefits to innovation-heavy firms. In contrast, Georgia, Arizona, and Connecticut provide more generous credit structures, some of which are refundable or transferable.


**Key Tip:** Even if a federal R&D credit is claimed, failing to tailor documentation to individual state requirements can result in disallowance during state audits.


#### Site Selection & Expansion


Beyond R&D, many states offer targeted incentives for capital investment, job creation, and facility expansion. These include:


- Investment tax credits for qualifying machinery or infrastructure spend
- Hiring and workforce training credits linked to headcount thresholds or local wage levels
- Discretionary grants offered by state economic development authorities, often triggered by early-stage engagement and pre-certification applications


States like Arizona offer specialized incentives for renewable energy and advanced manufacturing investments, while local jurisdictions frequently provide property and sales tax abatements for new plant construction or equipment upgrades.


For example, California offers a partial sales tax exemption on manufacturing equipment, but manufacturers must register and track usage to remain compliant.


## Payroll & Labor-Related Tax Items


### Work Opportunity Tax Credit (WOTC)


The[Work Opportunity Tax Credit](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/work-opportunity-tax-credit) (WOTC) has been officially extended through December 31, 2025, under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021. Designed to incentivize hiring from specific groups facing barriers to employment, the WOTC remains one of the most valuable yet underutilized federal employment credits, particularly in industries like manufacturing, where variable hiring patterns and onboarding inefficiencies can result in missed opportunities.


#### Targeted Groups and Credit Potential


The credit applies to employers who hire individuals from eligible groups, including:


- Qualified veterans
- Recipients of SNAP or TANF
- Long-term unemployed individuals
- Individuals with prior felony convictions


The maximum credit can reach $9,600 per qualified hire, primarily for certain veteran categories, with final credit size depending on both the target group classification and hours worked within the first year.


#### Compliance and Documentation Steps


To claim the credit, employers must follow a strict timeline:


- Screen new hires at the point of hire using IRS Form 8850 and ETA Form 9061
- Submit certification requests within 28 days of the employee's start date to the state workforce agency
- Track employee retention and hours worked to calculate the final credit value


### Employee Retention Credit (ERC) look-back


Originally introduced as a pandemic relief measure, the Employee Retention Credit (ERC) has now become one of the most heavily scrutinized areas of payroll tax compliance.


Following a flood of improper and ineligible claims, the IRS stopped processing any ERC claims submitted on or after September 14, 2023 (IR-2023-169). Beginning August 8, 2024, the IRS resumed only “risk-based” reviews of the backlog for claims filed between Sept. 14, 2023, and Jan. 31, 2024, while keeping a freeze on later-filed claims. At the same time, the Service has ramped up compliance—issuing disallowance letters, launching a voluntary-disclosure program (now closed), and pursuing audits and criminal investigations—an enforcement push that is slated to run well into 2025.


As of April 15, 2025, the window for filing ERC claims officially closed.


#### Key Risks Under Audit Focus


The IRS has identified several recurring issues that trigger audits or repayment demands:


- Eligibility misstatements, including claims filed without a qualifying decline in gross receipts or without a government-ordered suspension of operations
- Improper wage calculations, particularly cases involving double-dipping with PPP loan forgiveness
- Misleading marketing and filings promoted by third-party ERC firms, many of which are now under investigation


*IRS guidance emphasizes that businesses should not rely solely on credit promoters and must ensure that claims are grounded in verifiable data and legal interpretation.*


#### Recommended Actions for Manufacturers


- Conduct a full ERC review, especially if your claim was prepared by an outside advisor
- Prepare for potential audits by gathering gross receipts records, documentation of shutdowns, and wage allocation data
- Evaluate participation in the IRS’s voluntary withdrawal or repayment programs, which may help reduce penalties for claims made in error


## International & Cross-border Issues


### GILTI, FDII & Foreign Tax Credit Rules


Even as global minimum tax frameworks evolve, U.S. international tax regimes like GILTI and FDII remain firmly in place, with refinements that materially impact effective tax rates for multinational manufacturers.


#### GILTI


The Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI) regime subjects U.S. shareholders of controlled foreign corporations (CFCs) to U.S. tax on certain foreign earnings. However, under current regulations, businesses can elect to exclude high-taxed foreign income, defined as income subject to a foreign effective rate of at least 90% of the U.S. corporate rate (i.e., 18.9% or higher).


**Planning insight:** If a CFC’s income qualifies for the high-tax exclusion, manufacturers can avoid duplicative U.S. taxation and improve cash tax outcomes, particularly for operations in high-tax jurisdictions like Germany or Japan. But the election must be made per tested unit, annually, and requires GAAP-to-tax reconciliations at the local level.


#### FDII


The Foreign-Derived Intangible Income (FDII) deduction offers a preferential effective tax rate (13.125%) on qualifying income from U.S.-based goods and services sold to foreign customers. While FDII may eventually be reformed under OECD pressure, it remains a powerful tool for encouraging manufacturers to retain IP, engineering, and contract manufacturing activities in the U.S.


- Qualifying income must be from foreign use, not merely foreign delivery
- Proper segmentation of revenue streams is critical, especially for businesses with hybrid fulfillment models or U.S. drop shipments to foreign end users
- The deduction interacts with other international tax rules, so coordination with GILTI and FTC strategy is essential


#### Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) Mechanics


Recent regulations narrowed foreign tax credit eligibility—especially for royalty and service payments—by introducing a strict “jurisdictional nexus” requirement. This has raised concerns for manufacturers with intercompany licensing or cost-sharing arrangements, where foreign withholding taxes might no longer be creditable.


However, the IRS has provided transitional relief through **Notices 2023-55 and 2023-80** , allowing taxpayers to apply prior FTC rules until further notice. Companies should prepare for future enforcement by reviewing sourcing and attribution methods to avoid permanent double taxation when the relief period ends.


### BEAT & QDP


The Base Erosion and Anti-Abuse Tax (BEAT) remains a looming issue for U.S. manufacturers with significant related-party payments, particularly in the form of royalties, interest, and service fees. In 2025, the BEAT rate holds at 10%, but planning for its potential impact is far from static.


#### BEAT Mechanics


BEAT generally applies to U.S. corporations with:


- $500 million or more in average annual gross receipts, and
- Base erosion payments exceeding 3% of total deductions


For manufacturers, this often includes:


- Royalties paid to foreign IP holders
- Intercompany service charges
- Interest payments to related offshore lenders


Insight: Even companies under the $500 million threshold should model BEAT exposure in M&A or restructuring scenarios, as post-transaction combined entities may cross the limit unexpectedly.


#### QDP Election


One of the more nuanced strategies available is the Qualified Derivative Payment (QDP) election, which can shield certain derivative payments, especially those used to hedge commodity inputs, from BEAT treatment.


- This election is particularly relevant for manufacturers with exposure to raw material price fluctuations (e.g., metals, energy inputs)
- To qualify, derivative payments must be clearly identified, hedge accounting must be applied, and elective disclosures must be made annually
- QDP is often underused due to documentation complexity, but it can deliver meaningful BEAT relief when executed properly


### Pillar Two & QDMTT Exposure


The OECD’s Pillar Two global minimum tax framework is no longer just a policy concept. Dozens of countries have adopted or are implementing Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-Up Taxes (QDMTTs), and U.S.-based manufacturers with foreign subsidiaries must now prepare for real-world cash tax impacts.


#### Mexico, Ireland, and Other Jurisdictions


Key U.S. trading partners, including Mexico and Ireland, are among the countries implementing QDMTTs or in the process of doing so. These rules are designed to ensure that in-scope MNEs—those with €750 million or more in global revenue—pay at least a 15% effective tax rate in each jurisdiction where they operate.


For manufacturers, this means:


- Income earned through tax incentives or IP structures in low-tax jurisdictions may now trigger a top-up tax in that jurisdiction (under QDMTT) or in a parent jurisdiction (under the Income Inclusion Rule or IIR).
- Traditional planning structures involving low-taxed entities or cost-sharing arrangements may no longer produce the same benefit and may now carry compliance risk.


For example, a U.S. manufacturer operating through an Irish subsidiary with an effective tax rate below 15% may now face a top-up tax in Ireland under its QDMTT regime even if no additional U.S. tax is due under current GILTI rules.


#### Action Items for Manufacturers in 2025:


- Conduct a Pillar Two readiness assessment, especially for subsidiaries in low- or moderate-tax jurisdictions
- Reevaluate entity structures, IP ownership, and local tax incentives for long-term viability under QDMTT rules
- Align financial reporting systems to support Globe-compliant calculations and disclosures


### Customs & Duty Planning


While income tax planning often takes center stage, customs and duties remain a substantial and controllable cost for manufacturers with cross-border operations.


#### Section 321 de minimis


Under Section 321, qualifying shipments valued at $800 or less per day, per recipient can enter the U.S. duty-free. For manufacturers engaged in direct-to-consumer fulfillment or small-batch shipments, this remains a valuable customs exemption, but with caveats:


- Import volume thresholds are monitored, and misuse may flag customs audits
- Section 321 cannot be used to circumvent duties on higher-value orders by splitting shipments. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) has increased enforcement on this tactic
- Some product categories (e.g., regulated items, textiles) are excluded entirely


#### First-Sale Rule


The first-sale rule allows importers to use the price paid in the first arm’s-length transaction in a multi-tier supply chain as the customs value, potentially reducing the dutiable base when properly documented.


For example, a U.S. manufacturer sourcing from a contract factory in Vietnam via a trading company in Hong Kong may declare the factory’s invoice price if all regulatory conditions are met.


To leverage this:


- Contracts, invoices, and payments must clearly establish the bona fide nature of the first sale
- Documentation must be audit-ready and traceable through the supply chain
- Coordination between tax, legal, and procurement is essential to ensure compliance and audit defense


#### Emerging Considerations:


- Country of origin labeling and tariff engineering are growing areas of interest, especially under shifting geopolitical trade tensions
- CBP is deploying AI and data analytics tools to flag anomalies in declared value and sourcing, increasing audit probabilities for high-volume importers


## Accounting Alignment: GAAP, Cost Accounting & Tax


### LIFO Reserve, DTA & CAMT Reconciliation


Under the Corporate Alternative Minimum Tax (CAMT), manufacturers must compute a 15% tax on adjusted financial statement income (AFSI). This shifts focus from traditional taxable income to GAAP income, forcing closer reconciliation of book-tax differences.


#### The LIFO Reserve


For LIFO users, the LIFO reserve represents a deferred tax liability (DTL) under GAAP. But for CAMT purposes, this reserve can increase AFSI, effectively creating phantom income unless the reconciliation is addressed.


***Practical concern:*** *The larger your LIFO reserve (especially in inflationary periods), the higher the risk that CAMT accelerates tax liability before cash realization.*


#### Deferred Tax Assets (DTAs)


Some book DTAs, particularly those related to NOLs or timing differences, may not offset CAMT liability fully. Manufacturers should revisit their DTA schedules to assess:


- Which DTAs are usable under CAMT
- How valuation allowances affect CAMT relief
- Whether LIFO DTLs offset AFSI adjustments


**Insight:** CAMT is a new lens for interpreting GAAP income. Manufacturers with complex inventory and DTA positions must develop dedicated reconciliation workpapers to bridge these regimes.


### ASC 842 Lease vs. Buy


The sunset of 100% bonus depreciation changes the calculus of capital investment decisions, particularly for lease-versus-buy analysis under ASC 842.


#### Lease Accounting Under ASC 842


ASC 842 requires lessees to recognize right-of-use (ROU) assets and lease liabilities on the balance sheet, even for operating leases. While this is a non-cash entry for GAAP, it can obscure comparisons with tax-based depreciation models.


#### Bonus Depreciation


The reduced immediate deduction makes leasing relatively more attractive—but only if:


- The lease doesn’t trigger interest expense limitations under §163(j)
- The lessor structure doesn’t introduce BEAT or foreign withholding complications


**Note** : Manufacturers should refresh lease-versus-buy models to account for the declining bonus depreciation window and reconcile tax depreciation schedules with ASC 842 impacts on book income.


### CAMT Workpaper: Step-by-step Reconciliation Guide


Given CAMT’s reliance on financial statement income, traditional tax returns are no longer enough. Manufacturers should develop CAMT-specific workpapers that track book-tax differences and ensure accurate AFSI computation.


Here’s a high-level skeleton:


1. Start with net income from GAAP financials (AFSI base)
2. Add back tax-exempt income exclusions (e.g., municipal bond interest)
3. Add back certain deductions disallowed under CAMT (e.g., accelerated depreciation beyond book)
4. Remove income excluded from AFSI (e.g., qualified pension contributions, financial statement losses carried forward)
5. Adjust for consolidated reporting differences (GAAP vs. tax consolidation)
6. Reconcile deferred tax assets/liabilities and their treatment under CAMT
7. Calculate CAMT liability at 15% of AFSI
8. Compare against regular tax liability and pay the higher of the two


### IRS 10-return E-file Mandate for 2025


For returns filed on or after January 1, 2024, the IRS now requires e-filing for any business submitting 10 or more returns of any type, not just 10 of the same kind. This marks a significant shift from the prior 250-return threshold.


#### What Counts Toward the Threshold?


The 10-return threshold aggregates **all information and income tax returns** , including:


- Forms W-2 and 1099
- Form 941 (quarterly payroll returns)
- Form 1120, 1065, 5500, etc.


For instance, a manufacturer with 4 W-2s, 3 Forms 1099-MISC, 2 payroll returns, and 1 corporate return now meets the threshold.


#### Compliance Implications:


- Paper-filing is no longer viable for most manufacturers, even small ones
- Software systems must support e-file formats for multiple return types
- Inaccurate or late e-filing may result in penalties of $50–$290 per return, depending on timing


***Action item:*** *Conduct a filing volume audit now to identify affected return types, confirm e-file capabilities with your provider, and prepare for unified digital submission in the 2025 filing season.*


## Risk Management & Audit Defense


### IRS Red Flags: Lifo Liquidation, §174 Double-dips, and Ic-disc Pricing


As the IRS intensifies enforcement efforts post-IRA funding, several manufacturer-specific audit triggers have risen to the forefront, particularly those involving timing mismatches, credit misuse, and intercompany pricing.


#### LIFO Liquidation Events


Temporary inventory reductions may inadvertently trigger LIFO liquidation, where older, lower-cost inventory layers flow into cost of goods sold, boosting taxable income. Inconsistent application, especially in inflationary pricing environments, raises audit flags when:


- Layer liquidation creates a sudden margin distortion not reconciled to operational performance
- Disclosures in financials (10-Ks) acknowledge LIFO impacts, but tax returns don’t reflect corresponding income adjustments
- Excessive year-end purchasing aimed at avoiding liquidation lacks economic substance


Best practice: Maintain clear, contemporaneous documentation linking inventory movements to operational necessity, not tax minimization.


#### §174 and R&D Credit Double-counting


As covered earlier, R&D expenses must be capitalized under §174, even if they qualify for the R&D credit under §41. However, failing to “add back” amortized §174 costs when computing the credit can result in unintentional double-dipping, a red flag in IRS exams.


- Watch for software development or process improvement projects where tracking between R&D and book capitalization is loosely defined
- IRS guidance in Notice 2023-63 underscores the importance of project-level traceability


#### IC-DISC Transfer Pricing


For manufacturers with export sales using Interest Charge Domestic International Sales Corporations (IC-DISCs), transfer pricing remains one of the least defended audit areas. The IRS is increasingly focused on:


- Unrealistically high commissions not tied to economic substance
- Lack of benchmarking or cost-based substantiation
- Improper grouping of non-export transactions under DISC structures


***Tip*** *: If you haven’t updated your IC-DISC pricing model in the past two years, you may already be out of compliance.*


### State Audit Hot Spots


State revenue agencies, often more aggressive than the IRS, are using nexus expansion and property audits to raise revenue, and manufacturers are frequently in the crosshairs.


#### Economic Nexus Enforcement


Interactive websites and customer portals have redefined nexus thresholds in states like California, New York, and New Jersey. But beyond income tax exposure, sales tax and franchise tax filings are also being triggered.


- Many states now audit clickstream activity, digital invoices, and remote service logs to assert nexus
- “No-tax-due” filings or past non-filer status can draw scrutiny if activity is identified through third-party data (e.g., payment processors, shipping records)


#### Throwback Rule Misapplications


States like Massachusetts, Illinois, and Oregon aggressively enforce throwback rules. For manufacturers, incorrectly sourcing sales to states where no tax return is filed can result in a retroactive income inclusion back to the origin state.


- Risk heightens for drop-shipped or contract-manufactured goods
- Sales to foreign jurisdictions with uncertain nexus often get thrown back by default unless properly defended


#### Property Tax Audits


In jurisdictions with centralized business property tax systems, assessors increasingly audit:


- Outdated or unretired assets still showing on depreciation schedules
- Mismatched book and local asset registers
- Leased equipment improperly reported by both lessor and lessee


***Defensive move*** *: Conduct a fixed asset cleanup annually, flag retired or idle machinery, and match state depreciation methods with your local tax valuation schedules.*


### Conclusion


The 2025 tax environment brings heightened complexity for manufacturers, from federal shifts like §174 amortization and CAMT, to evolving state nexus rules and global minimum tax enforcement. These changes demand more than routine compliance, they require strategic coordination across tax, finance, and operations.


Manufacturers that respond with precision won’t just stay compliant. They’ll gain margin, reduce audit risk, and move faster than competitors still playing by outdated assumptions.


The rules have changed. So must the approach.
