# State & Local Tax (SALT) Research: A Guide to Nexus and Compliance

State and Local Tax (SALT) research involves identifying, interpreting, and applying tax rules across thousands of U.S. state, county, and municipal jurisdictions. Compared with federal tax research, which flows from a single, centralized code, SALT demands far more jurisdiction-specific analysis, because states, and for some taxes localities, may apply different tax bases, rates, nexus standards, sourcing rules, exemptions, filing obligations, and administrative guidance. No two jurisdictions treat a transaction, an asset, or a dollar of income exactly alike.


More than seven years after *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.* rewrote the rules on nexus, that fragmentation has only grown more consequential. For multistate businesses, SALT research now spans sales and use tax collection; income, franchise, and gross receipts taxes; property taxes; and excise and industry-specific taxes, each with its own registration, valuation, and filing regime. The result is a compliance matrix that varies not just by state, but often by locality, where accuracy and jurisdiction-specific expertise aren't optional; they're the baseline.


While our[Ultimate Guide to Tax Research](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/tax-research) provides a broad overview of tax research methods and tools, this guide focuses specifically on the nuances of SALT research, from determining nexus to managing apportionment and allocation. Unlike the uniform nature of[Federal Tax Research](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/federal-tax-research) , SALT is defined by its diversity, and a wrong answer can be costly, so it pays to build a workflow that produces[defensible tax research](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/there-is-a-difference-between-defensible-tax-research-and-just-tax-research-tools) , not just a fast one. Whether you're tracking down taxability rules in Ohio or sourcing income for a California return, the local landscape matters.


## What Are the Main Categories of State and Local Tax?


State and local governments impose a wide range of taxes, including sales and use taxes, income and franchise taxes, property taxes, excise taxes, gross receipts taxes, payroll-related taxes, and industry-specific fees. For many multistate businesses, SALT research is best organized around four core categories: **sales and use taxes** , **income, franchise, and gross receipts taxes** , **property taxes** , and **excise and industry-specific taxes** . Each is imposed on a different base, administered under different rules, and governed by its own registration, valuation, and filing regimes—so they should be researched separately rather than lumped together.


Let's understand the scope and structure of each category before tackling jurisdiction-specific rules.


### Sales and Use Tax


State and local governments impose a wide range of taxes, including sales and use taxes, income and franchise taxes, property taxes, excise taxes, gross receipts taxes, payroll-related taxes, and industry-specific fees. For many multistate businesses, SALT research is best organized around four core categories: sales and use taxes, income, franchise, and gross receipts taxes, property taxes, and excise and industry-specific taxes. Each is imposed on a different base, administered under different rules, and governed by its own registration, valuation, and filing regimes, so they should be researched separately rather than lumped together.


### Sales and Use Tax


Sales and Use Tax generally applies to sales, leases, or uses of tangible personal property, and some states also tax specified services; however, service taxability is highly state-specific. While sales tax is collected by the seller at the point of sale, use tax is generally imposed on the storage, use, or consumption of taxable property when sales tax was not collected, although modern state rules may require remote sellers or marketplace facilitators to collect and remit the tax.


What makes this area particularly complex is the variation across jurisdictions in:


- Tax rates (which can differ at the state, county, and city levels)
- Taxability rules for different products and services
- The determination of a taxable presence (nexus) and whether a remote seller must collect and remit tax


Compliance often hinges on understanding economic nexus thresholds, proper sales tax remittance procedures, and the unique guidance of each state's tax authority, such as the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration (CDTFA), which administers California sales and use tax and many special taxes and fees.


### Income, Franchise, and Gross Receipts Tax


While sales tax is tied to transactions, income taxes generally focus on net income, while franchise taxes may be imposed for the privilege of doing business and may be measured by income, capital, net worth, or fixed/minimum amounts depending on the state.


- Income Tax is typically levied on net income, requiring businesses to calculate and report earnings attributable to each state where they have a taxable presence.
- Franchise tax rules vary significantly by state. In some states, the tax may be measured by net worth, capital stock, or another business metric; in others, it may operate as a minimum tax or tax for the privilege of doing business, and it may apply even where the business has little or no net income.
- Gross receipts taxes are a distinct base and should not be confused with income tax. They are imposed on a business's total revenue regardless of profitability, so a company can owe them even in a loss year. Examples include the Washington Business & Occupation (B&O) tax, the Ohio and Oregon Commercial Activity Taxes (CAT), the Texas franchise/margin tax, and the Nevada Commerce Tax.


Franchise and gross receipts taxes can apply even if a business operates at a loss, which makes them especially important to monitor in SALT compliance research.


### Property Taxes


Property taxes are a core SALT category because they are generally imposed by state or local jurisdictions on real or personal property, rather than on sales transactions or net income. They should be analyzed separately from sales/use taxes and income/franchise taxes because the tax base, valuation rules, assessment procedures, exemptions, appeals, and local administration are different. For asset-heavy businesses, property tax is often one of the largest state and local liabilities, and it is typically administered at the county or municipal level rather than by the state revenue department.


This category may include real property taxes and, in some jurisdictions, business personal property taxes on machinery, equipment, furniture, fixtures, or inventory-type assets, depending on state and local law.


### Excise and Industry-Specific Taxes


State and local governments impose a wide range of taxes, including sales and use taxes, income and franchise taxes, property taxes, excise taxes, gross receipts taxes, payroll-related taxes, and industry-specific fees. For many multistate businesses, SALT research is best organized around four core categories: (1) sales and use taxes; (2) income, franchise, and gross receipts taxes; (3) property taxes; and (4) excise and industry-specific taxes. Each category is imposed on a different base, administered under different rules, and governed by its own registration, valuation, filing, and audit regimes, so they should be researched separately rather than lumped together.


## What Is Nexus in State and Local Tax?


Nexus is a foundational concept in SALT research. It refers to the minimum connection a business must have with a state before that state may impose tax or tax-collection obligations. If a business has nexus in a jurisdiction, it may be required to register, collect tax, file returns, remit tax, or otherwise comply with that jurisdiction's rules, depending on the tax type and state law.


But determining nexus is far from simple. The definition of a "sufficient connection" varies across jurisdictions, and the standards have evolved significantly over time, especially after the digital economy blurred the lines of physical presence.


### Physical Presence Nexus


Historically, physical presence was central to sales/use tax nexus. A business may still create physical-presence nexus by having in-state activity such as:


- An office, storefront, or warehouse
- Inventory stored within the state, including inventory held in third-party fulfillment centers, depending on the state's rules and the facts
- Employees, representatives, agents, or contractors conducting business on its behalf


Physical presence remains relevant for sales/use tax, income/franchise tax, and other SALT regimes, even though it is no longer a constitutional prerequisite for sales/use tax collection after *Wayfair* .


### Economic Nexus: The Impact of South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.


The *Wayfair* decision in 2018 fundamentally changed the SALT landscape. In *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.* , the U.S. Supreme Court overruled the prior physical-presence rule and held that substantial nexus may exist based on significant economic activity in a state, even where the seller has no physical presence.


The South Dakota law at issue applied to sellers delivering more than $100,000 of goods or services into the state or engaging in 200 or more separate transactions, and the Court found nexus clearly sufficient for the large remote sellers before it. Those specific figures describe South Dakota's statute at issue in *Wayfair* , each state sets its own threshold, and some states use sales-dollar thresholds without a separate transaction-count test.


For[California sales and use tax](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/california-state-tax-guide) , a retailer is treated as engaged in business in California if, during the preceding or current calendar year, the retailer and related persons have total combined sales of tangible personal property for delivery in California exceeding $500,000; such retailers generally must register with CDTFA and collect California use tax on taxable sales delivered into California.


Note that sales/use tax nexus thresholds and income/franchise tax "doing business" thresholds are not always the same. In California, CDTFA's sales/use tax collection threshold is separate from the Franchise Tax Board's (FTB) income/franchise tax doing-business tests, which look to whether the business engages in a transaction in California for financial gain, is organized or commercially domiciled in California, or exceeds California sales, property, or payroll thresholds.

Nexus thresholds vary by state, and missing one can lead to costly back tax liabilities, penalties, and interest. That's why nexus studies have become a common SALT research task for growing businesses.


For income/franchise tax, establishing nexus or "doing business" is only the first step. The next questions are whether a federal net-income-tax protection such as P.L. 86-272 applies and, if tax applies, how much of the business's income is apportioned or allocated to that jurisdiction.


## How Is Business Income Divided Between States?


Once a business has income tax nexus, or is otherwise taxable, in more than one state, the next challenge is determining how much income each state may tax. This requires understanding two related but distinct SALT concepts: apportionment and allocation.


### Apportionment vs. Allocation


At a high level:


- Apportionment divides a business's business income among states using a formula.
- Allocation assigns nonbusiness income to a particular state.


In California, these two categories are defined as:


- Business income — income arising from transactions and activity in the regular course of the taxpayer's trade or business, including income from tangible and intangible property when the acquisition, management, and disposition of that property are integral parts of the regular business operations.
- Nonbusiness income — all income other than business income.


### Apportionment Formulas


California generally uses a single-sales-factor approach for most apportioning businesses, while certain qualified business activities use a three-factor formula based on property, payroll, and sales:


- Most businesses apportion income using the single-sales factor.
- Businesses deriving more than 50% of gross receipts from qualified business activities use the three-factor formula.
- For tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2025, California's qualified business activities for the three-factor rule are agricultural and extractive activities only.


The sales factor is generally a fraction, California sales over total sales everywhere. For sales of tangible personal property:


- Sales are assigned to California if the property is delivered or shipped to a purchaser in California.
- California-origin shipments are also assigned to California when the purchaser is the U.S. government, or when the taxpayer is not taxable in the purchaser's state.


### Sourcing Complexities


Apportionment isn't always straightforward. States differ on issues such as throwback rules, market-based sourcing, and cost-of-performance sourcing. California applies market assignment for sales *other than* tangible personal property:


- Services — assigned to California to the extent the purchaser receives the benefit of the service in California.
- Intangible property — assigned to California to the extent the property is used in California.
- Real property (sales, leases, or licensing) — assigned to California if the real property is located in California.
- Rentals, leases, or licenses of tangible personal property — assigned to California if the property is located in California.


### Allocation


Allocation, by contrast, applies to nonbusiness income, which is assigned under specific state allocation statutes rather than the business-income apportionment formula. In California, rents and royalties from real or tangible personal property, capital gains, interest, dividends, and patent or copyright royalties are allocated under California's allocation rules to the extent they constitute nonbusiness income.


It's also worth noting that income excluded from California "net income" subject to apportionment is generally excluded from the apportionment formula as well.
