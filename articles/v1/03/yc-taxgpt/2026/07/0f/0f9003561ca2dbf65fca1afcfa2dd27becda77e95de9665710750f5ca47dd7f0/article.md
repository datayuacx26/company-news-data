# Filing US Taxes as an Expat

## FBAR and FATCA Compliance for Expats


U.S. expats must adhere to specific reporting requirements under the Foreign Bank Account Report (FBAR) and the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) to avoid penalties.


Follow these steps to ensure compliance:


### FBAR Requirements


- **Who Must File** : U.S. individuals and entities—including citizens, residents, corporations, partnerships, and trusts—are required to file an FBAR if they have a financial interest in or authority over[foreign accounts totaling over $10,000](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar) at any point during the calendar year.
- **Filing Deadline** : The FBAR is due by April 15 each year, with an automatic extension to October 15 if the initial deadline isn’t met.
- **How to File** : File the FBAR electronically through FinCEN’s BSA E-Filing System. For those unable to file electronically, FinCEN provides alternative filing options upon request.
- **Penalties** : Not filing an FBAR can result in severe civil and criminal penalties, which may include fines or imprisonment.


### FATCA Requirements


- **Who Must File** : U.S. taxpayers with certain foreign assets above set thresholds must submit Form 8938. These thresholds vary based on filing status and residency:some text


- **Single or married filing separately** : $50,000 on the last day of the tax year or $75,000 at any time during the year.
- **Married filing jointly** : $100,000 on the last day of the tax year or $150,000 at any time during the year.
- Higher thresholds apply to those living abroad.


- **Filing Deadline** : Form 8938 should be attached to the taxpayer’s annual return and filed by the standard tax deadline (including any extensions).
- **Penalties** : Failing to file Form 8938 may lead to a $10,000 fine, with further penalties for continued non-compliance, up to a maximum of $50,000.


### Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures


- **When to Use:** For taxpayers who do not need to use the streamlined procedures or the IRS Criminal Investigation Voluntary Disclosure Practice.
- **Requirements:** some text


- File all delinquent FBARs electronically.
- Include a statement explaining why the FBARs are filed late.
- Ensure all income from the foreign accounts was reported and taxed on U.S. tax returns.


- **Penalties:** The IRS will not impose a penalty if the taxpayer meets all the requirements and has not been previously contacted regarding an income tax examination or request for delinquent returns.


## Child Tax Credit for Expats


Many U.S. expats wonder if they’re eligible to claim the **Child Tax Credit (CTC)** while living abroad. The Child Tax Credit offers a potential tax benefit for parents, allowing qualifying taxpayers to receive a credit for each dependent child under 17. Although expats can typically claim this credit, there are specific conditions and limitations that apply.


### Eligibility for Expats


To qualify for the Child Tax Credit as an expat, you must meet certain requirements:


- **U.S. Citizenship** : The child must be a U.S. citizen, U.S. national, or U.S. resident alien.
- **Social Security Number** : Your dependent child must have a valid Social Security number.
- **Residency Requirement** : Expats must also meet the IRS residency requirement, which means living with the child for more than half the year.


### Limitations and Refundable Portion


While most eligible U.S. taxpayers can receive up to **$2,000** per child as a credit, expats living abroad face specific limitations:


- **Non-Refundable Credit** : For expats who claim the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE), only the non-refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit is available, meaning it can reduce your tax liability to zero but won’t provide a cash refund.
- **Refundable Credit Cap** : If you do not claim the FEIE and meet additional income requirements, you may be eligible for a partial refund of up to **$1,500** per qualifying child.


*If an expat with one qualifying child claims the FEIE, they may use the Child Tax Credit to lower their remaining U.S. tax liability to zero but will not receive a cash refund. However, if they forgo the FEIE, they may qualify for the refundable portion of the CTC if other eligibility criteria are met.*


### How to Claim the Credit


To claim the Child Tax Credit, expats must file **Form 1040** and complete **Schedule 8812** , where they’ll calculate the credit based on their income and dependents. This helps ensure that you correctly apply the credit amount, maximizing potential tax relief.


## Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures for Expats


The Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures are designed to help U.S. expats who have unintentionally missed filing their taxes to come into compliance with U.S. tax laws.


### Eligibility Criteria


To be eligible for the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures, U.S. expats must meet the following criteria:


- **Non-Willful Conduct:** The failure to report income, pay tax, and submit all required information returns, including FBARs, must be due to non-willful conduct.[Non-willful conduct](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) is defined as conduct that is due to negligence, inadvertence, or mistake, or conduct that is the result of a good faith misunderstanding of the requirements of the law.
- **Non-Residency Requirement:** For the Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures, the individual must meet the non-residency requirement. This means that in any one or more of the most recent three years for which the U.S. tax return due date has passed, the individual did not have a U.S. abode and was physically outside the United States for at least 330 full days.
- **Previously Filed Tax Returns:** For the Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures, the individual must have previously filed a U.S. tax return (if required) for each of the most recent three years for which the U.S. tax return due date has passed.


### Filing Requirements


The filing requirements for the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures include:


- **Amended Tax Returns:** U.S. taxpayers must file amended tax returns for each of the[most recent three years](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/us-taxpayers-residing-outside-the-united-states) for which the U.S. tax return due date has passed. These returns must include all required information returns (e.g., Forms 3520, 3520-A, 5471, 5472, 8938, 926, and 8621).
- **FBARs:** Taxpayers must file any delinquent FBARs (FinCEN Form 114) for each of the most recent six years for which the FBAR due date has passed.
- **Miscellaneous Offshore Penalty:** For the Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures, taxpayers must pay a[Title 26 miscellaneous offshore penalty equal to 5%](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/us-taxpayers-residing-in-the-united-states) of the highest aggregate balance/value of the taxpayer’s foreign financial assets that are subject to the penalty during the years in the covered tax return period and the covered FBAR period.
- **Certification of Non-Willful Conduct:** Taxpayers must submit a certification statement (Form 14653 for foreign procedures or Form 14654 for domestic procedures) certifying that their failure to report all income, pay all tax, and submit all required information returns, including FBARs, was due to non-willful conduct.


## Tax Treaties and Their Benefits for Expats


A tax treaty is an agreement between two countries designed to establish tax rules for individuals and entities that earn income across borders. These agreements help prevent double taxation, meaning individuals aren’t taxed on the same income by both the United States and the country where they reside. Each treaty has its own specific terms, but many include provisions for income types such as wages, dividends, interest, and pensions, providing tax relief on these earnings.


### Benefits of Tax Treaties for U.S. Expats


Tax treaties offer several advantages for U.S. expatriates, including:


1. **Reduced Tax Rates on Specific Income** : Many treaties lower tax rates on certain types of income—such as interest, dividends, and royalties—that would otherwise be subject to higher tax rates in either the U.S. or the foreign country.
2. **Exemption from Foreign Social Security Contributions** : Some treaties allow U.S. expats to avoid paying social security taxes abroad if they are already contributing to the U.S. Social Security system, resulting in potential savings.
3. **Prevention of Double Taxation** : Treaties typically contain provisions to ensure that income is only taxed once, either in the U.S. or the host country. Expats can often use credits or exclusions to offset any remaining tax obligations.


### Steps to Claim Treaty Benefits


To claim tax treaty benefits, U.S. expats should:


1. **Confirm Eligibility** : Review the terms of the tax treaty for your host country to determine which benefits apply to your situation.
2. **Submit Necessary Documentation** : Expats commonly use Form 8833 to report treaty-based tax positions to the IRS.
3. **Manage Social Security Taxation** : Under a Totalization Agreement (a specific type of tax treaty focused on social security),[U.S. expats can avoid social security taxes](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/totalization-agreements) in their host country. This can be especially beneficial in countries like the UK, which has such an agreement with the U.S.


### Limitations of Tax Treaties


While tax treaties provide valuable tax relief, they may not cover all types of income or offer complete exemptions from foreign taxes. Certain benefits require documentation, and not every country has a tax treaty with the U.S., so some expats may not qualify for treaty-based relief depending on their location.


## Final Thoughts

Take advantage of expat-specific provisions like the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion, Foreign Tax Credit, and Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures if you’re catching up on filings. Also, stay compliant with FBAR and FATCA requirements to avoid penalties, and review relevant tax treaties for relief from double taxation.


Whether you file independently or with professional help, understanding these steps empowers you to make informed decisions and maximize your tax benefits.
