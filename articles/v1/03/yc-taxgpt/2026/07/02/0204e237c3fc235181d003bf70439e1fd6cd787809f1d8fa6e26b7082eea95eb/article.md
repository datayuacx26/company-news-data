# 22 Tax Forms Every US Taxpayer Must Know in 2025 (Or Risk Leaving Money on the Table)

Don't leave money on the table — or trigger an IRS notice


You're staring at a pile of tax documents, and one question keeps nagging you: "Am I missing a form?"


It's not paranoia. The IRS maintains 800+ tax forms — and every year, errors and omissions cost taxpayers real money in missed deductions and credits, while mismatches between what you report and what the IRS already has on file routinely trigger automated notices (such as a CP2000).


Here's the good news: you don't need 800 forms. Most individual taxpayers file accurately with just a handful — Form 1040 plus a few schedules and the W-2s and 1099s you already receive in the mail.


This guide walks you through exactly which forms matter for your situation — from the essential Form 1040 to the specialized schedules used by freelancers, investors, homeowners, and retirees. Where a topic gets more technical, we've flagged it so tax professionals can dig deeper while everyone else can skim past.


📌 A quick note on tax years: This guide covers the 2025 tax year — the return you file in early 2026. The dollar figures below (standard deduction, SALT cap, etc.) are 2025 amounts. See the "Looking ahead to 2026" box near the end for what's changing on next year's return.

## Understanding the Foundation: Form 1040


Form 1040 is the core individual income tax return. Nearly every taxpayer uses it (or a variation) to report annual income, claim deductions and credits, and calculate the tax owed or refund due.


### What goes on Form 1040


Income reporting:


- Wages and salaries (from your W-2)
- Self-employment income (from Schedule C)
- Investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains)
- Retirement distributions
- Rental and other income


Deductions and credits:


- Standard deduction *or* itemized deductions
- Tax credits (Child Tax Credit, education credits, energy credits)
- Adjustments to income (IRA contributions, student loan interest, etc.)


Tax calculation:


- Total tax based on your income and filing status
- Payments already made (withholding, estimated payments)
- Refund due or balance owed


### Who files which version


- Form 1040 — the standard version for most taxpayers
- Form 1040-SR — same return, larger print, designed for taxpayers age 65+
- Form 1040-NR — for nonresident aliens

## The Supporting Cast: Schedules Attached to Form 1040


Most individuals never touch Schedules 1–3. They exist for income, taxes, and credits that don't fit on the two-page 1040 itself. Here's what they cover.


### Schedule 1 — Additional Income and Adjustments to Income


Reports income not listed directly on Form 1040, plus "above-the-line" deductions.


Part I – Additional Income includes taxable state/local refunds, business income (Schedule C), rental and pass-through income (Schedule E), farm income (Schedule F), unemployment compensation, and other income such as gambling winnings or jury duty pay.


For pros: Alimony is split by agreement date. Alimony from agreements executed before 2019 is reported as income here; alimony under agreements executed (or modified to adopt the new rules) after 2018 is *neither* taxable to the recipient nor deductible by the payer.


Part II – Adjustments to Income includes:


- Educator expenses — up to $300 of unreimbursed classroom costs ($600 if both spouses are eligible educators, max $300 each)
- [HSA deduction (Form 8889)](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/form-8889-checklist)
- Deductible part of self-employment tax (figured on Schedule SE)
- Self-employed SEP/SIMPLE/qualified-plan contributions
- Self-employed health insurance
- Penalty on early withdrawal of savings


New for 2025:[OBBBA's deductions for qualified tips, overtime, car-loan interest, and the senior deduction are claimed on a new Schedule 1-A (Form 1040)](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/obbba-hr1-tax-policy-changes) — see the "Looking ahead" box.


### Schedule 2 — Additional Taxes


Reports taxes beyond the basic income tax.


- Part I: Alternative Minimum Tax (Form 6251); excess advance premium tax credit repayment (Form 8962); recapture of certain clean-vehicle credits (using Schedule A (Form 8936)).
- Part II: Self-employment tax (Schedule SE); Additional Medicare Tax (Form 8959); Net Investment Income Tax (Form 8960); household employment taxes (Schedule H); additional tax on IRAs and other tax-favored accounts (Form 5329).


### Schedule 3 — Additional Credits and Payments


- Part I – Nonrefundable credits: Foreign tax credit (Form 1116); child and dependent care (Form 2441); education credits (Form 8863); retirement savings contributions credit (Form 8880); residential clean energy and energy-efficient home improvement credits (Form 5695).
- Part II – Other payments and refundable credits: Net premium tax credit (Form 8962); amount paid with an extension request (Form 4868); excess Social Security tax withheld.


## Schedule A — Itemized Deductions


Use[Schedule A](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/schedule-a-form-1040-checklist) only if your itemized deductions exceed your standard deduction. For 2025, the standard deduction is $15,750 (single / married filing separately), $31,500 (married filing jointly), and $23,625 (head of household).


Common itemized deductions:


- Medical and dental costs exceeding 7.5% of AGI
- State and local taxes (SALT) — capped at $40,000 ($20,000 if married filing separately) for 2025. *Note: this cap phases down for taxpayers with modified AGI over $500,000 ($250,000 MFS).*
- Mortgage interest (see Form 1098)
- Charitable contributions


## Schedule B — Interest and Ordinary Dividends


Required if you earned over $1,500 in taxable interest or ordinary dividends. It summarizes amounts reported to you on Forms 1099-INT and 1099-DIV.

## Schedule C — Profit or Loss from Business


Self-employed individuals and freelancers report business income and expenses here: gross income, business expenses, cost of goods sold, and vehicle information if applicable.


Tip:[Accurate Schedule C reporting](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/form-1040-schedule-c-checklist) — capturing every legitimate expense — can meaningfully reduce your tax. Keep clean records throughout the year, not just at filing time.


## Schedule D — Capital Gains and Losses


Reports sales of capital assets (stocks, bonds, real estate) and calculates your net capital gain or loss, typically alongside[Form 8949](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/form-8949-checklist) .


Why holding period matters: Assets held one year or less are short-term (taxed at ordinary rates); assets held more than one year are long-term (taxed at preferential rates). The exact crossover at one year is what determines the rate.


## Schedule SE — Self-Employment Tax


Calculates Social Security and Medicare taxes for people who work for themselves — the self-employed equivalent of the payroll taxes an employer and employee split.


Required if: your net self-employment earnings are $400 or more.
