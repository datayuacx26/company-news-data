# Taxes for Remote Workers

As remote work becomes the norm, ***where* you work can be just as important as what you do—especially when it comes to taxes.**


Remote employees today may face a more complex tax situation than they’re traditionally accustomed to. Individual situations vary greatly depending on whether they’re working across state lines, from a different country, or simply from home.


In this guide, we’ll break down the tax rules for different remote work arrangements, explain how federal and state laws affect your obligations, and share best practices to help you stay compliant and avoid unexpected costs.


### Key Takeaways:


1. **Remote Work and Taxes** : Your tax obligations vary based on factors such as your state of residence, the location where work is performed, your employer’s location, and the duration of your work in different locations.
2. **Types of Remote Work** : Different work arrangements, including telecommuting, temporarily working out of state, and digital nomadism, each have unique tax considerations.
3. **Employer and Employee Responsibilities** : Clear knowledge of tax responsibilities for both employers and remote employees ensures they meet federal and state compliance standards effectively.


## Types of Remote Work and Their Tax Implications


There are different **types of remote work that affect** taxes for both workers and employers. Each remote work arrangement—whether telecommuting, working temporarily out of state, or living as a digital nomad—carries unique tax considerations.


### 1. Telecommuting


**Telecommuting** refers to working remotely from home within the same state as the employer or a nearby state, typically with minimal travel involved. While telecommuting generally requires only a single state tax filing, certain cases may require tax withholdings in both the employer’s and employee’s state. For instance, if an employee lives in New Jersey but works for a New York-based company, they may need to pay **non-resident state taxes** in New York.


### 2. Temporarily Working Out of State


Employees working remotely from another state temporarily, such as for business trips or short-term projects, may face tax requirements in both their home and host states. Many states mandate **non-resident tax filings** for those who earn income in their jurisdiction. The rules vary significantly by state, often depending on the duration of the stay and the amount earned. Remote workers must understand these distinctions to avoid double taxation and[ensure compliance](https://www.taxgpt.com/blog/tax-compliance) .


### 3. Digital Nomadism


The rise of digital nomadism—working remotely from multiple locations, often internationally—presents additional tax complexities. U.S. citizens working abroad remain liable for federal taxes, state taxes, and even foreign taxes. They may also need to file to claim the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)** and/or **Foreign Tax Credit (FTC)** to avoid double taxation on income earned overseas.


## Federal vs. State Taxation for Remote Workers


For remote workers, understanding the distinction between **federal and state tax obligations** helps navigate the different rules for tax residency and income reporting.


### Federal Tax Obligations


At the federal level, remote workers are generally required to file taxes based on their **place of residence** rather than their employer’s location. Regardless of where you work, federal taxes are assessed on your income as a U.S. citizen or resident, making federal obligations consistent across the country. However, federal tax deductions or credits, like the **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion** (for those working internationally), may apply depending on specific remote work arrangements.


### State Tax Obligations


State taxes, on the other hand, vary significantly and can be complex for remote workers who work outside their employer’s state. State residency rules differ, with some states considering you a tax resident if you spend a certain number of days there or earn income within their jurisdiction. Remote workers may need to file **non-resident state tax returns** if they work in a different state than their employer, and, in some cases, even if they only occasionally work from a different location.


### Double Taxation and Credits


When remote workers are subject to taxes in multiple states, they may face **double taxation** on income. Some states offer credits for taxes paid to other states, reducing the risk of being taxed twice on the same income. Workers should also be aware of **state reciprocity agreements** that can simplify tax filing across state lines.


Let’s take an example to better understand this concept.


If you work for a company based in New York but reside in Pennsylvania, you may be required to pay **non-resident taxes** to New York while also paying resident state taxes to Pennsylvania. In such cases, Pennsylvania may offer a credit for the taxes paid to New York, offsetting the potential for double taxation.


**Federal vs. state taxation** are usually complex, especially when you’re working across multiple jurisdictions. Being aware of your filing responsibilities helps ensure tax compliance and can prevent unexpected tax liabilities.


## The “Convenience of the Employer” Rule


The **"Convenience of the Employer" rule** is a unique tax regulation that affects remote workers whose employers are based out of state. Under this rule, a remote worker may be required to pay state income taxes not only in the state where they reside but also in the employer’s state, depending on whether they work remotely for personal convenience or out of necessity.


### How the Rule Works


The "Convenience of the Employer" rule dictates that if an employee works remotely for convenience (i.e., personal choice) rather than because of a company requirement, the employee is still considered to be working in the employer’s state for tax purposes. However, if the remote arrangement is for the employer’s convenience (e.g., due to limited office space), this rule typically does not apply, and the worker may only need to pay taxes in their state of residence.


### States Applying the Rule


Not all states enforce the "Convenience of the Employer" rule, but those that do can significantly affect tax liabilities for remote workers. Currently, states such as **Connecticut, New York, Nebraska, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Arkansas** apply this rule, with Massachusetts enforcing a similar requirement for employees working remotely due to COVID-19. Remote workers employed by companies based in these states need to understand this rule to avoid unexpected tax liabilities.


### Applying the Rule


Suppose you live in Pennsylvania but work remotely for a company headquartered in Delaware. If you choose to work from home for personal convenience, Delaware’s “convenience of the employer” rule may still require you to pay state income taxes there, treating your remote workdays as if you were physically in Delaware.


However, if your employer mandates remote work due to business necessity, you might only owe taxes to Pennsylvania, based on state-specific guidelines. Because Pennsylvania and Delaware do not have a reciprocal tax agreement, it’s important for you to understand each state’s tax policies to avoid double taxation.


The **"Convenience of the Employer" rule** is a significant factor for remote workers to consider, especially for those employed by companies in states that enforce it. Clarifying with employers whether remote work is required or optional can help employees manage potential tax obligations effectively.


## Reciprocal Agreements Between States


For remote workers who live in one state but work for an employer based in another, **reciprocal agreements** can simplify the process of filing state taxes. These agreements are arrangements between certain states allowing residents to only pay income tax in their home state, even if they work across state lines.


### How Reciprocal Agreements Work


Reciprocal agreements are beneficial for remote employees who work in neighboring states, as they prevent the need to file non-resident tax returns and avoid double taxation on income. Instead, the employee pays state income tax only in their state of residence, making tax filing more straightforward.


### States with Reciprocal Agreements


Currently, 17 states in the U.S. have reciprocal agreements with neighboring states, including **Illinois, Indiana, Kentucky, Maryland, Michigan, New Jersey, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, West Virginia, and Wisconsin** , among others. Each agreement varies slightly in terms of rules, but the basic premise remains the same: income earned in the employer’s state is only taxed in the employee’s home state.


### Filing Considerations


If you live and work in states with a reciprocal agreement, it’s essential to complete a **non-residency certificate** for your employer. This document notifies your employer of the arrangement, so they withhold state income taxes only for your home state. Without this form, your employer may withhold taxes for both states, which can lead to complications at tax time.


## International Tax Considerations for Remote Workers


For remote workers employed internationally—whether U.S. citizens working abroad or foreign nationals working for U.S.-based companies—the tax landscape is often more complex than it is for domestic workers. Remote workers in this category must navigate both U.S. tax laws and the tax regulations of the countries where they reside or work.


### U.S. Citizens Working Abroad


U.S. citizens, regardless of where they live or work, are generally required to pay U.S. federal income taxes on their worldwide income, including earnings from remote work conducted outside the United States. The Internal Revenue Code mandates that U.S. citizens report and pay taxes on all income, no matter where it is earned.


### Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)


The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) allows qualified individuals to exclude a portion of foreign earned income from their taxable income, effectively reducing their U.S. tax liability. To qualify, individuals must meet either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test and maintain a tax home in a foreign country:


- **Bona Fide Residence Test** : Requires the individual to be a bona fide resident of a foreign country for an uninterrupted period that includes an entire tax year.
- **Physical Presence Test** : Requires the individual to be physically present in a foreign country or countries for at least 330 full days within any 12-month period.


The FEIE can significantly reduce taxable income for U.S. citizens working abroad; however, it does not eliminate the requirement to file a U.S. tax return.


### Foreign Tax Credit (FTC)


The Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) helps prevent double taxation by allowing U.S. taxpayers to claim a credit for income taxes paid to foreign governments. This credit can be claimed on income that is also subject to U.S. taxes, effectively lowering U.S. tax liability by the amount of foreign taxes paid.


- **Eligibility** : The taxpayer must have paid or accrued foreign taxes to a foreign country or U.S. possession.
- **Limitations** : The credit is limited to the amount of U.S. tax attributable to foreign income. Excess foreign taxes can be carried back one year and forward up to ten years.


### Foreign Nationals Working for U.S. Companies


For foreign nationals working remotely for U.S. companies, tax obligations depend on their country’s tax laws and any tax treaties in place with the United States. Generally, these remote workers do not owe U.S. income taxes if they are not U.S. residents, although they must adhere to local tax laws in their country of residence.
