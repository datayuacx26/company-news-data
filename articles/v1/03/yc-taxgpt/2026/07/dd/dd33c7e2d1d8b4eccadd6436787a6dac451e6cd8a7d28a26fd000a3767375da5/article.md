# General AI vs. Tax-Specific AI: What the IRS’s New Guidance Means for Your Tool Choice

The IRS did not set out to review your AI tools. But the guidance its Office of Professional Responsibility published reads like a checklist of everything that goes wrong when tax work happens in a general-purpose chatbot. Fabricated citations, opaque reasoning, client data going where it should not, and no record of any of it.


For most firms the real question was never whether to use AI. Virtually every practice already does, often without deciding to. The question the guidance forces into the open is which kind of AI your professional obligations can actually live with.


## The Short Version


The IRS did not write new rules. It confirmed the Circular 230 duties you already operated under apply in full to generative AI. Once you read the guidance that way, the choice between a general-purpose model and a tool built for tax stops being a matter of preference and becomes a matter of compliance.


Circular 230 Concern General-purpose AI Purpose-built tax AI


Verifiable citations (Section 10.22, Section 10.37) Produces fluent text; citations may be invented Answers tied to authority you can check


Confidentiality of return information (IRC Sections 6713, 7216) Real exposure when data enters public or unsecured models Built to handle tax data securely


Competence in the tool itself (Section 10.35) General-domain logic, hard to explain Tax-scoped behavior you can reason about


Auditability and firm procedures (Section 10.36) Little or no usage trail Designed to support a documentable workflow


Fee fairness (Section 10.27) Efficiency difficult to evidence Efficiency you can show


None of this means general-purpose AI has no place in a firm. For brainstorming a client newsletter, tightening the prose in an internal memo, or any task that touches neither client data nor authority you intend to rely on, a general model is perfectly reasonable. The argument that follows is narrower and more important: the moment a task touches confidential information or a conclusion a client will act on, the tool you choose becomes a professional-responsibility decision.


## Why this landed now


The guidance did not arrive in a vacuum. Courts have already sanctioned practitioners for AI hallucinations, with penalties running from financial sanctions and public censure to mandatory ethics coursework and referrals to state bar authorities. Those cases came out of the duties of candor and competence, the same duties that sit at the center of Circular 230.


The exposure is not limited to litigators either. In July 2025 the Australian government published a report prepared for it by Deloitte Australia that contained invented quotes, references to reports that did not exist, and books attributed to the wrong authors,[apparently produced by generative AI](https://www.cfodive.com/news/deloitte-ai-debacle-seen-wake-up-call-corporate-finance/802674/) . Deloitte reportedly agreed to refund part of its fee. The cost of the wrong tool, used the wrong way, is no longer hypothetical for accounting and advisory work.


## The real decision is not whether to use AI. It is which kind.


It helps to separate two things the word "AI" tends to blur together. The document review platforms and research products already embedded in your practice, from Westlaw to Bloomberg Tax to Lexis, have used AI for years, and the guidance does not take issue with them. What it targets is generative AI, the kind that produces original text and can make discretionary choices with no human in the loop.


That is the category this comparison is about, and it deserves a sharper test than "does the output look good." The output of a capable general model almost always looks good. The better question, and the one the IRS effectively poses, is whether the tool helps you discharge your duties or quietly transfers all the risk back onto you.


## Verification: Where general AI creates work instead of saving it


A general-purpose model produces fluent, confident text whose citations and figures you then have to verify from scratch. That is exactly the burden Section 10.22 and Section 10.37 place on you. Due diligence requires you to confirm the accuracy of every fact, citation, and calculation before anything reaches a client or the Service, and written advice has to rest on assumptions you can actually defend.


The trouble is that hallucinated citations are not an occasional glitch in a general model. They are a structural consequence of how it works, predicting plausible language rather than retrieving verified authority. A tool that surfaces checkable sources alongside its answer changes the nature of the task. Instead of reconstructing the research to find out whether the model was right, you are confirming a citation the tool has handed you.


This does not eliminate review, and any vendor who tells you otherwise is selling you a Section 10.22 problem. Even purpose-built output is a draft until a professional signs off on it. The honest difference is how much verification the tool demands and how easily you can do it.
