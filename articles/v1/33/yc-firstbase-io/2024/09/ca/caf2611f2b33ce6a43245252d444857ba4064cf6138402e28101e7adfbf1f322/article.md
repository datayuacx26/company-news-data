# Sole Proprietorship vs. LLC: What's the Difference?

## Sole Proprietorship vs. LLC: What's the Difference?


One of the most crucial decisions new business owners face is choosing the correct business structure for their venture. This choice can determine your tax strategy, funding opportunities, initial costs, and many other important factors that affect your business's viability. From LLCs to C-Corps, public or privately traded, the options can feel endless and overwhelming.


To help you make an informed decision, we will be covering the major differences between sole proprietorships and Limited Liability Corporations (LLCs). Both of these structures come with unique benefits and drawbacks. To make things as clear as possible, we will compare and contrast the tax implications, liability, and cost of each business type.


## TL;DR


- Sole proprietorships are easy to start, require minimal paperwork, and offer full control, but come with unlimited personal liability.
- LLCs provide liability protection, credibility, and flexible tax options, but have higher formation costs and compliance obligations.
- There is a significant difference in taxes; sole proprietors report business income as personal income, while LLCs offer pass-through or corporate taxation.
- LLCs are better for growth, offering easier access to funding, investor appeal, and legal continuity beyond the founder.
- Choosing the right structure depends on your risk tolerance, funding plans, and long-term goals.


## **Sole Proprietorship: The Basics**

A sole proprietorship is defined as a single individual who owns an unincorporated business by themselves. This makes it more or less the simplest business entity possible. If you[sell products on Etsy](https://www.firstbase.io/blog/do-you-need-a-business-license-to-sell-on-etsy) , you're a sole proprietor unless you decide to incorporate a business.


In a sole proprietorship, there is no legal separation between the entity and the owner. In practice, this means that the business owner is personally responsible for the actions of their business. Since there is no formal company, there is no legal difference between the owner's personal and business activities.


Additionally, a sole proprietorship is a status assigned to a business automatically. The moment you sell an item for a profit, you are considered a sole proprietor. The same reasoning applies to selling services as well.


### Advantages of Starting a Sole Proprietorship Over an LLC


#### 1. Immediate Formation and Minimal Paperwork


A sole proprietorship comes into existence the moment a person begins selling goods or services, requiring no state filings. Optional steps like registering a ‘doing‑business‑as’ (DBA) name or applying for an Employer Identification Number (EIN) typically cost under $50 and can be completed in minutes online or at a county clerk’s office.


#### 2. Total Managerial Control


Decision‑making authority rests entirely with the owner. No bylaws, operating agreements, or member votes delay price changes, marketing pivots, or product launches. This autonomy lets founders iterate quickly during the proof‑of‑concept stage and avoid formalities required of multi‑member or corporate entities.


#### 3. Straightforward Pass‑Through Taxation


The IRS treats business earnings as personal income reported on Form 1040 Schedule C, eliminating a separate entity‑level tax return. Deductible expenses, home‑office utilities, mileage, and health‑insurance premiums flow directly against the owner’s taxable income without double taxation or complex allocations.


### Disadvantages of Starting a Sole Proprietorship Over an LLC


#### 1. Unlimited Personal Liability


Because the business and owner are legally inseparable, personal assets may be seized to satisfy business debts or court judgments. High‑risk industries such as construction or food service magnify this exposure, and general liability insurance cannot fully replicate the protection of a separate legal entity.


#### 2. Restricted Access to Capital


Banks often treat one‑person businesses as riskier borrowers, leading to tighter lending standards and higher interest rates.


Equity financing is virtually impossible because the structure lacks transferable ownership interests, forcing growth to rely on retained earnings or personal assets.


#### 3. Lack of Business Continuity


If the owner retires, dies, or becomes incapacitated, the enterprise dissolves automatically unless heirs form a new entity. This fragility can deter long‑term contracts and reduce the resale value of the brand compared with entities that continue beyond changes in ownership.


## **Limited Liability Corporation (LLC): The Basics**


[Source](https://blog.opencorporates.com/2024/12/05/the-rise-of-the-llc/)


In contrast, a limited liability corporation (LLC) is more complex. This business entity is owned by one or more persons and is separate from them. This means that the owners of the business are not personally responsible; the LLC is.


An LLC also allows for a more complex business structure. Rather than only having one owner, an LLC can have multiple owners (also called members), managers, and shareholders/investors. This allows for more flexibility for funding and business operations.


### Advantages of Starting an LLC Over Sole Proprietorship


#### 1. Limited Liability Protection


An LLC separates personal and business assets, shielding members from most lawsuits and unpaid obligations incurred by the firm. Creditors generally cannot pursue a member’s house or savings unless the member personally guarantees a debt or commits fraud.


#### 2. Flexible Tax Treatment


By default, single‑member LLCs enjoy pass‑through taxation similar to sole proprietorships, while multi‑member LLCs are taxed like partnerships. Members may later elect S‑corporation status to reduce self‑employment taxes or C‑corporation status to retain earnings, creating room to optimize as profits scale.


#### 3. Enhanced Credibility and Fund‑Raising Potential


Including “LLC” in the business name signals permanence and professionalism to suppliers, customers, and lenders.


Because ownership is divided into membership interests, an LLC can admit new investors or key employees in exchange for capital or expertise; an option unavailable to sole proprietorships.


### Disadvantages of Starting an LLC Over Sole Proprietorship


#### 1. Higher Formation and Maintenance Costs


States charge filing fees that range from about $35 to more than $500, with annual reports or franchise taxes adding recurring expenses.


If you operate in multiple jurisdictions, you must also pay foreign‑qualification fees and maintain registered agents in each state.


#### 2. Additional Compliance Requirements


Most states require annual or biennial reports to keep the entity in good standing; missing a deadline can trigger penalties or administrative dissolution. LLCs must also maintain separate bank accounts, follow operating‑agreement procedures, and keep accurate records to preserve liability protection.


#### 3. Self‑Employment Tax Exposure for Active Members


Unless the LLC elects S‑corporation status, the IRS treats each member’s share of profit as self‑employment income subject to Medicare and Social Security taxes.


While the S‑corp election can mitigate this burden by splitting salary and distributions, it introduces payroll filings and “reasonable compensation” assessments that raise administrative complexity.


## **Key Differences**


Now that we have outlined the basics of each structure, we will examine the core differences for each in terms of tax implications, cost, liability, and structure.


### **1. Tax Implications**


As mentioned previously, because there is no distinction between a sole proprietorship and its owner, their taxes are filed the same way. This also means that your personal tax rate will be unaffected if you are a business owner or not. But you are eligible to deduct certain expenses from your gross income to lower your tax bracket.


For example, if you work out of your home as a data analyst, you will be able to deduct some of your home expenses (utility costs) from your income. It’s important to note that if your business makes more than $400 annually in profits, you will be required to file[self-employed taxes](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employed-individuals-tax-center) as part of your Form 1040, Schedule C.


On the other hand, because LLCs are an entity separate from their owner(s),[their taxes](https://www.firstbase.io/blog/the-firstbase-guide-to-us-llc-taxes-for-international-founders?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=sole) are filed separately. They also allow for multiple filing options. Even if you own an LLC, you can elect to file your taxes as a corporation. This allows for more strategic choices to realize significant tax benefits.


### **2. Cost**


Given that sole proprietors are automatically created with the sale of a good or service, there is no cost associated with setting one up. Unless you decide to register a name for your business, the lack of fees is a considerable plus.


In contrast, setting up an LLC costs a few hundred dollars and comes with recurring annual payments.[Firstbase](https://onboarding.firstbase.io/start/type?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=sole) is the simplest and most cost-effective way to create a new LLC.


### **3. Liability**


The most considerable difference between sole proprietorships and LLCs is the liability of their owners.


Since there is no legal difference between the owner of the sole proprietorship and the business itself, the owner carries unlimited liability. Practically speaking, this means that if your business is sued, you will be responsible for paying any claimants and fees out of your own pocket. Your personal assets could be seized to pay off said fees.


LLCs are entities legally separate from their members. Suppose your business is sued or required to pay certain fees. In that case, only the business will be required to fulfill those fiduciary obligations, not you personally, unless you have signed a personal guarantee.


Additionally, if you have employees working for you, your corporation can shield you from liability issues that come with employee mistakes. Let’s say you run a contracting business and one of your employees damaged a customer’s home. Because the damages are quite severe, the customer decides to sue. But, because the employee is hired by the LLC and not you, your assets are out of reach.
