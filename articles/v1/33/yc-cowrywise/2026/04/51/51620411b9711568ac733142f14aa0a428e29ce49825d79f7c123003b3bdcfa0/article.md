# Dangote Refinery IPO: The Naira-In, Dollars-Out Investment Explained

Most Nigerian investments follow one rule: naira in, naira out. The Dangote Refinery IPO is trying to break that rule.


When Aliko Dangote took the stage in Lagos to unveil his group’s Vision 2030 plan, one sentence did more work than the rest.


“You buy in naira, but you get dividends in dollars.”


For most Nigerians, that sentence shouldn’t be possible.


## **Why does this land so hard right now**


Think about the last two years of your own money.


Your salary, probably, didn’t move much. But your rent did. Your children’s school fees did. The price of rice, of fuel, of the data plan you use to work. Your savings account showed the same number at the end of every month, and somehow that number bought less of everything.


That’s the thing about naira depreciation. It doesn’t feel like losing money. It feels like the world is getting more expensive while you stand still.


Every saver with a dom account, every person receiving dollars from family abroad, every Nigerian who quietly prices things in dollars in their head before spending naira, is trying to solve the same problem. Dollar exposure has become the obsession of anyone paying attention.


The catch is that dollar exposure usually requires dollars to start. You need to source USD somewhere, open a domiciliary account, or go through a platform that handles the conversion for you.


The Dangote Refinery IPO proposes something the Nigerian market hasn’t seen before. You pay in naira, like any other NGX share. Your dividends arrive in dollars.


## **How the Dangote Refinery IPO dividend structure works**


The dollars aren’t coming from nowhere. By Dangote’s own account, the refinery’s projected export earnings is roughly $6.4 billion a year in export earnings, largely from petrochemicals such as polypropylene and fertiliser. Foreign buyers pay in dollars. Those dollars sit in the company’s accounts. The plan is to use that pool to pay shareholders directly in USD, rather than converting to naira first.


Three things have to be true for this to hold up.


Regulatory approval has to land. The SEC, NGX, and Finance Ministry all have to sign off. Dangote has said the Minister of Finance will approve it. A forecast is not a fact.


Without strong export revenue, the whole structure weakens. If refinery exports drop, because production falls, margins compress or trade routes shift, the dollar pool shrinks and the dividend with it.


FX rules can’t tighten unfavourably. The Central Bank has broad authority over how exporters use foreign currency earnings, and a future rule change could complicate everything.


Now the part most commentary is missing:


> **This is not a dollar investment. It is a Naira investment with a dollar income stream.**


If share prices rise and you sell your Dangote Refinery shares, that gain is realised in naira. The capital itself is a naira asset listed on a naira-denominated exchange, bought and sold with naira. Only the dividend stream is dollarised. Anyone calling this a pure dollar play is overstating what the structure actually does.


## **What it looks like if you invest in the Dangote Refinery IPO**


Picture it in numbers.


You invest ₦500,000 in the IPO. The exchange rate at listing is ₦1,500 per dollar. Your naira buys roughly $333 of exposure.


A year later, the company declares a 5% dollar dividend. That’s about $17, paid to you in USD.


By then, the naira has slipped to ₦1,800 per dollar. Your $17 dividend is worth around ₦30,600 in local terms, compared to ₦25,000 you’d have earned from a comparable naira dividend. The dollar link absorbs part of the naira slide. That’s the real appeal.


What this simple picture hides: the 5% yield is illustrative, not announced. The share price still moves in naira, so a 10% price drop is a real loss regardless of what the dividend does. And Nigerian withholding tax on dividends is 10%. Whether it applies to the dollar amount or the naira equivalent at payment will affect your take-home.


## **The subtle trade-off most people will miss**


The dollar dividend makes this IPO feel like a safety play. Pay in naira, collect in dollars, hedged against depreciation. Simple story.


What the story hides is an inverse relationship between entry price and real yield. If the IPO lists at $20 billion instead of $50 billion, you’re paying 2.5x less for the same underlying stream of export dollars. A 5% dividend yield at the lower valuation becomes closer to 2% at the higher one.


Here’s the awkward bit. The hype around “naira in, dollars out” is the exact thing that drives valuations toward the upper end of that range. Demand chases the promise. Book-builds clear at premium prices. By the time this story has been repeated enough for the average retail investor to get excited, the premium may already be priced into the allocation.


There’s a second layer worth sitting with. The dollar dividend is usually a single-digit percentage of total return on any stock. Share price movement is the rest. And share prices on the NGX move in naira, inside a naira-denominated market, subject to naira volatility. Your dollar income stream is real. But it’s sitting on top of an asset that’s still mostly exposed to Nigerian market conditions.


A safety play at any price isn’t a safety play. It’s a premium you’re paying for certainty, in a market where the certainty isn’t guaranteed.


## **What nobody should assume about the Dangote Refinery IPO**


Most Nigerian coverage so far has been cheerful. Before you take the optimism at face value, strip out the assumptions that don’t hold up.


**This is not a guaranteed dollar hedge.** The dollar dividend depends on export revenues, regulatory approval, and FX policy. Change any one of those, and the promise bends.


**This is not risk-free.** The refinery’s parent company is rated B+ by Fitch, which is a speculative grade. Crude supply has been inconsistent, forcing some dollar-priced imports that compress margins. Margins are what fund the dividend.


**This is not a quick win.** Dividends get paid after a company earns, declares, and distributes. That’s quarterly or annual, not weekly. If you need liquidity within twelve months, an IPO is the wrong vehicle.


**The prospectus isn’t public yet.** Everything you’ve read so far, including this piece, is secondary reporting. When the prospectus drops, that’s the document that matters. Read it.


## **So, is the Dangote Refinery IPO a good investment?**


The honest answer is that it depends on what gets priced in.


This becomes genuinely attractive if:


- The dollar dividend structure receives regulatory approval with clear mechanics
- Crude supply stabilises, and export margins hold up
- You’re buying as part of a diversified portfolio, not betting heavily on one name


It becomes risky if:


- Book-building pushes the valuation into premium territory on hype alone
- The dollar dividend remains a stated intention without a firm regulatory framework by listing day
- You put a large share of your savings into a single stock because the story sounds good


The structure is genuinely new for Nigerian retail investors. That’s not the same as it being automatically a good deal. When the prospectus lands, read it before you decide.
