# Why a War Thousands of Miles Away Can Move Nigerian Investments

One narrow waterway in the Middle East influences what Nigerian businesses pay for diesel.


When tensions rise around the Strait of Hormuz, a route through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supply passes, global oil markets react almost immediately.


Traders begin pricing in the possibility that supply could be disrupted. Oil prices rise. Shipping insurance costs increase. Energy markets tighten.


And within weeks, the effects can begin showing up thousands of kilometres away, in Nigeria’s inflation rate, the cost of transport, and even the performance of Nigerian stocks.


This is the chain reaction that connects global events to local investments.


## **Why Oil Prices Matter So Much to Nigeria**


Nigeria is one of the world’s oil-exporting economies. Crude oil exports account for a significant portion of government revenue and a large share of the country’s foreign exchange earnings.


As a result, movements in global oil prices quickly affect Nigeria’s economic outlook.


When oil prices rise, government revenue can improve, foreign exchange inflows may increase, and external reserves strengthen. These shifts can support exchange-rate stability and improve overall macroeconomic confidence.


But the story does not end there.


Higher oil prices can also push up domestic energy costs, especially diesel and logistics expenses, which ripple through the broader economy.


And this is where everyday Nigerian life begins to feel the effects.


## **When Oil Prices Rise, Nigerian Costs Often Follow**


For many Nigerians, global oil shocks first become visible at the fuel station or transport park.


Diesel prices affect everything from long-distance trucking to generator costs for businesses and households. When energy costs increase, transport fares often rise, logistics become more expensive, and businesses begin adjusting their prices.


Many Nigerians experienced this firsthand during recent petroleum crises, when higher fuel costs gradually filtered into grocery bills, transport fares, and operating expenses for small businesses.


This is how a geopolitical event thousands of miles away can slowly influence local economic conditions.


And those same pressures eventually show up in corporate earnings and stock market performance.


## **The First Winners: Oil and Gas Producers**


For upstream oil producers, higher crude prices are generally positive.


Companies involved in exploration and production benefit when global oil prices rise because their revenues increase while many operating costs remain relatively stable.


This can strengthen profitability, increase free cash flow, and support stronger dividend payments.


As a result, investors often pay close attention to oil prices when evaluating energy companies listed on the Nigerian stock market.


During sustained periods of higher oil prices, oil and gas stocks can sometimes become one of the strongest-performing sectors in the market.


## **The Pressure Point: Consumer Goods Companies**


Consumer goods companies often sit on the opposite side of this equation.


When energy prices rise, transportation costs increase, raw material logistics become more expensive, and distribution networks become costlier to operate.


Companies may attempt to pass these costs on to consumers through higher prices.


But consumer demand is not always able to absorb these increases, particularly during periods when household purchasing power is already under pressure.


When that happens, companies may face tighter margins, slower volume growth, or both.


For investors, this means rising oil prices can create a more challenging environment for parts of the consumer goods sector.


## **Banks: Caught Between Two Forces**


Banks tend to experience a more complicated set of effects when global oil prices move.


On one side, higher oil prices can strengthen Nigeria’s external position by increasing foreign exchange inflows. A more stable currency environment can improve investor confidence and support financial markets.


On the other side, rising energy costs can push inflation higher. When inflation rises, interest rates often remain high for longer.


Higher borrowing costs can slow loan demand from businesses and consumers, while also increasing financial pressure on companies that rely heavily on credit.


In practice, banks often find themselves balancing two opposing forces: stronger macroeconomic stability on one side and tighter financial conditions on the other.


## **Global Investors Also React to Uncertainty**


Beyond oil prices, geopolitical events can influence global investor behaviour.


During periods of uncertainty, international investors sometimes shift capital toward assets perceived as safer, such as government bonds or gold. This shift is often described as a **risk-off environment** .


When that happens, emerging markets, including Nigeria, may experience temporary capital outflows as global funds rebalance their portfolios.


This means the Nigerian stock market can experience volatility even if higher oil prices appear positive for the country’s economy.


## **The Global Shock Transmission Chain**


To understand how distant events influence Nigerian investments, it helps to think of the process as a chain reaction.


Here’s how each step in that chain typically unfolds:


**Global Event** **Immediate Effect** **Possible Nigerian Impact**


Conflict in oil-producing regions Oil prices rise Higher government revenue


Rising energy costs Higher logistics and transport costs Inflation pressure


Global uncertainty Investors move to safer assets Emerging market volatility


Inflation risk Interest rate adjustments Impact on businesses and lending


Each step in this chain gradually transmits global shocks into local economic conditions.


## **What Usually Happens After Oil Price Shocks**


Market reactions to geopolitical shocks rarely happen all at once. Instead, they tend to unfold in stages.


Understanding this pattern can help investors avoid reacting too quickly to early market volatility.


**Phase 1: Market Reaction** **** Oil prices often move first. Traders react to the uncertainty by bidding oil prices higher, which can cause crude prices to spike even before any actual supply disruption occurs.


**Phase 2: Economic Adjustment** **** As higher energy prices filter through the global economy, transportation, logistics, and production costs begin to rise. Inflation pressures may start appearing in different economies, including Nigeria.


**Phase 3: Market Repricing** **** Investors begin reassessing company earnings across sectors. Oil producers may benefit from higher prices, while sectors exposed to higher operating costs may face pressure.


**Phase 4: Stabilisation** **** Over time, markets adjust as investors gain more clarity about how long the shock will last and how economies will respond.


This process can take weeks or even months, which is why early market reactions do not always reflect the final economic outcome.


## **The Bigger Lesson for Investors**


Global events will always influence financial markets, but successful investing rarely depends on predicting global events perfectly. Instead, long-term investors tend to focus on strategies designed to withstand uncertainty, diversification, consistent investing, and a long-term perspective.


Because while headlines may change quickly, the principles of sound investing remain remarkably stable.
