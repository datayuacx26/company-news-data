# Retail Operations Manager Job Description

A retail operations manager job description has to answer one question before anything else: how many stores, and do they report to this person. Everything else about the role, the skills, the seniority, and the way success is measured, follows from that. Templates that skip it produce a posting that attracts store managers and district managers in the same pile.


This version assumes the common shape: a multi-store span, accountable for execution consistency across locations rather than for one store's P&L.


## What the role actually does


- Owns execution consistency across stores: planogram compliance, replenishment discipline, receiving accuracy, and the standards that determine whether a plan made at head office actually reaches the shelf.
- Translates chain policy into something stores can run. A replenishment policy that arrives as a sixty-page document does not change behaviour; the same policy as a twenty-line weekly exception list does.
- Closes the loop between store reality and head office assumptions, which is the part most often missing. When 40 percent of stores never fully implement a reset, someone has to say so before the reset is judged a failure.
- Manages the operational escalations that cross store boundaries: supplier service failures affecting a region, systemic receiving errors, inventory record drift concentrated in particular locations.
- Develops store managers, which in practice is the largest long-term lever the role has.


## A retail operations manager job description template


### Responsibilities


- Deliver operational standards across an assigned group of stores, measured on availability, shrink, labor against plan, and reset compliance.
- Run a regular store visit cadence with a consistent structure, so visits produce comparable observations rather than impressions.
- Own the feedback path from stores to merchandising and supply, including flagging plans that cannot be executed as written.
- Partner with buying on supplier service issues that show up as store-level symptoms, supplying the store evidence that separates a supplier problem from an execution one.
- Coach and develop store managers, and lead recruitment for those roles.


### Requirements


- Multi-site retail experience, ideally including time as a store manager, because credibility with store teams is load-bearing in this role.
- Comfort reading operational data and acting on exceptions rather than waiting for a monthly report.
- The ability to prioritize under genuinely competing demands, since every store will present its own emergency.
- Willingness to travel across the assigned estate at whatever cadence the span requires.


## Interview questions that predict performance


- "Head office says a reset is complete. You walk into a store and it is half done. What happens next?" You want a specific sequence: verify scope, understand why (labor, fixtures, stock timing), fix locally, and then escalate the systemic cause. Candidates who only fix locally will never change the pattern.
- "Three stores each tell you they are your top priority today. How do you decide?" Looking for an explicit basis, usually customer impact and reversibility, rather than responsiveness to whoever escalated loudest.
- "A store's inventory report says an item is in stock and the shelf is empty. What are the possibilities?" Tests whether they know that record accuracy, backroom stock, and lost facings are distinct causes with different fixes.
- "Tell me about a policy from head office you thought was wrong. What did you do?" The best answers involve implementing it while gathering the evidence to change it, which is the actual job.


## The first 90 days


Visit every store in the span at least twice, with the second visit structured to check what changed after the first. Establish one consistent visit format so observations are comparable rather than anecdotal. Identify the two or three operational issues that are systemic rather than store-specific, and take those to head office with evidence.


What to avoid: rolling out a new standard in the first month. The credibility to change how stores work is earned by first demonstrating that you understand why they currently work the way they do.


## What this role is not


It is not a store manager with a bigger title. A store manager optimizes one location end to end. An operations manager optimizes consistency across many, which is a different skill and often frustrating for someone who liked owning a single P&L.


It is also not a merchandising or category role. Deciding what to sell and how to allocate space sits with merchandising; this role owns whether those decisions survive contact with the store.


## How to structure the store visit


The visit is this role's primary instrument and it is usually unstructured, which makes observations incomparable between stores and between visits. A fixed format costs nothing and turns anecdote into evidence.


- Walk the sales floor before speaking to anyone. First impressions of availability and condition are the closest thing to a customer's view you will get.
- Check the same three departments in every store, every visit, so the comparison across the estate is meaningful.
- Pull the store's current exception list and check three lines physically. This tells you whether the data and the shelf agree, which is the fastest read on record accuracy.
- Close with the store manager on what changed since the last visit, not on what is wrong today.


The discipline that makes this work is writing observations against the same headings each time. Six visits in, patterns appear that no single visit would have surfaced, and those patterns are what head office needs to hear.


## Frequently asked questions


How many stores should one operations manager cover?It depends on format complexity and travel distance more than on a headcount rule. The practical test is whether every store can be visited on a meaningful cadence and whether the manager still has time for systemic work. When the span grows to the point where the role is purely reactive, the systemic half of the job stops happening.


Should the role own store P&L?Usually shared. Store managers own their own P&L, and the operations manager is accountable for the standards and consistency that drive it. Making the operations manager solely accountable tends to hollow out store manager ownership.


Do they need to have been a store manager?It is the most common and most reliable background, because store teams quickly detect whether the person has done the job. Strong candidates from adjacent operational disciplines can work, but they need a deliberate period actually running store shifts.
