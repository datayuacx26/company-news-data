# Store Manager Job Description

A store manager job description is usually a list of everything that happens in a store, which is accurate and useless for hiring. The useful version separates what the role genuinely controls from what it merely absorbs, because candidates are going to be judged on the first and frustrated by the second.


A store manager does not control the range, the planogram, the supplier minimums, or the cost price. They control availability on the shelf, the condition of the store, labor deployment, and the development of their team. That distinction should be visible in the posting and in how the role is measured.


## What the role actually does


- Delivers availability: the shelf is full of the right things, replenishment happens on schedule, and backroom stock reaches the floor. This is the single largest contribution a store makes to sales.
- Runs labor: scheduling against forecast demand, and deploying people to the hours that matter rather than evenly across the week.
- Owns store condition and standards, including the perishable rotation discipline that determines shrink.
- Develops the team, which is where good store managers separate themselves, since almost everything else is executed by other people.
- Manages the store P&L within the levers available, principally labor, shrink, and the sales effect of execution quality.


## A store manager job description template


### Responsibilities


- Deliver store sales, labor, and shrink targets.
- Maintain on-shelf availability, including acting on the weekly replenishment exception list and escalating minimum-bound or supplier-caused gaps rather than absorbing them silently.
- Execute planogram resets and merchandising plans, and report accurately when a plan cannot be executed as written.
- Recruit, train, and develop store associates and department leads.
- Own inventory record accuracy in the store: act on negative on-hand and no-movement worklists, and run cycle counts where they are directed.


### Requirements


- Prior retail supervisory experience with accountability for results, not only for shifts.
- Practical comfort with operational reporting: reading an exception list and acting on it without needing it interpreted.
- Genuine people-management capability, including hiring, difficult conversations, and development.
- Availability for the shift pattern the store actually runs, stated plainly in the posting.


## Interview questions that predict performance


- "The system says we have 11 units and the shelf is empty. Walk me through what you do." You want a shelf check first, then backroom, then a record correction, and an understanding that these are three different problems.
- "You are short two people on a Saturday. What gets cut?" Looking for an explicit priority order that protects availability and checkout over tasks that can slip a day.
- "Head office sends a reset your fixtures cannot accommodate. What do you do?" The right answer implements what is possible and reports the gap precisely, rather than improvising silently, which is how chains lose track of what their stores actually look like.
- "Tell me about someone you developed." Specific and verifiable beats philosophy. This question predicts long-term store performance better than any operational question on the list.


## The first 90 days


Learn the store's rhythms before changing them: which days and hours drive the week, which departments are chronically short, and which of the standing problems are the store's own versus inherited from range or supply decisions. Fix one visible thing early, ideally availability in a high-traffic department, because it demonstrates to the team that reporting problems leads to them being solved.


The most common first-90-days mistake is a wholesale reorganization of responsibilities before understanding why the current arrangement exists.


## Store manager versus general manager


In many chains these titles are used interchangeably, and where they differ, a general manager typically carries additional scope: multiple departments with their own managers, a larger P&L, or responsibility for a site with non-retail operations attached, such as fuel, pharmacy, or foodservice.


If you are hiring for a general manager, say what the additional scope actually is. Candidates read the title as a seniority signal, and a posting whose title outruns its responsibilities attracts the wrong applicants and loses them at offer stage.


## What to give a new store manager


Store managers are frequently hired into a store and left to discover its problems, which wastes the most valuable diagnostic window they will ever have. A short handover pack shortens that considerably.


- The store's standing exceptions: which items are chronically over- or under-covered, and which of those are minimum-bound and therefore not theirs to fix.
- The last two reset plans and an honest note on what was actually executed, because inherited half-completed resets explain a surprising share of a store's oddities.
- The store's record-accuracy position and the departments where drift concentrates.
- Labor pattern against demand by day and hour, with a note on where the current schedule is known to be wrong.


Handing over the known problems is not an admission of failure; it is the difference between a manager spending their first month diagnosing and spending it fixing. The stores that onboard managers worst are the ones where every arrival re-derives the same four issues.


One last note on the posting itself. State the shift pattern, the estate's expectations on weekend and holiday cover, and whether the role is single-site or occasionally covers a neighbouring store. Managers who discover those after accepting tend to leave inside a year, and a store manager vacancy is among the most expensive an operator carries.


## Frequently asked questions


What is the difference between a store manager and a general manager?Usually scope rather than kind. A general manager typically covers more departments, a larger P&L, or a site with attached operations like pharmacy or foodservice. Where a chain uses the titles interchangeably, define the span in the posting rather than relying on the title to carry it.


What should a store manager be measured on?Sales, labor, shrink, and availability, weighted toward what they control. Grading a store on weeks of supply for items bound by a supplier minimum is grading them on a purchasing term, and teams notice quickly when a target is not theirs to hit.


How much retail experience is required?Supervisory retail experience with real accountability is the strongest predictor. Managers from other high-volume operational settings can transfer well, but they need support on the merchandising and perishable-management specifics.
