# 409A Valuation vs. Fair Market Value (FMV): What’s the Difference?

When issuing stock options or structuring compensation plans, many founders encounter two important—yet often misunderstood—terms: **409A Valuation** and **Fair Market Value (FMV)** . At a glance, they might seem interchangeable. In practice, they serve different legal, tax, and operational purposes. Understanding the difference between 409A vs FMV is essential for private companies, especially startups, that want to remain compliant and competitive.


**Quick answer:** Fair Market Value (FMV) is the broad financial concept of what an asset is worth on the open market. A 409A valuation is the specific, IRS-compliant independent appraisal that private companies use to establish the FMV of their common stock for setting stock option strike prices. Every 409A valuation aims to determine FMV, but not every FMV estimate meets the standard required by a 409A.


In this guide, we’ll break down:

- How the IRS views each

## What Is a 409A Valuation?


A **409A valuation** is a formal, independent appraisal used to determine the fair market value of a private company’s common stock. It gets its name from Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code, which governs non-qualified deferred compensation.


Startups typically use a 409A valuation to set the strike price of stock options issued to employees. Legally, the strike price must not be lower than the FMV of the underlying common stock on the grant date. A 409A valuation helps ensure compliance with this rule.

### Key Features of a 409A Valuation:


- Conducted by a third-party valuation firm
- Required for privately held companies issuing stock options
- Must be updated every 12 months or after a material event (e.g., fundraising, acquisition offer)
- Provides a “safe harbor” to protect companies and employees from IRS penalties

## What Is Fair Market Value (FMV)?


**Fair Market Value (FMV)** is a broader financial concept defined by the IRS as:


> “The price at which the property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither being under any compulsion to buy or to sell and both having reasonable knowledge of relevant facts.”


FMV can be used in multiple contexts:


- Sale of company shares or assets
- Gifting of equity for estate or tax planning
- Purchase of stock options
- Mergers and acquisitions


While a 409A valuation aims to establish FMV for compliance, not all FMV assessments meet the standards required under 409A.

## How the IRS Views 409A Valuations vs. FMV


The IRS uses 409A valuations specifically to regulate deferred compensation, such as stock options. If a company issues options with a strike price below FMV without a valid 409A valuation, the employee could face:


- Immediate income tax on the deferred compensation
- An additional 20% penalty tax
- Interest charges on unpaid taxes


### [Safe Harbor Protection](https://blog.withmantle.com/409a-valuations/#understanding-409a-safe-harbor?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=website&utm_campaign=409a-vs-fmv)


A compliant 409A valuation provides a safe harbor, shifting the burden of proof to the IRS. To quality:


- The valuation must be performed by a qualified independent appraiser.
- It must be less than 12 months old.
- There must have been no material changes affecting the valuation.


**FMV** , on the other hand, is a general concept used by the IRS in many tax-related scenarios—not just equity compensation, to determine how transfers of property are judged for tax purposes.

## Why the Distinction Between 409A and FMV Matters


Understanding 409A valuations vs. fair market value is critical for:


- Issuing equity grants correctly
- Avoiding costly IRS penalties
- Preparing for audits, due diligence, and acquisitions
- Planning tax strategies for executives and employees


A common mistake is assuming the FMV equals the latest preferred stock price. However, preferred shares often carry liquidation preferences and investor protections, meaning they are worth more than common stock.


### A 409A Valuation accounts for:


- Capital structure (common vs. preferred shares)
- Discount for lack of marketability (DLOM)
- Financial performance and market comparables
- Risk profile and business stage


---


## Practical Example: VC Round vs. 409A Valuation


Let’s say your startup raises a Series A round at **$10/share** for preferred stock. That valuation includes investor rights that common shares don’t have.


A proper 409A valuation might assess the FMV of common stock at **$2.50/share** , reflecting:


- Illiquidity of private shares
- Subordination to preferred stock
- Risk profile for early-stage companies


Issuing options at $10/share instead of $2.50/share could:


- Trigger IRS scrutiny
- Deter employees from exercising options
- Overstate compensation expense on your books

## When to Update Your 409A Valuation


To maintain compliance, update your 409A valuation:


- **At least every 12 months**
- **After any material event,** such as:


- New funding round
- Acquisition offer
- Major business pivot
- Large revenue change


Failing to update your valuation can invalidate your safe harbor status and expose the company to tax and legal risk.

## Common Mistakes Companies Make


1. **Relying on Internal FMV Estimates:** These usually don’t meet safe harbor standards and often lack documentation.
2. **Confusing Preferred and Common Stock Valuation:** Valuing common stock at the same price as preferred can lead to overvaluation and compliance issues.
3. **Skipping Updates After Major Events:** Material changes like fundraising rounds must trigger a valuation update.
4. **Granting Options Without a Valid 409A:** Retroactive pricing can result in penalties and taxable income for employees.


---


## Best Practices for Managing 409A Valuations and FMV


- Work with a reputable third-party valuation firm
- Schedule valuation updates annually
- Update after material events
- Keep detailed records of all grants and valuations
- Educate employees on how stock options and FMV work

## Final Thoughts: 409A Valuations vs. Fair Market Value


While they’re closely related, 409A valuations and fair market value are not interchangeable. FMV is the broader financial concept, while a 409A valuation is a specific, defensible method to determine FMV for issuing stock options in private companies.


For startups, the safest route is to treat 409A valuations as a legal and compliance requirement, and FMV as the foundation of sound equity planning. Understanding and managing both ensures you stay IRS-compliant, attract top talent, and maintain transparency with investors and employees.


## Frequently Asked Questions


### Is a 409A valuation the same as fair market value (FMV)?


No. FMV is the broad concept of what an asset is worth on the open market, while a 409A valuation is the specific, IRS-defensible appraisal process private companies use to determine the FMV of common stock for stock option grants.


### How often do you need a new 409A valuation?


Most private companies need an updated 409A valuation at least once every 12 months, or sooner after a material event such as a new funding round, acquisition offer, or major business pivot.


### What happens if a company issues stock options without a valid 409A valuation?


Options priced below FMV without a valid 409A valuation can trigger immediate income tax for the employee, an additional 20% penalty tax, and interest charges, and the company loses its IRS safe harbor protection.


### Can a company use its latest funding round price as its FMV?


Not directly. Preferred stock sold in a funding round typically carries rights and protections that common stock doesn’t have, so its price usually overstates the FMV of the common stock used for option grants.


### Do public companies need 409A valuations?


409A valuations are primarily used by private companies. Publicly traded companies already have an observable market price for their stock, which serves as FMV.
