# What 12,000 Monthly Calls to a County Actually Sound Like

Pull a month of call logs from a mid-size county and the first thing that hits you is not the volume. It is the repetition.


A county fields thousands of calls a month, and the overwhelming majority are variations on the same short list. What are your hours. Is my permit ready. How do I license my dog. Where do I pay this. Who do I even talk to about a ditch. The questions feel infinite to the person answering them and are, in fact, a set of about twenty.


##


The shape of the volume


In Sumter County, Florida, the phone line now carries roughly 12,000 calls a month. Seventy-two percent of them are handled without a staff member picking up. That number is not made of exotic edge cases. It is made of the routine middle: the informational and transactional calls that do not need judgment, just an accurate answer and, sometimes, a clean handoff to the right department.


The remaining slice, the calls that genuinely need a person, is where you want your staff spending their attention. The failure of the old model is that it spent a clerk's morning on "what time do you close" while the genuinely stuck caller waited on hold.


##


Why the phone, specifically


Residents who have a choice use the website. The people on the phone are disproportionately the ones who could not find it online, do not want to, or cannot: older residents, people mid-task, people who are frustrated. In Sumter, the median resident is in their late sixties. The phone is not a legacy channel there. It is the channel.


That is why a chatbot deflects a dozen questions a day while the phone rings off the hook. The demand was never on the website. (We wrote about that mismatch in[voice AI versus chatbots](https://www.effigov.com/blog/voice-ai-vs-chatbot-local-government) .)


##


What the pattern means for staffing


If most of the volume is repetitive, most of it is automatable without anyone noticing a drop in service, as long as the answers are accurate and the escalations are clean. That is the whole game: get the routine 72 percent off your team's plate so the other 28 percent reaches a human who is not exhausted.
