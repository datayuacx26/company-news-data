## **How Hot Weather Affects Your Pet's Body**


Here is the thing[most pet parents](https://www.life360.com/pet-safety?icid=blog_summer-pet-safety-tips_us) don't fully appreciate until it matters: your dog and cat are working much harder than you are to stay cool. Dogs have sweat glands only on their paw pads, which means panting is their primary cooling mechanism. Cats groom themselves to spread moisture on their fur, which helps a little, but they rely heavily on shade and cool surfaces too. When the air around them is hot and humid, both methods lose effectiveness fast, and body temperature climbs quickly.


A healthy pet's normal body temperature sits between 100 and 102.5 degrees Fahrenheit. Heat stroke sets in around 105 to 106 degrees, and at 108 degrees, organ damage can begin. In extreme heat, that escalation can happen in as little as 30 minutes. Certain pets are at even higher risk: brachycephalic breeds like Pugs, Bulldogs, Boston Terriers, Boxers, and Persian cats have shortened airways that make efficient panting nearly impossible. Older pets, overweight animals, puppies, kittens, and those with heart or respiratory conditions also overheat faster than a healthy adult dog on a moderate day.


Knowing your specific pet's risk level is one of the most practical things you can do before summer is fully underway.


## **Signs of Heat Stroke to Watch For**


Catching heat-related illness early is the difference between a stressful afternoon and a genuine emergency. Early signs of heat stroke include excessive panting or drooling, bright red or dark gums, weakness, and stumbling. As the condition progresses, pets may vomit, develop bloody diarrhea, become confused or lethargic, and in severe cases, collapse or seize.


If you see any of these warning signs of heat stroke, move your pet to a cool, shaded area immediately. Apply cool water (not ice cold) to the stomach, groin, and armpits. Offer small amounts of cool water and reintroduce water gradually if your pet is alert enough to drink. Then get to a veterinarian right away, even if your pet appears to stabilize. Internal damage from heat stroke is not always visible, and what looks like recovery can mask serious complications.


## **Keep Activity Scheduled for Cooler Times**


The single most effective way to prevent heat-related illness is timing. The hottest part of the day typically runs from late morning through the mid-to-late afternoon, roughly 11 a.m. to 4 or 5 p.m. Schedule walks in the early morning or during the evening hours to avoid excessive heat and the day's high temperatures. Strenuous exercise should be avoided on days with high humidity, even if the temperature feels manageable, and during early summer when pets haven't yet adjusted to the season's higher temps.


Shorter, shadier routes also help. Aim for grassy paths or tree-lined streets when you can. Always bring water and a collapsible bowl. Pets need frequent drink breaks during any outdoor activity in warm weather, not just when they look thirsty.


## **Protect Your Pet's Paws from Hot Pavement**


Pavement absorbs heat and holds it well past when the sun goes down. On a day with an air temperature of 85 degrees, asphalt can reach surface temperatures of 135 degrees or higher. Your pet's paw pads can suffer burns quickly and with less drama than you'd expect. By the time your dog is limping, licking their feet, or refusing to walk, the damage is already done.


The five-second rule is your most useful tool here: press the back of your hand flat on the pavement. If you cannot hold it there comfortably for five seconds, it is too hot for your dog's paws. Choose grass, dirt trails, or shaded sidewalks instead because hot asphalt and hot pavement can quickly burn your dog's paw pads. Protective booties designed for hot surfaces are another option for pet parents who need to stick to paved routes.


## **Hydration and Water Safety**


Fresh water and clean water should be available at all times during the summer months. At home, keep multiple water bowls in cool spots and add ice cubes to help water stay cold longer. On outings, bring enough water for both of you and stop for breaks even if your pet hasn't asked. Pets won't always signal thirst clearly, especially when they're excited about being outside.


A quick hydration check at home: gently lift the skin over your pet's shoulders. If it snaps back immediately, hydration is good. If it returns slowly or stays tented, your pet may be dehydrated and needs water and a cool spot right away.


Near pools, lakes, and the beach, the risks shift. Not all dogs are strong swimmers, and even confident swimmers can tire quickly in open water or against a current. Introduce your pet to water gradually before any trip that involves swimming. Life jackets are essential on boats and paddleboards; if your dog falls off a paddleboard in open water, tipping the board trying to pull them back up is a real risk. Rinse your pet after any swim to remove chlorine, salt, or bacteria from their coat and paws, and never let your dog drink from the pool, ocean, or lake.


## **Never Leave Your Pet in a Parked Car**


This one has no gray area. On an 85-degree day, the interior of a parked car can reach 100 degrees in under 10 minutes. Cracking the windows makes almost no difference. Shade helps very little. Cars trap and amplify heat faster than almost any other environment, and pets cannot escape from them.


If you're running errands, your pet is safest at home in a cool space. If you travel with your pet frequently in warm weather, plan your stops around places where your pet can come with you or stay in a climate-controlled vehicle.
