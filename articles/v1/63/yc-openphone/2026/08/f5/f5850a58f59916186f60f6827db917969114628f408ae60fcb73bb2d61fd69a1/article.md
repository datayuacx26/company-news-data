# Average handle time: Is it costing you money?

One rep is on the phone with a customer for 20 minutes. Another rep wraps a call about a similar issue in 90 seconds. But there are repeat contacts for the same issue. Your instinct tells you the same issue should take your reps roughly the same amount of time to solve, but that’s not happening. Could it be a talkative customer? Or an undertrained employee?


Without a concrete number to point to, it’s hard to know where the problem is.


That number is average handle time. Calculating it is the first step toward spotting patterns that quietly drain your team’s time and your customers’ patience. On its own, the number isn’t good or bad. It’s a starting point — a way to spot which reps, calls, or issue types deserve a closer look.


This guide covers what average handle time means, how to calculate it, why it matters, and how to improve it.


##


**What is Average Handle Time?**


Average handle time, or AHT, is the average amount of time it takes your team to fully handle a customer interaction. You start tracking it from the moment the interaction starts to the moment your team finishes all the work tied to it. This includes any wrap-up after the call or conversation ends.


Calculating AHT tells you how long the entire[call handling](https://www.quo.com/blog/call-handling/) process takes. Plus, it shows how efficiently your team moves through interactions.


AHT also helps you determine how much of a rep’s time one interaction costs. That way, you can make better decisions about your staffing needs and budgeting.


Average handle time started as a call center KPI. It was created to measure call center agent performance. But businesses can, and should, also use this metric to understand how their team is handling communication.


##


**How do you calculate average handle time?**


The average handle time formula is straightforward. To find out how to calculate average handle time, you need three metrics for a given period:


- **Total talk time,** or the time spent on the interaction.
- **Total hold time,** or the time the customer spent waiting or on call transfers.
- **Total**[after-call work](https://www.quo.com/blog/after-call-work/) **or follow-up time** . ACW includes wrap-up tasks like notes, follow-up emails, or updating a ticket.


Once you have those, plug the numbers into this formula:


**AHT** = Total talk time + total hold time + after-call work time, divided by total calls handled


The same logic extends to non-call channels. You divide the total time spent resolving a request by the number of interactions handled.


**AHT formula for texting:** **** **AHT** = Total conversation time, from first message to resolution ÷ total text conversations handled


**AHT formula for email:** **** **AHT** = Total active working time + wait time between replies ÷ total emails handled


Let’s put this into practice.


### **Average handle time calculation example**


Say a small team handled 200 calls last month and their time breakdown was:


- Total talk time: 1,200 minutes
- Total hold time: 200 minutes
- Total after-call work: 400 minutes


Plug those into the formula, and the AHT is 9 minutes:


**(1,200 + 200 + 400) ÷ 200 = 9 minutes**


That number becomes the baseline they can track over time. They can compare team or individual rep performance to spot outliers.


##


Why does average handle time matter for businesses?


Here’s what calculating your AHT does for your business:


- **Higher customer satisfaction.** A high AHT can mean long waits and unresolved issues. A low one can mean customers feel rushed off the phone before their problem’s fixed. Both extremes hurt the customer experience and lead to higher customer churn.
- **Lower operational costs and improved efficiency.** A lower AHT means your existing team can handle more calls without adding headcount. This lowers your cost per interaction.
- **More targeted coaching.** A rep who’s consistently well above or below the team average is worth a closer look. It might point to a skills gap or to someone rushing calls because they’re busy with other tasks.
- **Easier staffing and capacity planning.** Knowing your AHT helps you estimate how many people you’ll‌ need to cover expected call, text, and email volume. No more following gut instincts when it comes to hiring and scheduling.

### **1. Measure your average handle time to get your baseline**

### **2. Give reps full customer context before the call starts**


A large share of AHT goes toward reps re-asking questions the business already has answers to. Across hundreds of calls, that adds up to a meaningful chunk of your AHT.


The fix is about technology *and* habit. Every time a rep learns something new about a customer or lead, they should log it in your phone system or CRM. And just as important, reps need to ‌check that information before and during the call.


In Quo, there are two ways to build that context:


- **Contact notes** give reps a place to log details from conversations. It’s a freeform hub to record details like past requests and preferences.
- **Custom properties** are more structured. Set up fields for things like customer type, service history, or plan details. That way, a rep can see the essentials at a glance without digging through notes.


If you use a CRM, take this a step further with CRM sync. Connecting Quo to a tool like[HubSpot](https://www.quo.com/integrations/hubspot) or[Jobber](https://www.quo.com/integrations/jobber) auto-syncs call summaries and context between systems. This means reps aren’t stuck copying notes back and forth or switching tabs mid-call to check a customer’s history.


### **3. Have an internal knowledge base for reps**


Plenty of the inquiries reps field come from the same handful of questions. When a rep doesn’t have a quick answer on hand, the call stalls. They may even put the customer on hold to track down a teammate or dig through old emails, and that hold time gets baked straight into your AHT.


Internal knowledge management takes care of this. Build out a reference document with answers to your most common questions. This could cover things like appointment rescheduling or your pricing tiers. Make it easily available so reps can answer on the spot instead of pausing the call to find out. This is especially valuable for newer team members, who won’t yet have those answers memorized and may have to lean on more experienced reps.


### **4. Route calls to the right person the first time**


Beyond wasting time, misrouted calls frustrate customers. If a call routes to someone who can’t‌ help, it might have to be transferred, which extends the call. Or the rep has to scramble to find an answer they’re not equipped to give. An unclear escalation path makes this worse, since reps may not be sure who to loop in or how.


There are a few ways to fix this at the call routing level:


- [Business-hours routing](https://www.quo.com/product/call-routing/business-hours-call-routing) **.** Send calls down a different path based on whether they come in during business hours or after. This keeps a call from landing with someone who isn’t available to help.
- **An**[IVR, or Interactive Voice Response](https://www.quo.com/blog/what-is-ivr/) **, menu.** This is a self-service option that lets callers route themselves to the right person or department. That cuts down on transfers before the call even reaches a rep.
- **Ring groups.** Once calls are routed, ring groups control how they’re distributed among your team. For example, you could choose to ring your senior reps first if the caller is calling with a billing issue. Or, for the fastest possible response, you can have everyone in a group ring simultaneously.


On Quo, you can set this up with a drag-and-drop visual call flow builder. It’s a tool that makes it easier to map out routing, hours, and ring groups without technical support.


### **5. Have a call control strategy for difficult or escalated calls**


Staying on the phone with an upset customer because of an issue you don’t have the authority or ability to resolve just extends the call. It can also leave the customer even more frustrated than when they started.


If it’s clear you’re not going to resolve the issue on the call, employ[de-escalation techniques](https://www.quo.com/blog/de-escalation-techniques-customer-service/) to cool things down. Then ensure you have the key details and get the caller to the right person or set up a[callback](https://www.quo.com/blog/customer-callback/) .


When you *do* hand the call off, use a warm transfer rather than a cold one. This means looping in the next person with context on who’s calling and what’s already been discussed. That way, they can hit the ground running.

When nobody’s available to pick up, what matters is capturing enough detail that the callback can resolve things quickly. A callback where the customer has to explain everything all over again increases AHT and frustrates your customer.

##


**Is there an industry benchmark for a good average handle time score?**


Some industries do publish AHT benchmarks. So it’s fair to ask whether you should aim for one of those numbers.


For example, customer service contact centers usually have an[average handle time of about 10 minutes](https://kayako.com/blog/average-handle-time-industry-standard/) .[Retail companies are closer to five minutes](https://kayako.com/blog/average-handle-time-industry-standard/) . Business, IT, and financial services companies often take under five minutes.


The trouble is, these numbers may not be a useful target for a small or growing service business. Benchmarks like these vary wildly by industry, issue complexity, and channel mix. A six-minute average for retail chatbots doesn’t reveal much about your own team’s phone consultations.


Chasing a number that has nothing to do with your business can backfire. Reps start optimizing to hit the target instead of resolving the customer’s issue.


Once you’ve got your own baseline, the more useful exercise is finding your outliers. Which reps or calls fall well outside *your* baseline in either direction and why? That’s where you’ll find the fixable problems that are relevant to your business.

FAQs


**What other metrics should you track to know if your AHT changes are working?**


AHT alone can’t tell you if a change helped. A shorter call duration could mean you’re putting speed of service over quality. That’s why you track it alongside other customer service metrics like:
–[First Call Resolution](https://www.quo.com/blog/first-call-resolution/) **or First Contact Resolution.** FCR measures the number of issues your reps resolved on the first call. It’s a signal of efficiency and quality service. Sometimes AHT drops and FCR drops along with it. That’s a sign the change rushed customers rather than made the process more efficient.
– **Customer Satisfaction Score, or CSAT.** Pairing it with AHT shows whether shorter calls are also satisfying ones.
– **Net Promoter Score®.** NPS measures how likely customers are to recommend a company to a friend or colleague on a scale of one to ten. A persistent AHT problem, whether too slow or too rushed, can eventually show up as a drop in NPS over time.


**How often should you review your average handle time?**


Match your average handle time review to seasonal shifts or new hires ramping up. You might also review the metric if you’ve implemented a new process change.


**What’s the difference between average handle time and average talk time?**


Average talk time is only one piece of AHT. It’s the time a rep spends‌ speaking with the customer. AHT is the broader metric. It comprises talk time plus hold time plus after-call work.
That distinction matters because a team can have low talk time but high handle time if your average hold time or after-call work is the real bottleneck.


**Do you need dedicated call center software to calculate average handle time?**


No. A business can calculate AHT manually using free tools like Excel or Google Sheets. The tradeoff is time and effort. Someone has to pull call, text, and email durations by hand on a regular basis to keep the numbers current.
