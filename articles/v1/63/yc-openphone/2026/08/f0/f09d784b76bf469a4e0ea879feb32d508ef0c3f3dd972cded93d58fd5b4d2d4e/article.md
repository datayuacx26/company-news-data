# Voice of Customer examples: 8 Real stories from growing businesses

When you look online for examples of how to use customer feedback in your business, you likely see the same ones over and over: Tesla. Disney. Apple. Netflix.


Sure, those companies are doing Voice of Customer analysis. But they’re also major brands with in-house CX research teams and enterprise tools. Not to mention, they have marketing budgets bigger than some companies’ annual revenue. Not exactly a replicable model to follow.


It can feel like VoC is something to come back to later, when the business — and your resources — are bigger. But if no one’s paying attention to VoC *now* , you might miss the exact signals you need to grow to the next stage.


Voice of Customer examples don’t have to come from huge companies with multi-million-dollar budgets. There *are* already small and growing businesses, like law firms and tax practices, running VoC programs. They’re doing it with tools and data they already have.


Better yet: they provide models you can follow, with data *you* probably already have too. This article shows you how.


##


What is Voice of Customer?


Voice of Customer, or VoC, is a form of[customer intelligence](https://www.quo.com/blog/customer-intelligence/) . It means having a system to collect what customers say to or about your business, in their own words. The goal: understand their needs, frustrations, preferences, and expectations across the customer journey. Then, you use that[customer sentiment](https://www.quo.com/blog/customer-sentiment/) to improve your business. You can reduce churn, improve customer lifetime value, and grow your business.


VoC data can come from all kinds of sources:


- [Call analytics](https://www.quo.com/blog/call-analytics/) , recordings, or transcripts
- Customer emails or support tickets
- Customer interviews
- Online customer reviews
- Post-purchase reviews or exit surveys
- Web forms or chatbot contact forms
- Social media platforms


You probably already have VoC data available. You just need to systematically review and analyze it. VoC analysis isn’t reserved for enterprises or companies with huge budgets. We’ll show you examples of how small and growing businesses are making it work across different industries — and letting it change and improve their businesses.


##


Voice of Customer examples for the legal industry


Potential legal clients are often in a stressful situation and making quick decisions. Slow or missed responses, or not knowing how to connect with customers, can cost more than just a lead. It can lose someone at their most vulnerable moment.


Using VoC analysis can help you pinpoint what clients want and need at their most vulnerable — and then provide it for them.


### 1. Hannon De Palma


**Business type:** Divorce and family law firm


**Location:** White Plains, NY


At[Hannon De Palma](https://www.quo.com/customers/hannon-de-palma) , after-hours inquiries went unanswered for 24–72 hours. Chris Sands, the CEO, found that by the time someone responded, the lead had already called someone else.


After reviewing their call and customer data, the evidence was clear. Potential clients were stressed and needed answers and help fast.


So Chris and the Hannon De Palma team implemented several changes:


- They started using Quo’s[Make integration](https://www.quo.com/integrations/make) to auto-text every website inquiry as soon as it came in. This lets the person know when to expect a response and what happens next.
- They routed all after-hours calls through an external intake team.
- They enabled[shared inboxes](https://www.quo.com/blog/how-to-share-a-business-phone-number-with-your-team/) , so any team member had full visibility into client communications. Clients didn’t have to keep rehashing what they needed. Team members didn’t have to waste time getting others up to speed.


As a result, their response time dropped from 24–72 hours to two to five minutes, up to 90% faster. Better yet, customers get help right away when they need it, know what to expect, and only have to share their case details once. And the whole team at Hannon De Palma stays up to date.


*“I can’t even imagine what kind of workflows we’d have without Quo. It’d probably be doing 10% of the efficiency that we’re doing now.”* — Chris Sands, CEO of Hannon De Palma


### 2. Cantor, Wolff, Nicastro and Hall


**Business type:** Personal injury law firm


**Location:** Buffalo, NY


Joe Nicastro, of Cantor, Wolff, Nicastro & Hall LLC, knew how important it was to meet customer needs. And not just *needs* , but also customers’ *expectations and preferences* .


When it comes to personal injury law, clients are often dealing with a lot at once. For example, doctors, medical bills, multiple insurances, and often, personal recovery. Their firm works out of Buffalo, NY. But the team quickly realized that[conversation analytics](https://www.quo.com/blog/conversation-analytics/) from customer data pointed toward another option customers wanted: the ability to coordinate with their lawyers remotely.


*“A lot of clients, maybe because of medical reasons, want to do things at a distance. Our society has become more comfortable with doing things remotely, and it’s more efficient. It’s cheaper, and it’s more convenient for a lot of clients.”* — Joe Nicastro, Founding partner and attorney at Cantor, Wolff, Nicastro & Hall LLC


Once they realized customers preferred a remote option, they made a change. They started using a legal practice management platform to help them do that. Now once the consultation is complete, the legal team can communicate, send and sign documents, and move cases forward without needing to meet in person.


It fits customer preferences and lifestyles, and it gives Joe’s team more flexibility as well.

### 4. Trio Heating & Air


**Business type:** HVAC company


**Location:** Greater San Francisco Bay Area


Trio Heating & Air was staring down stagnant growth. They do business in a tough place for HVAC — San Francisco, where the weather is cool and temperate — and were running into a very slow season in 2024.


But why? The Katz brothers, owners of Trio Heating & Air, asked their customers.


Many people had already done years’ worth of HVAC repairs in 2020, when they were spending most of their time at home. Plus, high inflation meant people were cutting back on nonessential repairs or upgrades.


So the Katz Brothers pivoted to new marketing and lead generation techniques. They wanted to meet customers where they were, with their current needs and limitations:


- They offered extra services that better matched what customers needed — for example, not necessarily a whole new A/C system but a small update.
- They set up financing options to make getting started easier.
- They offered free tune-ups to local potential leads to start a relationship so they could be there when needed.
- They tailored their messaging to their customer’s current pain points.


As a result, they continued to get more leads and create relationships with potential customers even during a slow season.

### 6. Edge Health Clinic


**Business type:** Holistic, integrative chiropractor and wellness clinic


**Location:** South Barrie, Ontario


Edge Health Clinic started as a two-person chiropractic practice. But Dr. Chuck and Dr. Ingoldsby noticed an interesting theme in patient feedback.


After their appointments, patients were asking similar questions: Who should I see for a massage? For my knee? For my ankle that never healed right?


Their hands-on VoC research was telling them something very clearly. Their patients needed more than just chiropractic support.


Instead of continuing to source and send referrals, they decided to provide the support patients were looking for. They expanded their practice.


In the past 10 years, they’ve brought on 10 new physicians in a variety of specialties. When patients need a referral or are looking for more support, they can send them down the hall. It’s more convenient for patients, and it’s good business for them.

Voice of Customer examples for ecommerce companies


Ecommerce may not have the emotional intensity of legal or healthcare professions. But it has something else: endless options. If you aren’t meeting your customers’ needs and preferences, they’ll have nearly unlimited other options to choose from. That’s what makes VoC so crucial for ecommerce.


### 7. Zazzy Box


**Business type:** Custom jewelry


**Location:** Houston, TX


Asit Shah continued the family business jewelry tradition with Zazzy Box. But mixing ecommerce with a big-ticket purchase like a custom diamond ring isn’t easy.


When a customer invests in a $3,000 custom diamond ring — a deeply personal purchase — they expect more than transactional service. ‍Customers want, and need, real connection. And they want it via a method that wasn’t ideal for Asit: texting.


*“I actually hated texting in the beginning. But I had to quickly learn to text with everyone because the supply chain side of our business uses WhatsApp and other texting tools. I just had to accept that this is how our supply chain likes to talk. And then I found out that customers also like that experience.”* — Asit Shah, founder of Zazzy Box


Asit listened to what his customers wanted: clear, personal customer service through texts. But he quickly became the bottleneck. He was managing vendor communications, customer questions, and more on his personal phone.


He knew that he needed to scale texting support without losing the personal touch. So he shifted to a text-first model with Quo’s shared numbers. They:


- Used[Zapier automations](https://www.quo.com/integrations/zapier) to send personalized order updates to hundreds of customers via text
- Allowed the whole team to field customer questions via text using Quo’s shared numbers


Customers embraced the change. They appreciated the ability to speak directly with different team members involved in their orders. It made the process feel even more personal and transparent.


The team now provides tailored quotes in 24 hours — twice as fast as competitors — and[saves over 160 hours a month](http://quo.com/customers/zazzy-box) .


### 8. Good Girl Snacks


**Business type:** Consumer-packaged goods — pickles


**Location:** West Hollywood, CA


Leah Marcus and Yasaman Bakhtiar, the co-founders of Good Girl Snacks, created a series of pickle flavors they really loved. But there was just one problem: the market for pickles isn’t exactly… booming.


So they set out to find a way to make their pickles irresistible — asking their customers what they could improve. They intentionally gathered customer feedback anywhere they could. They ran email surveys and polls on social media. The team even reached out to people directly and asked for feedback.


The #1 thing customers were saying: it was easier to snack on a smaller pickle.


So Good Girl Snacks pivoted. Instead of large, full-sized pickles, they swapped the recipe to use smaller gherkins. This made their snacks “smaller, crunchier, and saltier,” co-founder Leah says. But more importantly: they were able to give customers the satisfying snack they were looking for.


As a result, when they launched officially, they had a much easier time securing product-market fit. They grew quickly with a niche product their audience loved.

How to start with Voice of Customer analysis this week


All these VoC examples have one thing in common. They highlight situations where a small and growing business listened to their customers, made a change — and saw a reward as a result.


There was no top-down “invest in VoC!” push. They didn’t have complicated analysis tools or software.


They just paid attention to what customers were *already* telling them, in person, over the phone, via text, or in reviews. Plus, they acted quickly to close the loop.


You can do the same thing — and see results for your business, too. The first step: **Define** ***one*** **question you want to answer.** This should be the main pain point you’re experiencing at the moment. For example:

- What’s making leads hesitate instead of moving forward?

This question will help guide you toward the information you need to collect, where to look for customer feedback, and what to do with the results.

FAQs


****Do small businesses use Voice of Customer analysis?****


Yes, small businesses can use Voice of Customer analysis. It helps you systematically collect what customers are saying. Then you can look for patterns to understand customer needs. This helps you identify trends, improve customer satisfaction, and make better business decisions.


****What are some methods for collecting Voice of Customer data?****


You can start with the customer feedback you already have. Other options include:
– Analyzing call data and recorded calls
– Doing surveys
– Measuring your NPS® — Net Promoter Score — or CSAT — or customer satisfaction score
– Reviewing customer service tickets
– Looking for trends in social media comments, online reviews, or customer DMs, also known as social listening
– Hosting focus groups for specific feedback


****What are the benefits of a Voice of the Customer program?****


A Voice of the Customer program can help you:
**– Improve customer satisfaction.** Listen and implement customer feedback to strengthen the customer experience.
**– Make better business decisions.** Improve product-market fit and let customer feedback influence the product roadmap.
**– Reduce customer churn** and improve customer loyalty. ****
**– Market more effectively** by putting your marketing into customer language.


****How does AI identify themes in customer feedback for better VoC analytics?****


AI uses NLP, or Natural Language Processing. This technology reads and analyzes written feedback, emails, or transcripts from your customers at scale. Then, it uses text analytics. This searches for key themes, repeated phrases or questions, or patterns.


****How is Voice of Customer different from a customer satisfaction survey?****


A CSAT survey is one input for VoC. Voice of Customer is broader. It includes surveys, but also feedback from calls, texts, reviews, and support conversations. These are all analyzed together for patterns.


****Do you need expensive software to run a Voice of Customer program?****


No. Small businesses can start with data they already have, such as call recordings, text threads, and reviews. You can usually find this through your business phone system rather than a dedicated survey platform.
