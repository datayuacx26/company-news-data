# What if we could see the next opioid crisis before it takes hold?

# What if we could see the next opioid crisis before it takes hold?


### Using wastewater to track the evolving drug landscape


[Marisa Donnelly, PhD](https://substack.com/@marisadonnelly)


and[Kait Hess Jimenez](https://substack.com/@kaithessjimenez)


May 07, 2026


What if we could have seen the opioid crisis before it gripped the country? If we could have detected the shift from prescription opioids to fentanyl? If we had been able to identify communities where fentanyl was first prevalent and alerted them that a new, more potent opioid, with much higher overdose potential, was now in the drug supply. Could we have distributed more naloxone or drug testing strips to the communities that needed them most? How many lives could we have saved if we had recognized the ubiquity of opioid consumption sooner?


The four waves of overdose mortality. Figure from


[Friedman and Shover](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/add.16318) .


We can’t rewrite the early 2000s, when opioids were taking hold. But we can learn from them and build faster and stronger detection and treatment strategies. In the early days of Rx opioid’s and fentanyl’s spread, national surveillance tools weren’t built for rapid detection and response. Today, we’re in a different position. Wastewater monitoring gives us a faster, more complete way to see changes in substance use, so we can act quickly before problems escalate.


### Traditional surveillance data: necessary but lagging


Traditional substance use epidemiology and data are important and help us understand the bigger picture.


But, these data have real limitations, especially their lack of timeliness. Large national surveys take years to collect, analyze, and report results. CDC’s final national overdose data for 2024, for example, was


[just released in April, 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/vsrr/drug-overdose-data.htm) . Survey data are subject to under-reporting and recall bias (where people don’t remember exactly what they were exposed to, or may not be honest). These approaches also assume people know what they’ve consumed, and with emerging drugs and polysubstance use (the use or dependence on multiple substances), this assumption becomes increasingly difficult.


Healthcare data like emergency department visits, 911 or poison control calls, or hospitalizations can characterize burden, but these are often more reflective access to care, insurance coverage, and local policies, like Good Samaritan laws. They only capture those who interact with the system. Individuals who are silently struggling with substance use disorder but never interact with EMS, hospital systems, or community surveys are not included in the data, and thus excluded from the overall picture of community substance use.


Traditional data remain an important piece of the system. But they are often too delayed, too incomplete, or too dependent on health care access to keep pace with today’s rapidly changing drug supply.


### The drug supply is changing: it’s no longer just fentanyl


It’s true that fentanyl is still a huge issue—in 2024, an


[estimated 60% of overdose deaths](https://usafacts.org/articles/are-fentanyl-overdose-deaths-rising-in-the-us/) in the U.S. were due to synthetic opioids, mostly fentanyl. But newer drugs are increasingly apparent in the drug supply: nitazines and orphines (new opioids), xylazine and medetomidine (non-opioid sedatives), semi-synthetic cannabinoids (THC products), kratom-derived compounds (7-hydroxymitragynine), and combinations people may not even know they were exposed to, including other adulterants and stimulants like cocaine or methamphetamine. This is incredibly concerning.


Because the more complex the supply becomes, the harder overdose response becomes too.


Naloxone (also known as Narcan) is still a critical, life-saving tool and it should still be the first response when someone is overdosing. It’s also now widely available over the counter and through free public health programs. One of its benefits is that it won’t harm someone if opioids aren’t involved, but it could save their life if they are.


But, naloxone isn’t effective for everything—it won’t reverse non-opioids like medetomidine or xylazine, and with more potent opioids you may need multiple doses. And when people are exposed to multiple substances at once, treatment and recovery becomes much more complicated—you may end up needing to address dependence on multiple substances at once, each with its own treatment strategy.


We need better systems that keep up with the changing substance use landscape: faster tools to identify what’s circulating in the drug supply, warning systems to alert people who may be exposed, and more responsive strategies for poly-substance use and what communities are actually seeing on the ground, or in the case of wastewater, below the ground ;).


Right now, there are still huge gaps in what we can see and respond to in time.


### The case for wastewater—and downstream public health action


This is where wastewater can help.


Wastewater data can fill in some of the most important gaps: speed, geographic coverage, anonymity, and visibility into the rapidly changing drug supply.


Because of our infrastructure, Biobot’s wastewater monitoring network is uniquely positioned to help communities understand what substances relevant to overdose risk are circulating, where trends are changing, and when new drugs may be emerging—all while protecting individual privacy. Wastewater samples provide aggregated, community-level signals across communities.


Examples of substances relevant to overdose prevention and drug supply monitoring.


Biobot’s focus is on community-level indicators of the evolving drug supply and overdose risk (i.e. not individual medication use). We’re currently working in partnership with the


[White House Office of National Drug Control Policy (ONDCP)](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/biobot-analytics-selected-by-white-house-office-of-national-drug-control-policy-to-provide-nationwide-wastewater-intelligence-on-substance-use-trends-302654584.html) on a national wastewater monitoring effort focused on supporting overdose prevention, community health response, and emerging drug supply risks. Our data visualizations are helping identify where overdose-prevention resources may be needed most, and where emerging drug supply changes may require faster action and outreach. For example, because wastewater data can be generated much faster than traditional public health data, our data and insights showed that fentanyl consumption was decreasing more than a year before the CDC reported similar findings in their overdose data.


Biobot’s national and regional fentanyl averages from our national network of wastewater monitoring sites.


There are key features that make wastewater especially useful for getting ahead of community health issues:


**Near-real-time reporting:** Biobot’s Wastewater data can be processed and made available within days of substance use. It’s one of the fastest ways to identify community-level changes in the drug supply and overdose risk.


**Community-wide population coverage:** Wastewater captures a community-wide signal—not just people who appear in surveys, hospitals, or toxicology reports. A single wastewater sample represents an aggregated community signal from a sewer catchment area. It does not tell us who used a substance, who is taking medication, or what happened in any individual or household.


**Privacy and anonymity:** Data from wastewater is naturally anonymized because it’s aggregated and contains no identifying data. The wastewater samples Biobot typically works with are from thousands to millions of people and contain no personal identifiers. We report community-level patterns to guide public health action, and don’t collect any individual data, behaviors, or treatment status. Our goal is to help communities respond faster to overdose risk, not to identify or penalize people.


**Breadth of detection:** Biobot can test for and quantify more than 30 substances. We can also use non-targeted methods to detect unexpected or emerging compounds in the drug supply, like novel opioids or adulterants. This capability is intended to help public health teams identify new overdose risks (not monitor individual medication use). This matters because emerging substances can spread quietly before they appear in traditional data systems.


**Cost effectiveness:** Wastewater monitoring offers broad visibility at a very low cost. A single wastewater sample represents an entire community, often translating to literal cents per person per year. That is substantially cheaper than trying to understand local trends only through individual urine tests, toxicology screens, or clinical encounters, which are far more expensive and only represent those accessing the system.


### The next generation of substance use intervention


We are seeing the drug landscape change rapidly, with new substances, different combinations, and spread into new regions.


And we need community health infrastructure that can keep up.


At Biobot, we see wastewater as part of the next generation of substance use intervention, taking what we’ve learned from the opioid crisis to prevent it from ever happening again.


Communities need faster ways to understand what’s changing in the drug supply, where trends are shifting, and how to respond. Implementing wastewater monitoring to track these trends buys community stakeholders critical time to act faster than they ever could before.


That added time could translate to issuing alerts before someone unknowingly consumes something dangerously potent, distributing naloxone to the neighborhoods most in need, or preparing clinicians and outreach teams to effectively treat what’s showing up in their clinics.


By building wastewater monitoring into community health infrastructure now, we have a chance to identify shifts early, and respond faster.
