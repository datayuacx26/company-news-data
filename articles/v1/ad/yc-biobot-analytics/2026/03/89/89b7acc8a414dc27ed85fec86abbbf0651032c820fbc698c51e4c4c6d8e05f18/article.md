# The longest RSV season we’ve seen in years

# The longest RSV season we’ve seen in years


### And what it means for community health in the U.S.


[Marisa Donnelly, PhD](https://substack.com/@marisadonnelly)


Mar 30, 2026


This year’s RSV season has followed an unusual path: a later start, with a much longer tail. And wastewater is helping track why that’s so important to pay attention to.


Biobot’s national wastewater network shows that RSV is still hanging on later into the season than many expected, having real-world implications for treatment and prevention guidance. Our most recent data shows that while RSV levels are finally trending downward, overall RSV activity is still elevated nationally, with the highest levels in the Northeast and Midwest regions.


These signals and trends matter because RSV prevention timing has been built around a set time period coinciding with the classic RSV season. For infants, protection against severe RSV can come from maternal vaccination during pregnancy or from a monoclonal antibody after birth. CDC guidance says RSV antibodies for infants (nirsevimab or clesrovimab) should be administered from October through the end of March, to protect infants when risk is


*traditionally* highest. But CDC also explicitly allows public health departments to revise that window based on local RSV epidemiology, including continuing beyond March when local activity supports it.


In other words: the calendar is a guide, but the virus actually decides the season.


### **Wastewater shows the long tail, earlier**


Wastewater is particularly useful for RSV because traditional surveillance can miss part of the picture. Testing varies by clinic, milder infections often go undetected, and hospitalizations tend to reflect severe disease only after transmission is already underway. Wastewater can provide a population level read on whether transmission is truly fading or whether it’s still simmering. Especially with less hospitalizations, clinical data can lag for RSV, fluctuate with testing practices throughout the season, and miss milder infections that never make it into a doctor’s office. Wastewater helps fill in that gap by showing what’s circulating in communities in near real time.


This year, RSV rose later than usual in many places, and instead of dropping off quickly, as it often does following the peak, levels have stayed elevated deeper into early spring. Biobot’s latest national report described RSV as “declining but still at high levels,” with regional data showing that most parts of the country are still very much in season.


Hospitalization data also show that RSV is still contributing to severe illness, especially among the youngest children. CDC’s RSV hospitalization surveillance shows that RSV hospitalizations are highest among infants and children ages 4 and younger.


### **What this means for infant protection**


How does this translate to real-world impacts? A prolonged season can translate into a longer period of risk for families with infants who are young and vulnerable. For infant monoclonal antibody programs, seasonal dynamics create a practical challenge: babies born after the traditional cutoff may still face significant RSV exposure if transmission remains elevated locally. That makes timely, local surveillance especially important.


Without changes to the March cutoff for the RSV antibody schedule this season, babies born in April, when RSV will still be elevated, could be left unprotected while RSV continues to circulate. Thankfully, some states, like California, are stepping up to cover the gaps.


### **How California adapted: extending antibody recommendations for a longer season**


Earlier in March,


[California issued a Health Alert](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/OPA/Pages/CAHAN/CDPH-Recommends-Immunizing-Infants-with-RSV-Monoclonal-Antibodies-through-April-2026.aspx) notifying healthcare practitioners that the Department of Public Health recommended continuing RSV immunization of eligible infants and young children with RSV monoclonal antibodies through April 30, 2026:


-


All infants aged 0 through 7 months, within 1 week of birth, ideally during the birth hospitalization or as soon as possible after discharge (unless prenatal RSV immunization occurred between 32 through 36 weeks of gestation and at least 14 days before delivery)


-


Nirsevimab or clesrovimab are recommended


-


Children aged 8 through 19 months at high risk of severe RSV


-


Only nirsevimab is recommended


California’s wastewater data, as well as other sources, showed that the RSV season started later and remained elevated for longer. Because of these elevated levels, the Department of Public Health determined that RSV transmission would last into April.


Babies born later in the RSV season in California are now more likely to be given these monoclonal antibodies, and therefore, protected against RSV. When states, or other entities, consider extending RSV immunization windows, Biobot’s wastewater can provide an additional, community-level signal to support those decisions.


### **The takeaway**


The timing and intensity of the RSV season is not fixed. It shifts by year and by community. That is why wastewater is so valuable: it helps us see when the season is really ending, not when we assume it should end.


For manufacturers of RSV prevention products, tracking the dynamics of the season is not just interesting data. It affects when protection is most needed, where risk remains elevated, and how quickly medical, policy, communications, and commercial teams may need to adapt.


For families, clinicians, and policymakers, the takeaway is that if RSV is still elevated in wastewater, we should respond like the season is still here.


And this year, it is.
