# AI Spotlight: Perplexity AI CEO Challenges Nandan Nilekani's Vision for India's AI Pathway


In a heated debate over India's AI strategy, Perplexity AI CEO Aravind Srinivas opposes Nandan Nilekani's advice to focus solely on AI applications, advocating for a balanced approach that includes developing foundational models. Drawing inspiration from China's success with DeepSeek, Srinivas reinforces the importance of India's technological independence in the AI landscape.


## Introduction to India's AI Strategy Debate


India is currently facing a pivotal moment in its approach to AI strategy, sparked by a significant debate between two influential figures:[Perplexity](https://opentools.ai/tools/perplexity) AI CEO Aravind Srinivas and Infosys co‑founder Nandan Nilekani. At the heart of this debate lies the question of whether India should prioritize developing AI applications using existing[large language models](https://opentools.ai/categories/other) (LLMs) or invest in building foundational AI models. The outcome of this debate is likely to shape the future trajectory of India's AI capabilities and its position in the global AI landscape.


Aravind Srinivas argues that India must not limit itself to simply creating applications built on existing LLMs, but should also invest in developing indigenous foundational models. He points to China's DeepSeek as a successful example of a nation that has managed to establish its own AI models despite significant resource constraints. Srinivas believes that having the[capacity](https://opentools.ai/tools/capacity) to build AI models from the ground up will ensure technological sovereignty and empower India to set industry standards and influence policies globally.


In contrast, Nandan Nilekani holds a different perspective, advocating for a focused approach on developing AI applications by utilizing the existing open‑source models available in the tech ecosystem. He emphasizes the potential for India to excel by efficiently allocating resources towards improving AI infrastructure and cloud computing services rather than investing heavily in foundational model training, which he considers to be costly and complex. Nilekani's approach prioritizes immediate implementation and cost‑effectiveness, aligning with a pragmatic view of India's current technological landscape.


The stakes in this debate are high, impacting economic potential, technological independence, and India's standing in the global AI community. Advocates for developing foundational models argue that it could unlock new markets,[create](https://opentools.ai/tools/create) technology‑driven job opportunities, and attract sizable private investment through government‑supported initiatives. On the[other](https://opentools.ai/categories/other) hand, focusing on applications could lead to quick wins, leveraging India's existing IT strengths and maximizing cost efficiency.


Public opinion is divided, with many in the tech community resonating with Srinivas's vision of self‑reliance akin to ISRO's approach in space technology. His vision has gained traction particularly among those who foresee AI as a domain where India could be a leader, not just a participant. Conversely, Nilekani's supporters advocate for a more measured approach that avoids the significant risks and costs associated with pioneering new foundational models from scratch. This pragmatic stance highlights the challenges India faces, such as securing large‑scale funding and overcoming current infrastructural hurdles.


The future implications of this strategic decision are vast. Economically, the path chosen could significantly impact India's share in the burgeoning AI market, influence the creation of skilled jobs, and define the nature of public and private sector collaborations. Technologically, developing indigenous models could position India as a leader in AI innovation, decreasing dependency on foreign technology. Socially, it could lead to more culturally nuanced technological solutions that cater to India's diverse population, fostering greater inclusivity in AI‑driven benefits.


## Key Players: Srinivas vs Nilekani


Aravind Srinivas, CEO of[Perplexity](https://opentools.ai/tools/perplexity) AI, and Nandan Nilekani, co‑founder of Infosys, are at the center of a pivotal discussion regarding India's AI strategy. Their differing perspectives represent broader debates on how India should navigate the evolving AI landscape. Srinivas champions a comprehensive approach, supporting both the[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) of AI applications and foundational models, whereas Nilekani argues for focusing resources on the[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) of applications using existing[large language models](https://opentools.ai/categories/other) (LLMs).


The debate touches on vital issues such as the direction of AI[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) within India, technological independence, and India's competitive stance in the global AI arena. Srinivas highlights the strategic importance of building foundational models to ensure innovation and long‑term competitiveness, akin to China's achievements with DeepSeek. Moscow, the snag is the substantial financial and infrastructural hurdles, a lack of investment interest among private entities, and the necessity of governmental backing to support such ambitious ventures.


In contrast, Nilekani emphasizes pragmatic resource allocation by relying on open‑source models, easing the financial burden and enhancing immediate applicability to AI solutions. Nonetheless, Srinivas's vision involves structured government support, open sourcing of models, and fostering[collaboration](https://opentools.ai/categories/collaboration) between tech companies and researchers to overcome financial and technical challenges.


There's also significant discussion about the potential shift in India's AI strategy towards more profound and sustainable technological[capacity](https://opentools.ai/tools/capacity) . This shift could resonate beyond mere[business](https://opentools.ai/categories/business) implications, impacting social spheres and enhancing technological sovereignty by diminishing dependence on foreign technology. Key to this will be leveraging the inherent advantages of India's vast linguistic diversity and building AI models tailored for non‑English languages.


The direction India chooses to pursue could have widespread economic, technological, and social ramifications. Economically, it could shape its position in the burgeoning AI market and spur job creation within the tech industry. Technologically, succeeding in localizing AI solutions can advance sovereignty and inspire emerging markets similarly navigating AI strategy. Socially, this could democratize technology access across India's myriad linguistic communities, fostering a shift in the tech sector towards innovation over services.


## The Stakes for India's AI Future


The debate over India's AI strategic direction reflects a critical juncture in the nation's technological[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) trajectory. While Nandan Nilekani emphasizes the efficiency and cost‑effectiveness of building applications on existing AI models, Aravind Srinivas warns against the risks of over‑reliance on foreign technologies. Srinivas's advocacy for developing indigenous foundational models aligns with a broader vision for technological sovereignty, suggesting a path similar to India's space sector successes, which combined cost‑effectiveness with innovation.


The stakes for India's AI future encompass not just economic outcomes but also technological sovereignty and global positioning. Pursuing foundational models could elevate India's autonomy in technology creation, lessening its dependence on imported AI solutions and giving it more control over its technological future. This direction also promises to foster industry growth analogous to that seen in the space sector, potentially creating new streams of employment and areas of expertise.


The economic implications of India's decision are considerable. Investing in foundational AI models could pave the way for India to capture a significant share of the $1.3 trillion AI market. The government's decision to inject a Rs 2,000 crore investment in AI[research](https://opentools.ai/categories/research) underscores its commitment to fostering innovation, and its potential catalytic effect could stimulate private sector interest and investment in similar endeavors.


Technologically, the success in creating indigenous AI models could lead to the[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) of systems specifically tailored for the Indian milieu, including language‑specific AI applications that serve diverse linguistic communities across the country. This could democratize technology usage and promote broader societal inclusivity.


Globally, India's strategic choices in AI[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) could position it as a key influencer in international AI policies and standards. However, this ambition is tempered by the challenges of developing competitive models with constrained resources. Yet, as seen with China's DeepSeek, ingenuity could potentially overcome these barriers, presenting India as an exemplar for[other](https://opentools.ai/categories/other) developing nations seeking self‑reliance in AI technologies.


## Major Obstacles to AI Development in India


India's journey toward being a leader in AI[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) is fraught with numerous hurdles. Funding plays a critical role, with significant investment needed to develop foundational models. Estimates suggest over $200 million is required, a figure daunting enough to deter private investors with minimal appetite for risk in the tech innovation space. Government support is necessary, not only through direct funding but also through the creation of an ecosystem that encourages private participation.


Additionally, India faces the challenge of building the technical expertise and infrastructure necessary for advanced AI[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) . There is a notable gap in the availability of sophisticated computing facilities and experienced researchers dedicated to AI and[machine learning](https://opentools.ai/categories/machine-learning) pursuits. While educational institutions have begun ramping up efforts to produce AI‑ready graduates, the pace needs to accelerate to meet industry demands.


Strategic disagreements further complicate India's AI landscape. High‑profile tech leaders like Nandan Nilekani and Aravind Srinivas present conflicting visions, each pushing for different priorities; while Nilekani advocates for leveraging existing models to build innovative applications, Srinivas emphasizes the necessity for India to develop its own foundational models to gain true technological independence. This debate reflects broader concerns over whether to focus on immediate benefits through application‑based[development](https://opentools.ai/categories/development) or invest in long‑term foundational capabilities.


Furthermore, market dynamics are another obstacle, as the country navigates a competitive global landscape dominated by AI powerhouses like the U.S. and China. Domestic companies often face intense competition not only internationally but also locally from those who favor the less risky approach of modifying existing frameworks rather than creating new paths. Finally, achieving true innovation and self‑reliance in AI requires a cultural shift from a service‑oriented to a[research](https://opentools.ai/categories/research) -driven tech industry, a transformation that demands time, resources, and unwavering commitment.

Collectively, these proposed solutions aim to enhance India's ability to participate competitively in the global AI landscape while fostering an environment of innovation and self‑reliance.
