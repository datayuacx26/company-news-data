# Can You Become Emotionally Dependent on an AI Therapist?

At first, talking to an AI therapist can feel harmless.


You have a difficult day, open a chatbot, and explain what happened. It responds immediately. There is no appointment to schedule, no concern about bothering a friend, and no awkward pause while trying to explain something personal.


You return the next night. Then again a few days later.


Eventually, you may notice that you're turning to the chatbot before talking to anyone else.


That is where an interesting question begins: **Can you become emotionally dependent on an AI therapist?**


The short answer is yes, it is possible. But emotional dependence isn't as simple as using an AI chatbot frequently or finding comfort in it. The more important question is whether the relationship with AI is becoming your primary way of regulating emotions, making decisions, or seeking reassurance.


Research into AI companionship and mental health is still developing, but recent studies suggest that this deserves serious attention.


## **Why AI Can Feel So Easy to Rely On**


Human relationships are complicated.


Friends get busy. Partners have their own problems. Therapists have appointments and professional boundaries. Even when people care about us, they may not always have the emotional energy to listen.


An[AI therapy](https://abby.gg/) doesn't have those limitations in the same way.


It can respond at almost any hour. It doesn't visibly judge you. You can explain the same problem several times without worrying that you're being repetitive. And increasingly sophisticated systems can remember conversational context and respond in language that feels personal and emotionally attentive.


For someone who is lonely, anxious, overwhelmed, or uncomfortable opening up to other people, that accessibility can be incredibly appealing.


The American Psychological Association's 2026 guidance notes that AI is already becoming part of people's mental health support systems. **Seventy‑seven percent of psychologists surveyed said their patients had reported using AI, while more than one‑third said patients were using AI as an additional mental health provider.** ([American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/practitioners-talk-ai-patients.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com) )


That doesn't mean most people are becoming dependent on AI.


It does show that AI has moved beyond being simply a productivity tool. People are increasingly bringing personal and emotional problems to it.


## **Comfort Isn't the Same as Emotional Dependence**


It's important not to pathologize ordinary use.


Talking with an AI after a stressful day isn't automatically unhealthy. Someone might use it to organize their thoughts, practice a difficult conversation, learn a breathing exercise, or reflect on an argument before deciding what to do next.


The problem begins when AI becomes difficult to do without.


Emotional dependence can involve relying excessively on an external source to regulate your emotional state. With an AI chatbot, that might mean feeling unable to calm down unless you talk to it, repeatedly seeking its reassurance, or gradually preferring it over real‑world relationships.


There is a significant difference between:


*"Talking to the chatbot helped me think through this."*


and:


*"I don't know what I'd do without the chatbot."*


The first can describe healthy support.


The second may be worth examining.


## **New Research Is Starting to Show the Difference**


Researchers are only beginning to understand how repeated interactions with AI affect people's emotional lives.


A 2026 study published in *BMC Psychology* specifically examined **AI chatbot dependence among young adults** and explored the relationship between loneliness, perceived social support, and dependence on AI chatbots. The study is significant because it treats dependence as something measurable rather than simply an anecdotal concern.


Another large randomized study involving **981 participants and more than 300,000 messages** examined how chatbot interaction affected loneliness, social interaction, emotional dependence, and problematic use.


The researchers found that higher daily usage was associated with **greater loneliness, greater emotional dependence, and more problematic use, alongside lower socialization with other people** . The results were more complicated than simply saying AI was harmful: different voice styles and conversation types produced different effects, and some interactions appeared less problematic than others.


That nuance is important.


The research does not establish that using an AI chatbot causes emotional dependence in every user.


Instead, it suggests that **how often people use AI, why they use it, what they discuss, and how strongly they trust or identify with the system may influence the outcome.**


## **Loneliness May Make Dependence More Likely**


AI can be particularly attractive when someone feels disconnected from other people.


A chatbot is available without requiring the vulnerability that comes with approaching another person. You don't have to worry about rejection, disappointing someone, or being perceived differently after revealing something personal.


That can make AI feel safer.


But there is a potential paradox.


The more satisfying an AI interaction becomes, the easier it may be to avoid the uncertainty of human relationships.


Recent research presented at the 2026 CHI Conference examined the psychosocial effects of AI companion chatbots using longitudinal Reddit data and user interviews. The researchers found mixed effects. AI companions could support emotional expression and interpersonal reflection, but they also observed increases in language associated with **loneliness, depression, and suicidal ideation** among some users. Interviews identified patterns involving initiation, escalation, and bonding, alongside concerns about over‑reliance and withdrawal from human relationships.


That doesn't mean an AI companion inevitably makes loneliness worse.


It suggests that **the same technology can provide comfort while potentially changing how someone relates to other people.**


## **The Reassurance Trap**


Dependence doesn't always look like affection.


Sometimes it looks like reassurance‑seeking.


Consider someone with anxiety who repeatedly asks an AI:


*"Do you think I offended my coworker?"*


The chatbot reassures them.


For a few minutes, they feel better.


Then the doubt returns:


*"But are you sure? What if they actually hate me?"*


They ask again.


The chatbot responds again.


This can create a cycle where anxiety produces a question, AI produces temporary relief, and the relief reinforces the behavior of asking again.


Psychiatrists and researchers have recently raised concerns about precisely this possibility, particularly among people vulnerable to anxiety and obsessive reassurance‑seeking. AI's constant availability and conversational style can make it easier to continue seeking reassurance without the natural stopping points that exist in many human interactions.


A human therapist may eventually say, in effect, *"We've answered this question several times. Let's look at what happens when you don't seek certainty."*


An AI designed primarily to be helpful may be more likely to answer the question again.


That difference can matter.


## **When AI Starts Replacing Human Connection**


One of the clearest warning signs is not simply how much you use AI, but **what happens to your relationships outside of it** .


Ask yourself:


- Do I increasingly prefer talking to AI instead of friends or family?
- Do I avoid difficult conversations with people because AI feels easier?
- Do I turn to AI every time I feel anxious or upset?
- Do I feel distressed when I can't access the chatbot?
- Do I repeatedly ask AI to reassure me about the same concern?
- Am I spending less time interacting with people because of these conversations?
- Do I treat the chatbot's responses as more trustworthy than the people who know me?
- Am I using AI to avoid situations I actually need to face?


None of these automatically means someone is emotionally dependent.


But several occurring together deserve attention.


The goal isn't to eliminate AI from your life. It's to notice whether the tool is **supporting your ability to function in the real world or gradually becoming a substitute for it.**


## **Why "It Understands Me" Can Feel So Powerful**


There is another reason AI dependence can be difficult to recognize.


Modern chatbots are very good at reflecting emotional language.


If you describe feeling rejected, the system may acknowledge that rejection hurts. If you explain that you're frustrated with your partner, it may help you articulate what you're feeling.


That can create a powerful experience of being understood.


Research on therapeutic AI has found that users can perceive chatbots as warm, supportive, and trustworthy. A 2025 study examining the mental‑health chatbot Clare® specifically explored the development of a working alliance between users and the AI system, showing that researchers are now studying these relational qualities directly.


But feeling understood by AI is not the same as having a human therapeutic relationship.


A licensed therapist brings clinical training, professional accountability, knowledge of your history, and the ability to observe changes over time. They can also recognize when reassurance is counterproductive or when a problem requires a different level of care.


AI can simulate some elements of that interaction.


It does not have the same professional responsibility.


## **Counseling Experts Would Look at the Bigger Picture**


From a counseling perspective, the important issue isn't whether someone has developed an attachment to a chatbot.


People can become attached to many sources of comfort.


The question is whether the attachment is helping or limiting the person's life.


A counselor might explore what the AI relationship is providing.


Is it companionship?


Safety?


Validation?


A sense of control?


Freedom from judgment?


Help with anxiety?


Understanding the need underneath the behavior can be more useful than simply telling someone to stop using AI.


For example, if someone spends hours talking with an AI because they feel socially isolated, reducing AI use without addressing the loneliness may leave the underlying problem untouched.


A therapist can help the person gradually develop other sources of support, including friendships, family relationships, community involvement, and professional care when necessary.


## **AI Can Be Supportive Without Becoming Your Main Support System**


There is nothing inherently wrong with using AI as a mental health support tool.


It can help you organize thoughts before a therapy appointment. It can provide journaling prompts, explain psychological concepts, help you practice communication, or suggest evidence‑based coping exercises.


The American Psychological Association currently recommends that people **not rely on general‑purpose generative AI chatbots or wellness apps as replacements for qualified mental health providers** . Its health advisory describes these technologies as potentially useful supportive adjuncts, while warning that many lack clinical validation, adequate safety protocols, or regulatory approval for mental health treatment. ([American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/health-advisory-chatbots-wellness-apps?utm_source=chatgpt.com) )


That distinction offers a sensible way forward.


Use AI to support reflection.


Don't let it become the only place where you process your emotions.


Use it to prepare for difficult conversations.


Don't use it to avoid having them.


Use it to learn coping strategies.


Don't let it replace professional treatment when you need clinical care.


## **The Healthiest Relationship With an AI Therapist May Include Boundaries**


The goal isn't necessarily to use AI less.


It is to use it intentionally.


You might set limits around how often you seek emotional reassurance from a chatbot. You might pause before asking AI for advice and first consider what you already know. You might use it as a prompt for connecting with a friend rather than as a replacement for that conversation.


Most importantly, pay attention to what happens afterward.


Do you feel more capable of handling life?


Or do you feel like you need to return to the chatbot again and again?


That distinction can tell you more than the number of hours you spend chatting.


## **AI Can Help, But It Shouldn't Become Your Emotional Home**


AI therapy is becoming more convincing, accessible, and personalized. For some people, that can make emotional support easier to access than it has ever been.


But the qualities that make AI comforting can also make it easy to over‑rely on.


The emerging research doesn't support a simple conclusion that AI companionship is either good or bad. Instead, it points toward a more complicated picture involving loneliness, frequency of use, trust, reassurance‑seeking, social connection, and individual vulnerability.


The healthiest role for AI may therefore be as **one support among many** , rather than the center of someone's emotional world.


If an AI conversation helps you understand yourself and then return to your life with more clarity, it may be serving a useful purpose.


If you increasingly feel that you need the chatbot to feel okay, make decisions, or cope with ordinary relationships, that's a different situation.


And that may be the most important question to ask:


**Is AI helping you build a life you can participate in without it, or slowly becoming the life you retreat into?**
