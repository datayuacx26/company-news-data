# Looking for a ChatGPT Alternative for Studying? We Put Gauth's Four Core Tools to the Test

ChatGPT is most students' default AI tool for a simple reason: it's already open, it's already familiar, and it can solve a homework problem well enough most of the time. The catch is that it wasn't built for studying specifically — it was built to answer anything, which means a physics problem, an email draft, and a recipe all get the same generic chat treatment. Gauth is one of the more prominent tools built to fill that specific gap: an **AI study platform** with four dedicated tools — Pro‑Level Solving, the AI Tutor, Study Converter, and AI Course — instead of one general‑purpose chat box. We tested all four to see whether that added structure actually earns a spot next to (or instead of) ChatGPT on a student's phone.


## **Pro‑Level Solving**


This is the entry point most users will actually touch first: point a camera at a problem, or type it in, and Gauth returns a worked solution built with visuals, graphs, and tables rather than a bare final answer in plain text. On raw accuracy, this is the feature least likely to feel like an upgrade over ChatGPT — both tools get a standard algebra or physics problem right most of the time. The difference shows up in *how* the reasoning gets shown: a graphing problem comes back with an actual rendered graph, not a description of one, and a data‑heavy question comes back as a table rather than a paragraph of numbers. On multiple‑choice questions, Gauth also checked the answer against the remaining options rather than stopping once the correct one was found — something ChatGPT's replies didn't consistently do unless specifically prompted to. Turning an abstract concept into something a student can actually see, rather than just read, is the part of the pitch this feature is built to deliver on, and in testing it largely did.


## **AI Tutor**


The AI Tutor is Gauth's clearest departure from a standard chat interface — and the feature that has no real equivalent in ChatGPT's default study mode. It's voice‑based and live, paired with a visual "blackboard" that fills in progressively as the explanation unfolds, rather than dropping a finished block of text all at once. In practice, that means a student can interrupt mid‑explanation — asking about one specific line without losing the thread of the whole walkthrough — which is a genuinely different interaction than typing a follow‑up question and waiting for a new reply. It's also the feature most likely to have an adjustment period: talking through a math problem out loud isn't everyone's instinct, and it works better in a quiet room than a shared study space. For students who do take to it, though, it's closer to sitting next to a tutor than to using an app.


## **Study Converter: The Student's Second Brain**


Real student material rarely shows up clean, and Study Converter is built around that mess rather than around a tidy textbook problem. Dump a lecture recording, a photo of a whiteboard, or a page of handwritten notes in one tap, and it returns flashcards, a structured overview, and a practice quiz in a single pass. The point isn't just speed — it's freeing up the mental bandwidth normally spent transcribing and organizing, so a student can study on the go instead of doing that setup work first. This is the feature that saves the most actual time in testing — not because any single output is dazzling, but because it replaces three or four separate steps with one. It's a less flashy feature than the AI Tutor, but it's arguably the one doing the most unglamorous, practical work.


## **AI Course**


[AI Course](https://www.gauth.com/course) is the newest addition to the platform, and it's a different kind of feature from the other three: instead of solving a problem or organizing existing notes, it turns dense reading material — a chapter, a set of assigned notes — into AI‑generated video lessons with contextual explanations, and a real‑time Q&A layer connected directly to the AI Tutor for follow‑up questions mid‑lesson.


The logic behind it is sound: a lot of learning failure happens at the point a student disengages from a wall of text, not at the point the material actually gets hard. In testing, it delivered on that idea for shorter, more structured material, turning what would've been a flat reading assignment into something closer to a short video lecture. It was less consistent on longer or more conceptually dense chapters, which tracks — this is the least mature feature in the lineup, and it shows in places. ## **Pros and Cons**


**Pros:**


- All four tools live in one app, covering the full study cycle — preview, practice, review, and Q&A — instead of requiring three or four separate apps
- Solutions are consistently formatted with the reasoning shown, not just the final answer
- Study Converter meaningfully cuts down the manual work of turning raw lecture material into usable study content
- The AI Tutor's voice‑and‑blackboard format offers a genuinely different learning mode than a standard chat interface


**Cons:**


- AI Course is still the least consistent feature, particularly on longer or more conceptually layered material — better suited right now to shorter, well‑structured content
- The voice‑based AI Tutor has a real onboarding curve and isn't a fit for every environment or every learning style
- Because the platform routes questions across multiple models, answer formatting can vary slightly more than it would with a single consistent model


None of these are dealbreakers so much as fit issues. For a single, fast answer to check your own work, ChatGPT still does the job and is already installed. Where Gauth earns its place as an alternative — or a companion — is the recurring workload: a full course load, week after week, where consistent formatting, organized notes, and a tutor that doesn't need re‑explaining actually compound over a semester in a way a general chat tool isn't built to support.

## **Conclusion**


Gauth isn't trying to out‑answer ChatGPT on a single question, and on that narrow test, it mostly doesn't. Its case is built on everything around the answer: explanations you can actually see instead of just read, a tutor built for a genuine back‑and‑forth instead of a static reply, and a way to turn a semester's worth of messy material into something usable without four separate apps doing it piecemeal. For a student who only needs an occasional answer checked, that's more than necessary. For a student trying to get through an entire course, it's a meaningfully different tool than the one already open on their phone.
