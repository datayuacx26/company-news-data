# Summer AC Prep: The Complete Guide for HVAC Technicians

Every year around late March, the phones start ringing. Homeowners who haven't thought about their air conditioning since September suddenly remember it exists, and they want it running perfectly by Memorial Day. For HVAC technicians, the weeks between spring thaw and the first heat wave are the most profitable of the year — if you're prepared. This guide walks through a complete pre-season AC inspection workflow, highlights the failure modes that generate the most callbacks, and shows how structured documentation turns a routine maintenance visit into a revenue-building touchpoint.


## The Pre-Season Inspection Checklist


A thorough pre-season inspection catches problems while they're still cheap to fix. Skipping steps doesn't save time — it creates callbacks. Here's the sequence we recommend, ordered so each step informs the next:


1. Thermostat verification — Confirm mode switching (heat/cool/auto), calibration within 1°F, and Wi-Fi connectivity if applicable. Replace batteries in battery-powered models.
2. Electrical inspection — Check disconnect box, breaker amperage, contactor pitting, and wire insulation. Measure voltage at the condenser and compare to nameplate specs.
3. Capacitor testing — Use a multimeter to check microfarad ratings against the label. Replace any capacitor reading more than 5% below its rated value, even if the system starts fine today.
4. Condenser coil cleaning — Hose down the outdoor coil from inside out. Check fin straightness and clear debris from the unit perimeter (minimum 24″ clearance).
5. Refrigerant charge verification — Measure superheat (TXV systems) or subcooling (fixed-orifice systems) at manufacturer-specified conditions. Do not top off without finding the leak first.
6. Evaporator coil and drain inspection — Check for ice formation, clean the drain pan, and flush the condensate line with a wet/dry vac or nitrogen.
7. Blower motor and belt check — Verify amp draw, lubricate bearings if applicable, and inspect belt tension and wear on belt-drive models.
8. Airflow measurement — Check static pressure across the air handler. Anything above 0.5″ W.C. warrants a duct investigation or filter upsizing conversation.
9. Filter replacement and customer education — Install a fresh filter, mark the date on the frame, and explain the replacement interval to the homeowner.
10. System test cycle — Run the system through a full cooling cycle. Verify delta-T across the coil (typically 15–22°F) and listen for abnormal compressor sounds.


💡


Check Refrigerant Before Chasing Electrical Gremlins


Low refrigerant charge is one of the most common root causes of compressor short-cycling, frozen evaporator coils, and high head pressure — symptoms that often look electrical. Always verify charge levels early in your diagnostic process. It takes five minutes and can save you an hour of troubleshooting.


## The Summer Failures That Generate the Most Callbacks


Not all failures are created equal. Some kill the system immediately and the customer calls right away. Others are slow-burn problems that don't surface until the unit has been running hard for a few weeks — and those are the ones that show up as callbacks. Understanding the failure timeline helps you catch problems during inspection rather than on a 98°F Saturday.


### Capacitor Degradation


Run capacitors degrade gradually with heat exposure. A capacitor that tests at 38 µF on a 75°F spring day might test at 33 µF when the ambient temperature hits 100°F, and that's enough to prevent the compressor from starting. The system works fine during your maintenance visit, the customer assumes everything is good, and then it fails on the hottest day of the year. The fix is simple: replace any capacitor that reads more than 5% below its rated value during pre-season, regardless of whether the system starts.


📊


Capacitor Failure Is the #1 Callback Driver


According to field data from over 12,000 residential service calls, 42% of summer callbacks trace back to capacitor failure that was missed or borderline during the initial maintenance visit. Proactive replacement during pre-season inspection eliminates the most common source of callbacks.


### Condenser Coil Fouling


Cottonwood seeds, grass clippings, and pollen create a blanket on the condenser coil that restricts airflow and drives up head pressure. Even a coil that looks clean from the outside can have a layer of compacted debris between the fin rows. During pre-season service, remove the top grille and inspect the coil from the inside. A standard garden hose with a fan nozzle works for light fouling; heavily caked coils need a coil cleaner solution and 15 minutes of soak time before rinsing. Document the coil condition with a photo before and after cleaning — customers appreciate seeing the difference, and it builds trust for future recommendations.


## Customer Communication That Prevents Disputes


The gap between "the tech did a great job" and "the tech didn't do anything" is almost always a communication problem, not a workmanship problem. Homeowners can't see clean refrigerant lines or properly torqued electrical connections. What they can see is a written summary of what you found, what you fixed, and what they should watch for. A brief walkthrough at the end of the visit — three minutes, tops — transforms a routine maintenance call into a relationship-building moment.


The key is specificity. Instead of "system is running fine," try: "Your system is cooling at a 19-degree delta-T, which is right in the ideal range. The capacitor tested at 44 out of 45 microfarads, so it's still strong. I cleaned the condenser coil and flushed the drain line. The one thing I'd keep an eye on is your filter — with two dogs in the house, you'll want to swap it every 60 days instead of 90." That level of detail takes seconds to deliver but makes the customer feel like they got real value from the visit.
