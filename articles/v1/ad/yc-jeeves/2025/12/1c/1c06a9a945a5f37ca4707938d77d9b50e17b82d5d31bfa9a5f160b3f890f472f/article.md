# Tariffs: What they are and how to reduce their impact

The dynamics of international trade are shaped by various factors, such as political and geopolitical relations, trade partnerships, the abundance and availability of natural resources and goods, and economic and regulatory policies. In this complex and interconnected landscape, tariffs emerge as a strategic tool used by governments, primarily to promote national interests.

## **What is a tariff? / Tariffs Definition**


Tariffs are taxes or duties imposed by a government on imported or exported goods. They serve as a form of trade regulation used to control the flow of goods across borders, protect domestic industries, generate government revenue, or influence trade relations with other countries.


## **Types of tariffs**


There are two main types of tariffs:


- **Specific tariff** – A fixed fee applied per unit of a product, regardless of its value. For example, a $100 tariff on each imported cellphone.
- **Ad valorem tariff** – A tariff calculated as a percentage of the item's value. For instance, a 10% tariff on the total value of an imported good.


## **Main objectives of tariffs**


Tariffs play a strategic role in international trade and are used by governments for various purposes. The key objectives include:


**Protection of domestic industry** – By imposing tariffs on imported products, governments make these goods more expensive, encouraging the consumption of locally produced items and shielding domestic industries from foreign competition.


**Revenue generation** – Tariffs serve as a source of government revenue, helping to finance public policies and infrastructure investments.


**Regulation of foreign trade** – Controlling tariffs can be a tool to balance trade deficits, encourage or discourage imports and exports, and even serve as leverage in international trade agreements.


**National security and strategic interests** – Some countries impose tariffs to reduce dependence on foreign goods that are deemed essential for national security or economic stability.


**Environmental and social restrictions** – In some cases, tariffs are applied to discourage trade in products that do not comply with environmental regulations or international labor standards, promoting more sustainable and ethical practices.


## **Who authorizes tariffs?**


Tariffs are typically authorized by governments, but their implementation is guided by international trade regulations.


### **World Trade Organization (WTO) and global guidelines**


The **World Trade Organization (WTO)** oversees global trade rules and sets guidelines for tariff applications among its member countries. WTO agreements aim to prevent excessive tariffs and promote fair trade by encouraging transparency and gradual tariff reductions through negotiations. However, countries can impose tariffs under specific conditions, such as protecting national industries or addressing unfair trade practices.


### **Tariff Authorization in the U.S.**


In the **United States** , tariffs are primarily authorized by **Congress** , which has the constitutional power to regulate trade. However, the President can impose tariffs under certain circumstances, such as national security concerns (under the Trade Expansion Act), unfair trade practices, or to protect domestic industries from import surges (both under the Trade Act of 1974). The U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) also plays a key role in enforcing trade policies and negotiating tariff agreements.


While international guidelines shape tariff policies, each country has its mechanisms to implement and regulate them based on its economic and strategic interests.


## **Who pays for the tariffs?**


Tariffs impact the entire supply chain, influencing pricing, trade decisions, and consumer purchasing power. Although governments impose tariffs on imported goods, the cost is typically passed down to importers, businesses, and consumers. Importers pay the tariff upfront, increasing their expenses, which businesses may absorb or pass on through higher prices.


Consumers often face increased costs for imported goods or products reliant on foreign materials. Exporters can also be indirectly affected if tariffs reduce demand for their products in foreign markets.


## **What are the main impacts of tariffs?**


Many experts argue that the imposition of tariffs causes more harm than good, as individuals and businesses face higher costs when purchasing goods and services, reducing their purchasing power. Additionally, tariffs can create tensions between markets and affect the relationship, leading to trade retaliations and the risk of shortages of essential goods and services in the affected countries.


On the other hand, some experts highlight the positive impacts of tariffs, arguing that they stimulate the local supply chain's development, reduce dependence on international markets, and, in the long run, may contribute to lower prices for goods and services.


## **How to reduce the impact of tariffs**


Businesses can minimize the impact of tariffs through several strategic approaches:


**Optimize supply chains** – Diversifying suppliers and sourcing materials from countries with lower or no tariffs can help reduce costs.


**Leverage trade agreements** – Taking advantage of free trade agreements (FTAs) or special tariff exemptions can lower duty costs.


**Use foreign trade zones (FTZs)** – Storing goods in designated zones can defer, reduce, or even eliminate tariff payments until the products enter the domestic market.


**Reclassify goods** – Ensuring products are correctly classified under the Harmonized System (HS) can help secure lower tariff rates where applicable.


**Negotiate with suppliers** – Working with suppliers to share tariff costs or adjust pricing can help offset the financial burden.


**Pass costs strategically** – Businesses can manage tariff-related costs by adjusting pricing, optimizing operations, or absorbing costs selectively to maintain competitiveness.


**Advocate for policy changes** – Engaging with industry groups and policymakers can influence trade policies and tariff structures.
