# What a Commissary Kitchen Actually Costs — and What to Ask Before You Sign

Every commissary listing leads with an hourly rate, and that rate is the least useful number in the deal.


I've had this conversation with a lot of truck owners and the pattern is consistent. They compare two kitchens on dollars per hour, pick the cheaper one, and find out three months later that the cheap one costs more. Not because the rate was a lie — because the rate was maybe 60% of what they actually pay, and because the access terms quietly decided how many events they could work.


Here's the version I wish somebody had laid out for them.


## What is a commissary kitchen, and why do you need one?


A commissary is a licensed commercial kitchen you rent access to instead of building. For most mobile operators it isn't optional — many health departments won't permit a truck or trailer without a commissary agreement on file, because your truck isn't a legal place to do prep, store product, or dump greywater.


So you're usually buying three separate things:


1.


**Prep and cook time** in a licensed space.


2.


**Storage** — dry, cooler, freezer.


3.


**A piece of paper** your health inspector needs.


Sometimes a fourth: **a commissary address or parking** your permit requires.


Those unbundle differently at every single facility. Which is exactly why hourly rates don't compare.


## What does a commissary kitchen actually cost?


I'm not going to publish a national average, because it would be useless. Commissary pricing swings enormously by metro, and a number from one city will actively mislead you in another.


What I can give you is the full list of line items, so you build a real total instead of comparing hourly rates. Price all of these at every facility:


-


**Base rate** — Hourly, monthly block, or unlimited? Does it change nights and weekends?


-


**Minimum commitment** — Minimum hours per month? Do unused hours roll over or vanish?


-


**Dry storage** — Included, or per shelf / per rack per month?


-


**Cooler & freezer** — Almost always separate. Per shelf, per rack, or per cubic foot?


-


**Deposit** — How much, and what conditions return it?


-


**Insurance** — What limits must you carry, and do they need to be named as additionally insured?


-


**Greywater & grease** — Included, metered, or prohibited on site?


-


**Truck parking** — Included, extra, or unavailable? Overnight allowed?


-


**Access hours** — 24/7, or a window? Is there a hard out?


-


**Equipment** — What's shared, what's reservable, what's off-limits to renters?


-


**Cancellation** — Notice required to leave — 30 days, 60, or a full term?


Then do this: take the hours you'd genuinely book in a normal month, **plus one festival week** , and make each facility total that schedule. Compare those two numbers. That's the actual comparison, and it routinely reorders the ranking you started with.


## The question that really decides it: can you get in when you need to?


I'd put this above price.


A truck's prep demand is spiky. A normal week is one thing. The week you're working a Saturday festival plus two catering drops is a completely different load — and it all lands on the same two days. A kitchen with a great rate and a 6 AM to 2 PM access window can be unusable during exactly the weeks that make you money.


Ask specifically:


-


Can I book a **double shift on a Friday** , or is there a per-user cap?


-


How far ahead do bookings open, and **who gets priority?** Is there a tier of tenant that books before I do?


-


What happens when **two of us want the same station** the Friday before a holiday?


-


Is there a **hard out** — and is cleanup inside or outside my booked hours?


That last one is worth real money. If cleanup counts against your booked time, your effective rate is higher than the sticker by however long your breakdown takes. Ask, then add it to the table above.


## Red flags


Things that have made operators I've talked to regret a signature:


-


**No written access policy.** "It's usually fine" is not a policy, and it stops being usually fine the month the facility fills up.


-


**A term with no exit.** A 12-month commitment to a facility you've used zero weeks is a bet on a workflow you haven't tested yet.


-


**Shared storage with no lock.** Your walk-in shelf is your inventory. Four operators sharing an unsecured cooler means you will lose product, and there will be nobody to bill for it.


-


**Vague grease and greywater terms.** These get expensive, and they're a common source of surprise line items.


-


**A facility that can't produce the health department paperwork** in the form your inspector accepts. Ask exactly what they provide, then confirm with your inspector that it's sufficient — *before* you pay a deposit.


## How do I find a commissary kitchen near me?


Roughly in order of how well it works:


1.


**Ask other operators directly.** The best kitchens are often full and unlisted, and the person parked next to you at an event will tell you the truth about access and management in ninety seconds. Highest-yield item on this list, and it costs a conversation.


2.


**Your health department.** Many keep a list of permitted commissaries — and by definition, everything on it already satisfies the person who inspects you.


3.


**Shared-kitchen directories and networks.** Good for discovering facilities. Still verify every access term yourself.


4.


**Restaurants with unused hours.** A dinner-only restaurant has an empty kitchen every morning, and some will sublet. Check with your health department first, because whether that arrangement is permissible varies by jurisdiction.


5.


**Churches, event halls, VFW posts.** Licensed kitchens that sit idle most of the week. Often the cheapest option that exists where it's allowed — the paperwork is the hard part, not the negotiation.


One rule regardless of route: **walk the facility during a busy block, not a quiet tour slot.** You're evaluating congestion, not countertops. A kitchen that's serene at 10 AM on a Tuesday tells you nothing about 4 PM on a Friday.


## Then run the number that actually matters


Once you're in, the commissary stops being a rent line and becomes a cost-per-batch question: what does it cost you in kitchen time to produce the thing you sell, and which menu items justify the block?


That's where most operators stop — because the data lives in two places. Hours are in a booking system or a notebook; sales are in your POS. Doing it properly means exporting sales, estimating prep time from memory, and building a spreadsheet you'll never update a second time.
