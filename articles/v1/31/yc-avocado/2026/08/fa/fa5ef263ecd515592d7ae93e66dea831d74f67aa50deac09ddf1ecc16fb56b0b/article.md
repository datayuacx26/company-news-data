# 2026 Lettuce and Jalapeño Recalls: What Restaurant Owners Should Do Now

**Last updated August 12, 2026.**


Restaurant owners are dealing with two major produce recalls at the same time: an iceberg lettuce recall connected to a multistate Cyclospora outbreak and a fresh jalapeño recall connected to a Salmonella outbreak. Both ingredients appear across restaurant menus, often in foods prepared in batches, including salads, tacos, burgers, salsa, pico de gallo, and guacamole.


The right response is not to remove every lettuce leaf or pepper indefinitely. It is to identify affected products quickly, stop service, document what your restaurant received, clean the areas those products touched, and make temporary menu changes using ingredients from verified sources.


This guide explains what restaurant owners should do now and how to keep serving customers safely while the investigations continue.


## What restaurant owners need to know about the 2026 recalls


### Iceberg lettuce recall connected to Cyclospora


The FDA and CDC are investigating a Cyclospora outbreak linked to iceberg lettuce sourced from central Mexico and recalled by Taylor Farms de Mexico. According to the FDA's August 5 update, the outbreak included 6,358 reported illnesses in 15 states, 278 hospitalizations, and two deaths. Recalled lettuce was distributed through foodservice channels in numerous states.


Cyclospora is a microscopic parasite that can cause prolonged or recurring watery diarrhea, stomach cramps, nausea, fatigue, loss of appetite, and weight loss. Symptoms commonly begin about one week after exposure, although the timing can vary.


Restaurants should not assume that extra washing will make affected lettuce safe. The FDA says washing may not reliably eliminate Cyclospora, and the parasite is resistant to standard chlorine-based sanitizers.


[Read the FDA iceberg lettuce and Cyclospora outbreak advisory.](https://www.fda.gov/food/outbreaks-foodborne-illness/investigation-15-state-outbreak-cyclospora-illnesses-iceberg-lettuce-july-2026)


### Fresh jalapeño recall connected to Salmonella


The FDA is also investigating a Salmonella Javiana outbreak linked to fresh jalapeños from Sinaloa, Mexico, distributed by Coast Citrus Distributors. As of the FDA's August 10 update, the outbreak included 345 illnesses across 27 states and 36 hospitalizations, with no reported deaths.


The recall covers affected fresh jalapeños supplied to wholesalers, restaurants, distributors, and foodservice companies. It has also led to downstream recalls of prepared products containing those peppers, including salsa, pico de gallo, guacamole, taco dip, burritos, sandwiches, and meat or poultry products.


The FDA advises restaurants to discard affected jalapeños. If you cannot determine who supplied the peppers, work with your distributor immediately. FDA specifically advises discarding them if they came from the implicated source or if their source cannot be confirmed.


[Read the FDA jalapeño and Salmonella outbreak advisory.](https://www.fda.gov/food/outbreaks-foodborne-illness/outbreak-investigation-salmonella-jalapeno-august-2026)


## What to do in the first hour after receiving a recall notice


1.


**Stop serving the ingredient.** Disable affected dishes and modifiers in your POS, online ordering system, kiosks, and delivery apps.


2.


**Quarantine the product.** Move affected cases, containers, and prepared batches away from usable inventory. Clearly label them “Do Not Use.”


3.


**Check every location.** A distributor notice sent to headquarters does not guarantee every store has removed the product.


4.


**Match the recall to your records.** Review supplier names, invoices, receiving dates, case labels, lot codes, brands, and date markings.


5.


**Identify every recipe involved.** Look beyond obvious menu names. Jalapeños may be in salsa, marinades, dressings, dips, and garnishes. Iceberg lettuce may be in sandwiches, tacos, wraps, salads, bowls, and catering trays.


6.


**Hold prepared batches.** A salsa made with affected jalapeños remains affected even if the visible pepper pieces are removed. The same principle applies to salads and sandwich components that contacted recalled lettuce.


7.


**Document the response.** Record the quantities removed, affected locations, disposal or return method, cleaning completed, and the manager responsible.


## How to change your restaurant menu during the lettuce and jalapeño recalls


A temporary menu should be smaller, easy for employees to explain, and built around ingredients whose sources you can verify.


### Replace or remove iceberg lettuce


-


Serve burgers, sandwiches, and tacos without lettuce when it is not essential to the dish.


-


Replace raw-lettuce salads with grain bowls or roasted-vegetable salads.


-


Convert lettuce wraps into tortilla wraps, pita sandwiches, or bowls.


-


Use cooked peppers, onions, mushrooms, or other verified ingredients to add volume and texture.


-


Use an unaffected lettuce only after your supplier confirms the grower, processor, distributor, and relevant lot information.


### Replace or remove fresh jalapeños


-


Offer pepper-free pico de gallo or guacamole.


-


Use verified peppers from a different grower and distribution chain.


-


Substitute cooked poblano or bell peppers from a confirmed source when the flavor profile allows.


-


Use commercially processed hot sauce, dried chile, or chile powder for heat.


-


Temporarily remove raw jalapeño garnishes, jalapeño cocktails, fresh salsa, and batch-made sauces when the ingredient source cannot be verified.


### Use a red, yellow, and green menu review


-


**Red:** Items containing recalled lettuce or jalapeños. Stop selling them and discard or return the affected ingredients as directed.


-


**Yellow:** Prepared foods that may contain the ingredients, shared containers, or items made on exposed equipment. Hold them until a manager verifies the recipe and handling history.


-


**Green:** Dishes made with documented unaffected ingredients on cleaned equipment. These can remain available.


## Do not try to rescue recalled food


Restaurants should not wash, trim, freeze, cook, or pickle a recalled product in an attempt to make it usable. Recall instructions call for the affected product to be discarded or returned. Cooking can reduce risks in normal food preparation, but it is not a reason to keep a recalled ingredient.


House-made quick pickling should not be treated as a validated pathogen-kill step. If you need a temporary replacement, use a commercially processed product or a verified unaffected ingredient.


## Clean and sanitize every area the products touched


After removing recalled ingredients, clean and sanitize refrigerators, produce bins, hotel pans, cutting boards, knives, food processors, prep tables, storage containers, display cases, and utensils that may have contacted them. Discard ready-to-eat foods that may have been cross-contaminated.


Cleaning and sanitizing still matter even though routine chemical sanitizers may not reliably kill Cyclospora on produce. Their purpose in the restaurant response is to remove residue and reduce cross-contamination after the recalled food has been discarded.


## Questions to ask your produce supplier today


-


Did our restaurant receive any product covered by either recall?


-


What grower, processor, and distributor supplied our iceberg lettuce and jalapeños?


-


Which delivery dates, lot codes, case labels, and locations are affected?


-


Do any prepared products we buy contain recalled jalapeños?


-


Can you provide written confirmation that the replacement product is from an unaffected source?


-


How should recalled product be returned or destroyed?


-


Who will notify us if the recall expands?


Save the supplier's written response with invoices, case-label photographs, disposal records, and your internal recall log.


## Update every place customers can order


Changing a kitchen recipe is not enough. Update menu boards, printed menus, your POS, online ordering, QR-code menus, kiosks, catering menus, and third-party delivery platforms. Remove unavailable modifiers as well as full dishes.


Tell employees what changed before the next service. Give front-of-house staff a short explanation and make sure substitutions do not introduce undisclosed allergens.


## How to communicate with customers


Keep public communication factual and specific. Do not speculate about the source or promise that no exposure occurred unless health officials have confirmed it.


> Due to a supplier recall, we temporarily removed certain iceberg lettuce and jalapeño products from our menu. We discarded the affected ingredients, cleaned and sanitized the areas where they were stored and prepared, and are using replacement ingredients only after verifying their source. Guests with health concerns should contact a healthcare provider and consult the current FDA advisories.


## What to do if an employee or customer reports symptoms


Employees experiencing vomiting or diarrhea should not handle food. Follow your local health code and employee illness policy for exclusion and return-to-work requirements.


Customers or employees who develop symptoms should contact a healthcare provider. Cyclospora commonly causes prolonged watery diarrhea and may require a specific stool test. Salmonella commonly causes diarrhea, fever, and abdominal cramps. Restaurant managers should cooperate promptly with state or local health departments and preserve purchase and preparation records that may assist an investigation.


## Restaurant recall FAQ


### Are all iceberg lettuce and jalapeños recalled?


No. These recalls apply to particular products and supply chains. However, a restaurant should stop using an ingredient when its source cannot be established and the FDA advisory instructs operators to discard unknown-source product.


### Can recalled lettuce or jalapeños be washed and served?


No. Recalled food should be discarded or returned according to the recall instructions. Washing does not reliably remove Cyclospora from produce, and washing is not a substitute for following a Salmonella recall.


### What records should a restaurant keep?


Keep invoices, receiving logs, supplier communications, case labels, lot codes, delivery dates, photographs, menu recipes, disposal records, and a list of locations that received the product.


### When can a restaurant put the ingredient back on the menu?


Resume service when your supplier can document that the replacement product is not covered by the recall and your restaurant has completed its cleaning, inventory, menu, and staff-communication steps.


## Build a recall plan before the next alert


Every restaurant should be able to trace a high-risk ingredient from invoice to menu item within minutes. Assign one recall owner per location, retain case-label information, subscribe to FDA and local health alerts, and run a mock recall at least once a year.


Your POS and online ordering tools should also make it easy to disable an ingredient, modifier, or menu item across every ordering channel. Fast menu control protects guests and prevents employees from accidentally selling an item after it has been removed in the kitchen.
