# Flood Zone A99 Explained: Flood Risk, Insurance Requirements, and FEMA Rules

Flood Zone A99 doesn’t get the attention that Zone AE or Zone X does. But for homeowners whose properties fall under this designation, it is crucial from an insurance and regulatory point of view. Zone A99 is a Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) classification reserved for geographic areas within a Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) that are slated for protection by a flood-control project. This article explores what it requires now, how it differs from other high-risk zones, and what changes once a project finishes.


## What Is Flood Zone A99?


Flood Zone A99 is a FEMA designation applied to areas within the SFHA, where a flood-control project, such as a levee, dam, or flood barrier system, is under construction or nearly complete. These areas currently carry 1% annual flood risk. Unlike Zone AE, no Base Flood Elevation is published for A99 zones.


## Why did FEMA Create the A99 Designation?


Flood-control projects, like levees, dams, floodwalls, and detention basins, take years or decades to build. During that construction window, the land they’re designed to protect is still exposed to flood damage. FEMA needed a way to recognize that consequential protection was on the way without prematurely reclassifying a community out of the[Special Flood Hazard Area](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/special-flood-hazard-area/) .


The A99 designation signals to insurers, lenders, and local governments that a community is on a defined path toward reduced flood risk under a federally sanctioned project. But it hasn’t reached the finish line yet. Until a project is complete and independently certified, it cannot be treated as protective infrastructure.


## Is Flood Zone A99 a High-Risk Flood Zone?


Yes. A99 lies within FEMA’s Special Flood Hazard Area, in the same high-risk category as Zone AE and Zone A. The anticipated future protection from a flood-control project does not change the current risk classification.


The residual risk in A99 areas must be seen as credible risk. Flood-control projects can face delays. They can underperform during storm events that exceed their design parameters. And levees often fail. Post-Katrina breaches, the 2008 Iowa floods, and multiple smaller failures since have all demonstrated that infrastructure under construction or recently completed does not hold up the same as infrastructure proven against a major event.


There’s also the question of scope. Levees and flood barriers are engineered for a specific design event. A storm that routes water around a partial structure, or exceeds the system’s rated capacity, can flood areas that residents had reason to believe were protected.


The long-term risk profile in A99 may improve once the flood-control project is complete and certified. Before the infrastructure starts functioning, the area is classified as a[high-risk flood zone](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/high-risk-flood-zones/) .


## Does Flood Zone A99 Require Flood Insurance?


Yes. If you hold a federally backed mortgage on a property in a FEMA-designated A99 zone,[flood insurance is mandatory](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/is-flood-insurance-mandatory/) , because A99 is a Special Flood Hazard Area. The same mandatory purchase requirements that apply to Zone AE and Zone A apply here.


“Federally backed” covers most residential mortgages: FHA loans, VA loans, USDA loans, and conventional loans purchased by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac. For the majority of homeowners, that’s the mortgage already on their property.


If you own your property outright, without a mortgage, flood insurance is not legally required, even inside an SFHA. But the financial exposure due to possible flood damage still remains. A single major flood event in a high-risk zone can generate repair and replacement costs well into the tens of thousands of dollars.


The mandatory purchase requirement is enforceable until FEMA formally revises the zone designation. Some homeowners in A99 zones secure voluntary coverage precisely because of this gap between a project’s progress and its formal accreditation. Insurance requirements may vary based on loan type, lender, and property circumstances.

## FEMA Flood Zone A99 Rules and Building Requirements


A99 zones fall under FEMA’s[floodplain](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/what-are-floodplains/) management regulations, which means local governments must enforce minimum building standards for new construction and substantial improvements. Unlike Zone AE, A99 carries no published Base Flood Elevation. So building requirements typically reference data from the associated flood-control project or the available local flood study, whichever applies.


What those standards look like depends on the jurisdiction. Some communities adopt requirements stricter than FEMA’s minimum, while others apply only the baseline. Before any construction or major renovation in an A99 zone, verify the current FEMA map status and consult your local floodplain administrator. Requirements vary between counties, and non-compliance can affect insurance eligibility, permit approvals, and property resale value.


## Flood Zone A99 vs AE vs X: Key Differences


Three[flood zones](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/what-are-flood-zones/) are commonly considered to understand the risk and insurance obligations: A99, AE, and X. These differences affect what insurance you need, what you can build, and what your property costs to insure.


**Zone A99** **Zone AE** **Zone X**


**Inside SFHA** Yes Yes No


**Base Flood Elevation** Not published Published Not applicable


**Flood-control project** Under construction None None


**Mandatory insurance (federally backed loan)** Yes Yes No (typically)


**Current risk level** High High Moderate to low


**Building requirements** Yes – local standards Yes – tied to BFE Minimal or none


The gap between A99 and AE is a factor of BFE.[Flood zone AE](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/zone-ae-flood-insurance/) has a published elevation figure that drives everything from floor height to how an insurer calculates your premium. A99 doesn’t have one. The flood-control project hasn’t yet produced certified protection, so the detailed elevation study hasn’t been completed or recognized.


[Flood Zone X](https://neptuneflood.com/blog/flood-zone-x/) is outside the SFHA entirely. There is no mandatory purchase requirement. It generally comes with lower premiums if you choose to purchase private coverage and has fewer building restrictions.

Flood Zone A99 is categorized as a Special Flood Hazard Area. The flood-control project meant to protect it isn’t finished yet. Until FEMA formally revises the designation, mandatory insurance requirements, building standards, and high-risk classifications remain in force.

## FAQs


### Does flood zone A99 require flood insurance?


Yes. A99 is within FEMA’s Special Flood Hazard Area, which mandates flood insurance for federally backed mortgages. Voluntary coverage is advisable even without a mortgage. The zone’s current high-risk classification doesn’t change because a flood-control project is underway.


### What are the building requirements in an A99 flood zone?


A99 requires compliance with local floodplain management standards for new construction and substantial improvements. No Base Flood Elevation is published, so requirements typically reference the associated flood-control project data. These standards vary by jurisdiction. Consult your local floodplain administrator before building.


### How can I estimate flood insurance premiums for an A99 flood zone?


Premiums in A99 are property-specific. The structure type, elevation, foundation type, and coverage amount all factor in.


### Does flood zone A99 have a Base Flood Elevation (BFE)?


No. A99 zones do not carry a published base flood elevation. BFE data is typically generated through detailed flood studies, which are completed after the associated flood-control project is finished and formally accredited by FEMA.


### Can flood zone A99 be removed from FEMA flood maps?


Yes – but only after the flood-control project is complete and the area receives formal accreditation from FEMA. At that point, FEMA may remap the zone, potentially redesignating it outside the SFHA and removing mandatory insurance requirements.


### What happens when a flood-control project is completed?


Once complete and accredited, FEMA reviews the protected area for potential remapping. If it meets the required protection standard, the area may be redesignated outside the SFHA. This would remove the mandatory flood insurance requirements and reduce applicable building restrictions.
