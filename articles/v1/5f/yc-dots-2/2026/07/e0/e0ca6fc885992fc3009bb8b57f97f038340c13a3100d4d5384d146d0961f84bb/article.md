## How Signature Gatherers Are Compensated


When you pay signature gatherers, your compensation model sets collection speed and administrative workload. Campaigns balance daily motivation against budget predictability.


### Hourly Pay


Canvassers earn a fixed wage for their time. Jurisdictions that restrict volume-based pay require this setup. It keeps costs predictable but removes financial incentives for rapid collection.


### Pay-Per-Signature


Payouts scale directly with verified output: gatherers earn more by collecting more valid signatures. This model appeals to campaigns under tight deadlines, since motivated collectors work faster when compensation is tied to results. Where state law permits it, per-signature rates typically range from $2 to $12 per signature ([Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/Ballot_measure_signature_costs,_2024) ) depending on campaign urgency and regional labor costs. If your target state prohibits this structure, hourly pay is the only compliant alternative.

### Support for Performance Pay


- Advocates argue that piece-rate compensation directly incentivizes productivity and keeps daily volume high.
- Tying expenses exactly to verified results allows underfunded initiatives to compete against larger budgets.


### Criticism of Piece-Rate Models


- Critics argue that per-signature pay encourages gatherers to favor volume over validity: collectors who earn more by gathering faster have a direct financial incentive to skip verification steps, resulting in higher rates of invalid or fraudulent signatures that campaigns must later discard.
- State legislators in jurisdictions like Colorado, Montana, and Oregon have concluded that piece-rate structures corrupt the integrity of the petition process itself, treating a civic mechanism as a sales commission model.
- Gatherers under piece-rate arrangements often work multiple campaigns simultaneously and may misrepresent petition content to obtain signatures quickly, a risk that falls on the sponsoring campaign when signatures are challenged.
- From a budget standpoint, per-signature pay can create unpredictable cost spikes: if a campaign sets rates too high to attract collectors quickly, total spend can overshoot projections by a wide margin before finance teams can intervene.

## Employee vs. Independent Contractor Classification


[Worker classification](https://usedots.com/blog/what-to-know-about-gig-worker-classification/) determines your tax withholding and payout structure. Before you pay signature gatherers, you must designate them as independent contractors or standard staff.


The federal government applies a three-factor test to verify this status. According to[IRS worker classification guidelines](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee) , the test covers behavioral control (does your organization direct how, when, and where gatherers work?), financial control (do workers set their own rates and work for multiple campaigns?), and the type of relationship (is there a written contract, and do you provide benefits?). If gatherers set their own hours, supply their own materials, and work across multiple petition drives, independent contractor status is typically defensible. If your campaign controls schedules, assigns territories, and directs day-to-day methods, employee classification is the safer and legally required call: misclassifying employees as contractors exposes your organization to back taxes, penalties, and unpaid withholding liability under IRS rules.


## Tax Compliance When Paying Gatherers at Scale


When you pay signature gatherers, tax reporting begins the moment you classify them as independent contractors. Collect a W-9 (US tax identification form) before the first payment clears.


If a canvasser provides no valid Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN), IRS rules mandate 24% backup withholding on their earnings. The liability for incorrect withholding falls on your organization, never the worker.


Tracking temporary workers across multiple states compounds your 1099 reporting obligations. Once cumulative payments to a U.S. independent contractor cross $600 in a calendar year, IRC §6041 requires a 1099-NEC filing. Miss that threshold and your organization faces penalties up to $310 per unfiled form under IRC §6722: the liability sits with your campaign, not the gatherer. Automating payment tracking from day one keeps every disbursement logged against the correct payee, so your year-end 1099 filings are accurate without a manual reconciliation sprint.
