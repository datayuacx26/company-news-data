# What is IRS Form W-9? Everything You Need to Know

## The Ultimate Guide to IRS Form W-9


Preparing and filing taxes on time can feel like a master class in organization and bookkeeping. It's challenging, especially when your business hires several independent contractors, freelancers, and other non-employees. Companies must keep accurate records after collecting and validating tax information for any individual or company to which they make payments.


One of the most important forms you'll need to collect is IRS Form W-9. Officially called Form W-9, Request for Taxpayer Identification Number, and Certification, this document is a vital piece of the tax reporting puzzle, allowing businesses to file appropriate information returns to the Internal Revenue Service (IRS).


Not all tax situations are the same, so it's important to check current tax laws and work with professionals to understand your company's unique obligations. But in this guide, you'll learn everything there is to know about this essential document, including how to use it for accurate tax reporting.

### **A Brief Overview**


IRS Form W-9 serves a vital purpose: It helps verify the correct taxpayer identification number (TIN), name, and address of the individual or company receiving money from your business. Think of it as a formal written request for information. Its sole purpose is to verify critical information you'll later use to report paid income and generate[1099 forms](https://dots.dev/platform/tax) .


This document differs from a traditional W-4. Employees provide companies with IRS Form W-4 and Employee's Withholding Allowance Certificate, to ensure employers withhold the appropriate amount from paychecks. Typically, employees will complete and submit a W-4 during the onboarding process.


Form W-9 is for non-employee contractors, freelancers, gig workers, etc. It doesn't inherently arrange for any tax withholding, as companies generally don't withhold taxes of individuals not on their payroll. Both forms are important for tax compliance, but W-9s focus exclusively on non-employees, ensuring businesses have accurate information to report taxable payments and transactions.


### Why Is It Essential?


Business owners must collect W-9s in several situations. They are a common component of real estate transactions, dividend payments, stock sales, etc. However, the most common reason for requesting a W-9 is to pay non-employees. If your business plans to make reportable payments to independent contractors, self-employed freelancers, and any individual or company not included in your payroll, you must collect a W-9.


Businesses paying non-employees must report payments above the minimum annual income threshold of $600. The information collected on IRS Form W-9 helps companies create one of many types of 1099 forms. Form 1099s are information returns businesses must file with the IRS listing reportable payments. One copy goes to the IRS, while another goes to the payment recipient, allowing them to complete their taxes for the given year.


Without Form W-9, companies can't generate and file 1099s.


Contrary to popular belief, W-9s don't go to the IRS directly. Instead, businesses hold onto the document internally, using it exclusively to create 1099 forms and report payments accordingly. Several Federal laws protect the information collected on W-9s, and civil or criminal penalties could arise from misusing it.


### Its Role in Establishing Taxpayer Identification


This essential document is all about information verification. A properly completed W-9 includes relevant data that the IRS needs to tie reportable payments to individual contractors and non-employee entities. It's what the IRS uses to confirm identities. The most important elements of the form are the tax identification numbers. When creating 1099s, businesses will use TINs to verify information with the IRS, ensuring proper accounting and accurate taxation.


### Who Needs to Fill It Out?


Any individual or business entity not included in your payroll must supply you with a complete W-9. Official full- or part-time employees deal with IRS Form W-4, but non-employees must furnish a W-9. Companies generally request a W-9 when engaging with contracted workers. It's often part of the contract process when entering a work agreement with non-employees. You must collect a W-9 before you make any payments to ensure accurate bookkeeping and 1099 filing during tax preparations.


The best approach is to collect the IRS tax form before work commences. Failing to get a W-9 can lead to substantial issues later, and your business may have to pay withholding taxes on payments.


### Required Personal Details


The purpose of IRS tax Form W-9 is to verify the identification of payment recipients. Therefore, contractors must supply relevant personal details. The most important is the taxpayer identification numbers. We'll go into more detail about TINs soon, but this number plays a vital role in the IRS's administration of tax laws.


Other required personal details include the taxpayer's full legal name. If operating as an official business, taxpayers must provide their business name and the type of business entity they have. In certain situations, business entities must also provide exempt payee codes to cover exemptions.


Additional information requested in the W-9 includes the taxpayer's address and signature. It's important to note that you must collect W-9s from those defined as U.S. citizens and resident aliens.


### Understanding the Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN)


A taxpayer identification number is how the IRS administers tax laws in the United States. Every individual and business entity has a unique number for identity verification purposes, ensuring proper application of relevant tax laws.


There are several types of TINs.


• **Social Security Number (SSN):** An SSN is the most common. The Social Security Administration provides this number, which self-employed individuals will supply in their W-9 as their TIN.


• **Employer Identification Number (EIN):** Contractors operating as an official business entity, such as a Limited Liability Company, Partnership, or C Corporation, will supply an employer identification number (EIN). Some call it a federal tax identification number, and its prime purpose is to identify businesses.


**• Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN):** An ITIN is a tax processing number typically given to those who can't get a traditional SSN, such as nonresident aliens, resident aliens, and their dependents. It has the same nine-digital format as an SSN.


### Step-by-Step Guide for Filling Out Form W-9


Filing is straightforward. The IRS-supplied form is short and simple, containing properly labeled sections and instructions to input relevant information. Here's a simple step-by-step guide on how to fill it out properly.


• **Line 1 - Name:** This section is where self-employed individuals put their full legal name as shown on their tax returns.


• **Line 2 - Business Name:** Business entities completing the form as a Partnership, S Corporation, C Corporation, or multi-member Limited Liability Company (LLC) will include their business name here.


• **Line 3 - Federal Tax Classification:** This section includes several options. Self-employed individuals will check the first box to indicate they're a sole proprietor or single-member LLC. Businesses will check the appropriate business entity type.


• **Line 4 - Exemptions:** Exemptions only apply to certain business entities. Those with an applicable exempt payee code or exemption from the FATCA reporting code will provide it here.


• **Line 5 - Address:** This section is for providing a street address.


• **Line 6 - City, State, and ZIP Code:** Here is where contractors will provide the rest of their mailing addresses. Businesses use Lines 5 and 6 to mail the taxpayer's copy of the 1099.


• **Line 7 - Account Number:** If the taxpayer has an official internal account number with the company sending payments, they'll provide it here.


• **Part I - TIN:** Here is where individuals and businesses will provide their taxpayer identification number. There's a separate section for an SSN and one for an EIN.


• **Part II - Certification:** The final section to complete is certification. Filers will sign and date the form, declaring the information is correct. Any individuals or companies subject to backup withholding must cross out Item 2 of this section.


### Common Mistakes to Avoid During the Process


While the tax form is simple, there are a few common mistakes you'll want to avoid.


• **Incorrect Name:** Individuals and sole proprietors should provide their full legal name on Line 1. However, those operating under a different name should also give the business name in Line 2. Those without a unique business name should leave Line 2 blank.


• **Wrong TIN:** Always double-check the TIN. It's the most important piece of data on the form, and an inaccurate number can be a costly mistake for taxpayers and businesses.


• **Inaccurate Tax Classification:** Tax obligations vary based on federal tax classifications, so filers must choose the correct option on Line 3 based on their business structure to prevent issues.


• **Failing to Indicate Backup Withholding:** Taxpayers may need to use backup withholding. Ignoring this part of tax Form W-9 may result in a lower paycheck as businesses send a portion to the IRS.


### Submission and Record-Keeping


Businesses must collect Form W-9 before they send any payments to contractors. Taxpayers who fail to submit the form may experience significant payment delays, and companies that don't enforce the collection of a W-9 might encounter problems later.


W-9s should be part of a contractor's in-house file. Businesses must take all measures to safeguard the information collected and accurately record payments to ensure proper reporting to the IRS.


### W-9s for Businesses and Freelancers


Businesses and freelancers not part of a company's payroll must complete and file a W-9. It's a critical piece of the non-employee arrangement, ensuring tax compliance for both parties. Failing to collect a W-9 will make it impossible for you to inform the IRS of reportable payments. Not only will that lead to steep penalties, but it can also create problems for your contractors.


The 1099 forms you create with data on the W-9 go to both the IRS and the taxpayer. Incomplete or inaccurate W-9 forms will result in delays during tax season.


### Penalties for Noncompliance


Noncompliance will cost all parties. Contractors who refuse to provide a W-9 may be subject to a penalty, and many businesses will not work with individuals or companies that don't comply. Taxpayers who provide false information directly violate federal law, resulting in possible criminal penalties, including fines or prison time.


Businesses may also incur penalties for noncompliance. Because a W-9 is crucial in creating 1099 forms, your company might face steep fines if it fails to collect relevant information. In 2024, the penalty for filing a 1099 30 days late is $60 per form. The figure climbs the more you wait. Intentionally disregarding 1099 forms can lead to a fee of $630 per form. If you work with several contractors, total penalties can cost your business thousands!
