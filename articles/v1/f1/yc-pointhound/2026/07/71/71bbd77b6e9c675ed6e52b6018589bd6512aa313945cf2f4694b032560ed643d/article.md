# How to Fly First Class with Points

# How to Fly First Class with Points


A first-class suite from New York to Tokyo sells for $12,000 to $20,000 in cash. The same seat can book from around 85,000 credit card points. That is not a typo. Flying first class with points is the single most dramatic value in travel, and it is not reserved for the wealthy or for people who fly for work.


Here is the value gap. Redeem your points through a credit card portal and they are worth about 1 to 1.5 cents each ([CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_credit-card-rewards_issue-spotlight_2024-05.pdf) ). Transfer them to the right airline and they can be worth 2 to 20 times that. First class sits at the top of that range, the full 20x, because the cash fares are so high.


First class is also the hardest award to find. The cabins are tiny, seats are released unpredictably, and some airlines open first class only to their own members. This guide is honest about both sides, because the value is real and so is the scarcity.


You will learn which programs open first class to everyday points holders, what it costs in points versus cash, why the space is so hard to find, and how to catch and book it. At Pointhound, we track award availability across 200+ airlines and 21 loyalty programs on 75,000+ routes, so you can see which first-class seats your points can book before you transfer anything.


## First Class vs Business Class with Points: What You Get and What It Costs


First class costs more points than business, often 1.5 to 3 times as much on the same route, but it delivers a private suite instead of a lie-flat seat and the widest cash-to-points gap in travel.


The cabin difference is real, not marketing. Business class means a lie-flat seat that turns into a bed, usually in a pod with some privacy. First class means an enclosed private suite, and on the top products a separate bed, a closing door, standalone dining, or even a shower in the sky on the Emirates A380. First class exists on far fewer aircraft and routes, which is part of why the seats are so scarce.


The point cost climbs with the cabin. A business class award to Asia runs roughly 60,000 to 115,000 points one-way. First class on the same kind of route runs roughly 80,000 to 160,000. You pay more points, but the cash price you are replacing jumps even faster, which is why first class has the best cents-per-point value of any redemption when you can find it.

One honest recommendation: if this is your first premium redemption, start with business class. It is cheaper in points, far easier to find, and still a lie-flat seat across an ocean. ## Which Programs Actually Open First Class to Transferable Points?


A handful of programs open first class to holders of transferable points: Singapore KrisFlyer, Virgin Atlantic for ANA, Cathay Pacific Asia Miles, American AAdvantage for Japan Airlines, and Emirates Skywards. Each is reachable from a different set of cards.


The program you book through matters more than the airline you fly. Here is where the value lives, with the lowest realistic point costs and the honest limitation for each.


**Singapore Airlines first class, through KrisFlyer.** Singapore keeps its lowest-priced first class and Suites awards for its own KrisFlyer members and does not release them to partner airlines ([The Points Guy](https://thepointsguy.com/loyalty-programs/ultimate-guide-singapore-krisflyer/) ). The upside for beginners is huge: all five transferable programs feed KrisFlyer, so whatever points you hold, you can reach it. First class runs from around 132,500 miles one-way on routes like Los Angeles to Tokyo. The catch: the famous A380 Suites do not fly to the United States, so US departures use aircraft without a first cabin.


**ANA first class, through Virgin Atlantic Flying Club.** ANA runs one of the best first-class cabins in the sky, and it books from around 85,000 Virgin points one-way, less from the West Coast, for a seat that sells for $10,000 to $15,000 ([NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/travel/learn/ana-airlines-mileage-club-guide) ). Virgin Atlantic takes transfers from all five major programs. One quirk: ANA awards through Virgin have to be booked by phone, and ANA releases few first-class seats, often only close to departure.


**Japan Airlines first class, through American AAdvantage.** This is the low-fee standout. Japan Airlines first books for about 80,000 AAdvantage miles one-way plus roughly $5.60 in taxes ([The Points Guy](https://thepointsguy.com/loyalty-programs/first-class-with-points-miles/) ), for a cabin that sells above $12,000. The limit is access: among the five transferable programs, Bilt is the one that feeds AAdvantage at 1:1 (Marriott transfers at 3:1). If you hold only Chase or Amex points, this one is out of direct reach.


**Cathay Pacific first class, through Asia Miles.** Cathay first runs about 125,000 miles one-way from Los Angeles and 160,000 from New York ([The Points Guy](https://thepointsguy.com/loyalty-programs/cathay-pacific-asia-miles/) ), for a seat that sells above $12,000. Asia Miles takes transfers from Amex (at a 5:4 ratio), Capital One, Citi, and Bilt. Just know that Cathay releases little first-class space to partner programs, so open seats are rare.


**Emirates first class, through Emirates Skywards.** Emirates first, with the onboard shower and lounge, runs about 163,500 to 186,000 Skywards miles one-way from the US to Dubai, plus roughly $800 to $900 in carrier surcharges ([One Mile at a Time](https://onemileatatime.com/guides/redeem-miles-emirates-first-class/) ). Bilt transfers to Skywards at 1:1, Amex and Citi at 5:4, and Capital One at a reduced ratio as of January 2026 ([Emirates](https://www.emirates.com/us/english/skywards/partners/capital-one/) ). The catch is a big one: Emirates now requires Skywards elite status to book first class with its own miles, and the surcharges are steep, so this is not a beginner-friendly pick.

## What First Class Actually Costs in Points vs Cash


First-class awards run from roughly 80,000 to 186,000 points one-way, for seats that sell for $10,000 to $25,000 in cash. That is the widest cash-to-points gap of any redemption you can make.


The math is what makes people do a double-take. A $12,000 seat for 80,000 points works out to about 15 cents per point, roughly ten times what the same points return through a card portal. That is the 20x end of the transfer-value range made concrete.

Two honest notes before you get carried away. First, the point cost is high in absolute terms, and one welcome bonus often is not enough for a round-trip first-class ticket. Second, surcharges vary widely: Japan Airlines charges about $5.60 in fees, while Emirates can add $900. Always check the full cash-plus-points total, not just the miles. One upside worth knowing: award bookings can sometimes earn miles, depending on the program, so a points ticket is not always a dead end.

## Why First-Class Award Space Is So Hard to Find


First-class award space is scarce because airlines release few first seats as awards, some open them only to their own members, and the rules change without notice.


There are three reasons the seats are so hard to catch. The cabins are tiny to begin with, sometimes only eight seats on the whole plane, so there is little to release. Some programs never share first class with partners, like Singapore, or open it only close to departure, like ANA. And the rules shift under your feet.


Lufthansa is the cautionary tale. For years, its first-class award space opened to partner programs like Air Canada Aeroplan close to departure, a well-known way to book that cabin. Then, as of June 1, 2026, Lufthansa stopped releasing first-class award space to partner programs entirely ([One Mile at a Time](https://onemileatatime.com/insights/lufthansa-first-class-awards-partner-miles/) ). A path that worked last year can vanish this year. That is why you never assume a route is bookable and never transfer points on speculation.


Some of this scarcity is a timing problem: a few programs release premium space in a second wave close to takeoff. That is a whole strategy on its own, and we cover it in[last-minute award flights](https://www.pointhound.com/blog/last-minute-award-flights) . The short version is that a search across many programs at once beats refreshing airline sites by hand, which is exactly what a tool that scans 21 loyalty programs is for.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### Can you really fly first class with credit card points?


Yes. A handful of programs, including Singapore KrisFlyer, Virgin Atlantic for ANA, Cathay Asia Miles, American AAdvantage for Japan Airlines, and Emirates Skywards, book international first class, and all take transfers from major credit card programs. The space is limited, but it is real and repeatable.


### How many points do you need for a first-class flight?


Roughly 80,000 to 186,000 points one-way, depending on the airline and route. Japan Airlines first runs about 80,000 AAdvantage miles, ANA first about 85,000 Virgin points, Cathay first 125,000 to 160,000 Asia Miles, and Emirates first 163,500 or more Skywards miles plus surcharges.


### Is first class worth the extra points over business class?


Sometimes. First class costs 1.5 to 3 times the points of business on the same route, for a private suite instead of a lie-flat seat. The value per point can still be excellent because first-class cash fares are so high, but business class is the better everyday value for most travelers.


### Which credit card points are best for first class?


It depends on the airline. Amex reaches ANA, Singapore, and Cathay. Capital One and Citi reach several first-class programs. Bilt is the transferable program that feeds American AAdvantage for Japan Airlines first, and it also transfers 1:1 to Emirates Skywards, which makes it unusually useful for premium cabins.


### Why can't I find first-class award seats?


First-class cabins are tiny, some airlines never release first to partners or open it only near departure, and the rules change. Lufthansa stopped sharing first-class awards with partner programs in mid-2026. Search live availability instead of assuming, and set an alert to catch a release.
