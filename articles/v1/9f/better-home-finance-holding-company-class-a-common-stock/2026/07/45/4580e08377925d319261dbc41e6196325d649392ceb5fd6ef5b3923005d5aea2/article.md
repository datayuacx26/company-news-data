# How to spot AI-altered real estate listing photos before you tour a home

Do you know an AI-edited photo when you see it?


More home shoppers are discovering, upon visiting a listing, that the pictures they saw online don't match the rooms they're seeing in person.


Now, a handful of cities are proposing limits on how much listing photos can be edited.


There are also several ways you can protect yourself from being misled by artificial images.

## Why AI-edited listing photos are becoming so common


AI photo-editing tools have gotten faster, cheaper, and easier to use. This is changing what a typical online listing looks like.


Some of the editing is uncontroversial: virtual staging fills an empty room with digitally added furniture so buyers can picture how a space might function, and most listings disclose when a room shown is virtually staged rather than furnished in person.


But some listings go farther: repainting walls, replacing flooring, brightening dim rooms, removing clutter that's covering a real defect, or adding architectural features that don't exist.


A handful of cities have proposed disclosure requirements for AI-altered listing images, and at least one state already requires it. But coverage is uneven, and most of the country has no legal requirement that a listing disclose photo edits beyond standard staging.


## The visual tells. How to spot an altered photo


You don't need special software or skills to catch most AI-edited listing photos.


A few visual patterns tend to show up again and again:


-


**Walls, countertops, and floors that look unnaturally smooth or uniform.** AI editing tools tend to flatten texture, so surfaces can look almost plastic or airbrushed compared to the natural variation you'd expect in a lived-in home.


-


**Grass and landscaping that look painted on.** Real grass has uneven color and shadow. AI-enhanced lawns often look flat, oversaturated, or suspiciously uniform in color from edge to edge.


-


**Warped or mismatched lines around windows and doors.** Look closely at door frames, window mullions, and ceiling lines. AI edits that add or remove features sometimes leave subtly curved or misaligned edges where a straight line should be.


-


**Lighting or furniture that doesn't match across photos of the same room.** If the same living room appears twice in a listing with different furniture, different wall colors, or light coming from a different direction, that's a strong signal at least one version was digitally altered.


-


**Shadows and reflections that don't line up.** Real light sources cast consistent shadows. Edited images can leave shadows falling in a direction that doesn't match the visible windows or fixtures in the room.


See some of these tells? Be sure to ask the listing agent before visiting the property.


## What to do before you schedule a tour


Before you spend a Saturday touring a home, a few minutes of extra scrutiny online can save you the trip — or at least set realistic expectations.


Start by comparing every photo in the listing against the other photos. Inconsistent wall colors, flooring, or fixtures between photos of what's supposed to be the same room are one of the clearest signs of editing.


Next, ask the[listing agent](https://better.com/content/what-does-a-real-estate-agent-do) directly whether any photos were digitally staged or enhanced, and in what way. Agents aren't always the ones who commissioned the edits, but a direct question puts the disclosure on record and often gets a straight answer.


If you're working with a[buyer's agent](https://better.com/content/what-is-a-buyers-agent) , ask them to flag anything unusual before you book a showing. They tour homes constantly and develop a good eye for staged versus altered photos. If you're shopping remotely, request an unedited or live video walkthrough before building your decision around the listing photos alone.


## Should altered photos change how you make an offer?


It depends on what kind of editing you're looking at. Standard virtual staging, like furniture added to an empty room, is common, generally disclosed, and shouldn't affect how you evaluate a home's condition. It's a marketing tool, not a misrepresentation of the structure itself.


Deceptive editing is different. If a listing photo shows a feature that doesn't exist, hides visible damage, or misrepresents the layout or condition of the home, that's a reason for real caution but not necessarily a reason to walk away from the property. It is a reason to verify everything in person before you commit.

## Frequently asked questions


### I'm about to tour a house tomorrow and the listing photos look almost too perfect. How can I tell if they were edited with AI before I go?


Compare every photo in the listing against the others for mismatched lighting, wall colors, or furniture in what's supposed to be the same room, and look for the visual tells described above: unnaturally smooth surfaces, painted-looking grass, and warped lines around windows or doors. If anything looks off, ask the listing agent directly whether the photos were digitally staged or enhanced before you commit to the showing.


### Is it illegal for a real estate agent to use AI to edit listing photos without telling me?


It depends on location. A small number of cities and at least one state now require disclosure of AI-altered listing images, but most of the country doesn't yet have a specific rule. That said, misrepresenting a home's condition can still raise legal issues separate from any AI-specific disclosure requirement.


### What's the difference between normal virtual staging and the kind of AI editing people call "housefishing"?


Virtual staging adds furniture and decor to an empty room so buyers can picture the space furnished, and it's typically disclosed as staged. The editing people call "housefishing" goes further. These edits include repainting walls, replacing flooring, hiding damage, or adding features that don't actually exist. This misrepresents the home's real condition rather than just its potential.


### If I already made an offer and then noticed the listing photos were AI-edited, can I back out without losing my earnest money?


That depends on the contingencies written into your offer, not on the photos themselves. A standard[home inspection contingency](https://better.com/content/what-is-a-home-inspection) generally lets you renegotiate or walk away if the inspection reveals problems, regardless of what the listing photos showed.


### Do lighting and staging tricks in listing photos affect a home's appraisal value?


No. An appraisal is an independent, in-person evaluation of the home's condition, size, and comparable sales. It shouldn't rely on the listing's marketing photos. Staged or even deceptively edited images can influence how many buyers show interest, but they don't factor into the appraised value your lender uses.


### Are there any real estate platforms or cities that require disclosure when listing photos are AI-edited?


Yes, a small number of cities have proposed or introduced disclosure requirements for AI-generated or digitally altered listing images, and at least one state already has an AI-disclosure requirement in place for real estate listings. Rules vary widely by location, and most of the country doesn't have a specific requirement yet, so it's worth asking the listing agent directly regardless of where you're buying.
