# What everyone gets wrong about making friends in a new city

You did the hard part. You moved. New apartment, new job, new coffee shop you're slowly making "yours." Everyone told you a fresh start would be exciting. So why does Saturday afternoon feel so quiet?


Here's the thing almost everyone gets wrong: making friends as an adult isn't a *meeting people* problem. Cities are full of people. The hard part is turning a friendly stranger into someone who'd actually pick you up from the airport. That takes three ingredients we stop getting for free after school: **proximity, repetition, and a little vulnerability** . Every tip below is really just a way to manufacture those on purpose.


## 1. Optimize for repetition instead of an instant spark


Most real friendships are built by boring repetition: the same class, the same run club, the same trivia night. Pick things that *recur* and show up more than once. Familiarity does the heavy lifting.


## 2. Say yes to the mediocre invite


The coworker's birthday drinks you're lukewarm about? Go. Early on, your job isn't to find your people; it's to widen the top of the funnel so your people have a chance to appear.


## 3. Be the one who follows up


Almost everyone leaves a nice conversation thinking "I hope we hang out again" and then does nothing. Be the person who texts first: "Loved chatting, want to grab coffee next week?" You have nothing to lose!


## 4. Trade novelty for a standing plan


One great brunch you never repeat isn't a friendship. It's a nice memory. A recurring plan (same people, same time, low effort) is how acquaintances quietly become your people.


## 5. Go first on vulnerability (a little)


Small talk keeps things safe and shallow. Sharing something slightly real, like "honestly, moving here has been lonelier than I expected," gives the other person permission to be real back. That's the moment an acquaintance transitions into a friend.

## 6. Put yourself where the same faces come back


Choose venues and activities designed for return visits, not one-time crowds. A weekly workshop beats a giant festival. A small book club beats a massive networking mixer. Smaller and repeating wins.


## 7. Don't outsource your whole social life to your partner


If you're partnered, it's easy to let them become your only plan. If you're remote, it's easy to let colleagues-on-a-screen count as "seeing people." Neither is a substitute for friends who are yours.


## 8. Give it a real season, not a weekend


[Research on adult friendship](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend) suggests it takes roughly 50 hours together to become casual friends, 90 hours to become friends, and more than 200 hours to become close friends. You're not behind. You're on schedule. Judge your progress in months, not by whether last Tuesday "worked."


## 9. Embrace the awkwardness


The first few hangouts are supposed to feel a little awkward. Expect the first 3 or 4 meetups to be a bit stiff. Give it at least 5 before deciding it is not working. As you build familiarity, conversation can start to flow, silences feel less heavy, and you may realize you actually look forward to seeing them. Do not quit at meetup 2 just because it still feels new.
