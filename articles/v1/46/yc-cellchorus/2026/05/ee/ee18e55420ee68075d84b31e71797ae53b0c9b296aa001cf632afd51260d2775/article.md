# CellChorus and Stanford Medicine Awarded NCI Grant to Develop AI-Driven Functional Assay Predicting CAR-T Response in Lymphoma Patients

# CellChorus and Stanford Medicine Awarded NCI Grant to Develop AI-Driven Functional Assay Predicting CAR-T Response in Lymphoma Patients


### SBIR funding from the National Institutes of Health will support development of TIMING-based functional assays to predict patient response to cell therapy


PRESS RELEASE


HOUSTON, Texas — May 7, 2026 — CellChorus Inc., the leader in applying artificial intelligence to visually evaluate how cells perform and interact over time, announced today that the National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) has awarded CellChorus® and Stanford Medicine a Phase I Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) grant to profile cells from relapsed/refractory large B cell lymphoma (r/rLBCL) patients after treatment with CAR-T cell therapy to develop a functional signature of response to therapy.


While nearly half of patients with r/rLBCL who receive chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T) cell therapy achieve a durable, long-term remission, many do not respond to therapy or experience relapse. Early identification of patients who are unlikely to achieve a durable response remains a major unmet need in CAR-T cell therapy. A functional assay capable of predicting response soon after treatment could guide clinical decision-making, enable more personalized treatment strategies, and identify mechanisms of failure to accelerate development of next-generation cell therapies.


***Overview of TIMING*** *. (A) Cells and optionally beads are loaded on arrays with 1000s of nanowells, which are (B) imaged at regular intervals to create 1000s of time-lapse videos. (C) Images are processed by AI/ML software pipelines with cell segmentation, classification, tracking, and event detection algorithms (D) to produce a database of single-cell functional and performance data (e.g., migration, contact, survival, killing, biomolecule secretion) to enable (E) generating, for example, functional signatures of response.*


The CellChorus Time-lapse Imaging Microscopy In Nanowell Grids (TIMING™) platform uses artificial intelligence to directly observe how thousands of individual immune cells behave over time. Unlike conventional assays that measure either bulk activity or single-cell features at a single point in time, TIMING captures functional behaviors of single cells to reveal mechanisms that determine therapeutic success. TIMING single-cell data include immune cell migration, contact dynamics, killing, survival, biomolecule secretion, and other functional readouts. This project will build on prior results based on TIMING data, such as a publication in Nature Cancer demonstrating that TIMING can identify CAR-T cell subsets with superior migratory behavior and serial killing capacity associated with clinical response ([Rezvan et al, PMID: 38750245](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12345446/) ).


***Serial killing and migration of T cells from clinical axi-cel samples and correlation with complete clinical response*** *. (Left) Plots comparing serial killing, wherein a CAR T effector cell (E) conjugates and kills two NALM-6 target cells (T) in all 1E:2T nanowells between T cells from patients with complete response (CR) or with partial response or progressive disease (PR/PD). (Right) Plots comparing migration of T cells from patients with either CR or PR/PD within 1E:0T nanowells. Each dot represents an average T-cell migration rate for an individual patient. (*[Rezvan et al, PMID: 38750245](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12345446/) *)*


“The sooner we know whether a cancer therapy is working, the better. To maximize patient benefit, we need technology that can provide a robust and early prediction of response to therapy. The technology needs to be scalable, cost-efficient, and capable of rapid turnaround times,” said Rebecca Berdeaux, PhD, Chief Scientific Officer of CellChorus. “We are excited to work with Drs. David Miklos and Saurabh Dahiya and their colleagues on this very important project.”


“Predicting which patients will achieve durable responses after CAR-T therapy remains one of the most important challenges in the field,” said David Miklos, MD, PhD, the James and Katherine Lau Professor of Medicine at Stanford Medicine and the Chief of the Bone Marrow Transplantation (BMT) and Cell Therapy (CT) Program at Stanford Medicine. “We aim to uncover functional cellular signatures that can guide treatment decisions and improve patient outcomes.”


Miklos and his Stanford Medicine colleagues have built the BMT-CT Biobank, which manages the collection, storage, and distribution of biological samples from patients treated at their clinic. The Stanford BMT-CT Biobank includes samples collected from patients treated on investigator-initiated clinical trials, as well as standard-of-care CAR-T cell therapies. The samples are annotated with clinical data, including prior treatment, disease progression, and response, to provide an essential resource for ongoing and future correlative research.


The project represents an important step toward developing TIMING-based functional prognostics capable of informing patient selection, monitoring cellular therapies, and accelerating the development of next-generation immune therapies.
