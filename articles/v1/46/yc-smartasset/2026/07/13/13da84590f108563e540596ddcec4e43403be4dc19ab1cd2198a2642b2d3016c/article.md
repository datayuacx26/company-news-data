# We Put Our $600k Home in a Trust to Protect It. Here’s the One Thing It Won’t Shield Us From

Putting a $600,000 home in a trust may feel like you’re protecting your estate. But a trust cannot shield your home from every financial or legal risk. You may not have the protection you expect unless you take one planning step that many homeowners overlook.


## The Missing Step Needed to Make a Trust Work


[Creating a trust](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/can-you-set-up-a-trust-without-an-attorney) and[putting your home into it](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/pros-and-cons-of-putting-your-house-in-a-trust) are two different things. A trust outlines who will manage and receive your assets. Simply signing the trust doesn’t transfer ownership of your home. Until the property is retitled in the trust’s name, it generally remains in your name, and many of the trust’s intended benefits may not apply.


That transfer requires recording a new deed, not just signing the trust. Many homeowners choose a[revocable trust](https://smartasset.com/retirement/what-is-a-revocable-living-trust) because it lets them keep control of the property while they’re alive. They can generally change the trust, remove the home, or dissolve the trust altogether.


> That flexibility also makes it easy to overlook the final step. Homeowners sign the trust, assume the work is finished and never transfer ownership. The trust exists, but[the home never actually becomes part of it](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/what-happens-to-property-not-in-a-trust) . Funding the trust is what makes it work, not what it protects.

## What a Revocable Trust Won’t Protect Your $600,000 Home From


You transfer your $600,000 home into a revocable living trust. The property[avoids probate](https://smartasset.com/insights/how-to-avoid-probate) , and if you become incapacitated, a successor trustee can step in and manage it. So far, the trust is doing exactly what it was designed to do.


Then something happens. You’re sued, face a large judgment or need Medicaid to help pay for[long-term care](https://smartasset.com/insurance/what-does-long-term-care-insurance-cover) . This is where many homeowners discover—the trust has limits that they never expected.


**Here’s the one thing it won’t shield you from:** You still legally own the property. Because you remain the legal owner, a revocable trust generally doesn’t protect your home from creditors, lawsuits or Medicaid spend-down rules. The trust changes how your home is managed and eventually transferred. It doesn’t change ownership during your lifetime.


If shielding your home from those specific risks is your actual goal, a revocable trust is only part of the plan. Certain[irrevocable trusts](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/irrevocable-trust) and other strategies can offer protections that a revocable trust simply can’t. The right choice depends on the specific risk that you are trying to address.

## What You’d Actually Need to Protect Your Home


Real protection, in this case, means giving something up. An irrevocable trust takes away your ownership and control. That trade is exactly what lets your home sit outside your estate, safe from creditors, lawsuits and Medicaid spend-down rules. But here’s the catch: It generally works only if the transfer happened at least[five years before you need long-term care](https://smartasset.com/retirement/how-to-avoid-medicaid-5-year-lookback) .


How much protection you get depends on the type of trust you choose. A[Medicaid asset protection trust](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/medicaid-trust) removes your home from your countable assets, which can help you qualify for long-term care coverage. A[domestic asset protection trust](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/domestic-asset-protection-trusts-estate-planning) , available in a handful of states, is designed to shield assets from certain creditors while still letting you remain a beneficiary.


A trust isn’t the only way to protect your home. Depending on your state, a[homestead exemption](https://smartasset.com/taxes/what-is-a-homestead-tax-exemption) can shield part of your home’s equity from creditors. And married couples in some states also have access to[tenancy by the entirety](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/tenancy-by-the-entirety) , which protects a jointly owned home from the creditors of one spouse.


Your trust may avoid probate and cover incapacity exactly as planned. But as long as you remain the legal owner, it generally won’t protect your home from creditors, lawsuits or Medicaid spend-down rules.
