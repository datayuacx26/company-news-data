# What to Do With a $250K Inheritance

A $250,000 inheritance could offer you the chance to pay off debt, build up your savings or invest long-term. While this amount is smaller than a multimillion-dollar estate, you may assume it doesn’t require much planning. That assumption could cost you. Even a modest inheritance can create avoidable tax consequences if you make the wrong moves in the first year after inheriting it.


## Know How Your Inheritance Gets Taxed


A $250,000[inheritance](https://smartasset.com/investing/how-does-inheritance-work) may be simpler than a larger estate, but it’s still important to understand exactly what you’ve received before making any decisions. At this level, an inheritance is often concentrated in one or two asset types, such as an[inherited IRA](https://smartasset.com/estate-planning/inheriting-an-inherited-ira) and a taxable brokerage account. Each comes with different tax rules and planning considerations.


Asset Type Key Considerations


Traditional IRA Inherited IRAs are generally subject to distribution rules under the SECURE Act, and withdrawals are typically taxed as ordinary income.


Taxable brokerage account Investments often receive a step-up in basis at the original owner’s death, which can reduce capital gains taxes if they’re sold.


Taking inventory first can help you avoid unnecessary taxes, prioritize the decisions that require immediate attention and build a plan for the money over time. Even if your inheritance consists of only one or two accounts, understanding how each is taxed and how it fits into your overall financial picture can help you make better decisions from the start.

## What a Mistake on a $250,000 Inheritance Could Cost You


How you inherit assets can have a major effect on what happens next. You might receive a $150,000 taxable brokerage account that was originally purchased for $50,000 and a $100,000 traditional IRA from a parent. Even though both accounts hold investments, the IRS applies different tax rules to each.


Account Type Amount Inherited Tax Treatment Estimated Tax Owed


Taxable Brokerage Account $150,000 (basis: $50,000)[Step-up in basis](https://smartasset.com/financial-advisor/fair-market-value) ; no capital gains owed on inherited appreciation $0


Traditional IRA $100,000 Taxed as ordinary income upon withdrawal $23,886 (if withdrawn in one year)


The brokerage account gets what’s called a[step-up in basis](https://smartasset.com/financial-advisor/stepped-up-basis) , which resets your cost basis to the fair market value as of the date of death. Practically speaking, that means the $100,000 in gains your parent’s[investments](https://smartasset.com/investing/investment-calculator) built up over the years disappears for tax purposes, and you could sell right away without owing capital gains on any of it.


The IRA, by comparison, doesn’t get that treatment. Every dollar you pull out is taxed as ordinary income, on top of whatever else you’re already earning. If you’re bringing in $100,000 in[taxable income](https://smartasset.com/taxes/income-taxes) from your job and then withdraw the full $100,000 IRA balance in the same year, your taxable income jumps to $200,000, and that jump doesn’t land evenly across your existing tax bracket.


Here’s what that costs you in 2026:1


Tax Bracket Amount Taxed at This Rate Tax Owed


22% $5,700 $1,254


24% $94,300 $22,632


**Total** **$100,000** **$23,886**


That’s $23,886 in added federal tax, before factoring in deductions, credits or whatever your state charges on top.


Spreading withdrawals over time can reduce the tax impact. Non-spouse beneficiaries generally have up to 10 years to empty an inherited IRA, so withdrawing a $100,000 balance over several years instead of taking it all at once may keep more of the money in the 22% tax bracket instead of pushing part of it into the 24% bracket in a single year.

## Make Your Legacy Work for You


After you’ve accounted for taxes and understand what you’ve inherited, think about how the money fits into your overall financial situation. Keep enough cash available for near-term expenses or emergencies, then invest the remainder in a way that complements your long-term goals rather than reacting to short-term market movements.
