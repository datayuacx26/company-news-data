# If You Invested $5K in the SpaceX IPO, How Much Do You Have Now?

SpaceX began trading on June 12, 2026, with an IPO price of $135 per share, giving retail investors an opportunity to buy into one of the world’s most closely watched aerospace companies. A $5,000 investment at the IPO price would have purchased about 37.04 shares. By June 16, those shares had climbed to $225.64, increasing the investment’s value to roughly $8,352. On July 31, with the stock trading at about $108 per share at close, that same investment is worth around $4,000, a reminder that even the most anticipated IPOs can lose value.1

Selling near the June 16 high would have locked in that gain. Holding the same shares through July 31 would have resulted in an overall loss relative to the original investment. That entire round trip unfolded in just over six weeks, highlighting how quickly sentiment could shift after a high-profile public offering.

## Why SpaceX Fell After a Strong Start


Even though Wall Street analysts had published higher price targets, the stock’s rapid climb from $135 to $225 prompted some investors to cash out their positions. This selling pressure, combined with concerns that company insiders would soon be allowed to unload their shares, pushed the price lower.2


Broader market conditions worsened the decline in late July. Alphabet and Tesla reported earnings that sparked concerns about heavy AI spending.3 Rising oil prices also added to inflation worries about increasing fuel and transportation costs across the economy.4 These declines weren’t specific to SpaceX, but they hit high-growth technology stocks and recently public companies particularly hard.


When insiders are allowed to sell shares they’ve held since the company’s founding, additional stock may enter the market. This increased supply can potentially put downward pressure on the stock price. The prospect of insider selling, whether it happens immediately or gradually, often prompts investors to reassess valuations and adjust positions accordingly.


IPO prices do not move based on company fundamentals alone. The six-week decline shows how profit-taking, anticipated insider selling, earnings surprises and broader market shifts could reshape a stock’s trajectory. What began with strong investor enthusiasm also gave way to a market reassessment as early SpaceX investors locked in gains and the broader market reevaluated expectations.


## What Investors May Do Next


IPO investing is different from investing in an established company. Newly public stocks have limited trading history, and their prices can change quickly as investors reassess growth expectations and valuation. Before investing, it can help to decide whether your goal is a long-term investment, a shorter-term trade or a specific exit strategy.


SpaceX’s early trading also highlights the importance of diversification. Limiting the size of any single position can reduce the impact of sharp price swings and help keep one investment from having an outsized effect on your portfolio.
