# Is $2.5M Enough to Spend $100K a Year in Retirement, Or Will Taxes Leave Me Short?

A $2.5 million portfolio could support $100,000 in annual retirement spending, but taxes might require you to withdraw more than that amount. Account type determines the portion of each distribution you keep. Knowing that amount can help you estimate whether your spending goal is sustainable and what adjustments you may need to make.


## How Much Do You Need to Withdraw to Spend $100,000?


The[4% rule](https://smartasset.com/retirement/how-long-will-money-last-using-4-rule) is a common benchmark that retirees use to estimate how much they can withdraw from their savings each year. It generally calls for taking 4% of the portfolio in the first year of retirement and[adjusting that dollar amount for inflation](https://smartasset.com/investing/inflation-calculator) in the following years. On a $2.5 million portfolio, a 4% starting withdrawal would provide $100,000:


Annual Withdrawal Portfolio Balance Starting Withdrawal Rate


$100,000 $2.5 million $100,000 ÷ $2.5 million = 4%


The calculation assumes that $100,000 is the[amount withdrawn from your portfolio](https://smartasset.com/retirement/safe-withdrawal-rate-by-age) , not what remains after taxes. If you need $100,000 available for spending, the amount you withdraw could be higher. Depending on the account, a your distribution could be fully taxable, partially taxable or tax-free. This table compares the general tax treatment and potential impact on your spending money:


Withdrawal Source General Tax Treatment Effect on a $100,000 Distribution


[Traditional 401(k) or IRA](https://smartasset.com/retirement/ira-vs-401k) Pretax distributions are generally subject to ordinary income tax Taxes reduce the amount available to spend


[Roth IRA](https://smartasset.com/retirement/what-is-a-roth-ira) Qualified distributions are generally tax-free The full $100,000 may be available


[Taxable brokerage account](https://smartasset.com/investing/when-you-should-use-a-taxable-brokerage-account)[Capital gains tax may apply when investments are sold](https://smartasset.com/investing/capital-gains-tax-calculator) Tax generally applies to gains rather than the full amount sold


Taxes that reduce a $100,000 withdrawal below your spending goal would require a larger distribution. If a big part of the $2.5 million is held in[pretax retirement accounts,](https://smartasset.com/retirement/pretax-contributions) the higher amount could push your withdrawal rate above 4%. Your filing status, taxable sources and other retirement income would affect the final figure.

## How Taxes Could Eat Into the Longevity of Your Portfolio


Federal taxes will require you to take larger distributions. For example, if you are single and take all distributions from a fully taxable pretax retirement account, you would need to withdraw about $116,885 to net that amount. This estimate assumes that you have no other income, uses the $16,100[standard deduction](https://smartasset.com/taxes/standard-deduction) and[2026 federal income tax brackets](https://smartasset.com/taxes/current-federal-income-tax-brackets) .1


The $116,885 withdrawal would leave $100,785 in taxable income after the standard deduction. The[federal tax calculation](https://smartasset.com/taxes/income-taxes) would look like this:


2026 Bracket Tax Calculation


10% $12,400 × 10% = $1,240


12% $38,000 × 12% = $4,560


22% $50,385 × 22% = $11,084.70


**Total** **$16,884.70**


Holding that annual distribution constant and excluding investment returns, inflation and state income taxes, your $2.5 million portfolio could decline as follows:


Year Annual Withdrawal / Rate Portfolio Remaining


1 $116,885 / $116,885 ÷ $2,500,000 = 4.68% $2,500,000 − $116,885 = $2,383,115


2 $116,885 / $116,885 ÷ $2,383,115 = 4.90% $2,383,115 − $116,885 = $2,266,230


3 $116,885 / $116,885 ÷ $2,266,230 = 5.16% $2,266,230 − $116,885 = $2,149,345


4 $116,885 / $116,885 ÷ $2,149,345 = 5.44% $2,149,345 − $116,885 = $2,032,460


5 $116,885 / $116,885 ÷ $2,032,460 = 5.75% $2,032,460 − $116,885 = $1,915,575


By comparison, taking exactly $100,000 annually with no federal tax liability would leave $2 million after five years. Withdrawing enough to cover the estimated bill would leave $84,425 less in your portfolio.


These figures are simplified. Investment gains could offset some of the decline, while market losses may accelerate it. Inflation might also increase future spending needs, and changes in tax rates, deductions or other income can alter the amount required each year.


## Tax Gap Strategies to Help Reach Your Spending Goal


If taxes push your required withdrawal above $100,000, you could take larger pretax distributions in lower-income years and reduce them when other taxable income goes up. Social Security, pension payments,[annuities or a bond ladder](https://smartasset.com/financial-advisor/bonds-vs-annuities) could also cover part of your spending goal. The tax treatment of each source will affect how much you ultimately need from your portfolio.
