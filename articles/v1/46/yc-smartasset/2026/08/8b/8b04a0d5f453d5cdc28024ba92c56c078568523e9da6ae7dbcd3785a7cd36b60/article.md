# How to Avoid Taxes When You Inherit Cash: Strategies and Examples

Receiving a cash inheritance can provide financial security, but it can also raise questions about taxes. Most inherited cash isn’t subject to federal income tax. However, certain situations, such as inherited retirement accounts and state inheritance taxes, can create unexpected tax obligations. Understanding how inherited cash is taxed and what tax minimization strategies are available can help you preserve more of your inheritance.

## Do You Owe Taxes on Inherited Cash?


In most cases, simply inheriting cash does not create a federal income tax bill for the beneficiary. Whether the money comes from a bank account, a brokerage account that has been liquidated or another cash asset distributed through an estate, the inheritance itself is generally not considered taxable income. That means you typically do not report inherited cash on your federal income tax return.


However, that does not mean inherited cash is always completely tax-free. In some cases, taxes may apply before the money reaches the beneficiary. For example, if the estate is large enough to be subject to the federal estate tax, the estate generally pays that tax prior to asset distribution. A handful of states also impose an[inheritance tax](https://smartasset.com/taxes/all-about-the-inheritance-tax) . This may require certain beneficiaries to pay tax based on the amount they inherit and their relationship to the deceased.


The source of the inherited cash also matters. If the cash comes from an inherited traditional[IRA](https://smartasset.com/retirement/what-is-an-ira) , 401(k) or other tax-deferred[retirement account](https://smartasset.com/retirement/types-of-individual-retirement-accounts-iras) , future withdrawals may be subject to ordinary income tax even though the inheritance itself is not. Likewise, if inherited cash remains in an interest-bearing bank account or is invested after you receive it, any interest, dividends or investment gains earned going forward may be taxable.


For example, suppose you inherit $200,000 from your parent’s savings account. You generally would not owe federal income tax simply because you received the inheritance. However, if you deposit the money into a high-yield savings account that earns $8,000 of interest during the following year, that interest would generally be taxable even though the original inheritance was not.


## Strategies to Reduce or Avoid Taxes


Although most inherited cash is not subject to federal income tax, there are still steps you can take to reduce or avoid taxes that may apply before or after you receive the inheritance. The best strategy depends on what the source of the funds is, where you live and how you plan to use or invest the money.


One of the most important steps when evaluating tax obligations on an inheritance is determining where the cash came from. Cash inherited from a checking or savings account is generally not taxable as income. In contrast, distributions from inherited traditional IRAs,[401(k)s](https://smartasset.com/retirement/401k-calculator) and other tax-deferred retirement accounts are often subject to ordinary income tax. Knowing the source of the funds can help you plan for any future tax obligations.


While there is no federal inheritance tax, a small number of states impose one. In those states, the tax owed often depends on your relationship to the deceased. Spouses are typically tax-exempt, while close family members receive favorable treatment. Understanding your state’s rules can help you estimate any tax liability before you receive the inheritance.


Once inherited cash is in your possession, any income it generates may become taxable. If you invest the money or place it in an interest-bearing account, then any future interest,[dividends](https://smartasset.com/taxes/dividend-tax-rate) and[capital gains](https://smartasset.com/investing/capital-gains-tax-calculator) are generally subject to the normal tax rules. Choosing tax-efficient investments or contributing some of the funds to tax-advantaged accounts, if you’re eligible, may help reduce your ongoing tax burden.


If part of your[inheritance](https://smartasset.com/investing/how-does-inheritance-work) comes from a tax-deferred retirement account, carefully timing withdrawals may help lower the taxes you owe. Spreading distributions over multiple years, when permitted under IRS rules, can reduce the risk of pushing yourself into a higher income tax bracket in a single year.

## Common Tax Mistakes After Receiving an Inheritance


Even if the inherited cash itself isn’t taxable, the financial decisions you make afterward can affect your tax bill. Avoiding these common mistakes can help you preserve more of your inheritance and reduce the risk of unexpected taxes.


- **Failing to seek professional advice:** Inheritances involving trusts, retirement accounts, multiple beneficiaries or assets in different states can present complex tax issues. Working with a tax professional or financial advisor can help you avoid costly mistakes and identify planning opportunities.
- **Assuming all inherited assets are tax-free** : While inherited cash is generally not subject to federal income tax, distributions from inherited traditional IRAs, 401(k)s and other tax-deferred retirement accounts are often taxable when withdrawn.
- **Ignoring state inheritance tax rules** : A handful of states impose inheritance taxes, and the amount owed may depend on your relationship to the deceased. Failing to understand your state’s rules could lead to an unexpected tax obligation.
- **Overlooking taxes on investment earnings** : Once you inherit cash, any interest, dividends or capital gains it generates are generally taxable. Many beneficiaries focus on the inheritance itself and forget to plan for taxes on future investment income.
- **Taking large retirement account distributions all at once** : If your inheritance includes a[tax-deferred retirement account](https://smartasset.com/taxes/tax-deferred-pension-and-retirement-savings-plans) , withdrawing the balance in a single year could push you into a[higher tax bracket](https://smartasset.com/taxes/income-taxes) . A more strategic withdrawal plan may help reduce the overall tax impact. Just make sure this is permitted under applicable rules.
- **Spending the inheritance without a financial plan** : Making major purchases or investments without considering the long-term tax consequences can reduce the value of your inheritance. Taking time to evaluate your financial goals can help you make more tax-efficient decisions.


## Build a Tax-Efficient Plan Before You Invest or Spend


Receiving a cash inheritance can create new financial opportunities, but making decisions too quickly may lead to unnecessary taxes or missed planning opportunities. Before spending or[investing the money](https://smartasset.com/investing/how-to-invest-inheritance) , take time to evaluate how the inheritance fits into your overall financial goals, existing investments and future cash flow needs.


Start by identifying any immediate financial priorities. Using inherited cash to pay off high-interest debt, establish or strengthen an emergency fund or cover upcoming expenses can improve your financial stability without creating additional tax consequences. Once you’ve addressed those needs, you can decide how much of the inheritance to[invest for long-term growth](https://smartasset.com/investing/long-term-investing-strategy) .


If you plan to invest the money, consider placing assets in accounts that align with their tax characteristics. For example, if you’re eligible, contributing to tax-advantaged retirement accounts or a[health savings account](https://smartasset.com/checking-account/what-is-an-hsa) can reduce your current or future tax burden. You may also want to diversify your investments to balance growth potential with your[risk tolerance](https://smartasset.com/investing/risk-tolerance) and time horizon.


It’s also important to consider the long-term tax impact of any investment decisions. Any interest, dividends and capital gains generated after you receive the inheritance may be taxable. Selecting tax-efficient investments and managing asset location can help preserve more of your returns over time.


## Bottom Line


Most people who inherit cash won’t owe federal income taxes simply because they received an inheritance. However, taxes may still apply depending on the source of the funds. Other factors that determine whether tax is owed include whether state inheritance taxes are involved and how the money is invested or withdrawn after it’s inherited.
