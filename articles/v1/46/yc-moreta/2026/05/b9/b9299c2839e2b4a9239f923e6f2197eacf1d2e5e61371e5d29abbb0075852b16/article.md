# How to Remit Money to The Philippines

Sending money to the Philippines isn't a niche activity. It's one of the biggest financial flows on the planet. The country pulled in over[$38 billion in personal remittances in 2024](https://gulfnews.com/your-money/ofw-remittances-hit-record-high-3834-billion-in-2024-1.500038796) , with most of it coming from Overseas Filipino Workers wiring funds back to family. For millions of households, those transfers pay for groceries, school tuition, medical bills and rent.


Despite the size of the market, the actual experience of remitting to the Philippines still feels stuck in the past for a lot of senders. High fees, poor exchange rates, long waits and pointless trips to physical branches continue to dominate the space. The good news is that you have more options than you probably realize, and some of them are dramatically better than others.


Here's a breakdown of how people remit money to the Philippines today, what each method actually costs, and why Moreta Pay has become a go-to alternative for families and travelers alike.


### The Traditional Way: Remittance Centers and Wire Transfers


For decades, sending money to the Philippines meant walking into a Western Union, MoneyGram or Cebuana Lhuillier branch with a stack of cash. You'd fill out a form, hand over the money, pay a fee and text the tracking number to your relative. They'd then queue up at a branch on the receiving end to claim it.


It works. But it's slow, inconvenient and expensive. The[World Bank tracks the global average cost of sending $200 across borders](https://remittanceprices.worldbank.org/) , and the figure has been hovering around six percent for years. On a $500 transfer that adds up fast. International wire transfers from your bank can be even worse, often charging $25 to $50 in flat fees on top of a markup baked into the exchange rate.


Then there's the time cost. Standing in line, filling out paperwork, dealing with branch hours and showing ID at both ends of the transaction is a hassle that doesn't really fit modern life anymore.


### The Shift to Mobile Wallets: GCash and Maya


Over the last few years, the Philippines has gone through a rapid digital payments shift. GCash and Maya now sit on tens of millions of phones across the country. Filipinos use them to pay for groceries, jeepney rides, electricity bills and online shopping. They've quietly become the default way money moves around inside the country.


Naturally, this changed how remittances work too. Instead of sending cash to a branch for pickup, many overseas senders now top up a relative's GCash or Maya wallet directly. The recipient sees the funds in their app within minutes and can spend it without ever touching cash.


The catch? Funding those transfers from abroad usually still routes through one of the older remittance providers. So while the recipient experience is modern, the sender is often still paying outdated fees and getting hit with a poor exchange rate on the way in.
