# The Country That Killed Cash

The strangest payment I've ever made in China wasn't to a person. It was to a beggar on a pedestrian bridge in Shenzhen, sitting cross-legged behind a laminated QR code propped against a paper cup. No cash bowl. No coins. Just a printed square of black and white pixels, because he'd learned years ago that nobody walking past him was carrying money anymore.


That was 2019. People wrote articles about it like it was a curiosity. It wasn't. It was a preview of where the entire planet was heading, and China got there first by close to a decade.


Every QR payment story we've written on this blog —[Vietnam](https://moretapay.com/blog/why-qr-codes-took-over-vietnam) ,[Argentina](https://moretapay.com/blog/why-qr-codes-took-over-argentina) ,[Laos](https://moretapay.com/blog/how-laos-learned-to-scan) , Brazil's Pix — is, in some sense, a footnote to what happened in China between 2014 and 2018. So it's worth telling the original story properly. Not least because China, the country that invented all of this, remains the single trickiest place on earth for a foreigner to actually pay for lunch.


### It Started With a Red Envelope


The moment most people point to is Chinese New Year, 2014. WeChat, then just a messaging app, launched a digital version of the hongbao — the red envelope of cash that Chinese families gift each other over the holiday. To send one, you had to link a bank card. It was framed as a game: you could fire a lump sum into a group chat and let friends race to grab random shares of it.


Sixteen million envelopes went out in two days. Jack Ma, whose Alipay had spent a decade patiently building payment infrastructure for Taobao shoppers, called it a "Pearl Harbor attack" on his business. Overnight, Tencent had converted a chat app into a wallet, and the two biggest tech companies in China spent the next several years burning billions in subsidies fighting over who got to sit between Chinese consumers and their money.


The weapon they both chose was the QR code. Not NFC, not chip cards, not tap-to-pay terminals. A printed square that costs nothing to produce and works on a phone from 2012. Sound familiar? It's the exact playbook that[Southeast Asia ran a few years later](https://moretapay.com/blog/why-sea-skipped-credit-cards) — because China proved it worked at a scale nobody thought possible.


### Cards Never Stood a Chance


Here's the part that surprises Western visitors most: China didn't abandon credit cards. It never really had them.


In 2014, credit card penetration in China sat somewhere around 16%, mostly concentrated in a handful of tier-one cities. UnionPay had a monopoly on card processing, terminals were expensive, and the average shop owner in Chengdu had no reason to buy one. The country was running on cash — and cash in China meant counting out ¥100 notes, checking them for counterfeits (a genuine daily ritual; shopkeepers had UV lamps at the register), and hauling change.


So when Alipay and WeChat Pay showed up offering merchants a free piece of paper instead of a $300 terminal, and offering customers cashback just for scanning it, there was no incumbent to displace. The leap went straight from paper money to phone. By 2025, roughly 92% of urban transactions were flowing through Alipay or WeChat Pay, with cash and cards splitting the scraps. Street musicians take QR tips. Temples take QR donations. Wet market vendors selling a single fish take QR. There are noodle shops in Chongqing where the cash register is, functionally, decoration.


### The Super-App Lock-In


What made China's version different from everywhere that copied it is that the QR code was never the product. The app was.


WeChat isn't a payment app that added chat. It's the operating system of Chinese daily life — messaging, ride-hailing, utility bills, doctor appointments, train tickets, government services — with payments stitched through all of it. Alipay did the same from the commerce side. Order at a restaurant? Scan the code taped to your table, and the menu opens inside the app. Rent a power bank, unlock a share bike, pay your electricity bill — same app, same thumb.


That depth is why the habit never reversed. In Vietnam, VietQR is an open national rail any bank can plug into. China's system grew up as two privately-owned walled gardens, and for years the walls were the whole point. Which brings us to the problem.


### The Great Tourist Wall


For most of the last decade, China's payment miracle came with an asterisk the size of the Forbidden City: it didn't work for you.


Alipay and WeChat Pay both required a Chinese bank account and a Chinese phone number. Foreign cards wouldn't link. So travelers landed in the most cashless country on the planet carrying the one thing nobody wanted — cash — and discovered that vendors couldn't break a ¥100 note because their till had been empty since 2017. International cards worked at the Marriott and roughly nowhere else. It was a genuinely absurd situation: the society that killed cash was forcing its visitors to use it.


Beijing knew this was a bad look, especially heading into a post-pandemic tourism push. Starting in late 2023,[regulators leaned on both platforms to open up](https://www.reuters.com/technology/china-payment-giants-make-it-easier-foreigners-pay-2023-07-21/) , and the reforms have kept coming. As of 2026, foreign visitors can link Visa, Mastercard, Amex and JCB cards directly to both apps with passport verification, single-transaction limits have been raised to around $5,000, and cross-border fees are waived on transactions under ¥200 — which covers most meals, metro rides and market hauls.


It's dramatically better than it was. It's still not seamless. Verification can stall, banks flag the 3DS checks as fraud, some merchant codes only accept domestic wallets, and above that ¥200 line a 3% cross-border fee kicks in on top of whatever your card charges for the currency conversion. Most travelers end up setting up both apps and toggling between them when one throws the red error screen.


### What This Means If You're Visiting


If you're heading to China in 2026, internalize this before you board: the country runs on the scan, full stop. Taxis, street food, museums, convenience stores, temple donation boxes. Bring a modest amount of cash for the edge cases and a physical card for hotel deposits, but plan your daily spending around QR from day one.

China spent a decade building the future of payments and then, almost as an afterthought, made it nearly impossible for outsiders to join. The door is finally open. Walk through it prepared, and you'll spend your trip eating dumplings instead of hunting for an ATM that takes your card.


The beggar on the Shenzhen bridge figured out cashless before your bank did. No reason you should be the last one scanning.
