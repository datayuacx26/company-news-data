# 7 Challenges Faced by Frontline Workers (and What Employers Can Do)

Frontline worker challenges raise labor costs, leave shifts unfilled, and hurt service quality. Deskless workers make up a large share of the global workforce, so when that many people deal with burnout, unstable schedules, or stalled careers, the effects don’t stay inside HR. They surface as unfilled shifts, overtime spend, service quality slips, and replacement costs that compound across every location.


The sections below pair each challenge with one employer fix and the metrics HR or ops leaders can track by location.


## What are the biggest challenges frontline workers face?


[Frontline workers](https://www.fountain.com/frontline-hiring) are the employees who do their jobs away from a desk: store associates, drivers, warehouse staff, nurses and care aides, cooks, and cleaners. The biggest challenges these workers face are below, and managers usually see the problem first in absenteeism, turnover, uncovered shifts, or injury reports.


### 1. Burnout and mental health strain


[Employee burnout](https://www.fountain.com/posts/frontline-workers-retention-insights-2025) costs employers roughly


[$3,999 per year](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749379725000236) for every nonmanagerial hourly employee experiencing it, which turns burnout into a measurable labor-cost problem: each unresolved case adds absence, replacement, and productivity pressure to the location budget.


Physical demands and emotional labor are common contributors, and understaffing compounds them by leaving the remaining team to absorb extra hours. In operational data, it shows up as rising absenteeism and turnover risk.


### 2. Unpredictable scheduling and work-life conflict


Schedule instability is a


[strong turnover predictor](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/uncertain-time-precarious-schedules-and-job-turnover-in-the-u-s-service-sector) for hourly retail and food-service workers. Six-month turnover reached 39% for workers with less than 72 hours of schedule notice, against 24% for those with at least two weeks.


Last-minute changes and


[clopening shifts](https://www.fountain.com/posts/ai-shift-scheduling-tools) (closing one night, opening the next morning) erode sleep, trust, and eventually retention, which makes schedule notice a retention lever managers can actually control.


### 3. Pay pressure and perceived inequity


Pay pressure is acute on the frontline, where over half of frontline employees earn


[below $20 per hour](https://www.catalyst.org/insights/2025/frontline-employee-satisfaction-drivers-us) . The squeeze is mostly about cost of living: among workers dissatisfied with pay,


[80% told Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2024/12/10/job-satisfaction) their wages haven’t kept up with cost-of-living increases.


[Opaque pay bands](https://www.fountain.com/posts/understanding-pay-parity-of-the-hourly-worker) make that pressure harder to manage, because workers who can’t see how pay is set have less context for fairness conversations.


### 4. Lack of recognition and feeling undervalued


Recognition gaps make frontline work feel invisible, especially when employees absorb customer pressure, safety risk, or last-minute coverage without any feedback. The fix is structural, not a surface perk: recognition should be frequent, specific to the work, and visible to peers, so employees can connect it to what they actually did.


That kind of recognition comes from a manager’s weekly routine rather than an annual award.

### 5. Communication breakdowns and fragmented tools


Fragmented communication channels make it harder for frontline workers to get timely updates, raise issues, and stay aligned with managers. Shift updates may arrive by group text, printouts, and word of mouth, so a message meant for every worker reaches some late and others not at all.


When the routes are inconsistent, small problems get harder to resolve before they grow.


### 6. Safety risks and physical demands


Safety risks and physical demands leave frontline work more exposed to injuries, illness, and workplace violence.


[Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/osh.nr0.htm) data show health care and social assistance recorded 3.4 recordable injury and illness cases per 100 full-time workers in 2024, well above the 2.3-case average across private industry.


Fatal risk concentrates in frontline work too: transportation and material-moving occupations logged the most


[fatal work injuries](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cfoi.nr0.htm) of any occupational group, 1,391 in 2024, and healthcare workers face a higher risk of


[workplace violence](https://www.osha.gov/workplace-violence) , per OSHA.


Those rates make safety reporting, staffing levels, and hazard controls location-level operating metrics, because an injury removes a worker from the schedule and pushes overtime up.


### 7. Limited career growth and skill development


[Career development](https://fountain.com/posts/continuous-learning-the-essential-next-step-after-onboarding) affects retention directly:


[41% of frontline employees](https://www.catalyst.org/insights/2025/frontline-employee-satisfaction-drivers-us) cite lack of career advancement as a reason for wanting to leave. That makes promotion criteria and skill development a retention tool, not just an employee benefit, because unclear next steps push trained workers back into the hiring market.


Growth and pay also compound: workers who quit usually cite pay and advancement as co-leading reasons, so employers that treat compensation and career paths separately can miss the combined quit risk.


## What employers can do about each challenge


Each challenge has a fix a manager can start this week, plus the signal to watch by location. Consistency is the point: workers only feel these changes when they show up every week in their schedules, check-ins, recognition, and growth conversations.


- **Staff to real demand, and catch burnout before it becomes absence.** Staffing plans built on actual demand keep chronic understaffing from becoming the default, and short pulse check-ins at set milestones surface disengagement early. Continuous listening can flag missed check-ins, low scores, or repeated schedule complaints for a manager to act on, so risk shows up before the absences spike.


- **Publish schedules two weeks out and let workers swap from their phones.** Advance notice plus manager-approved mobile swaps directly attack the


[schedule instability](https://www.fountain.com/workforce-management-guide) tied to turnover. Agentic scheduling can draft coverage and flag gaps before a callout, with a manager approving every change, so coverage holds instead of collapsing into an uncovered shift.


- **Audit pay against local market rates, and show workers the bands.** Regular audits plus pay bands workers can actually see address both real and perceived inequity. Fairness conversations then rest on posted criteria and next-step requirements by role, not on anecdotes.


- **Make recognition a weekly manager habit, not an annual award.** Recognition lands when it’s frequent, tied to specific work, and visible to peers. Built into the weekly routine, it keeps good work from disappearing inside one-on-ones and lets peers and leadership see it.


- **Move every shift update to one mobile channel workers can reach.** A single channel for updates, reminders, and approved swaps replaces the patchwork of apps, printouts, and group texts. It also gives frontline staff the same direct line to leadership that corporate colleagues already have, so everyone gets the same version of the same update.


- **Match safety training to the sector’s real injury profile, and assign it before day one.** Hazard controls, role-specific training, and reporting tools workers use without fear of blame cut both incidents and the stress that compounds them. Clearing required training before the first shift keeps unprepared workers off the floor.


- **Post promotion criteria and put upskilling on workers’ phones.** When a frontline worker can see exactly what stands between them and the next role, and complete the steps from their phone, growth stops being guesswork. Pay conversations can then include a concrete next promotion step instead of ending at the wage.


None of these work as one-time gestures. Publishing schedules earlier reduces psychological distress and improves sleep, but that and every other fix only holds if managers run it every week.


## How to measure whether the fixes are working


Track four metrics monthly by location: turnover, first-90-day retention, cost-per-hire, and absenteeism. Reviewing them site by site each month shows which locations are losing people, spending more to replace them, or straining early, instead of surfacing companywide attrition after the month closes.


- **Turnover rate:** Track it monthly and by location, since variance between sites tells leaders where to look, whether the cause is manager practices, scheduling, or local market conditions.


- **First-90-day retention:** A dip here warns that the first-week experience needs attention before early attrition becomes a recurring vacancy cost.


- **Cost-per-hire:** SHRM’s 2025 benchmarking put the


[average cost-per-hire at $4,129](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/shrm-benchmarking-report-4129-average-cost-per-hire) , so every point of retention improvement carries a dollar value.


- **Absenteeism trends:** Rising unplanned absence is an early strain signal, and highly engaged teams show


[78% less absenteeism](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/321725/gallup-q12-meta-analysis-report.aspx) , which makes it a practical place for managers to intervene first.


Engagement shows up in the P&L: teams in the


[top engagement quartile](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/321725/gallup-q12-meta-analysis-report.aspx) run 23% more profitable than bottom-quartile teams, so the


[drivers of employee engagement](https://www.fountain.com/posts/drivers-of-employee-engagement) belong on the operating dashboard, not just in an annual HR review.

## Frequently asked questions about frontline worker challenges


### What are the top challenges frontline workers face?


The 7 most damaging are burnout, unpredictable scheduling, pay pressure, lack of recognition, fragmented communication, safety risks, and limited career growth. Research on


[schedule instability](https://shift.hks.harvard.edu/its-about-time-how-work-schedule-instability-matters-for-workers-families-and-racial-inequality) , pay, and career growth repeatedly points to those areas as major retention risks, so employers can treat the list as an operating checklist rather than a general engagement theme.


### What counts as a frontline worker?


A frontline worker is any employee whose job happens away from a desk: retail associates, drivers, warehouse workers, healthcare aides, cooks, and cleaners. Deskless workers make up a large share of the global labor force, so even small improvements in scheduling, communication, or retention can affect a meaningful share of labor cost and service reliability.


### Why is frontline turnover so high?


Frontline roles often combine conditions linked to quitting, including pay and advancement pressures alongside physical strain and few visible promotion paths. Schedule instability is also a strong turnover predictor for hourly retail and food-service workers, and risk climbs further when onboarding and early check-ins miss problems in a worker’s first weeks.
