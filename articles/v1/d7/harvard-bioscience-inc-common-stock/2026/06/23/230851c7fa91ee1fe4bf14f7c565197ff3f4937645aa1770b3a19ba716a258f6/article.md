# Seeing the Invisible: Advanced Electrochemical Microscopy Captures Live Cancer Cells Sharing Mitochondria

Pancreatic cancer is notoriously hard to treat, and part of the reason may be that tumor cells help each other survive chemotherapy. A new study from[HEKA ElProScan](https://heka.com/products/products_main.html#echem_secm) 's power users at Xi'an Jiaotong University, published in \[ *Anal. Chem. 2025, 97, 49, 27418–27426* \] used two complementary scanning probe microscopy techniques to observe this survival strategy unfold in real time, inside living cells.


The researchers treated PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cells with the chemotherapy drug doxorubicin, which triggered the formation of tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) — thin, fragile membrane bridges, mostly 300–800 nm in diameter, that connect neighboring cells. TNTs are notoriously delicate, easily damaged by fluorescent labels or chemical fixation, which has made it hard to study them as they actually form and function. To get around this, the team turned to label-free scanning probe methods.


First, they used[scanning ion conductance microscopy (SICM)](https://www.heka.com/products/products_main.html#echem_secm) technique with a 26-nanometer nanopipette probe, operated in non-contact "hopping mode," to map the 3D topography of TNTs as they grew and broke apart — without ever touching the fragile structures. This revealed a previously undocumented four-step TNT life cycle: initial cell-cell contact, formation, elongation, and cleavage (marked by a distinctive Y-shaped structure) — along with traveling membrane bulges that tracked mitochondria being shuttled between cells.


Next, the team switched to[scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM)](https://www.heka.com/products/products_main.html#echem_secm) technique, carefully inserting a 100-nm platinum/carbon nanoelectrode into individual living cells to measure intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) in real time. Cell-viability staining confirmed this caused no significant harm. The results were striking: doxorubicin-treated cells linked by TNTs showed a dramatically lower ROS-related charge signal (0.66 pC) compared to similarly treated cells lacking TNT connections (5.87 pC) — nearly a nine fold difference — suggesting that TNT-mediated mitochondria transfer helps stressed cells offload oxidative damage to, or import healthy mitochondria from, their neighbors.


### What do these findings mean for cancer research?


Together, the findings suggest pancreatic cancer cells use TNTs as a kind of cellular mutual-aid network, helping each other resist the oxidative stress chemotherapy is designed to inflict — a potential contributor to treatment resistance. Disrupting TNT formation or blocking mitochondrial transfer could be a promising way to make existing chemotherapies hit harder.


### SICM and SECM's use in cancer research and capturing dynamic cellular processes


Just as notably, the work showcases how pairing SICM's nanoscale topographic imaging with SECM's electrochemical sensing — exemplified here by the ElProScan platform — can capture dynamic, living cellular processes that conventional microscopy simply can't reach without destroying the very structures it's trying to observe. This novel research opens new avenues for applications extending beyond cancer into neurodegenerative diseases and cellular aging.
