## How to Protect Margins from FX Risk Using Budget Rates and Hedging


Although **accrual accounting** has been the **standard** since the[Italian Renaissance](https://www.accaglobal.com/us/en/study-with-acca/blog/how-humans-invented-accounting.html) , its application can miss the impact of foreign exchange (FX) movements have on financial performance, and, and like life, it all comes down to timing.


Revenue and expense recognition criteria dictate when forecasted transactions are recorded on financial statements. That means the impact FX movements have also doesn’t ‘show up’ on financial statements until these recognition criteria are met.


**In practice** , FX risk can impact margins and cash flow **long before** it appears in financial statements.


Earlier, we explored **how FX flows through and is captured on financial statements** .


Following the process chronologically, much of the impact FX movements have on a business's financial performance happens before the first invoice, purchase order, and corresponding asset or liability are recorded. This **early-stage exposure** is often referred to as forecast FX exposure or budget FX risk.


**If this is the case** , how do finance departments and FP&A teams identify and visualize the materiality of this FX risk?


---


## Why Budgeting Is the Foundation of FX Risk Management


**The budget is the baseline** .


Until something is recorded, it can’t be **measured** —and when transactions are recorded, or recognized, they are governed by the accounting standards applied.


That being said, currency movements affecting future sales, costs, or cash flows **don't** typically trigger accounting entries until a transaction occurs. Fundamentally, an FX exposure is not going to be captured in financial statements until after an expense or revenue in foreign currency is recognized. This creates a **gap** between economic FX exposure and accounting recognition.


In fact, without a budgeting process that includes explicit FX budget rates, there is **no way** to capture margin erosion from FX or cash flow volatility. **It simply results in** lower revenue, higher expenses, and less predictable cash flows.


Nearly **every year** I encounter businesses with nine-figure toplines and seven-figure margin erosion from FX movements. These businesses simply did not know how much FX exposure they had in their business and how **badly** it impacted them, other than having a vague understanding that they didn't do as well as they wanted to because their FX exposure was not properly measured through budget assumptions.


This is why budgeting and cash forecasts that contain explicit FX budget rates are **essential** for finance teams. It creates the yardstick to decompose variances driven from FX movements versus other factors.


**Budget FX rates** serve as **the benchmark** for measuring FX rate variance and protecting operating margins. As shown below, decomposing budgets versus actuals generates powerful insights in highlighting the factors that drive a business’ financial performance. FX rates are one of these factors.

## How to Measure FX Rate Variance: Budget vs. Actual Analysis


Going back to **Managerial Accounting 101** , we know that many FP&A teams apply a Price-Volume-Mix decomposition of their variance-to-actual to capture the variables that are impacting their financial performance.


**FX rates** are simply **another component** of this process, which is why setting budget FX rates, as outlined in the earlier piece, is a critical component of effective FX risk management. Once those budgeted rates are applied to budgets and cash forecasts, FP&A teams can apply the below.


### What Is Constant Currency Reporting?


**Constant Currency Reporting** records the actual results using the budgeted FX rate. This reveals how the business performed operationally (e.g., did we sell more units?).


**Constant currency analysis** removes FX volatility to isolate true operational performance.


This shows what results would have been had FX movements not occurred: the **‘true’** operational performance of the business.


### What Is FX Rate Variance?


**FX Rate Variance** compares actual results at *actual* rates versus actual results at *budgeted* rates. This isolates the exact amount that currency volatility reduced or boosted your margins.


This calculation directly quantifies the **financial impact of foreign exchange risk on profitability** . This is simply the realized result. You can extract this information directly from your accounting/ERP system and compare it to the budget results.


Applying the same process to cash forecasts to assess their accuracy will also reveal **how FX movements can impact the volatility of cash flow.**


This is particularly important for treasury teams managing liquidity risk and debt obligations, especially in heavily levered firms or those in workout situations.


### FX Variance Formula: How to Calculate FX Impact


The **variance formula** is quite simple in both instances:


*FX Impact = (Actual Rate − Budget Rate) × Foreign Currency Amount*


This formula **provides a direct measurement** of how exchange rate movements impact margins, revenue, and cash flow.

## Managing FX Risk Through Hedging Strategies


As highlighted earlier, **spot FX rates can be quite volatile** , and even developed market currencies can expect high single-digit annual movements. This math is unforgiving. FX variances can easily be much larger than other variance drivers that can result in unemployment for the managers involved.


While explicitly **including** FX rates in the budgeting process will help businesses uncover the source and materiality of FX risk in their business, it is not a panacea. **Budgeting** allows businesses to measure FX risk, but the exposure itself remains. **Hedging** is the primary tool used to actively manage and reduce that risk.

## FAQs


**What is accrual accounting?**


Accrual accounting is the recognition of an expense or revenue and corresponding balance sheet item at the time it is incurred – not when cash changes hands.


The most common example is the recognition of revenue from a sale, with payment terms, while cash has not been received from the sale. The sale is recognized on the income statement but because cash has not been received from the sale a corresponding accounts receivable asset is created.


The subsequent receipt of cash reduces the accounts receivable balance while cash increases. Both items are working capital items which are subject to remeasurement, in future periods, to account for how changes in FX rates effect their valuation, if these items are denominated in a different currency, then the reporting currency of the accounts.


The challenge accrual accounting presents for FX risk is that the impact **FX rate variations have** on forecasted transactions **is not recorded** because those transactions are not recognized until they occur.


**What is FX risk in budgeting?**


FX risk in budgeting is the potential for exchange rate movements to cause actual revenue, expenses, and margins to differ from planned budget assumptions. When companies set budgets using specific FX rates, any deviation from those rates creates variances that directly impact financial performance.


**What is constant currency reporting?**


Constant currency reporting is a method that measures financial performance using budgeted or prior-period exchange rates instead of actual rates. This isolates operational performance by removing the impact of currency volatility.


**How do FP&A teams measure FX impact?**


FP&A teams measure FX impact by comparing actual financial results at budget rates versus actual exchange rates. This process isolates FX rate variance and separates currency effects from operational performance.


**How can companies protect margins from FX risk?**


Companies protect margins from FX risk by identifying exposures in budgets and implementing hedging strategies such as forwards or cash flow hedges. These tools help stabilize cash flow and reduce earnings volatility.


**What is the formula to calculate FX impact?**


The formula to calculate FX impact is (Actual Rate – Budget Rate) × Foreign Currency Amount. This calculation shows the direct financial effect of exchange rate changes.


**What is forecast FX exposure?**


Forecast FX exposure, also called budget FX risk, is the potential impact of exchange rate movements on future revenues, costs, or cash flows that have not yet been recorded as accounting transactions. It is measured through the budgeting process using explicit FX budget rates.
