# How to Choose the Right Payroll and HR Service Model as Your Company Grows

Growth-stage companies often evaluate payroll and HR vendors before settling on a service model. The setup that fits today, whether self-service software, a bureau relationship, or a partial bundle, often breaks at the next inflection, such as a multi-state hire, a benefits launch, or a compliance notice. The vendor is rarely the problem. The model is.


That choice does not have to be a guess, and it does not have to be permanent. Whether the question in front of you is how to outsource payroll for small business operations for the first time, where to find solid payroll services for startups, or whether it is finally time to move to a fully managed outsourced payroll service instead of self-service, the underlying decision is the same one this article walks through. Asure runs both ends of that spectrum on one platform. With AsureCentral, you and your team run payroll, HR, benefits, and compliance yourselves, with one login, shared data, and full visibility into every payroll cycle. With AsureWorks, Asure specialists run payroll and handle day-to-day HR administration for you, with no co-employment, so you remain the employer of record and keep your own choice of broker, benefits, and retirement partners. It is one platform, two ways to work, so the model you choose now will not force a system change later.


## The Service Model Decision Comes First, Not the Vendor Decision


When you start comparing payroll and HR vendors on per-employee pricing, feature lists, and brand names you already recognize, you are already several steps into a decision you have not fully made yet. The choice that actually determines whether your setup holds up over time, self-service, hybrid, or fully managed, tends to get answered by default, not on purpose.


Three service models exist across payroll and HR, and each carries a different operating assumption.


- Run it yourself through self-service software, where your team handles payroll, HR, benefits administration, and compliance tasks directly inside one platform, and the vendor provides the system rather than the labor.
- Blend the two through a hybrid model, keeping the platform while adding selective vendor-delivered services, such as a compliance review or benefits coordination, for the areas that carry the most risk.
- Hand off execution through a fully managed model, where a vendor's own specialists run payroll processing, tax filing, and routine HR administration on your behalf, while you retain decision authority over people and policy.


If you are like most growth-stage operators, you picked your current setup based on what was in front of you at the time, current headcount, current budget, current internal bandwidth. That is a reasonable starting point, but none of those three inputs stays fixed. A team of 15 without a dedicated HR hire looks very different at 45 employees running payroll across four states with an open enrollment period on the calendar. The model that fit your company's first version is rarely the model its next version needs. Reworking that choice after the fact typically requires migrating payroll history, rebuilding integrations, and retraining your entire team, which is considerably more disruptive than choosing deliberately the first time.


Reversing the order also carries a hidden cost beyond the eventual re-platform. Comparing vendors before settling on a model means comparing a self-service platform's per-employee price against a fully managed provider's bundled service fee, without a shared basis for what you are actually paying for. That comparison rarely produces a clear winner, since it tends to reward whichever number looks smallest on a pricing page rather than reflecting what you are actually paying for.


Asure built its own platform around this order directly. AsureCentral and AsureWorks run on the same underlying payroll and HR system, offering two ways to operate it rather than two competing products aimed at different buyers. You can run payroll and HR yourself inside AsureCentral, with one login, shared data, and role-based access across payroll, HR, tax, benefits, time, and recruiting, plus Luna AI embedded in the platform to answer questions and flag issues, always with human review, never operating on its own. Or you can have Asure specialists run payroll and day-to-day HR administration through AsureWorks, which operates without a PEO co-employment arrangement. The model question is the one Asure designed its own platform around, precisely because vendor selection is downstream of it, not the other way around.


Getting the service model right is harder than it looks, though, because the correct answer changes. It shifts as your company adds states, adds headcount, adds benefits complexity, and adds compliance exposure, and a model that was the right call at the start can quietly stop being the right call well before anyone notices.


## Three Patterns That Drive Payroll and HR Re-Platforming


Across payroll and HR buying decisions, three recurring patterns explain most forced provider or model changes. Each has a predictable trigger, surfacing at a specific operational event rather than emerging gradually over time.


The first pattern is premature full-outsourcing. A growth-stage company with light payroll and HR complexity decides to outsource payroll services entirely, handing the whole function to an outside provider before building any internal literacy in how payroll, tax filing, or HR administration actually works. That is not automatically a mistake, but it becomes one the first time something goes wrong and no one inside the company can evaluate whether the provider handled it correctly. The trigger is usually the first compliance notice, an IRS deposit penalty letter or a state unemployment insurance discrepancy, something that requires the employer to understand what happened well enough to confirm the fix. The IRS's Failure to Deposit penalty illustrates why this matters. A late federal payroll tax deposit is penalized at two percent if it is one to five days late, five percent for six to 15 days late, 10 percent beyond 15 days late or if the deposit was made using the wrong method, and 15 percent if the amount is still unpaid more than 10 days after the IRS issues its first notice demanding payment ([IRS.gov, "Failure to Deposit Penalty"](https://www.irs.gov/payments/failure-to-deposit-penalty) ). Those tiers do not stack, but the jump between them means a company that cannot audit what its provider filed, and when, stays exposed longer than it realizes. This is one reason AsureWorks pairs managed execution with a platform the client can still see into. Even when Asure specialists are running payroll and filing taxes, the client is not locked out of the underlying records.


The second pattern is under-scoped self-service. A company selects a self-service platform assuming an internal capacity that does not actually exist yet, such as a person who owns payroll, a documented process, and someone who understands multi-state withholding. This works fine until the first out-of-state hire. Multi-state payroll introduces overtime exemption thresholds that vary by jurisdiction. The federal Fair Labor Standards Act salary threshold for the executive, administrative, and professional exemption is $684 per week ($35,568 per year), effective May 15, 2026, but several states set their own, higher thresholds for the same exemption test, including California at $1,352 per week and Washington at roughly $1,541.70 per week for large employers, both state figures current as of 2026 and subject to change in future years ([U.S. Department of Labor and Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/15/2026-09839/defining-and-delimiting-the-exemptions-for-executive-administrative-professional-outside-sales-and) ). A self-service setup that was never configured to track jurisdiction-specific thresholds tends to fail quietly, surfacing months later as a misclassified employee or a missed filing rather than as an obvious, immediate error. This is the gap Asure HR Compliance is built to close for companies running on AsureCentral. Certified HR professionals review classification and multi-state exposure before it becomes a filing problem, without requiring the company to hire that expertise internally.


The third pattern is bundling blind spots. A company selects a bundled payroll-plus-HR-plus-benefits provider on the strength of the payroll piece, without evaluating the depth of the HR or benefits administration behind it. The trigger is usually the first open enrollment period or the first benefits dispute, the moment a bundled provider's thin HR bench becomes visible. For applicable large employers, the operational stakes around benefits are concrete and dated. Form 1095-C must be furnished to full-time employees by March 2, 2026, for the 2025 tax year, and filed electronically with the IRS by March 31, 2026 ([IRS.gov, Instructions for Forms 1094-C and 1095-C](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i109495c) ). Employers that fail to offer minimum essential coverage to enough full-time employees face an Employer Shared Responsibility Payment of $3,340 per full-time employee for 2026, calculated after the first 30 employees are excluded from the count. A separate penalty of $5,010 per full-time employee applies specifically when a full-time employee receives subsidized coverage through a Health Insurance Marketplace because the employer's own coverage was unaffordable or failed to provide minimum value, not to every full-time employee whenever coverage is judged inadequate ([IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-26](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-26.pdf) ). Both figures are current as of tax year 2026 and adjust annually. Asure keeps benefits coordination inside the same connected system as payroll and HR administration rather than as a separate bundled add-on, whether a company runs that system itself through AsureCentral or has Asure specialists manage it through AsureWorks, so a benefits question does not require piecing together records across disconnected tools.


These three patterns share a root cause. In each case, the service-model decision was made once, at a single point in time, without accounting for where the company was headed next. Recognizing which pattern is closest to your own situation right now is usually enough to tell you which of the three service models actually fits, before the next trigger event forces the question.


## Why the Right Service Model Changes as Your Company Grows


Your internal capacity, your compliance complexity, and your growth trajectory move independently, and together they determine the right service model far more than your company's industry or size does. Your company can look identical on an org chart at two different points in its life and need a completely different model both times.


Early on, if you are a small team with no dedicated HR or payroll resource, often the owner, a bookkeeper, or an office manager handling it, you can run payroll and HR reasonably well inside a self-service platform, because the volume and complexity are still low enough for one person to hold in their head. This is also the stage where companies searching for payroll services for startups usually land, since the complexity that would justify a fully managed model has not shown up yet. At this stage, self-service software tends to be the right fit rather than a compromise forced by budget.


As you add headcount, states, or benefits programs, your compliance complexity tends to grow faster than your internal capacity does. If you are approaching thresholds that trigger new state and federal obligations, such as additional withholding jurisdictions, applicable large employer status, and expanded leave requirements, you may still have the same one or two people handling payroll and HR that you had a year earlier. The work itself has grown at a faster rate than your team has, even if the org chart looks similar to a year ago. This is the point where a hybrid model, keeping your platform but adding specialist support for the areas that now carry real compliance risk, tends to outperform either pure self-service or a full handoff.


Later still, you may have some internal HR or payroll capacity in place, but that capacity is stretched thin across a wider set of responsibilities than it was built for. Here, the work is less about replacing internal capability and more about extending it. Fully managed support, where a vendor's specialists take over defined operational work while your internal staff focus on higher-value decisions, tends to fit this stage better than either extreme.


Demand for this kind of support keeps climbing as more companies choose to outsource payroll services in some form rather than build every function in-house. The global market for payroll outsourcing was valued at $12.44 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $17.83 billion by 2031, a compound annual growth rate of 6.19 percent (Mordor Intelligence, "Payroll Outsourcing Market"). That growth is one more reason the service-model decision deserves the same deliberate attention as any other vendor choice, rather than being left to whatever setup a company happened to start with.


None of this has to force a system change at each stage. That is the practical case for a single platform with more than one operating mode. You can run entirely inside AsureCentral early on, add Asure HR Compliance support as complexity increases, and shift some or all payroll and HR execution to AsureWorks later, all without moving your data to a new system or retraining your team on a new interface. What changes is the model you are running, not the underlying platform.


## How to Match Your Service Model to Your Current Stage


Matching your service model to your actual stage, rather than to your org chart or your budget alone, starts with a short set of diagnostic questions.


- Count how many people currently touch payroll or HR administration in a given cycle, and confirm whether that knowledge lives anywhere besides one person's memory.
- Identify how many states your company employs people in today, and how many you expect to add within the next year.
- Determine how close your headcount is to common compliance inflection points, including applicable large employer status for ACA reporting purposes.
- Assess whether your current benefits program is administered inside the same system as payroll, or pieced together across separate tools.
- Map out what happens, specifically, if the person who currently owns payroll leaves your company next month.


Your answers point toward a model. If your internal capacity is real and documented, and your compliance complexity is low to moderate, self-service software fits, and running payroll and HR yourself inside AsureCentral keeps you in full control with one connected system rather than a patchwork of spreadsheets and point tools. If your compliance complexity is rising faster than your internal capacity, whether that shows up as multiple states, an approaching enrollment period, or headcount nearing a reporting threshold, a hybrid approach fits. Keep your platform, and add support such as Asure HR Compliance for the specific areas carrying the most risk. If your internal capacity is minimal, already stretched thin, or effectively a single point of failure, fully managed support fits, and AsureWorks lets Asure specialists run payroll and handle day-to-day HR administration while you remain the employer of record.


These questions rarely produce a unanimous answer on the first pass. Finance may look at headcount and budget and see plenty of room; whoever is actually running payroll day to day may see a process that lives in one spreadsheet and one person's memory. When the answers pull in different directions, weight the compliance-complexity signal over the capacity signal. A payroll or HR gap that goes unnoticed for a quarter is rarely expensive to fix. A compliance gap that goes unnoticed for a quarter can trigger the kind of IRS or state penalties described above, and those get more expensive the longer they sit undetected. That asymmetry is why leaning toward more support, not less, tends to be the safer read whenever the diagnostic questions do not point cleanly toward one model.


Answer these questions on a set cadence rather than once and then forgetting them. Revisit them at least once a year, and again immediately after any of the trigger events described earlier, a new state hire, an approaching open enrollment period, or a compliance notice, instead of waiting for the next budget cycle to force the conversation. Growth-stage companies rarely stay in one model long enough for the original answers to still hold up as complexity increases.


It is worth being explicit about why these particular triggers (a compliance notice, a multi-state hire, an open enrollment event) anchor this framework, rather than a list of vendor features. Each one maps to a real regulatory deadline or operational threshold, not a marketing category. A missed federal tax deposit has a defined, published penalty structure. A multi-state hire has a defined, published wage-and-hour threshold that differs by state. An applicable large employer has a defined, published filing deadline and penalty exposure. Building the framework around those thresholds, instead of around which platform has the most modern interface, is what makes it hold up regardless of which provider you eventually choose.


## Choosing a Model You Won't Have to Replace


The cost of getting the service model wrong shows up less in the software or the vendor you picked and more in the disruption that follows, pulling payroll history into a new platform, rebuilding integrations, and training your team on unfamiliar workflows, all while payroll still has to run correctly every cycle during the transition. That disruption is the real price of choosing on price or brand recognition alone, without first asking what model your company's current and near-term complexity actually calls for.


The alternative is choosing a platform built to hold more than one operating mode, rather than trying to predict the future perfectly, so you are not choosing a vendor and a model at the same time, and you are not locked into either once chosen. That is the specific problem AsureCentral and AsureWorks solve together. Both run on the same underlying system, so you can start on AsureCentral, running payroll and HR yourself with full visibility into every record, add Asure HR Compliance support as complexity rises, or shift execution to AsureWorks specialists as your internal capacity gets stretched, without a system migration or a re-platform. AsureWorks itself remains a fully managed outsourced payroll service, not a PEO. There is no co-employment, you stay the employer of record, and you keep your own choice of benefits, broker, and retirement partners rather than being pushed into a bundled package.


The service-model decision deserves the same deliberate attention you already give to choosing a bank or an accountant, because it carries similar long-term weight. Get it right, and you spend less time reconciling errors, less time explaining a compliance notice, and less time wondering whether your current setup will survive its next stage of growth. Get it wrong, and the bill comes due later, usually at the least convenient moment, such as a multi-state hire, an open enrollment period, or a compliance notice that lands on your desk with no one able to explain what happened.


Start with the five questions in the section above, and answer them honestly for where your company is today, not where it was when you first set up payroll. If the answers point toward running things yourself, AsureCentral gives you one connected system for payroll, HR, benefits, and compliance.
