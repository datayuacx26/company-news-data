Wildfires have become[more frequent, more intense, and larger](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/explore/wildfires-and-climate-change/) as the planet warms, creating a growing crisis for both outdoor environments and indoor air quality (IAQ). While most attention is placed on the immediate threats of flames and evacuations, their effects often extend far beyond the burn zone. Fine smoke particles and airborne pollutants can infiltrate homes and linger for months, or even years, after the smoke has cleared, making IAQ an important consideration for homeowners during wildfire season and beyond.


## Understanding Wildfire Smoke and Its Effects on the Home


Wildfire smoke isn’t just made up of charred wood and plant matter; it is a[complex mixture](https://www.epa.gov/wildfire-smoke-course/wildfire-smoke-complex-mixture) composed of water vapor and numerous pollutants, including, gases, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and particulate matter (PM), some of which have been associated with[irritation of the eyes, nose, and throat](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) , and other adverse health effects depending on the type and level of exposure.


According to the[Environmental Protection Agency](https://www.epa.gov/emergencies-iaq/wildfires-and-indoor-air-quality-iaq) (EPA), wildfire smoke can enter through open windows and doors, HVAC systems, and even small openings, joists, and cracks. This makes indoor air management not just a short-term need, but a long-term priority. It is also why more homeowners are searching for air filters that may help reduce the impact of wildfire smoke and improve IAQ during a wildfire event, while also addressing everyday air quality concerns such as pollution, dust, allergens, and other common contaminants, and looking to adopt HVAC strategies for poor air quality that can help their homes year-round.


HVAC systems play a vital role when it comes to defending indoor air from the impacts of wildfire smoke, but they must be set up, operated, and managed properly. To minimize exposure to harmful wildfire smoke indoors, homeowners should focus on three essential strategies: source control, ventilation management, and filtration.


## Source control: four ways to keep wildfire smoke out of the home


The first step is preventing smoke and particulates from entering the home. During active wildfires or periods of drifting smoke, outdoor air can carry fine particles that are difficult to remove once inside. To minimize their entry and help reduce the burden on HVAC systems and filtration setups, homeowners should:


1. Close windows and doors tightly during wildfire events.
2. Seal air leaks around windows, doors, and other openings with weather stripping or caulk.
3. Limit the use of kitchen and bathroom exhaust fans, as well as other fresh air ventilation systems that can draw contaminated air in.
4. Avoid indoor activities that generate additional pollutants, such as burning candles or using gas stoves without ventilation.


While blocking smoke and pollutants from entering the home is critical, it’s equally important to rethink how the home brings in and circulates air during a wildfire.

## Frequently Asked Questions:


**How does wildfire smoke affect indoor air quality (IAQ)?**
Wildfire smoke contains a[complex mixture](https://www.epa.gov/wildfire-smoke-course/wildfire-smoke-complex-mixture) composed of water vapor and numerous pollutants, including, gases, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and particulate matter (PM), some of which have been associated with[irritation of the eyes, nose, and throat](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality) , and other adverse health effects depending on the type and level of exposure. It can also enter through open windows and doors, HVAC systems, and even small openings, joists, and cracks.


**What HVAC strategies help reduce wildfire smoke indoors?**
Homeowners can reduce exposure by focusing on three key HVAC strategies: source control (sealing the home and limiting outdoor air intake), ventilation management (pausing fresh-air systems during poor outdoor air quality), and high-efficiency filtration (capturing fine particles once they are indoors).


**What type of air filter works best for wildfire smoke?**
During smoky periods, upgrading to an HVAC filter rated[MERV 13 or higher](https://www.epa.gov/wildfire-smoke-course/preparing-smoke-and-heat) is recommended to effectively remove fine particle pollution from smoke in the indoor air. Check filters during wildfires and replace them as needed afterward by following the manufacturer's recommendations and consulting an HVAC professional if assistance is required.


**Should ventilation systems be turned off during wildfire smoke events?**
In many cases, yes. When outdoor air quality is detrimental, mechanical ventilation systems that bring in outside air may introduce additional pollutants indoors. Temporarily disabling these systems and resuming ventilation once air quality improves can help limit indoor exposure to wildfire smoke.
