# What Is HACCP? A Plain-English Guide for Operators

Most operators meet HACCP the hard way: an inspector asks to see the plan, and someone starts digging through a binder nobody has opened since the last manager left. It doesn't have to be like that.


> **HACCP in one sentence:** HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) is a written food-safety system that finds the points in your process where food can become unsafe and puts specific controls in place to stop it. It's the logic behind almost every clean health inspection.


HACCP isn't paperwork for its own sake. It's a way of thinking about your kitchen that keeps people from getting sick — written down so anyone on the line can follow it, and so you can prove it happened. It started in the 1960s as a collaboration between Pillsbury, NASA, and the U.S. Army to keep astronauts from getting food poisoning in space, and it's since become the framework the[FDA builds retail food safety around.](https://www.fda.gov/food/hazard-analysis-critical-control-point-haccp)


## What does HACCP stand for?


Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points. Break it in half and it explains itself:


- **Hazard Analysis** — figure out what could make your food unsafe.
- **Critical Control Points** — find the specific steps where you can stop those hazards, and control them every time.


Everything else in HACCP is the machinery that makes those two ideas repeatable across every shift and every store.


## What are the three types of hazards?


Before you can control hazards, you have to know what you're looking for. HACCP groups them three ways:


1. **Biological** — bacteria, viruses, parasites. Raw poultry, unwashed produce, a sick employee. This is where most foodborne illness comes from.
2. **Chemical** — cleaning agents, sanitizer residue, allergens treated as a chemical hazard.
3. **Physical** — anything that shouldn't be in the food: a metal shaving from a worn slicer, glass, a bit of packaging.


Most of your day-to-day risk is biological, which is why so much of HACCP comes down to time and temperature. (If you want the foundation there, start with[what counts as a TCS food](https://www.transact-tech.com/resources/blog/what-are-tcs-foods) and the[temperature danger zone](https://www.transact-tech.com/resources/blog/temperature-danger-zone) .)


## What are the 7 principles of HACCP?


This is the heart of it. The[FDA lays out seven principles](https://www.fda.gov/food/hazard-analysis-critical-control-point-haccp/haccp-principles-application-guidelines) , and they run in order — each one builds on the last. Here they are in kitchen terms.


Principle


What it actually means


Kitchen example


Conduct a hazard analysis Walk your menu and process; find where a hazard could get in Raw chicken cross-contaminating a prep surface


Determine critical control points (CCPs) Find the steps where you can actually stop or reduce the hazard The cook step; the cooling step


Establish critical limits Set the exact number that separates safe from unsafe Cook poultry to 165°F


Establish monitoring Decide who checks, how, and how often Probe the thickest part of every chicken batch


Establish corrective actions Decide what happens when a limit is missed — and record it Re-cook or discard; log what you did


Establish verification Prove the system is working Calibrate thermometers; a manager reviews logs


Establish recordkeeping Keep the documentation that proves all of the above Temperature logs, corrective action logs

## What's the difference between HACCP and a food safety plan?


People use the terms loosely, but they aren't the same thing.


**HACCP** is the specific, hazard-based system with the seven principles above. A broader **food safety management** **system** wraps HACCP together with your prerequisite programs — supplier controls, sanitation, personal hygiene, pest control. The FDA's model[Food Code](https://www.fda.gov/food/retail-food-protection/fda-food-code) leans on the idea of "active managerial control," which is really just HACCP thinking applied to daily operations instead of living in a static document.


The short version for a multi-unit operator: **the seven principles are the engine; the prerequisite programs are the chassis.** You need both.


## Do restaurants legally need a HACCP plan?


It depends on what you do. A full, formal HACCP plan is federally required for specific operations — juice, seafood, and USDA-regulated meat and poultry processing. Most retail restaurants aren't *required* to hold one unless they do something a health department flags as specialized: reduced-oxygen packaging (sous vide, vacuum-sealing), curing, smoking for preservation, or acidifying rice for sushi. Those usually trigger a **HACCP plan variance** from your local authority.


But here's the thing: whether it's legally mandatory or not, running your operation on HACCP logic is how you pass inspections and how you survive an outbreak investigation with your brand intact. The plan is the point, not the paperwork.


## How do you run HACCP across many locations?


This is where it gets real for anyone responsible for more than one kitchen. A plan that lives in one good manager's head doesn't scale, and a plan that's different at every store is impossible to audit. Three things make it work across an estate:


- **Standardize the process, not just the recipe.** The critical limits and monitoring steps should be identical whether the store is in Toledo or Tucson. That's what lets a regional director compare stores honestly.


- **Make monitoring hard to skip and easy to prove.** Paper logs get "pencil-whipped" — filled in from memory at the end of a shift. A check that records itself in the moment removes both the temptation and the doubt.


- **Give HQ a live view.** The whole reason to run a system is that someone above store level can see when a limit gets blown and whether the corrective action actually happened. Without that, you have 40 binders and no visibility.

## The bottom line


HACCP isn't exotic. It's hazard analysis, a handful of control points, the right limits, honest monitoring, and a record you can defend. The operators who sleep well are the ones who made those things automatic — at every location, not just the flagship.

## Frequently asked questions


**Is HACCP the same as ServSafe?**


No. ServSafe is a food-safety training and certification program from the National Restaurant Association. HACCP is the underlying system that training teaches you to run.


**What are the 7 principles of HACCP?**


Conduct a hazard analysis; determine critical control points; establish critical limits; establish monitoring; establish corrective actions; establish verification; and establish recordkeeping.


**How often should a HACCP plan be reviewed?**


At least annually, and any time you change a menu item, a process, a supplier, or equipment that affects a critical control point.


**Does a small or single-unit restaurant need HACCP?**


The principles apply at any size — a single kitchen can run the same seven-principle logic on a one-page plan. Whether a formal, written plan is legally required depends on your processes and your local health department.
