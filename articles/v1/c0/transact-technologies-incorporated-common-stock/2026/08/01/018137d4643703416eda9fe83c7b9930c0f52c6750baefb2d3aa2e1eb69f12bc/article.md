# Catering & Commissary: The New Back-of-House Growth Engine

## Can your back-of-house scale catering and commissary orders without adding another system?


For a long time, catering was something a lot of operators did on the side. A few trays for lunch at the office down the street, an order for graduation parties in June. Nice to have, but not exactly a growth engine.


That's changed. Catering, commissary production, and grab-and-go have quietly become some of the most lucrative growth channels in foodservice — and the operators who treat them that way are pulling ahead. Recent trade reporting from Nation’s Restaurant News shows catering emerging as a genuine revenue engine for some of the country's largest chains, with catering average unit volumes running several times higher than a typical mealtime transaction. Workplace demand is holding, too: catering research covered by Food On Demand found that 91% of workplaces plan to spend the same or more on food this year – up from 82% in 2024–and one in five plan to bump their food spending by more than 25%.


Here's the part that doesn't make the headlines. The technology most operators bought to chase that growth was built for the front of house — taking orders and routing them. Saying yes to that demand is exactly the right call and is how a counter quietly grows into a real program. But an order booked isn’t an order fulfilled, and the setup behind the counter was never built to scale production, food safety, and labeling all at once. Nail those three and the catering program starts to compound on itself; leave them running on memory and sticky notes, and it buckles under its own success.


## Why catering exposes a back-of-house problem


When a catering order comes in, the hard part isn't accepting orders. It's filling those orders safely, labeling them correctly, and getting them out the door without an allergen error or a missing date code — at a volume the kitchen wasn't staffed for on an ordinary Tuesday.


So what now? Many operators need a quick solution and start bolting things on. A standalone system for specialty catering labels. A manual workaround for large-batch prep. Extra staff hours spent on tasks crutching up a process a single-transaction system was never designed to handle. Every one of those additions is another line item eating into the margin catering was supposed to create. You grow the top line and quietly give it back on the bottom.


The blind spot isn't the ordering technology. It's that the ordering technology only solves half of the equation needed for a successful catering program.

## Frequently asked questions


**What's the difference between a catering kitchen and a commissary kitchen?**


A catering kitchen produces food for off-site events and orders. A commissary (or central) kitchen produces food centrally for distribution to multiple locations. Many operators run both, and the same production, labeling, and food-safety workflows apply to each.


**Why does catering create food-safety risk?**


Volume and distance. High-volume production increases the chance of an allergen or date-code error, and off-premise food is held and transported longer — so labeling accuracy and prep-and-expiration tracking matter more, not less.


**Do you need separate software for catering labels?**


Not if your in-store labeling platform already scales. Running separate systems for in-store, catering, and grab-and-go labels adds cost and integration risk; a connected platform prints all of them from the same place.


**Does this apply outside restaurants?**


Yes. Convenience stores, sushi and contract foodservice programs, foodservice management companies, and casino banquet operations all run catering or commissary production and face the same back-of-house scaling challenge.
