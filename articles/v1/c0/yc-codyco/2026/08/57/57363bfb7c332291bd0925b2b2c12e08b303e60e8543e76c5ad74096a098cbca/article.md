# What Happens When a Guest Calls Your Hotel After Hours

Frequently asked questions


## FAQ


### How common are missed after-hours calls at hotels?


Missed after-hours calls are common enough to appear as a market-wide pattern. In a study of 26,707 hotels in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, at least 52.7% showed one or more detectable phone or reachability friction signals. No 24/7 reception was the most common signal at 27.9%. Because detection depends on what hotels publish about themselves, the true prevalence is higher. At one high-volume budget-chain hotel, 71% of front-desk calls went unanswered over a 31-day window. Most properties have no report that would show them this, which is why the problem stays invisible.


### What should hotel after-hours phone coverage include?


Hotel after-hours phone coverage should give every caller a usable next step, not an operating-hours recording. It should cover late arrivals, key or keybox access, directions, parking, booking confirmation, and genuine emergencies. Self-check-in and keybox access are the most frequent informational needs in large-scale call analysis, so a hotel that handles only those two well has already covered most of its overnight volume. Anything that cannot be resolved immediately should end with a specific callback time rather than an open-ended promise.


### Does hotel voicemail work for after-hours guest calls?


Hotel voicemail rarely solves an after-hours guest problem. A guest outside a locked door or sitting in a taxi needs an answer within minutes, so they will usually call someone else instead of leaving a message. If a hotel uses voicemail, the greeting should provide the late-arrival procedure, key or keybox instructions, an emergency number, and the exact time someone will respond.


### Should a night porter answer hotel calls after hours?


A night porter can answer hotel calls after hours when the phone is not competing with a guest standing at the desk. The issue is availability, not willingness. One person cannot be reliably reachable while handling arrivals, the night audit, and a locked-out guest at the same time. The same structural problem appears in daylight at food-led properties: at 12.8% of hotels in the DACH study, the restaurant line is the hotel line, so a waiter mid-service is the person answering booking calls. Coverage has to work when the night porter is busy, not only when they are free.


### How can hotels measure missed after-hours calls?


Four steps, in order of effort. First, ask your phone provider or PBX administrator for inbound calls by hour, with[offered and answered counts separately](https://www.codyco.ai/research/hotel-phone-report-2026) : the gap during unstaffed hours is the number of missed after-hours calls. Second, sanity-check the volume against a reference point, since a roughly 200-room city hotel receives about 700 to 800 calls a month, or 20 to 30 a day. Third, search recent reviews for reachability complaints, which appear around 3 times per 1,000 reviews and often sit inside otherwise positive feedback. Fourth, measure each property separately if you run more than one, because 89% of large groups in the DACH study had a mix of reachable and unreachable sibling hotels. To estimate potential value, compare unanswered booking inquiries with the roughly two-thirds conversion rate reported by an outsourced reservations team serving around 70 UK four and five star hotels.
