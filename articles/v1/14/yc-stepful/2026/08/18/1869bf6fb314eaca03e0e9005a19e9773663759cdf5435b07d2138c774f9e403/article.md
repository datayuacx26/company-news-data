# How to Become a CNA: A Step-by-Step Guide

Becoming a[certified nursing assistant (CNA)](https://www.stepful.com/post/what-is-a-cna) is one of the fastest ways to start a career in healthcare. CNA training is designed to get you from application to paycheck in months rather than years, making it much faster than becoming an LPN or RN.


CNAs are regulated by state and federal government rules, and each state has its own specifics for training and registration. However, the path to becoming a CNA follows the same basic pattern no matter where you live.


In some states, there are basic eligibility requirements like having a high school diploma/GED or being a certain age. You then have to complete a CNA training program and pass an exam. Finally, you’ll get listed on a state registry.


The exact hours, costs, and paperwork differ by state, but the steps themselves are the same.


Since free or employer-paid training options exist in most areas, the cost to becoming a CNA is often much lower than similar healthcare roles.


This guide walks you through each step of becoming a CNA, from eligibility requirements to certification to registration. We’ll also cover what to expect in terms of cost, timeline, and life after certification.


## How to Become a CNA


Here's what the process looks like to become a CNA, from meeting basic requirements to getting listed on your state's registry.


### Step 1: Meet the Basic Eligibility Requirements


Before you can work as a CNA, you need to meet some basic qualifications in some states.


These are eligibility requirements set by the state that you need to meet before enrolling in a training program.


There are also requirements set by your training provider, the clinical site where you will learn hands-on skills, and your future employer. So, some apply when you enroll, while others apply when you begin working.


If you look at[job postings](https://www.indeed.com/hire/job-description/cna-certified-nursing-assistant) , you’ll see that some employers may require a TB test. Others may require CPR certification. The specifics depend on the employer and work setting.


#### **Common requirements to enroll in a training program**


While requirements vary by state, here are some common examples:


- Meet the minimum age requirement, usually 16 to 18 depending on the state


- A high school diploma or GED (though not all states require this)
- Pass any required criminal background screening
- Certain basic health screenings and proof of vaccinations, to clear you for the clinical placement built into your program


#### **Common requirements to work as a CNA**


These may be required by your state when you apply to be on the state registry, or by your employer before you can begin working.


- Government-issued identification
- Proof you are authorized to work in the U.S.
- Specific health screenings and vaccinations
- CPR certification
- Drug screening


### Step 2: Complete a State-Approved CNA Training Program


Nursing assistants need to complete a state-approved training program that includes academic coursework and hands-on clinical training before they can take their state’s competency exam. A few states offer alternate pathways to the exam, but it’s not the norm.


Federal law states that in order for a training program to be approved by the state, it must include at least[75 hours of instruction](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-483/subpart-D/section-483.152) . This must include at least 16 hours of supervised practical training. Some states require more, ranging from 75 to 180 hours of instruction.


Programs typically take 4 to 12 weeks to complete, depending on whether you attend full-time or part-time. The classroom topics cover things like infection control, safety and emergency procedures, and respecting patients' rights.


From there, training moves into hands-on skills like taking vital signs, bathing and dressing, feeding and toileting assistance, and safely transferring and repositioning patients. There are also specialized topics like caring for cognitively impaired patients.


#### **Where to take CNA classes**


CNA training is widely available, so you likely have several options nearby, including:


- Community colleges and vocational schools
- Online programs that pair virtual coursework with in-person clinical hours
- Hospitals and nursing homes that train their own staff
- Job Corps centers
- Approved community organizations


#### **How much CNA training costs**


CNA programs typically cost anywhere from $1,000 to $2,000 from an online provider or community college. They can run several thousand dollars more at a private vocational school. The price depends on your state, the program length, and whether it's public or private.


On top of tuition, extras like background checks, immunizations, uniforms that might be required for clinical settings, and your exam fee can add a few hundred dollars more.


The good news is that there are many free CNA training programs available.


- **Nursing home training and reimbursement:** Nursing homes[generally cover CNA training and testing costs](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-483/subpart-D/section-483.152) for nurse aides they employ or have offered employment. You may also qualify for reimbursement if you complete approved training elsewhere and are hired by a qualifying nursing facility within 12 months.
- ‍ **Employer-sponsored training:** Many nursing homes and hospitals pay for your CNA training if you commit to working for them afterward. **‍**
- **Job Corps:** This[free federal program](https://www.jobcorps.gov/jobs/certified-nurse-assistant) offers CNA training to eligible young adults ages 16 to 24. **‍**
- **Local workforce development boards:** Many communities offer tuition assistance for approved healthcare training through[American Job Centers](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/american-job-centers) .


Keep in mind that "free" usually covers tuition only. You may still owe money for books, uniforms, or your exam fee, so ask upfront exactly what's included.


### Step 3: Pass the CNA Certification Exam


Once training is complete, you'll apply to take the state competency exam. You may have to complete basic state requirements at that time, like fingerprinting or providing a background check.


CNA competency exams typically include a written test and a hands-on skills evaluation, although the components you must complete can depend on your state.


For example,[Florida](https://www.prometric.com/files/Nurse-Aide/FL-CNA-CIB.pdf) has both a written portion and clinical skills evaluation, but if you’ve passed an approved training program in the past six months, you don’t have to take the clinical skills portion.


Here’s a look at how each of the components works:


- **A written portion:** The written test has 60 multiple-choice questions that evaluate nursing assistant knowledge and skills. You have 90 minutes to take the test. There is a list of topics available as well as practice exams.
- **A clinical skills evaluation:** This is a timed test that will ask you to perform five nursing assistant skills in front of two nurses. Three of the skills are randomly chosen, and the other two are Handwashing and Indirect Care. You must show competency in all five skills to pass.


Other states follow a similar format, though the exact number of questions and skills can vary.


CNA exams don't necessarily use a standard percentage, such as 70%, as the passing score. States select their own passing standard, but many do not publish it.


Florida, to continue our example, says that you have to pass all five of the skills but does not[publish the passing standard](https://www.prometric.com/nurse-aide-frequently-asked-questions/) for the written exam.


If you fail, you'll usually get another chance. In Florida, you have up to three attempts to pass the exam. After three failed attempts, you must complete an approved training program before testing again.


#### **Tips for passing the CNA skills test**


Here are some ways to prepare for the CNA skills test:


- Practice the entire skill from start to finish, even if some of the parts seem very easy. This will help you remember every step.
- Narrate your actions out loud, since evaluators often listen for safety steps like hand hygiene.
- Time yourself so you're comfortable working within the time limit.
- Review your state's official skills list ahead of time, and practice all the skills listed, even if you think they are easy.
- If you make a mistake, keep calm and continue. You need to achieve competency, not perfection.


### Step 4: Get Listed on Your State's Nurse Aide Registry


In most states, being listed on the state’s nurse aide registry is required to legally work as a CNA. This registry confirms that your certification is active and lets employers verify your credentials before hiring you.


Typically, the organization that gives the exam sends your results directly to the state. Processing time varies, but many states add your name within a few days to a couple of weeks of receiving your passing results.


Some states issue a physical certificate or ID card, while others rely entirely on an online, searchable registry. Either way, keep a copy of your certification for your own records.


As with other requirements we’ve discussed, this varies by state.


For example, federal nursing-home rules let some aides work while completing approved training or awaiting registry listing, generally up to four months.

## How Long Does It Take to Become a CNA?


Altogether, most people can become a CNA in 6 weeks to 6 months, broken down roughly like this:


- **Training program:** These take anywhere from 4 to 12 weeks, depending on whether you attend full-time or part-time.
- **Exam scheduling and results:** This typically takes an additional 1 to 4 weeks, depending on the state and testing vendor. In Florida, for example, your exam is scheduled within 7 to 14 business days, and results are returned within 72 hours.
- **Registry listing:** This can take from a few business days to a few weeks after you pass, depending on your state. Most will tell you what to expect.


Allow extra time if you need to reschedule your exam or wait on something like background check results.


Because timelines vary so much by state and by your own schedule, you should consider these numbers as rough estimates and check your state requirements and timeline to get a true idea of how long it’ll take you to become a CNA.


## How CNA Requirements Vary by State


Every state sets its own training hours, minimum age, exam vendor, and renewal timeline, so requirements can look pretty different depending on where you live.


For example, state-by-state requirements show[Iowa](https://dial.iowa.gov/licenses/health-professions/cnas) and[Indiana](https://www.in.gov/health/ltc/aide-training-and-certification/cna/nurse-aide-training-programs/) require the federal minimum of 75 hours of training, while[Connecticut](https://portal.ct.gov/dph/facility-licensing--investigations/health-care-quality/nurse-aide-training-program-information) requires at least 100 hours and[Maine](https://www.maine.gov/dhhs/node/2449) requires 180.


[Florida](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/464.203) stands out because some applicants can qualify for certification without completing a formal CNA training program.


Because of this range, check your state's board of nursing or health department page for the exact requirements before you enroll in a program.


## Keeping Your CNA Certification Active


CNA certification isn't permanent. Most states require renewal every 24 months, and the process is usually simple if you've stayed employed as a CNA.


Most states require you to perform some paid nursing or nursing-related work during each renewal period to keep your registry status active. The exact work and continuing-education requirements vary by state.


If you didn't work during that time, expect a more involved process, which could include retraining or passing a competency exam again. Check your state's specific rules well before your certification expires.


## What Happens After You're Certified?


Once you're certified, you can start looking for a job in the setting that appeals to you most. That may be a hospital, nursing home, or private home doing home healthcare.


Each setting has a different pace and patient load, so it's worth thinking about which environment suits your working style before you apply.


Many CNAs use this role to build experience before pursuing further education, like enrolling in an LPN or RN program. Many LPN and RN programs view CNA experience as a plus, since it shows comfort with hands-on patient care.


Alternatively, some CNAs add optional certifications, like phlebotomy or EKG monitoring. These may help if you decide to move on to another role, like a[patient care technician](https://www.stepful.com/post/patient-care-technician) or[medical assistant](https://www.stepful.com/post/what-is-a-medical-assistant) . These add-on certifications typically take just a few weeks to complete and can lead to expanded duties and higher pay.


Whichever direction you choose, the experience you gain as a CNA will help. Employers value experience with hands-on patient care and being part of a healthcare team.

## FAQs on How to Become a CNA


**What are the minimum requirements to become a CNA?**
Requirements vary by state and training program. Common requirements include meeting a minimum age, passing background screening, completing a state-approved training program, and passing the state competency exam. Some states or programs also require a high school diploma or GED.


**How much does CNA training cost?**
Programs typically range from $1,000 to $2,000 at community colleges to a few thousand dollars more at private schools. However, there are many[free training options](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/nursing-home-careers/overview/certified-nursing-assistant-cna) through employers, Medicare- or Medicaid-certified facilities, or workforce development programs.


**Can I become a CNA online?**
In-person clinical training is required by the[federal government](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-483/subpart-D/section-483.152) . So, some CNA programs offer academic coursework online, but every program will still require an in-person component.


**Do I need a high school diploma to become a CNA?**
It depends on the state and training program. Some require a high school diploma or GED, while others have lower education requirements or none at all. Check your state's requirements and the admission requirements for the program you're considering.


**How many times can I retake the CNA exam if I fail?**
Each state has its own rules about retaking the exam. In[Florida](https://www.prometric.com/files/Nurse-Aide/FL-CNA-CIB.pdf) , you have up to three attempts to pass the exam. After three failed attempts, you must complete an approved training program before testing again. Check with your state's board of nursing or health department.


**Is CNA training free?**
There are many free options. Nursing homes[generally cover CNA training and testing costs](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-483/subpart-D/section-483.152) for nurse aides they employ or have offered employment. You may also qualify for reimbursement if you complete approved training elsewhere and are hired by a qualifying nursing facility within 12 months. There are also[employer-sponsored programs](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/nursing-home-careers/overview/certified-nursing-assistant-cna) ,[Job Corps](https://www.jobcorps.gov/jobs/certified-nurse-assistant) , and[workforce development](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/american-job-centers) programs.


**Can I transfer my CNA certification to another state?**
Probably. Each state maintains its own Nurse Aide Registry, so you'll usually need to apply for reciprocity or certification in your new state, which may include extra paperwork or a new competency exam.


**Can I work while waiting for my CNA exam or registry listing?**
It depends on your state, employer, and where you work. Some nursing facilities can employ nurse aides who are still completing approved training or waiting to be added to the registry, subject to federal and state requirements. Check your state's rules before starting work.


**Does CNA certification expire if I stop working?**
Yes. If you don't meet your state's minimum work-hour requirement during the renewal period, usually every 24 months, your certification can lapse. You may need to retrain or retest to reactivate it.


**How is a CNA different from a home health aide (HHA)?**
CNAs typically work in facilities like nursing homes as part of a larger care team, while HHAs mostly work one-on-one with clients in private homes. That is the biggest difference, but[HHAs and CNAs](https://www.stepful.com/post/home-health-aide-vs-certified-nursing-assistant) also have different training requirements, duties, and salaries.


**Could becoming a medical assistant be a good next step after working as a CNA?**
[Becoming a medical assistant](https://www.stepful.com/training-for-caregivers) is one possible next step for a CNA.[MAs and CNAs](https://www.stepful.com/post/medical-assistant-vs-certified-nursing-assistant) share some overlapping skills, but medical assistants perform a different mix of administrative and clinical tasks, typically in outpatient settings.
