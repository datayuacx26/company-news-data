# What Is a CNA? Duties, Requirements, and Career Path Explained

NAs work closely with nurses to provide hands-on care to patients in hospitals and residents in nursing homes or assisted living facilities. The role is entry-level, but they are a vital part of the healthcare team.


CNAs make up one of the largest groups of healthcare workers in the country, with more than[1.4 million employed](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nursing-assistants.htm#tab-3) . One reason why is that it requires less schooling than becoming an LPN or RN, and training is low-cost or free.


If you're considering becoming a CNA, this guide will give you key information you need to decide whether it’s the right career for you. We cover what a CNA does day-to-day, where they work, their pay, job outlook, and how it compares to similar jobs.


## What Does CNA Stand For?


CNA stands for Certified Nursing Assistant. This job is regulated by federal and state rules, so each state has a specific title it uses for licensing and registration. Some states use slightly different titles, like:


- Licensed Nursing Assistant
- State-Tested Nursing Assistant
- Nursing Assistant Certified
- Registered Nurse Aide


Employers may use slightly different titles too, but they are all essentially the same job.


## What Is a CNA?


A CNA is a healthcare worker who provides hands-on personal care, helping patients with activities of daily living. They work under the supervision of a registered nurse (RN) or a licensed practical nurse (LPN) and support a healthcare team.


There are[different types of caregivers](https://www.stepful.com/post/what-is-a-caregiver-responsibilities-types) in healthcare, some informal, some professional. The "certified" part of the CNA title means this role is professional.


CNAs complete a state-approved training program and pass a competency exam. Once certified, they are listed on their state's registry, which employers check before hiring.


CNAs work closely with patients, often spending more one-on-one time with them than any other member of the care team. The close relationships they develop with the people they help make the job meaningful for most who do it.


Because the training period is relatively short and the job doesn't require a college degree, becoming a CNA is one of the more accessible ways to start a career in healthcare. Many CNAs use the role as a stepping stone toward[becoming a medical assistant](https://www.stepful.com/training-for-caregivers) or nurse.

## What Does a CNA Do? Common Responsibilities


Each state’s nursing board or certifying agency makes rules about what a CNA can and cannot do. This is referred to as their “scope of practice.” Employers may also have a list of do’s and don’ts they follow in their facilities.


When a CNA starts a job, they will be trained on what they are allowed or expected to do. In general, however, CNAs help patients with the[basic daily tasks](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nursing-assistants.htm#tab-2) that keep them safe, clean, and comfortable.


Common CNA responsibilities include:


- Helping patients with bathing, dressing, grooming, and using the bathroom
- Assisting patients with eating and drinking, including feeding those who can't feed themselves
- Helping patients move safely, including transferring them between beds and wheelchairs
- Taking and recording vital signs, like temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and breathing rate
- Turning and repositioning patients who can't move on their own, to help prevent bedsores
- Keeping patient rooms and supplies clean and organized
- Observing patients for any changes in condition and reporting those changes to nurses
- Collecting samples, like urine, for lab testing
- Documenting the care provided in a patient's chart


## What Can’t a CNA Do?


Staying within your scope of practice is important for your patients’ safety and your own.


What CNAs cannot do depends on the state they work in, but in general, CNAs cannot:


- Administer medication
- Insert or remove medical devices like IVs, catheters, or feeding tubes
- Perform wound care beyond basic first aid
- Make clinical assessments or diagnoses
- Change physical, occupational, or speech therapy routines


In some states, CNAs can do additional training to expand their scope of practice. Several states offer special certifications to administer medication, collect blood ([phlebotomy](https://www.google.com/goto?url=CAESfwHuR6pNpAvscl4HzdlknyYT8VN4Dr3avjMLDU9L0WmaD-WJdClo8-gRslGucUfzvs8lIXiy_tMLg5D5EMz90KCIDbRD7eGkZrNiuV6rG4_7RBnbsJ4UXpF9dlFBetde48tat-uQyjQxv_K_b6ha0MTqBAz7yuKti5N7sKKWgOk=) ), perform EKGs, or manage dialysis treatment.


There are also specializations in some states that would expand scope of practice. These could be for areas like geriatric care, dementia and Alzheimer’s, or hospice and palliative care.


Special certifications often allow you to do more for patients, be eligible for more jobs, and earn higher pay.


You can check your state’s board of nursing or department of health for the specifics on scope of practice in your state.


## A Day in the Life of a CNA


When you are considering a new career, it can be helpful to learn what the day-to-day experience of it is like.


A CNA's shift usually starts with reviewing patient charts and reading the reports from the outgoing shift about any changes in patient conditions. From there, much of the shift is spent moving from patient to patient, helping with daily routines, like bathing, dressing, and eating.


Throughout the day, CNAs check in regularly to take vital signs, assist with bathroom needs, and help patients move safely. They note basic health signs in patients’ charts.


They also watch for anything unusual, like a patient who seems more confused than normal or a wound that looks infected. They report those observations to their supervising nurse.


The number of patients a CNA is responsible for varies a lot by setting. In a single shift, a CNA might care for anywhere from about 6 to 20 patients, depending on the facility and staffing levels.


Nursing homes and long-term care facilities tend to have higher patient loads than hospitals. CNAs who work in private homes as part of a home healthcare team usually only see one or two patients in a day.


Before their shift ends, CNAs make notes for the next shift, alerting them to anything they need to be aware of.

CNAs work in a range of healthcare settings, and the day-to-day experience can look pretty different depending on where you land.

Here is a closer look at what each of the major work environments is like:


- **Nursing care facilities:** This is where the largest share of CNAs work, providing round-the-clock care to residents who often live there for months or years. CNAs in this setting build long-term relationships with residents and are usually responsible for more patients per shift than in a hospital.
- **Hospitals:** Hospital CNAs support a wide mix of patients, from short-term recovery to more complex conditions. They follow the lead of RNs on their assigned floor. Shifts can move quickly since patient turnover tends to be higher than in long-term care.
- **Assisted living and residential care communities for the elderly:** Residents in these settings generally need less intensive medical care than a hospital or nursing home, so CNAs spend more time on daily living support and social engagement.
- **Home healthcare services:** CNAs who work in home health travel to patients' houses to provide one-on-one care, often for older adults who want to stay in their own homes rather than move to a facility. This setting usually comes with more flexible scheduling but less immediate backup from other staff.


## How to Become a CNA


Becoming a CNA takes months rather than years. It's much quicker than becoming an LPN or RN. And free training options exist in most areas, so don’t let the cost of training hold you back.


Each state has its own specifics for training, certification, and registration. However, the path to becoming a CNA follows the same basic pattern no matter where you live.


### 1. Meet the basic eligibility requirements


These typically include being at least 16/18, having a high school diploma or GED, and passing a criminal background check. Some states require basic health screenings, drug screening, and vaccinations.


### 2. Complete a state-approved CNA training program


Programs include academic coursework and hands-on skills training in a clinical setting. The federal minimum is[75 hours of instruction](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-483/subpart-D/section-483.152) , including at least 16 hours of supervised practical training.


Some states require more, ranging from 75 to 180 hours of instruction. Programs usually take 4 to 12 weeks to complete and prepare you for the state exam. There are many free options.


### 3. Pass your state's CNA certification exam


State competency exams have two parts: a written test and a hands-on skills demonstration. Your state should provide information about how many questions there are, how many skills you need to demonstrate, and how long the test takes.


### 4. Get listed on your state's Nurse Aide Registry


After passing their exams, qualified nursing assistants are added to their state’s nurse aide registry. Registry listing is required to work as a nurse aide in a nursing home, although requirements for other settings vary by state.


### Timeline to Becoming a CNA


This entire process of becoming a CNA typically takes 3 to 6 months. The timeline depends on whether you do training full-time or part-time and how many hours of training your state requires.


You may also need to add time for things like exam scheduling or waiting for a background check to clear.


Once you are certified and working as a CNA, you will have to keep your registration current. Most states require a renewal every two years and may have a fee or continuing education requirement.


State-by-state requirements for certification and registration vary, so check your state’s specific pathway before starting any training program.

## CNA Salary and Job Outlook


CNAs have steady, reliable work and earn a decent wage. The BLS reports that the national median is[$42,260 a year](https://data.bls.gov/oesprofile/?major_group=310000&occupation=311131&measure=04&areas=INDUSTRY,STATE,MSA) , or $20.32 an hour, as of May 2025.


Many factors can affect pay. Experience level, location, and employer type are all important variables. The BLS shows that the lowest-paid 10% of CNAs earn $33,940 or less, while the highest-paid 10% earn $51,980 or more.


One important variable is geographic location. States like Washington, Oregon, and New York pay CNAs more. The median salary in those states ranges from $48,590 to $49,830.


The setting you work in can also affect your pay. Hospitals and skilled nursing facilities[generally pay more](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nursing-assistants.htm#tab-5) than assisted living communities and home health services.


Looking ahead, the BLS predicts growth for the healthcare industry overall. They project a[2% employment growth](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nursing-assistants.htm#tab-6) rate for nursing assistants in particular from 2024 to 2034. This translates into about 211,800 openings a year for nursing assistants and orderlies combined.


That growth is slower than the average for all occupations. However, a large, aging population needing long-term care will mean CNA jobs remain widely available across nearly every part of the country.


## Is Becoming a CNA Right for You?


Like any job, being a CNA has pros and cons. Here's a closer look at what makes the role rewarding and what can make it challenging. How you feel about these tradeoffs can help you decide if it's a good match for you.


## Pros of Becoming a CNA


- **Fast path into healthcare:** Training and certification for the CNA role takes months instead of years. You can go from enrollment in a training program to a paycheck faster than in almost any other healthcare role.
- **Low cost of entry:** Compared to degree programs for nurses or other clinical roles, CNA training is relatively inexpensive. Some nursing homes, workforce programs, and employers offer free or reimbursed CNA training. Under federal rules, qualifying nursing facilities must cover training costs for certain current or newly hired nurse aides.
- **Meaningful, hands-on work:** CNAs build real relationships with the patients they care for. Many say that personal connection is one of the most rewarding parts of the job.
- **A stepping stone to other roles:** Many CNAs use the role to gain healthcare experience while working toward[becoming a medical assistant](https://www.stepful.com/training-for-caregivers) , LPN, or RN.
- **Steady demand:** Nursing assistants are needed in nearly every care setting, so job opportunities tend to be widely available.


### Cons of Becoming a CNA


- **Physically demanding:** The job involves a lot of lifting, standing, and moving throughout a shift. It has a[higher rate of injury](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nursing-assistants.htm#tab-3) than many other occupations.
- **Lower pay than other nursing roles:** While the pay is comparable to that of other caregiving roles, it is lower than LPN or RN pay. That’s because it requires less training and carries less clinical responsibility.
- **Emotionally demanding at times:** Caring for patients who are sick, in pain, or nearing the end of life can be difficult, especially for CNAs early in their careers.
- **High patient loads:** Depending on the setting, CNAs can be responsible for a large number of patients per shift, which can make the pace of the job intense.
- **Limited scope of practice:** Because CNAs can't perform clinical tasks like medication administration in most states, the role has a ceiling on responsibility without further certification.

### CNA vs. Patient Care Technician (PCT)


A patient care technician, or PCT, is another entry-level role that sometimes gets confused with CNA. Both provide hands-on patient care under the supervision of nurses. They make similar salaries, have similar schedules, and have some overlapping responsibilities.


However, there are key differences between these jobs. Differences include:


- **Certification requirements:** Training and certification requirements for CNAs are set by both federal and state rules and differ in each state. PCTs can earn a nationally recognized certification that is often portable between employers and states.
- **Clinical experience requirements:** PCTs can earn their certification online without any in-person clinical hours, while CNAs need at least 16 hours of in-person clinical practice before they are eligible for certification. **‍**
- **Basic responsibilities:** CNAs typically spend more time helping patients with activities of daily living, while PCTs perform more medically focused tasks such as drawing blood or assisting with procedures. **‍**
- **Work setting:** CNAs are more likely to work in nursing homes and assisted living facilities, while PCTs are more likely to work in hospitals and outpatient clinics.


If you're trying to decide between these two jobs, you can learn more from our full[PCT vs. CNA comparison](https://www.stepful.com/post/patient-care-technician-vs-certified-nursing-assistant) , which breaks down the training, pay, and day-to-day differences in more detail.


## CNA FAQs


**What does CNA stand for?**
CNA stands for certified nursing assistant. This job may also be called licensed nursing assistant, registered nurse aide, or state-tested nursing assistant.


**Is a CNA the same as a nurse?**
No. A CNA is a member of the nursing team and works under the supervision of nurses, but they aren't licensed nurses themselves. LPNs and RNs both complete additional training and pass licensing exams that let them take on more clinical responsibilities than CNAs.


**How long does it take to become a CNA?**
Most CNA training programs take anywhere from 4 to 12 weeks. Adding in time for state testing and registration, the whole process can take between 3 and 6 months.


**Can a CNA give medication?**
In most states, CNAs cannot dispense medication. Some states offer an additional certification that allows CNAs with extra training to give certain medications under supervision.


**What's the difference between a CNA and a home health aide (HHA)?**
[CNAs and HHAs](https://www.stepful.com/post/home-health-aide-vs-certified-nursing-assistant) both provide personal care, but CNAs typically train for and work across a range of settings, including hospitals and nursing homes. HHAs most often work in patients' homes. Certification and training requirements also differ by state.


**Do CNAs need to be recertified?**
Yes. CNA certification isn't permanent. Most states require periodic renewal, usually every two years. Renewal may require continuing education hours or proof of recent work. It varies by state.


**Do you need a high school diploma to become a CNA?**
*Most* states require a high school diploma or GED to enroll in a CNA training program. Requirements vary by state, so it's worth checking your state’s nursing board or health department to find out what its exact requirements are.


**Can a CNA become an LPN or RN?**
Yes. Many CNAs use the job as a starting point and go on to do[additional training](https://www.stepful.com/training-for-caregivers) . They often pursue careers in nursing, like LPN or RN. The hands-on experience gained as a CNA is useful preparation when you are training for more clinical roles.


**What's the difference between a CNA and a medical assistant?**
[CNAs and medical assistants (MAs)](https://www.stepful.com/post/medical-assistant-vs-certified-nursing-assistant) both provide hands-on patient support, but medical assistants also do administrative work like billing and scheduling patient appointments.[Medical assistants work in](https://www.stepful.com/post/where-do-medical-assistants-work) outpatient settings like physician offices and do more clinical tasks, like drawing blood or preparing patients for exams. They[primarily support doctors](https://www.stepful.com/post/what-is-a-medical-assistant) , whereas CNAs primarily support nurses.
