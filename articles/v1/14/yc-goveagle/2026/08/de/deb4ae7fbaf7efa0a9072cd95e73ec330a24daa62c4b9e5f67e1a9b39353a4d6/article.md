## What Capture Management Is (and What It Is Not)


Capture management is the structured, pre-RFP work of identifying a specific federal opportunity, qualifying it against your strengths, and positioning your firm to win before the solicitation drops. It spans customer engagement, competitive intelligence, win theme development, teaming decisions, and the bid/no-bid gate. By the time an RFP is published, a well-run capture effort has already shaped how your firm will respond.


That scope is worth distinguishing from three things it often gets confused with:


- A CRM tracks accounts, contacts, and pipeline activity. It does not structure the pursuit or document competitive positioning against a specific opportunity.
- A pipeline tracker shows what opportunities exist and their status. It does not drive the qualification work or feed intelligence into proposal development.
- Proposal software handles drafting, compliance matrices, and color team reviews. It operates after capture ends, not during it.


Capture management sits between BD and proposal execution. Its job is to convert opportunity awareness into pursuit strategy, so that when the RFP arrives, your team has already done the positioning work that determines whether you can win.

## The Capture Management Process: Phase by Phase


Capture for a complex federal competition typically runs 12 to 24 months before RFP release. Each phase produces specific outputs the next phase depends on.


Phase What the Capture Manager Does Key Output


Opportunity Identification Monitors agency forecasts, RFIs, and incumbent contract expiration dates Qualified opportunity list


Qualification / Bid-No-Bid Scores PWin against capability fit, competitive exposure, and resource cost Bid decision memo


Customer Intelligence Conducts agency meetings, RFI responses, and site visits to map requirements and relationships Contact map and requirement assumptions


Competitor Research Identifies likely offerors, incumbent positioning, and pricing history Competitor assessment


Teaming Assessment Matches capability gaps against potential partners with relevant past performance Teaming strategy and NDAs


Win Strategy Development Drafts win themes, discriminators, and ghost strategies tied to evaluation criteria Capture plan with documented win themes


Proposal Readiness Review Confirms team alignment, resource assignments, and intel currency before RFP drop Capture-to-proposal handoff package


Each[capture gate](https://www.goveagle.com/govcon-glossary/capture-gates) is a checkpoint, not a formality. Skipping competitor research before teaming decisions, for instance, means you may partner with a firm that an incumbent already locked up.


## Structured Bid/No-Bid: The Gate That Protects Pipeline Discipline


Contractors without a formal bid/no-bid process average a 12% win rate. Those with[structured go/no-bid criteria average 28%](https://fed-spend.com/blog/federal-contract-win-rates-data) . That gap has nothing to do with proposal quality. It reflects which pursuits ever reach the writing phase.


The bid/no-bid gate exists to protect pipeline discipline. Without it, teams chase volume, spread resources thin, and produce weaker responses across the board. Capture managers typically score six criteria before committing:


- Incumbent position and re-compete risk, since defending against an entrenched incumbent without a clear displacement strategy rarely warrants the spend
- Depth of customer relationship and access, because pre-RFP engagement shapes evaluator expectations long before Section L is published
- Past performance relevance to the specific requirement, beyond the general domain
- Competitive field and likely offerors, with particular attention to teams already positioned on the vehicle or with the agency
- Internal capacity to staff both the pursuit and the proposal without pulling resources from higher-[PWin](https://www.goveagle.com/govcon-glossary/pwin-probability-of-win) work
- Realistic PWin given the full picture above, not the optimistic version


A no-bid decision is not a failure. It frees bandwidth for pursuits where your firm has genuine positioning. The contractors compounding win rates over time tend to pursue fewer opportunities, not more.


## What a Capture Plan Should Include


A[capture plan](https://www.goveagle.com/govcon-glossary/capture-plan) that describes an opportunity is not the same as one that analyzes it. Listing the agency, NAICS code, and estimated value gets you a summary. A defensible plan tells you whether your firm can win, who will beat you if you can't, and what the agency actually weighs when scoring offerors.


The structural components that separate analysis from description:


- Agency and customer intelligence: decision-makers by name, their stated priorities, any documented requirement shaping from RFI responses or pre-solicitation meetings, and your firm's current relationship depth with each
- Competitive analysis: likely offerors, incumbent positioning, probable team structures, and any pricing history from USASpending that informs your cost approach
- Teaming strategy: capability gaps mapped to specific partner candidates, with notes on exclusivity risk and partner past performance relevance to the PWS
- [Win themes](https://www.goveagle.com/govcon-glossary/win-themes) and discriminators: three to five differentiators tied directly to Section M evaluation criteria, with evidence for each, not assertions
- PWin scoring with gate thresholds: a scored assessment updated at each phase gate, with a pre-agreed threshold below which the pursuit moves to no-bid
- Accountability framework: named owners for each intelligence gap, with deadlines tied to gate review dates


The plan should be updated at every gate review, not filed after the kickoff call and reopened at RFP drop. By the time the solicitation publishes, a well-maintained capture plan has already flagged which assumptions held and which intelligence gaps remain unresolved.


## The Capture-to-Proposal Handoff: Where Intelligence Gets Lost


The capture plan exists. The proposal team just can't find it.


Win themes live in a capture manager's inbox. Competitive intelligence sits in a slide deck that never got shared. Relationship context from six months of agency meetings exists only in someone's memory. When the RFP drops and the proposal team stands up, they spend their first week reconstructing work the capture team already did.


That duplication has a direct cost. It extends proposal cycles, raises labor spend per submission, and produces first drafts disconnected from the agency intelligence that should be shaping every section. The gap between firms running structured capture and those responding cold is not proposal quality. It is whether the proposal team had access to what the capture team knew.


The handoff typically breaks in one of three places:


- Capture notes stored in a CRM that proposal writers can't access, leaving intelligence siloed at the exact moment it should be driving Section L responses.
- Win themes that were verbal strategy instead of documented artifacts, with no traceable record of what was decided or why.
- A kickoff meeting that attempts to compress months of agency relationship context into a two-hour briefing, which rarely transfers enough to matter under deadline. None of those mechanisms move context reliably when a proposal clock is running.

## Security and Compliance Considerations for GovCon Capture Tools


Capture workflows regularly touch data that falls under federal handling requirements. Agency relationship notes, competitive intelligence tied to specific programs, and opportunity data connected to active procurements can all constitute CUI depending on the contract type and agency involved.


Cloud-based tools that process, store, or transmit CUI generally need a FedRAMP Moderate or higher authorized offering, or one that meets DoD FedRAMP Moderate equivalency requirements. Security certifications such as FedRAMP and NIST 800-171 may be required depending on agency and contract type. The applicable standard depends on your specific contract terms, data flows, and whether the tool qualifies as a cloud service provider under your CMMC scoping.


The practical due diligence step is straightforward: verify compliance posture directly with vendors before procurement, not after onboarding. Ask directly whether they hold FedRAMP Authorization or equivalency, how CUI is handled in their environment, and whether they support GCC High if your contract requires it. Vendors that cannot answer those questions precisely are worth flagging before you commit.
