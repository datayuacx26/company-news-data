# Best Math Curriculum for Dyslexia: How to Choose What Really Fits Your

No single math curriculum is the best fit for every dyslexic learner. The right choice depends on a child's specific cognitive profile, the delivery features built into a program, and how well daily lessons can be implemented by the adult teaching them. This guide cuts through the brand hype and gives you a criteria-first framework. It includes a dyslexia-informed checklist, neutral comparisons of frequently recommended programs, grade-band starting points, a practical daily lesson template, and guidance on monitoring whether your choice is working.


## Overview


Dyslexia is primarily a language-processing difference, but it consistently affects math instruction in ways that textbook selection cannot fix alone. Recognizing how language, working memory, and retrieval interact with math tasks is essential before comparing any programs.


Working memory limitations make it harder to hold a multi-step procedure in mind while executing each step. Phonological processing weaknesses can disrupt the retrieval of number words and fact-word associations. Rapid naming deficits slow automatic recall of math facts. Because oral instruction alone often does not stick for these learners, multisensory anchors and visual supports become structural necessities rather than enrichment add-ons.


These constraints explain why multisensory instruction, the Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) progression, cumulative review, and reduced writing load consistently appear in practitioner and research-informed guidance for dyslexic math learners. The[Yale Center for Dyslexia & Creativity](http://dyslexia.yale.edu/resources/educators/instruction/math-introduction/) highlights that physical motion and contextual cues reinforce learning — for math, that means pairing an abacus, a sketch, and a written equation to encode a single concept three ways. Curricula that embed multisensory engagement as a lesson core, rather than as optional extension, give dyslexic learners structural advantages over text-heavy programs.


The research base supporting CRA and multisensory math is meaningful but limited by small samples and short intervention windows. What is well supported is the principle that reducing cognitive load through concrete materials, structured cumulative review, and explicit math language instruction improves access. Use that evidence-aligned logic to prioritize program features rather than chasing a single "best" label.


## What "best" really means for dyslexic math learners


Choosing the best math curriculum for dyslexia is less about brand and more about fit. Fit means alignment between a learner's profile, an instructor's capacity, and a program's structural features.


A highly visual, manipulative-rich curriculum can still fail if math vocabulary is not taught explicitly. Conversely, a program with strong audio support can backfire for a student with auditory processing differences if captions or visual anchors are absent. Marketing labels like "dyslexia-friendly" are not guarantees; inspect the delivery details: lesson length, handwriting demand, fact-practice method, placement flexibility, and whether review is embedded.


These delivery variables determine whether instruction actually reaches a dyslexic learner. Framing the decision this way lets you evaluate programs against concrete criteria and plan supplements for predictable gaps, rather than repeatedly switching curricula — a pattern that tends to create more holes than it fills.


Worked example. A parent is choosing between two programs for a rising third-grader who reads two years below grade level, has strong number sense, but struggles with multi-step written procedures. Constraint: one parent runs every lesson, 30 minutes per day, with no math-specialist support. Program A offers scripted manipulative-based lessons with verbal response options throughout. Program B features animated lessons and auto-graded typed responses but has no captions. Given the strong number sense, the student does not need intensive number-sense remediation — she needs reduced language and writing load. Program A fits because verbal responses replace written output and the scripted language reduces the parent's planning burden. Program B's audio reliance without captions introduces a risk that can't be verified without a trial. The decision rule: request Program B's caption status and run one week of Program A's free sample; choose whichever sustains engagement and produces fewer procedural stalls by day five.


## Decision criteria: a dyslexia‑informed checklist


Use these criteria before purchasing any program. They reflect dyslexia-informed principles such as multisensory encoding, reduced cognitive load, and explicit language instruction. No program will satisfy every item perfectly; a strong match will meet most items and make required adaptations manageable.


Instruction and delivery:


- Multisensory engagement built into every lesson (concrete objects, visual models, verbalization) — not offered only as optional extension
- Short lessons (20–35 minutes) with natural pause points for movement or a break
- Explicit instruction in math vocabulary and language rather than assuming understanding from context
- Verbal response accepted as a valid alternative to written response throughout the program


Writing and copying load:


- Minimal requirement to copy problems from a page or board before solving them
- Worksheet design with adequate spacing, readable fonts, and low visual clutter
- Answer recording kept brief; multi-step work can often be done verbally or with manipulatives


Fact fluency approach:


- Strategy-based fact instruction (doubles, make-ten, near-squares) rather than timed drills as the primary route to automaticity
- Spaced practice and game-based review rather than speed-pressure activities


Review and pacing:


- Cumulative review embedded in every lesson, not saved only for unit tests
- Clear mastery expectations and allowance to loop back without penalty
- Placement tools that identify an appropriate starting point without forcing grade-level entry


Accessibility and fit:


- Digital features: text-to-speech, captions, and adjustable playback speed
- Manipulative kits that are manageable in your physical setting without becoming sensory overload
- Parent or teacher workload realistic for your daily schedule


Before committing, request a demo, download a placement test, or watch a sample lesson so you can judge how these criteria play out in practice rather than in a publisher's marketing summary.


## Compare popular programs by the features that matter


These programs appear frequently in homeschool and interventionist discussions about dyslexia-friendly math. The comparisons below synthesize published descriptions and practitioner accounts to orient — not to rank — your decision.


### RightStart Math (hands‑on, game‑based)


RightStart Math centers manipulatives in every lesson and uses card and tile games as its main practice format, which aligns well with multisensory and low-writing principles. The[RightStart publisher](https://rightstartclassroom.com/rightstart-math-dyslexia/) notes that at least one manipulative is incorporated into every lesson, with the AL Abacus serving as a central tool from the beginning.


Lessons are short and scripted, designed for adult-led delivery rather than independent student work. That scripted structure reduces a parent's planning burden while keeping language consistent and precise. Parents and practitioners often report higher engagement and less frustration in students who struggle with extended pencil work. Tradeoffs include upfront cost for the complete manipulative kit and the requirement for consistent adult involvement. Placement tests are available, and the publisher recommends placing by readiness rather than strictly by grade level.


### Math‑U‑See (mastery with manipulatives)


Math-U-See combines mastery pacing with color-coded interlocking blocks that make place value, multiplication, and fractions concrete and visible. Lessons follow a predictable sequence — instructor video, guided block work, worksheet practice — which supports routine but does include a moderate writing load.


The mastery approach benefits students who need time to secure each concept before moving on. It can create friction if progression depends heavily on written fluency rather than demonstrated understanding; for a spiky learner, place at the level where conceptual introduction is comfortable even if written output is still imperfect. Math-U-See's manipulative sets are reusable across levels and siblings, which reduces per-child cost over time.

Teaching Textbooks delivers animated, audio-narrated lessons with on-screen problems that students type or click. This format minimizes handwriting and copying, and step-through explanations for incorrect answers give students rapid corrective input without waiting for adult review.


The format substantially reduces parent workload and supports independent practice — practical for families where adult math confidence or daily time is limited. Fit depends on auditory processing: students who process well through audio benefit most. Screen time accumulation and fewer opportunities for verbalized reasoning are practical tradeoffs to weigh against the reduced adult burden.

Its story-heavy format, however, increases reading load considerably and does not map closely to typical grade-level scope-and-sequence.


It is rarely sufficient as a core program for students who need explicit, cumulative instruction. Use Life of Fred as a supplement: read the text aloud to eliminate decoding barriers and treat embedded problems as conversational practice rather than the primary source of structured skill work.

## Dyslexia vs. dyscalculia: why the distinction changes your choice


Distinguishing dyslexia from dyscalculia is one of the most consequential decisions you can make before selecting a curriculum, because the supports they require differ significantly. Dyslexia affects language-mediated aspects of math; dyscalculia affects core number sense itself.


Dyslexia typically impairs verbal retrieval of facts, reading of math vocabulary, working memory, and processing speed, while intuitive number sense — magnitude understanding, quantity comparison — is often intact. Dyscalculia involves difficulty with numerical magnitude, quantity comparisons, and one-to-one correspondence at a foundational level.


A curriculum that reduces reading and writing demands may still be insufficient if a student has dyscalculia. That student often needs intensive remediation focused on foundational number sense — subitizing, magnitude comparison, early quantity relationships — before mainstream curricula will be effective. If you suspect dyscalculia, a formal evaluation from a neuropsychologist or educational diagnostician is the most reliable path forward. In the interim, watch for persistent inability to compare quantities without counting, confusion about numeral meaning even after extensive concrete work, and poor estimation skills in contexts where the child shows otherwise adequate reasoning.


Providers such as[Made for Math](https://madeformath.com/) describe structured approaches that make mathematical thinking visible and language precise for students with co-occurring profiles.


## Grade‑band guidance: K–2, 3–5, and 6–8+ starting points and pacing


Match instructional focus to the cognitive and conceptual demands of each grade band. Doing so avoids both unnecessary remediation and skipped foundations that surface as gaps later.


### K–2: foundations with manipulatives and language routines


In K–2, the priority is establishing habits that support long-term success: concrete number sense, explicit math vocabulary, and short, movement-friendly lessons. Prioritize subitizing and quantity recognition before counting-on strategies, and teach place value with physical objects before introducing numerals.


Introduce strategy-based addition and subtraction facts — doubles, make-ten — without speed pressure. Vocabulary should be taught with hearing, saying, physical actions, and seeing the word together; this multisensory pairing mirrors the same approach effective for reading instruction. Keep lessons around 15–20 minutes with movement breaks to support attention. A student who can reliably count, compare, and compose quantities to ten with objects has a stronger foundation than one who has memorized facts under pressure.


### Grades 3–5: fractions, multi‑step procedures, and memory supports


Grades 3–5 bring fractions, multi-digit operations, and proportional reasoning — all of which increase working memory demands significantly. Use physical fraction models such as tiles or folded paper before introducing notation, and break multi-step procedures into named steps with consistent verbal scripts that a student can eventually self-narrate.


Embed spaced practice so earlier skills reappear regularly rather than disappearing after a unit test. Reduce copying load through pre-printed problem sets or oral response options, and provide scribe support for longer calculations when written output is the barrier rather than the target skill.


### Grades 6–8+: backfilling gaps without losing dignity


Middle-school learners with gaps need efficient remediation that preserves dignity and challenges reasoning simultaneously. Use bar models, number lines, and area diagrams to backfill operations, fractions, and decimals. Introduce pre-algebra through visual patterns and concrete contexts that do not require fluent symbolic manipulation at the start.


Frame review work explicitly as professional tools — "mathematicians use diagrams to organize complex problems" — rather than as starting over. Keep written output minimal and rely on oral explanations and visual work to demonstrate competence. Students at this band are particularly sensitive to anything that reads as regression, so the framing matters as much as the content sequence.


## Daily lesson flow that works for dyslexic learners


A predictable lesson format reduces transition overhead and supports working memory. The template below fits a 30–45 minute block and is adaptable to any curriculum.


Warm-up review (5–7 minutes): two or three problems revisiting recently taught content to provide spaced retrieval. Prefer verbal or physical responses over written work.


New learning with manipulatives (10–12 minutes): introduce one concept concretely first. Narrate actions aloud and invite the student to narrate back. Follow scripted language when available to support precise encoding.


Guided practice with verbalization (8–10 minutes): solve two to four problems together. Ask the student to explain each step aloud before writing. Correct conversationally rather than punitively.


Short independent practice (5–8 minutes): three to five problems with minimal prompting. Keep written output proportional to the student's current capacity.


Quick exit check (2–3 minutes): one or two questions — oral, thumbs-up/thumbs-down, or a single written problem — to confirm learning and inform the next day's warm-up.


Include a brief physical break between guided practice and independent work when attention flags. Total time should generally stay under 45 minutes for elementary students to avoid diminishing returns.


## Building math facts without speed pressure


Timed drills often backfire for dyslexic learners because time pressure elevates anxiety and disrupts the retrieval process itself. Strategy-based instruction is a more reliable route to automaticity.


Teach a small set of anchors — doubles, make-ten, near-doubles, skip-counting — so students can derive related facts through reasoning. Use games such as card games, dice, and board games to provide repeated exposure in low-pressure contexts. Many dyslexia-informed programs integrate games into lessons as a core practice format rather than treating them as rewards or filler. Over time, derived facts tend to become automatic through repeated, meaningful use rather than forced speeded recall, a pattern consistent with how fluency develops in word reading for students with dyslexia as well.


## Word problems and math language: make it visual and verbal


Word problems layer decoding, working memory, quantity extraction, and calculation all at once — a compound challenge for dyslexic learners. The goal is to reduce the language barrier without reducing the reasoning demand.


Read problems aloud while the student follows, then have the student retell the scenario in their own words to separate decoding from comprehension. Use a simple graphic organizer — what I know / what I need to find — to hold information visually and free working memory for the calculation. Act out problems or use manipulatives with younger students. Teach math-specific vocabulary explicitly: terms like "altogether," "remaining," "each," and "per" should receive the same direct instruction as reading vocabulary, with visual anchors and repeated contextual use.


## When to adapt a mainstream program instead of switching


Switching curricula can create new gaps and erode confidence. Adapt first when the current program can be modified to address dyslexia-related barriers without replacing its core sequence.


Common adaptations that work across many mainstream texts:


- Reduce copy load with pre-printed problems or by reading problems aloud and covering the board
- Accept verbal responses and reserve writing for representative problems only
- Insert manipulatives before printed representations at each new concept
- Add spaced review problems from prior units into each lesson
- Increase font size and spacing; reduce problems per page to ease visual crowding


Switch if the program's core sequence relies on unmodifiable features: dense text that cannot be read aloud effectively, entirely absent cumulative review, or pacing that cannot be slowed to mastery without breaking the program's structure.


## Accessibility and screen‑time: choosing hands‑on, digital, or a hybrid


The hands-on vs. digital choice has functional implications for dyslexic learners that go beyond personal preference. Manipulatives provide tactile and kinesthetic encoding that can support memory consolidation, while digital platforms typically reduce setup and often include built-in audio support.


For digital programs, verify text-to-speech, captions, and adjustable playback speed before purchasing; lacking these features can make audio reliance inaccessible for some learners. A hybrid approach — manipulative-based core instruction combined with digital spaced practice — often captures the strengths of both formats. Ensure that digital practice supplements rather than replaces guided verbalization and discussion, which remain among the most effective tools for helping dyslexic learners consolidate procedural knowledge.


## Placement tests and leveling: interpreting spiky profiles


Placement tests are valuable precisely because dyslexic learners often show spiky profiles: conceptual strength in some areas alongside significant gaps in others. Use placement results strategically rather than as a single verdict.


Start at the level indicated when placement suggests one or more years below grade level, and frame it as the level where new learning happens efficiently. For mixed results, place at the level of the foundational skill that blocks further progress rather than the highest area of strength. Reassure students who resist lower placement by explaining that solid foundations accelerate long-term progress — an explanation that is both honest and motivating. Reassess placement annually and after extended breaks, since retrieval and working-memory demands change with disuse.


## Progress monitoring: when to move on, spiral back, or reteach


A simple weekly progress-monitoring habit yields reliable decision data in about five minutes per concept cluster. After a cluster, give a short mastery check — three to five representative problems completed independently — and record date, skill, accuracy, and whether errors are procedural or conceptual.


Decision rules to apply consistently:


- Move on when the student scores 80%+ accuracy across two sessions with inconsistent errors
- Loop back when errors are systematic and repeat the same step incorrectly, indicating a specific reteaching target
- Reteach using a different entry point — a new manipulative, visual model, or verbal script — when accuracy stays below 60% even with prompting

## IEP/504 alignment: accommodations that pair with any curriculum


Accommodations do not replace instruction; they shape how the curriculum is delivered so a student's disability does not block demonstration of competence. Common math accommodations for dyslexia include extended time, reduced writing requirements through oral responses or scribe support, and read-aloud of instructions and word problems via an adult or text-to-speech tool.


Additional accommodations that pair well with any curriculum: calculator use when computation is not the target skill, alternate response formats such as pointing or manipulatives, preferential seating with uncluttered materials, graphic organizers, and elimination of copying tasks when copying is irrelevant to the skill being measured. Write IEP goals that name the skill, the condition, and the mastery criterion — for example: solving five two-digit addition problems with regrouping using a visual model and verbal explanation at 80% accuracy across three sessions. That specificity keeps accommodations focused on enabling performance rather than lowering standards, and it gives you a precise basis for progress monitoring.
