# Is a conventional loan or FHA the right mortgage for you?

#### KEY POINTS


- FHA loans may be a better option for borrowers with lower credit scores, limited credit history, smaller down payments or higher debt-to-income ratios.
- Conventional loans can offer lower long-term costs because private mortgage insurance may be removed once sufficient home equity is reached.
- The right mortgage depends on your overall financial picture, including your credit profile, down payment, debt levels and future homeownership plans.


Buying a home comes with plenty of decisions. How much can you afford? Which neighborhood is the right fit? What type of mortgage should you get?


For many buyers, the answer to the last question is a choice between two common options—a Federal Housing Administration (FHA) loan or a conventional loan—and the decision comes down to more than whether you have owned a house before.


As Julie Haddock, a home loans market leader at BOK Financial Mortgage®, explained, “The biggest misconception with FHA is that FHA loans are only for first-time homebuyers, and that's just not the case. There are many situations where buyers have already owned a home where FHA is still a better option for them.”


The same goes for conventional financing. Many buyers assume they need a 20% down payment to qualify, but Haddock said that's another common myth. “In reality, on conventional loans, you can put as little as 3% down,” she noted.


And so, if neither loan is as straightforward as many people think, how do you decide?


The answer comes down to your credit profile, down payment, debt level and long-term financial goals, Haddock said.


**When an FHA loan may make sense**


Not every buyer has perfect credit, years of established borrowing history or a large down payment saved up. That's where FHA loans often come into the picture.


According to Haddock, FHA loans can be a good fit for borrowers with credit scores in the 600s, limited credit history, smaller down payments or higher debt-to-income ratios. That’s because FHA guidelines tend to be more flexible in those situations than conventional financing.


For instance, imagine a buyer who's financially stable but hasn't had much opportunity to build savings, or someone who pays bills responsibly but doesn't have a long credit history because they've relied mostly on debit cards or cash. Those borrowers may find FHA financing offers a more realistic path to homeownership.


**When a conventional loan may be the better choice**


Conventional loans tend to be a better fit when a borrower has stronger credit, more savings and lower debt relative to income, Haddock said. While conventional loans don't require 20% down, borrowers who can contribute more upfront may have opportunities to reduce their long-term mortgage insurance costs, she added.


In fact, that’s where one of the biggest differences between FHA and conventional loans comes into play: mortgage insurance.


**Why mortgage insurance matters**


Mortgage insurance protects the lender in the event a borrower defaults on the loan. Many buyers know they'll likely encounter it, but fewer understand how differently it works depending on the type of mortgage they choose.


With an FHA loan, borrowers pay both an upfront mortgage insurance premium and an annual premium that's included in their monthly payment. The upfront premium is 1.75% of the loan amount and is typically financed into the mortgage, Haddock explained.


However, the bigger issue for many borrowers is how long that insurance remains in place, she noted. For FHA loans with less than 10% down, mortgage insurance generally remains for the life of the loan. With down payments of 10% or more, it's typically required for 11 years.


With conventional loans, borrowers who put down less than 20% may still need private mortgage insurance, but that coverage can usually be removed once enough equity has been built, Haddock said. In most cases, borrowers can request removal when the loan reaches 80% loan-to-value, and it falls off automatically at 78%.


That distinction can make a significant difference over time, which is why it’s often the better option for borrowers who can afford a larger down payment. “If you're going to put 15% down, then you would probably want to go the conventional route,” Haddock said.


**What about loan limits?**


Both FHA and conventional loans are subject to loan limits, which determine the maximum amount you can borrow.


FHA loan limits are set by the Federal Housing Administration, while conventional loans follow conforming loan limits established by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Those limits vary by county and are updated periodically.


For most buyers, loan limits won’t affect the mortgage decision, Haddock noted. However, if you're shopping in a higher-priced market or considering a more expensive home, it's worth discussing with a mortgage lender to understand whether loan limits could influence your financing options.


**Looking beyond the monthly payment**


When comparing mortgage options, it’s easy to focus on interest rates or whichever loan produces the lowest payment today.


However, Haddock encourages borrowers to take a longer view. “It's important to look at the total cost over time,” she said. For example, an FHA loan may be the better choice if it allows someone with limited savings or higher debt levels to qualify for a home purchase. At the same time, a borrower with strong credit and a larger down payment might find that a conventional loan becomes less expensive in the years ahead because mortgage insurance can eventually disappear.


Future plans matter, too.


Some homeowners anticipate selling a previous residence after purchasing a new one and using those proceeds to make a large payment toward their mortgage balance. In some situations, conventional loans offer additional flexibility, such as the ability to recast the loan after a substantial principal payment, which can lower future monthly payments. FHA loans typically don't offer the same option, Haddock explained.


**Why talking with a lender matters**


Many buyers begin their home search with assumptions about what they can afford, what their credit score is or which loan they think they’ll qualify for.


The reality is often more complicated. A mortgage credit score may differ from the score a consumer sees through a banking app or credit-monitoring service. Income, commission earnings and debt obligations may also be evaluated differently during the mortgage approval process, Haddock said.


That's why she recommends speaking with a mortgage professional before deciding which loan is right for you. A lender can review your financial picture, compare options side by side and help you understand both the immediate and long-term costs associated with each choice.


“There is no right or wrong answer between conventional and FHA,” Haddock said. “It really just depends on your scenario.”
