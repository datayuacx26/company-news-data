# The only way to see 'Hamilton': In the room where it happens

As soon as I turned onto 46th Street and spotted the goldenrod marquee beaming with the *Hamilton* logo, I was starstruck. Sure, the historic Richard Rodgers Theatre is the venue where the **[11-time Tony Award-winning musical](https://ad.doubleclick.net/ddm/trackclk/N30602.3428069TRAVELZOOUSA/B34683913.453027813;dc_trk_aid=646551906;dc_trk_cid=261297963;dc_lat=;dc_rdid=;tag_for_child_directed_treatment=;tfua=;ltd=;dc_tdv=1)** comes to life eight times a week—but even among the 41 theaters on Broadway, it represents so much more.


These are the exact doors Lin-Manuel Miranda walked through that transformed him from New York playwright into a household name. These are the actual steps in front of the character silhouettes on which Leslie Odom Jr., Ariana DeBose and Jonathan Groff sang pre-show Ham4Ham songs in its early years. And these are the very glimmering lightbulbs that have made the word *Hamilton* synonymous with its global legacy, as the banner stretching across the theater reads: “History is Happening in Manhattan.”


Indeed, that is exactly what is happening in the very spot I’m standing. Sure, watching the filmed version on TV at home is a wonderful way to tiptoe into the world of the musical about the Founding Father. But as I step into the theater, I immediately realize that nothing can replicate experiencing *Hamilton* **[live on Broadway](https://ad.doubleclick.net/ddm/trackclk/N30602.3428069TRAVELZOOUSA/B34683913.453027813;dc_trk_aid=646551906;dc_trk_cid=261297963;dc_lat=;dc_rdid=;tag_for_child_directed_treatment=;tfua=;ltd=;dc_tdv=1)** .


## **The Thrill is in the Details**


Once the lights dimmed, I was transported 250 years back into living history. In a year when our country is celebrating its **[semiquincentennial](https://ad.doubleclick.net/ddm/trackclk/N30602.3428069TRAVELZOOUSA/B34683913.453027813;dc_trk_aid=646551906;dc_trk_cid=261297963;dc_lat=;dc_rdid=;tag_for_child_directed_treatment=;tfua=;ltd=;dc_tdv=1?https://hamiltonmusical.com/new-york/nyc-tourism/?utm_source=travelzoo&utm_medium=display&utm_campaign=rpm_hamilton_2026&utm_content=native-article_conversion_nyctravelers_national)** , there is no better way to connect with our nation’s past than by having the actors portraying the Founding Fathers make eye contact with you while recounting their stories. That connection—for a moment in time—is what Broadway is all about.


In so many ways, *Hamilton* live is a true spectator sport. As we followed the journey, we laughed, applauded, and screamed out loud ... like the cheers erupting after “Immigrants, we get the job done!” There was a communal unity with my fellow theatergoers in the shared joy of just being there together in a way that’s impossible from the couch at home.


Here, we could be taken by the little details that just can’t be relayed on the screen. I sensed Eliza Schuyler’s heartbreak by smelling the smoke from the flames (spoiler alert!) when she set her love letters on fire in “Burn.” When George Washington reckoned with giving up his leadership power in “One Last Time,” I felt the emotional pull of being right in front of someone with tears in his eyes.


And throughout the whole show, there was the dazzling power of theater magic, showcased through the rotating stage as I chose my own adventure: studying the conductor's arms keeping the show in sync through the orchestra pit opening below, focusing on a company member reacting in the background, or honing in on the thrill of a fellow audience member catching a piece of confetti from the stage. I had the freedom to make the *Hamilton* experience entirely my own.


Yet there was a constant that all of us in the audience could literally feel together: the reverberation from the live orchestra music, a unifying throughline of the beauty of live performance.


## **An Immersive Experience**


From the World Cup to major concert tours, this summer has been all about actually being there for the moments. There’s an innate camaraderie of being immersed among the hundreds of other theatergoers as the pre-show buzz hums through the 1,321-seat venue—that feeling of strangers coming together for a common purpose.


Behind me, a traveler from Istanbul chatted up an American mother-daughter next to her, bonding over their obsession with the musical and the anticipation of finally being in the theater. Next to me, a teen boy had traveled here with his grandmother, while a multigenerational family filed in behind me, and a group of work colleagues settled in ahead of me. Witnessing this diverse group of people coming together made me feel even more grateful to be part of the growing legacy of this Broadway phenomenon.


During intermission, palpable excitement exploded into chatty conversations. A woman in front of me Facetimed her family, showing off her seat as they conversed in Spanish. The boy next to me called a friend, completely awestruck by what he had just been part of. After he hung up, he turned to his grandma and said, “I must have gotten chills at least 10 times!”
