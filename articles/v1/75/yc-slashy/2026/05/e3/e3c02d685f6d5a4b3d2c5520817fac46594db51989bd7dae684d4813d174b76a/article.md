# How to write a follow-up email that gets a reply

Most replies are lost to silence, not rejection. The recipient meant to answer, the email slid down the inbox, and the moment passed. A good follow-up is not nagging. It is a second, easier chance for someone who already wanted to say yes. The catch is that a bad follow-up ("just checking in", "bumping this up", "any thoughts?") gives the reader nothing new to act on, so it gets ignored a second time. This guide covers the structure, the exact wording, and the cadence that turns a dead thread back into a conversation.


## Step 1: Decide whether a follow-up is even the right move


Before you write anything, ask one question: did the last email give the reader a clear, low-effort action? If the original was a vague "let me know what you think", the problem is not that they forgot. The problem is they did not know what you wanted. In that case, do not "follow up" on the old email. Send a new, sharper ask instead.


Follow-ups work when the original request was clear and the reader simply ran out of attention. They fail when you are reheating an unclear request. So the first move is honest triage: was the ball in their court, with an obvious next step, or did your first email leave them with homework?


## Step 2: Open with context, not an apology


Do not start with "Sorry to bother you" or "I know you're busy." Apologizing frames your own message as an interruption and trains the reader to treat it as skippable. Instead, anchor the reader in the original thread in one line so they do not have to scroll or remember.


Reply on the same thread whenever you can. Keeping it in the existing chain preserves the subject line, the prior context, and any attachments, so the reader has zero reconstruction cost. A fresh email with a new subject forces them to rebuild the situation from memory, which is exactly the friction that kills replies.


One clean opener: "Following up on the proposal I sent last Tuesday." That is it. Context set, no groveling.


## Step 3: Make the ask a single, specific, yes/no-able question


The most common reason a follow-up dies is that it asks for "thoughts." Thoughts are open-ended and expensive to produce. A reply-worthy follow-up narrows the ask to one decision the reader can make in seconds.


Compare these:


-


Weak: "Any updates on the contract?"


-


Strong: "Are you good to sign the contract this week, or should I push the start date to the 15th?"


The strong version offers two concrete options, so the reader can reply with one word. Give people a question they can answer without thinking, and far more of them will answer.


If you genuinely need a longer response, say what you need and how long it will take: "Could you send the two logos by Thursday? Should be a 5-minute pull from your brand folder." Naming the effort lowers the perceived cost of replying.


## Step 4: Get the timing and cadence right


Send the first follow-up 2 to 3 business days after the original, not the next morning. Same-day nudges read as anxious and arrive before the reader has had a fair chance to act. A few business days is long enough to be reasonable and short enough that the thread is still warm.


For sales or partnership threads, a workable cadence is roughly:


-


Follow-up 1: 2 to 3 business days after the first email


-


Follow-up 2: 4 to 5 business days after that


-


Follow-up 3: about a week later, framed as a soft close


-


Then stop, or move to a different channel


Space each touch a little wider than the last. And send during the reader's working hours in their time zone, not yours. An email that lands at 6am their time gets buried under the morning flood.


## Step 5: Write a real subject line and end with an easy out


If you must start a new thread, the subject line carries the open. "Quick question on the Q3 numbers" beats "Following up" because it tells the reader what is inside. Specific subjects get opened. Vague ones get archived.


Close with a genuine off-ramp on your final touch: "If now is not the right time, just say the word and I will check back next quarter." Giving the reader permission to say no often produces a yes, because it removes the pressure that makes people avoid the reply altogether. An easy out also keeps the relationship intact for later.


## Copy-paste follow-up templates


Here are three templates you can adapt. Keep them short. The whole message should be readable in one screen without scrolling.


Gentle first nudge:


> Subject: (reply on the original thread)
>
>
> Hi Priya,
>
>
> Following up on the partnership deck I sent last Tuesday. Are you open to a 20-minute call this week to walk through it? Tuesday or Thursday afternoon both work on my end.
>
>
> If the timing is off, no problem, just let me know and I will work around your schedule.
>
>
> Thanks, Sam


Decision-forcing second nudge:


> Hi Priya,
>
>
> Circling back on the partnership deck. Two quick options: we can lock in the pilot for a June 1 start, or push to July if your team needs more runway. Which works better for you?
>
>
> Happy to answer anything before you decide.
>
>
> Sam


Soft close (final touch):


> Hi Priya,
>
>
> I do not want to keep cluttering your inbox, so this is my last note on the deck for now. If a pilot is interesting this quarter, I would love to set it up. If not, I will check back in the fall.
>
>
> Either way, thanks for the time.
>
>
> Sam
