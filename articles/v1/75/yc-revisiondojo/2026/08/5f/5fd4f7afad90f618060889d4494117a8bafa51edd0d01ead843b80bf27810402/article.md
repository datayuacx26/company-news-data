# Best IB Subject Combinations for Medicine

Choosing IB subjects can feel uncomfortably permanent. A parent sees six boxes on a form; a student sees two years of lessons, assessments, and examinations. Somewhere beyond both sits a medical school admissions page with requirements that may change by country and university.


The reassuring answer is straightforward: **for the widest access to undergraduate medicine, the safest combination is Biology HL and Chemistry HL, followed by a third HL subject in which the student can earn a strong grade** . Mathematics, Physics, Psychology, and a language can all work as the third HL, depending on university requirements and the student's strengths.


This is not a universal rule. Oxford currently requires Chemistry HL plus at least one of Biology, Physics, or Mathematics at HL, while institutions such as UCL require both Biology and Chemistry at HL. Australian prerequisites vary considerably, and medicine in the United States is normally entered after an undergraduate degree. The right decision therefore begins with a shortlist, not with assumptions.

### The short answer for parents


Use this checklist before confirming any IB HL and SL subjects:


- Put **Biology and Chemistry at HL** if the goal is to preserve the broadest range of direct-entry medicine options.
- Select the third HL according to both admissions requirements and genuine academic strength.
- Check at least five likely medical programs across the countries being considered.
- Record required subjects, required levels, minimum HL grades, total IB scores, and any mathematics or English conditions.
- Avoid choosing four HL subjects merely because the combination appears more impressive.
- Recheck every requirement on the university's official admissions page before applying.


Parents who want a broader framework may also find this guide to[discussing IB subject choices and careers](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/ib-subject-choices-and-careers) helpful. The goal is not to dictate a combination. It is to ensure that the student understands which choices open doors and which may quietly close them.


### Why Biology HL and Chemistry HL are usually safest


Medicine is not one admission system. It is hundreds of institutions making slightly different decisions about academic preparation.


Chemistry HL is especially important for UK applicants. Some universities pair it with Biology, while others accept another laboratory science or Mathematics. Biology HL remains the prudent companion because several competitive programs explicitly require both sciences at Higher Level.


This combination also gives students relevant preparation. Biology develops understanding of systems, cellular processes, genetics, physiology, and experimental interpretation. Chemistry builds quantitative reasoning and an understanding of structure, reactions, equilibrium, energetics, and measurement. Neither guarantees admission, but together they satisfy more prerequisite patterns than most alternatives.


Students debating the second science can explore the practical differences between[IB Chemistry and Physics for medicine](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/should-you-choose-ib-chemistry-or-ib-physics-for-medicine) . Physics can be valuable and is accepted by some universities, but it does not replace Biology everywhere.

### Biology, Chemistry, and Mathematics HL


This is often treated as the default high-achieving combination. It is rigorous, coherent, and suitable for students who genuinely enjoy mathematical problem-solving.


But rigor is useful only when it produces sustainable performance. Most medical programs do not automatically prefer Mathematics HL over a different third HL. If Mathematics HL is likely to absorb time needed for Chemistry, Biology, the Extended Essay, or admissions preparation, it may weaken rather than strengthen the overall profile.


The choice between mathematics courses also requires institution-level checking. Oxford currently states that it has no preference between Mathematics: Analysis and Approaches and Mathematics: Applications and Interpretation when Mathematics HL is used to meet its medicine requirements. That does not establish a rule for every university. ### Biology, Chemistry, and Psychology HL


Psychology can complement medicine through research methods, critical evaluation, and the study of behaviour. It may also suit a student whose strengths extend beyond calculation-heavy subjects.


However, it should be viewed as the third HL rather than a substitute for a required science. The distinction matters because IB Psychology belongs to Group 3, not Group 4. Families considering it can read whether[IB Psychology suits aspiring medical students](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/is-ib-psychology-a-good-subject-for-aspiring-med-students-honest-insights-and-strategy) .


### Biology, Chemistry, and a strong language or humanity


A future doctor does not need every HL to look clinical. Medicine requires comprehension, communication, structured argument, and judgement under uncertainty. A language, History, English, or another humanity may therefore be a sensible third choice when it aligns with the student's abilities.


The strategic principle is simple: meet the non-negotiable science prerequisites, then protect the likelihood of excellent overall results. A balanced combination is not a less ambitious one.

### How HL and SL choices affect medicine applications


The IB normally involves six subjects, with at least three and no more than four studied at Higher Level. According to the IB, an HL course represents 240 recommended teaching hours, compared with 150 at SL. That difference explains why changing one subject level can reshape a student's entire week.


For medicine, universities may specify not only the subjects but also their levels and grades. An excellent total score does not normally compensate for missing a compulsory HL prerequisite. Conversely, once the required sciences are present, adding another difficult HL does not necessarily create an admissions advantage.


A sensible pattern is therefore:


- **HL:** Biology, Chemistry, and the student's strongest suitable third subject.
- **SL:** Mathematics, languages, and humanities selected to satisfy the diploma and any university-specific conditions.


This is a starting framework rather than a prescription. ### Country matters more than many families expect


### United Kingdom and other direct-entry systems


UK medicine programs often publish precise IB conditions. Current examples show why checking individual universities is essential: Oxford requires Chemistry HL and another approved science or Mathematics at HL, whereas UCL specifies Biology and Chemistry at HL. Requirements may also include minimum total points, particular HL grades, English or Mathematics conditions, an admissions test, and an interview.


Requirements elsewhere in Europe and Asia vary by institution, applicant status, and language of instruction. Do not apply a UK combination automatically to another country.


### Australia


Australian medical pathways are diverse. Some direct-entry courses specify Chemistry or Mathematics, while others focus more heavily on academic rank, an admissions test, and an interview. Certain programs recommend Biology without making it a formal prerequisite. Applicants should also distinguish direct-entry, provisional-entry, and graduate-entry routes before comparing requirements.


### United States and Canada


In the United States, students typically complete an undergraduate degree before medical school. The Association of American Medical Colleges notes that prerequisite coursework varies, although biology, English, and chemistry commonly appear. IB Biology and Chemistry can provide useful preparation, but high-school subject selection is not equivalent to completing university premedical requirements.


Canadian pathways also vary and generally require university study first. Families considering North America should choose an IB combination that supports admission to an appropriate undergraduate course without assuming that it provides direct medical entry.


### Common subject-selection mistakes


The first mistake is searching for one globally perfect combination. There is none. The best IB subjects for medicine depend on where the student may apply.


The second is treating difficulty as a signal of commitment. Three demanding sciences or four HLs may sound formidable, but universities primarily need evidence that published requirements have been met. An overloaded student still has to manage internal assessments, TOK, the Extended Essay, CAS, examinations, and potentially admissions testing.


The third is overlooking SL conditions. A medical program may specify acceptable Mathematics, English, or language qualifications even when those subjects are not central to the degree.


The fourth is checking requirements once and assuming they will remain unchanged. Admissions rules can change between subject selection and application. Keep a dated spreadsheet and revisit official course pages before each major decision.


### Turn the right combination into strong results


Subject selection protects eligibility. Preparation turns that eligibility into a competitive application.


Once Biology HL and Chemistry HL are chosen, students should build a repeatable cycle: learn a concept, retrieve it without notes, answer exam-style questions, examine errors, and revisit weak areas under time pressure. Reading alone can create familiarity without reliable recall.

### A calm final decision


For most students pursuing direct-entry medicine, **Biology HL and Chemistry HL are the safest foundation** . The third HL should meet any remaining university requirements while giving the student a realistic opportunity to earn an excellent grade.


Before confirming the subjects, build a university shortlist, verify every prerequisite on official admissions pages, and discuss the workload honestly with the school. Then stop searching for a combination that looks perfect to everyone. Choose the one that keeps the right doors open and gives the student enough capacity to walk through them.
