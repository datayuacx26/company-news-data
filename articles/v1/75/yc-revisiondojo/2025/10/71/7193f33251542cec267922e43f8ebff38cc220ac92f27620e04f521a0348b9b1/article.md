# Academic Honesty in IB Biology Labs: Do the Data…

In the middle of a lab, it’s rarely the microscope that tests you.


It’s the moment you see a data point that “shouldn’t” be there.


Maybe your catalase bubbles slow down for no reason. Maybe your quadrat count spikes in the one location you were sure would be average. And suddenly you feel that quiet pressure: *If I adjust it slightly, the graph will look cleaner. The conclusion will be easier. The grade will feel safer.*


In **IB Biology** , that moment matters more than the final trendline. Because the course isn’t only asking whether you can run an investigation. It’s asking whether you can be trusted with evidence.


This guide is about academic honesty in **IB Biology** labs: how to record data responsibly, report results transparently, and protect your work (and your exam readiness) without sliding into common integrity traps.


### Academic honesty in IB Biology labs: a fast checklist


Keep this checklist in your notebook so you don’t have to “remember” integrity when you’re tired.


-


Record raw data immediately (with units and uncertainties).


-


Keep every trial, including anomalies and failed runs.


-


Describe what you actually did, not what the method “was supposed” to be.


-


Separate raw results from processed calculations.


-


Cite any background theory, images, or protocols you didn’t create.


-


Collaborate on planning, but write your report independently.


-


Use digital tools (spreadsheets, AI) to support clarity, not replace thinking.

### What academic honesty looks like in IB Biology (in real life)


Academic honesty in **IB Biology** isn’t just “don’t copy.” In labs, it’s the daily practice of three habits:


### Authenticity


Your measurements, your tables, your graphs, your evaluation. Even if you used shared class equipment or a group setup, your thinking must be your own.


### Transparency


You show the full story of the investigation: what happened, what went wrong, what you changed, and where your background information came from.


### Reflection


You treat uncertainty like a feature of science, not a flaw in your character. Strong **IB Biology** evaluations don’t hide mess; they explain it.

### Recording data responsibly in IB Biology labs: accuracy over aesthetics


In **IB Biology** , the cleanest report is not the most honest report.


A real scientist’s notebook is allowed to look like reality: crossed-out numbers, rushed notes, a reminder that the room was hotter than expected, and a comment like “accidentally used 0.2 M instead of 0.1 M for trial 2.” Those details aren’t embarrassing. They’re evidence that the work happened.


Here are practical ways to record data responsibly:


### Write it down as you observe it


Don’t “hold it in your head” and rewrite later. If you’re measuring heart rate, record the count immediately. If you’re timing a reaction, note the exact time and conditions right away.


### Keep raw and processed data clearly separate


Raw data is what you directly measured (mass, time, temperature, number of colonies). Processed data is what you calculate from it (mean, standard deviation, rate, percentage change). In **IB Biology** , mixing them makes it harder to prove your results are authentic.


### Record units and uncertainties every time


A number without a unit is a rumour. A measurement without uncertainty is pretending tools are perfect. Both are avoidable.


### Don’t delete “bad” trials


If a trial failed because of a genuine procedural issue, keep it and label it. If it failed for unknown reasons, keep it and discuss it. Either way, you learn more by keeping reality than by polishing it.

### Reporting results responsibly: don’t “massage” the graph


Most integrity problems in **IB Biology** labs aren’t dramatic. They’re subtle.


It’s nudging one number. Rounding a little too aggressively. Omitting one point because it “ruins the line.” Calling something an outlier without explaining why.


Responsible reporting means:


### Show anomalies, then interpret them


An anomaly is not a stain on your report. It’s a clue.


If your data point is unusually high, ask:


-


Was there a measurement error (timing, parallax, misreading the meniscus)?


-


Was a controlled variable not controlled (temperature drift, light intensity change)?


-


Is the biology itself variable (living systems often are)?


In **IB Biology** , a thoughtful discussion of anomalies often reads more “scientific” than a perfect result.


### Be honest about limitations


“Human reaction time affected timing” is fine, but better is: *how much* could it affect it, and *where* in your method did it matter most?


### Don’t retrofit the conclusion


Write conclusions that follow the evidence you recorded, not the hypothesis you hoped would be true.

### Referencing in IB Biology lab reports: credit builds credibility


Even if your experiment is hands-on, your report still leans on other people’s work: definitions, biological theory, diagrams of enzymes, or a standard method for serial dilution.


To reference ethically in **IB Biology** :


-


Cite background information you didn’t generate yourself.


-


Credit any diagram you didn’t draw (and any image you adapted).


-


Avoid copying procedural language from online lab write-ups; describe your method in your own words.


-


Keep a simple running list of sources while you research so you’re not scrambling at the end.

### Collaboration without collusion in IB Biology labs


Group work is normal in **IB Biology** . One set of equipment, one bench, four people, and a shared sense of urgency.


The boundary is simple to state but easy to blur:


-


Collaborate on planning and setup.


-


Collaborate on safety.


-


Discuss results.


-


Write your own report, with your own interpretation and wording.


If you collected a shared dataset, say so. Transparency is not a weakness. It’s a protective layer.


Photocopier screams about collusion


### Technology and AI in IB Biology labs: use tools, keep ownership


A spreadsheet can help you calculate a mean. A graphing tool can help you visualise a trend. An AI assistant can help you clarify writing.


But in **IB Biology** , you must still be able to explain every step:


-


Why did you choose that graph type?


-


What does the gradient represent biologically?


-


Why did you include or exclude a point?


-


How did you calculate uncertainty?


A good rule: if a tool gives you output you can’t defend, you can’t ethically include it.
