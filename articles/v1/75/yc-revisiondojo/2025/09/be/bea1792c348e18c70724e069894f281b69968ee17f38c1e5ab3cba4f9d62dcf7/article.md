# 3 in IB Math: How to Get Back to a 4+

### The moment you realize “I’m not failing… but I’m not safe either”


A **3 in IB Math** can feel strangely confusing. You’re not completely lost. You’ve answered *some* questions correctly. But when the grade lands, it quietly says: *you understand parts of the IB course, yet the basics aren’t reliable under pressure.*


And in **IB** , reliability is the whole game. Not brilliance, not lucky questions, not last-minute motivation. Just being able to reproduce methods when the clock is loud.


The good news: moving from a 3 to a 4 is one of the most realistic jumps in IB Math, because you’re not starting from zero. You’re usually missing consistency, method marks, and a small set of repeat topics.


Student vs the method marks superhero tool


### Quick checklist: your “3 to 4” rescue plan


Use this short IB-focused checklist before you change anything:


-


Check where you’re dropping marks: Paper 1 vs Paper 2 vs coursework.


-


Identify your top 3 repeat weaknesses (usually algebra, functions, trig, or stats).


-


Practice **non-calculator** techniques weekly.


-


Use the formula/data booklet actively (not passively).


-


Build a feedback loop: attempt, mark, fix, re-attempt.

### What a 3 in IB Math actually means


In the **IB** grade descriptors, a 3 generally signals partial achievement: you can handle familiar procedures but struggle when questions twist the context or require multi-step reasoning.


Practically, that looks like:


-


You can start problems but don’t finish cleanly.


-


Your algebra slips when you’re stressed.


-


You get the idea, but you don’t show enough working to earn method marks.


-


You rely on your calculator for steps the IB expects you to do by hand.


A 3 is also risky because **IB** considers 4 the “safe” passing benchmark for many goals, especially if math is one of your tougher subjects.

### You’re losing “invisible” method marks


In **IB** Math, the final answer is only part of the score. If your working is unclear, you can’t collect the marks that were designed to reward correct setup. Practicing with feedback is key, which is why targeted drilling in a question bank helps.


Try a focused session using[Number and Algebra - IB Questionbank](https://www.revisiondojo.com/ib/ib-math-ai/math-ai-number-and-algebra/questionbank) or AA-specific practice like[Calculus Topic Questionbank (Math AA)](https://www.revisiondojo.com/ib/ib-math-aa/calculus/questionbank) .


### You don’t train the “no calculator” muscle


Paper 1 punishes over-dependence. The fastest way to climb in **IB** is to become calm with exact values, algebraic manipulation, and sketching.

### You revise what feels productive, not what moves marks


Many IB students over-study one comfortable unit (often calculus) while ignoring high-frequency foundations like functions and trig.

### How to move from a 3 to a 4 in IB Math (step-by-step)


### Secure the easiest marks first


Your goal is not to “learn everything” overnight. Your goal is to make the most common IB question types predictable.


Start with:


-


algebra accuracy (rearranging, factoring, solving)


-


functions (domain/range, transformations, intercepts)


-


trig basics (identities, exact angles, equations)


-


core stats/probability routines


Then add one harder extension per week.


### Practice like the IB examiner is watching


Each time you solve, force yourself to write:


-


what you’re finding


-


the method (even if it feels obvious)


-


the final answer in correct form

### Turn errors into a system (not a mood)


After every timed set, write a short “error log”:


-


What type of mistake was it (concept, algebra, interpretation, time)?


-


What would a perfect solution *look like* ?


-


Which micro-skill should I drill tomorrow?
