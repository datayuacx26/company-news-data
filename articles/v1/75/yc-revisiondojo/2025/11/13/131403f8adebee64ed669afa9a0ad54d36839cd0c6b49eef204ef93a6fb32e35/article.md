# Abiotic Factor in IB Biology: A Clear, Exam-Ready…

### The moment a “non-living” thing starts controlling everything


In **IB Biology** , there’s a quiet plot twist hiding in almost every ecology question: the most powerful forces in an ecosystem often aren’t alive.


A sudden temperature drop. A lake with low dissolved oxygen. Soil pH that locks nutrients away. None of these “do” anything in the way a predator hunts or a plant grows. And yet, they decide who survives, where species can live, and why your data-based exam question has three confusing axes.


So, what is an abiotic factor in **IB Biology** ? Let’s make it simple, memorable, and easy to use in exam responses.


Stressed student meets abiotic reality


### What is an abiotic factor in IB Biology?


In **IB Biology** , an **abiotic factor** is a **non-living physical or chemical part of the environment** that affects organisms’ survival, growth, and reproduction.


Abiotic factors are different from **biotic factors** , which involve living interactions like competition, predation, disease, and symbiosis.


### Common abiotic factors you’re expected to know


-


**Temperature** (controls enzyme activity and metabolism)


-


**Light intensity** (limits photosynthesis and influences behaviour)


-


**Water availability** (affects transport, reproduction, and plant turgor)


-


**pH** (affects nutrient availability and protein function)


-


**Salinity** (affects osmosis and water balance)


-


**Oxygen availability** (especially important in aquatic ecosystems)


-


**Soil composition/mineral content** (affects plant growth and productivity)

### Quick checklist: how to answer abiotic factor questions in IB Biology


When you see an abiotic-factor prompt in **IB Biology** , run this quick mental checklist:

### How abiotic factors shape ecosystems (and your marks)


Abiotic factors in **IB Biology** are rarely “background info.” They often explain the entire pattern in the question.


### Abiotic factors and species distribution


Species don’t live everywhere they could in theory. They live where abiotic conditions sit inside their tolerance range. That’s why you see zonation on rocky shores, plant gradients along mountains, and different communities across biomes.


RevisionDojo’s notes on this are especially exam-useful:[B4.1.3 Abiotic variables affecting species distribution](https://www.revisiondojo.com/ib/ib-biology-new/ib-biology-new-b413-abiotic-variables-affecting-species-distribution/notes) .


### Abiotic factors and adaptation


If an environment is consistently hot, dry, salty, or acidic, selection pressures build. Over time, populations shift toward traits that cope better with those abiotic constraints.


To connect abiotic factors to adaptations (a common **IB Biology** move), see:[B4.1.2 Adaptations of organisms to the abiotic environment](https://www.revisiondojo.com/ib/ib-biology-new/ib-biology-new-b412-adaptations-of-organisms-to-the-abiotic-environme-28924/notes) .


### Abiotic factors as selection pressures


A key exam phrasing is that many abiotic factors act as **density-independent** pressures. A frost, heatwave, or pollution event doesn’t care how crowded the population is; it still hits.


This is covered clearly here:[D4.1.4 Abiotic factors as selection pressures](https://www.revisiondojo.com/ib/ib-biology-new/ib-biology-new-d414-abiotic-factors-as-selection-pressures/notes) .
