# Heat Pump vs Furnace: Which Is Right for Your Home? (2026)

If you are replacing your heating or cooling, or outfitting a home you just bought, you will hit this fork fast: stick with a furnace, or switch to a heat pump? The two do overlapping jobs in very different ways, and the right answer for a Bay Area home is usually clearer than the amount of conflicting advice online would suggest. Here is the honest comparison, what actually separates them, and where each one still makes sense.


## The short answer


For most Bay Area homes, a heat pump is the better choice. It heats and cools with a single system, runs two to three times more efficient than a gas furnace, and fits the mild local climate almost perfectly. A furnace only heats, so you still need a separate air conditioner for cooling, which means two systems to buy, maintain, and eventually replace. A furnace mainly still makes sense in very cold climates or when you have a newer furnace you are not ready to replace yet. In the Bay Area, the heat pump wins on comfort, running cost, and simplicity.

## The core difference: one system that does both, versus two that each do one


A furnace makes heat. It burns natural gas to warm air and pushes it through your ducts. That is all it does, so for cooling you need a separate air conditioner alongside it. Two machines, two sets of maintenance, two things that can eventually fail.


A heat pump moves heat instead of making it. In winter it pulls heat from the outside air and brings it in. In summer it runs in reverse and pushes heat out, which is exactly how an air conditioner works. One system, both jobs. Because it moves heat rather than burning fuel to create it, it delivers far more heating and cooling per dollar of energy.


That difference is worth hearing it from someone who installs these every day:


> "A heat pump is the best way to do it. It gives you the most comfort, the quietest operation, and the most efficient performance, and it is more affordable both upfront and long term, because it is simple. One device heats and cools. You do not have a furnace and an AC that both need maintenance and can both fail. First principles, it is the simplest way to heat and cool a home, and you should choose it."

## Why the Bay Area is close to a best case for heat pumps


Heat pumps are most efficient in mild and moderate climates, which is exactly what the Bay Area has. Winters are cool but rarely brutal, and summers are warm without being extreme. That is the sweet spot where a heat pump does its best work and where the "but what about freezing weather" worry simply does not apply. Modern cold-climate heat pumps handle far harsher winters than the Bay Area ever sees, so here you have plenty of margin.


You also get cooling you may not currently have. Plenty of older Bay Area homes were built with a furnace and no AC. Replacing that furnace with a heat pump adds whole-home cooling in the same move, which is becoming a lot more valuable as summers warm up.


## Where a furnace still makes sense


This is not a one-size-fits-all answer, and it is worth being straight about the cases where a furnace is still reasonable. In very cold climates where the temperature sits below freezing for long stretches, a gas furnace or a dual-fuel setup can still be the practical call, though that is not the Bay Area. And if you have a newer furnace that is working fine and you are only trying to add cooling, keeping it and adding an AC is a legitimate short-term choice, even if a heat pump would consolidate both down the road. Outside of those situations, in a mild climate like ours, the reasons to choose a furnace get thin quickly.


## What about cost?


Sticker-to-sticker, a furnace on its own is cheaper than a heat pump. But that is not the real comparison, because a furnace does not cool. Once you add the air conditioner you would need anyway, a heat pump is frequently comparable or cheaper than a furnace-plus-AC pairing, and it costs less to run every month after that. Rebates tilt it further: California rebate programs are aimed at heat pumps, not furnaces, so the incentives land on the heat pump side of the ledger.

## Frequently asked questions


### Is a heat pump better than a furnace?


For most Bay Area homes, yes. A heat pump heats and cools with one efficient system and suits the mild climate, while a furnace only heats and needs a separate air conditioner. The main exception is very cold climates, where a furnace or dual-fuel system can still make sense. In the Bay Area, that exception does not apply.


### Do I need a furnace with a heat pump?


In the Bay Area, no. A heat pump handles both your heating and your cooling on its own, and the local climate is well within its comfort zone. Backup or dual-fuel setups that pair a heat pump with a furnace exist mainly for very cold regions, and they are not necessary here.


### Is a heat pump cheaper to run than a gas furnace?


Usually, yes. Because a heat pump moves heat rather than burning gas to create it, it delivers two to three times more heating per unit of energy, which shows up as lower combined heating and cooling bills, especially when it replaces older equipment.


### Will a heat pump keep my house warm enough in winter?


Yes. Bay Area winters are mild, and even entry-level heat pumps handle them easily. Cold-climate models are rated for far lower temperatures than we get here, so a correctly sized system will keep your home comfortable through the coldest local nights.


### Does a heat pump cool as well as a separate air conditioner?


Yes, because it uses the same technology. A heat pump in cooling mode is essentially an air conditioner, so you get the same cooling performance while also covering your heating, all from one system.


### How much does a heat pump cost compared to a furnace?


A furnace alone is cheaper up front, but it does not cool. Against a furnace-plus-AC replacement, a heat pump is often comparable or cheaper once you count rebates, and it costs less to run. ## The bottom line


A furnace makes heat and needs a second system to cool. A heat pump does both, more efficiently, from one machine, which means less to maintain and less that can fail. In a mild climate like the Bay Area, that simplicity and efficiency add up to more comfort for less money over the life of the system. Unless you are heating through long deep-freeze winters or nursing a newer furnace along, the heat pump is the straightforward choice.
