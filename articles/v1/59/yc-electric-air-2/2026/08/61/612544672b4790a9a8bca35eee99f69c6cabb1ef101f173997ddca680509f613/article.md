# Mini Split vs Central Air: Which Is Right for Your Home? (2026)

When you are choosing how to heat and cool your home, one of the first forks is how the air actually gets around: through ducts from one central unit, or through individual units mounted in each room. That is the real difference between central air and a ductless mini split. Which one fits comes down mostly to whether your home already has good ductwork, and it changes the cost, the install, and how much control you get room to room. Here is the honest comparison.


## The short answer


Central air uses one indoor unit to push heated or cooled air through a network of ducts to the whole house. A ductless mini split skips the ducts entirely, mounting a small unit in each room or zone. If your home already has good ductwork, central air is usually the simpler, cleaner-looking, and cost-effective choice. If your home has no ducts, or leaky old ones, a mini split avoids the large expense and mess of installing ductwork and gives you precise room-by-room control. Both can be heat pumps, so both can heat and cool. For many older Bay Area homes without ducts, a mini split is the standout.

## What each one actually is


Central air is a ducted system. One indoor unit conditions the air, and a network of ducts in your walls, floors, or attic carries it to vents in each room. It is what most people picture when they think of central heating and cooling: hidden equipment, vents in the ceiling or floor, one thermostat for the house.


A ductless mini split has an outdoor unit connected to one or more indoor heads, usually mounted high on a wall, with no ducts at all. Each head conditions the room it is in and has its own control. You can have a single head for one room or several heads covering a whole home, each set to its own temperature.


One important point that cuts through a lot of confusion: both of these can be heat pumps. "Central air" does not have to mean cooling only, and a mini split is not just for one room. A ducted heat pump and a multi-zone mini split heat pump both heat and cool your whole home. The real question here is how the air is distributed, not whether the system can heat.

## When central air is the right call


If your home already has ductwork in decent shape, central air is usually the most straightforward and cost-effective path. A ducted heat pump can often reuse those existing ducts, which keeps the install cost down and gives you clean, hidden equipment with vents instead of wall-mounted heads. For homeowners who want one thermostat, a uniform look, and whole-home conditioning without visible indoor units, ducted is hard to beat, as long as the ducts are worth keeping.


## When a ductless mini split is the right call


If your home has no ducts, a mini split is often the clear winner, because it skips the single biggest hidden cost of going ducted: installing ductwork from scratch, which is expensive and invasive. Mini splits also shine for room-by-room control, so you can keep bedrooms cooler than living areas or condition only the rooms you use. They are ideal for additions, ADUs, converted garages, and older homes that were built with radiators or wall heaters and never had ducts. And because there are no ducts, there is no duct leakage, which is a real efficiency gain in homes where the old ductwork was doing a poor job anyway.


## The Bay Area angle


A lot of Bay Area housing stock is old enough that it either has no ducts or has aging, leaky ducts that are not worth keeping. That tilts many homes here toward ductless. But plenty of homes do have solid ductwork from a central furnace, and for those a ducted heat pump that reuses the existing ducts is often the better value. There is no universal answer. The honest one depends on what is already in your walls, which is exactly the kind of thing worth having someone actually look at before you decide.


## What about cost?


Neither is universally cheaper. A single-zone mini split is inexpensive, but a multi-zone system covering a whole house adds up because you are paying for each indoor head. A ducted system is very cost-effective when it reuses good ducts, but expensive if ducts have to be added or heavily repaired. The deciding cost factor is almost always your existing ductwork. ## Frequently asked questions


### What is the difference between a mini split and central air?


Central air distributes conditioned air through ducts from one central unit. A mini split is ductless, with individual indoor units in each room or zone. The main practical difference is whether your home needs ducts at all.


### Are mini splits cheaper than central air?


It depends on your home. A single-zone mini split is cheaper than a full ducted system, but a multi-zone mini split covering a whole house can cost as much or more, since you pay per indoor head. Central air is most cost-effective when it reuses existing ducts in good condition.


### Is a mini split as good as central air for a whole house?


Yes, a properly sized multi-zone mini split can condition an entire home, and it gives you room-by-room control that central air usually does not. The trade-off is visible indoor units rather than hidden vents.


### Are ductless systems more efficient than central air?


Often, yes, mainly because they have no ducts to leak. Central air can be just as efficient with well-sealed ductwork, but in homes with old or leaky ducts, going ductless removes a significant source of waste.


### Can a mini split heat my home, or just cool it?


It can heat too. A mini split is a heat pump, so it both heats and cools, which is exactly why it can replace an old furnace and add cooling in one system.


## The bottom line


Central air and ductless mini splits both heat and cool a home well. The choice is mostly about your ducts. Good existing ductwork points toward a ducted system that reuses it. No ducts, or ducts not worth saving, points toward a ductless mini split that skips that cost and adds room-by-room control. For a lot of older Bay Area homes, that makes ductless the natural fit, but the only way to know for sure is to look at what your home already has.
