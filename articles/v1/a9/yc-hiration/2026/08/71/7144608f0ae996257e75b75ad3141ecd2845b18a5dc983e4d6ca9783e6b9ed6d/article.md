# USAJOBS Now Caps Federal Resumes at 2 Pages (2025 Rule): What Changed and How to Fit It

## Does USAJOBS really cap federal resumes at two pages now?


Yes. As of September 27, 2025, USAJOBS holds resumes to two pages, and the limit reaches your profile resume, uploaded files, builder resumes, and searchable resumes in the Agency Talent Portal. Go over and you're out of the running for that job, not trimmed to fit. The federal content still has to be there though, so the real work is condensing decades of detail into two pages without dropping the fields that qualify you. Read your job announcement first. It spells out the requirement and any exception.


## The rule that flipped twenty years of federal-resume advice


For most of your working life, one piece of resume advice sat outside the "keep it short" gospel: the federal resume. Everywhere else you fought to hit one page. On USAJOBS you did the opposite. You wrote long, you wrote everything, and you were told the longer and more keyword-dense the better. Five pages was ordinary, eight raised no eyebrows, and people in scientific and technical series ran fifteen pages while nobody blinked.


That is over.


Since September 27, 2025, USAJOBS restricts resumes to two pages. If you built your federal-resume habits any time in the last two decades, almost everything you learned about length now works against you. The reflex to pad, to prove twelve months of specialized experience with a wall of bullets, to list every duty you have ever performed since 2004, is the reflex that gets you screened out today.


Here's the part that trips people up. The cap did not make the federal resume shorter in the way a private-sector one is short. It kept every content requirement that made federal resumes long in the first place and then told you to fit all of it into two pages. So the skill you need now is harder than either writing a sprawling federal resume or writing a tidy corporate one. You have to compress without gutting.


## What actually changed on September 27, 2025


The change is narrow and specific, which is why it catches people off guard. It is not a suggestion buried in a formatting guide. It is a technical limit wired into the system.


As of that date, the two-page limit applies to your resume in every place USAJOBS stores or reads one: the resume saved in your job-seeker profile, any new resume you upload, a resume you make in the USAJOBS resume builder, and searchable resumes in the Agency Talent Portal, the pool recruiters mine for candidates. If you had a searchable resume sitting in that pool, it came out on September 27 and stays out until you post a two-page version back.


Reach matters here. A lot of applicants keep one long master resume in their profile and forget it exists between job hunts. That old resume is now a liability. The cap is not something you meet once at the moment you click apply. It is a standard your stored profile has to satisfy too.


The limit covers competitive-service and excepted-service announcements under Title 5, internal and external. That is the vast majority of what you see posted on USAJOBS. There are edges, which I'll get to, but assume the rule applies unless a specific announcement tells you otherwise.


## Why this one isn't advice. It's a gate


Plenty of resume "rules" are really preferences. A recruiter would rather you keep it to a page, but a good third page won't sink you. This is not that.


OPM's guidance to agencies is blunt about the consequence. If the only resume an applicant submits runs longer than two pages, that applicant is ineligible for further consideration. The rejection notice even has standard wording, telling you the application isn't being considered because it didn't meet the resume page requirement in the announcement.


Sit with that for a second. It doesn't matter how strong your experience is. It doesn't matter that a human would have loved page three. A resume over the limit takes you out of the pool before anyone reads a word of your qualifications. Of every formatting decision in federal hiring right now, this is the one with the highest stakes, because it is the one that can end your candidacy on a mechanical count rather than the merits.


The system is built to hold you to it. USAJOBS pushes you to choose a resume of two pages or less to make it searchable and to apply. You are not going to sneak a longer one through and hope a reviewer forgives it. Plan for the count.


## Where the rule came from


You don't need the policy history to write a good resume, but a sentence of context helps it make sense. The two-page cap is one piece of a broader federal hiring-process reform.


It traces to Executive Order 14170, "Reforming the Federal Hiring Process and Restoring Merit to Government Service," issued in January 2025 and published in the Federal Register on January 30, 2025. OPM's Merit Hiring Plan, dated May 29, 2025, is what actually implements the change. It prohibits the old longform federal resume structure and sets the two-page limit in its place. OPM announced implementation in a release dated September 2, 2025, a few weeks before the limit went live.


The stated logic is efficiency. Reviewers were drowning in long packages, and the theory is that a shorter resume forces the most relevant experience up front and makes federal applications look more like the private sector, so people can move between the two more easily. You can agree or disagree with that. For the purposes of your next application, it doesn't matter. The rule is the rule, and this is how you work inside it.


## Two pages, but the content requirements didn't shrink


This is the trap, and it's where good candidates lose the plot. Reading "two pages," people reach for a sleek corporate resume, a crisp summary, a few punchy lines, done. Then they get rated ineligible and can't figure out why.


The cap changed the length. It did not change what a federal resume has to prove. Federal HR still rates you against the qualifications in the announcement, and it does that literally, line by line, checking whether your work history contains the specific evidence the job requires. A resume can be two clean pages and still fail if it dropped the detail that establishes you meet the grade.


So the job in front of you is not "shorten." It is "compress." Keep the content that qualifies you, cut the content that doesn't, and make every remaining line pull its weight. That is a genuinely harder task than the old approach, where you could just keep typing until every base was covered. Now you have to make choices, and the wrong choices are expensive.


## What USAJOBS still requires in every job entry


Before you cut anything, know what has to survive. For each relevant position, USAJOBS and OPM guidance still expect the same core fields that made federal resumes distinctive:


- **Employer name and your job title.** For federal positions, include the series and grade, for example GS-1102-12.
- **Start and end dates with the month and year** , like 05/2019 to 08/2022. A bare year isn't enough. Dates by month are how HR measures the length of your qualifying experience.
- **Hours worked per week.** This one is load-bearing and people cut it to save space, which is a mistake. Without your hours, HR can't confirm you have the twelve months of specialized experience the grade demands, and part-time work prorates. Even at a standard forty, you have to say forty.
- **A description of duties and accomplishments** that lines up with the announcement and addresses its required qualifications, written so a reviewer can see you can do the work at the level stated.


One nuance matters here, because guidance on it is genuinely mixed. The traditional federal resume also carried a supervisor name and phone number and a salary for each job. Under the two-page cap those rank below the fields above: USAJOBS marks supervisor contact as optional, and your GS grade already signals your pay band, so a separate salary figure is largely redundant. But this is not settled the way the load-bearing fields are. Some agencies still look for supervisor information, and some announcements ask for specific details, so treat these as the first things to trim for space rather than fields you must delete, and check your announcement before you strip them entirely. References, by contrast, never belonged on a resume in the first place and always come off.


## How to actually fit it in two pages


Here is the method that works, drawn straight from OPM's own condensing hints and from how federal reviewers actually read.


**Lead with the most recent and most relevant experience, and detail it fully.** Your last job, and any earlier job that directly matches the announcement, gets the real estate: three to five tight, results-focused bullets each. This is where you prove the specialized experience. Don't ration it here.


**Compress the older and less-relevant roles.** A job from twelve years ago that has nothing to do with the posting doesn't need six bullets. It might need one line: employer, title, dates. Keep that line so you don't leave an unexplained gap, but don't spend your two pages narrating it. What you did a decade ago matters far less than what you do now, and every reviewer knows it.


**Cut the filler that never earned its place.** A generic objective statement, an unrelated early-career job, the same bullet repeated across three positions, a skills section listing things you'll cover in your experience anyway. Gone.


**Write in results, not narration.** Reviewers scan hundreds of these. A bullet that says what you accomplished and at what scale beats a paragraph describing your daily tasks. One more practical point. The USAJOBS resume builder is not your friend on length. Its spacing, font, and line formatting will push you past two pages with only a handful of jobs, and a short reference list alone can eat half a page. If the builder resume looks bloated, that's the tool, not you. Many applicants draft the two-page version in a word processor with half-inch margins and a 10-point body font, then bring it over. ## Mirror the announcement's exact language


If you take one thing from this piece, take this. Federal screening is more literal than most people expect, and two pages makes precision matter more, not less.


When HR rates your resume, a reviewer often works through the required qualifications one at a time and checks your work history for the matching evidence. If the announcement asks for a specific number of years doing a specific thing, they scan your bullets for that specific thing, in that specific language. Miss the phrasing and you can be rated ineligible even when you clearly have the experience, because the person screening you frequently isn't the subject-matter expert who would read between the lines. They match words.


So mirror the announcement. Pull the specialized-experience statement and the key duties, and make sure your top-of-page bullets echo that language where it's honestly true of your work. This isn't keyword stuffing in the old federal sense of cramming in everything. It's the opposite. With only two pages, you spend your limited space on the terms that qualify you and drop the rest. ## What to cut first


When you're a few lines over and every bullet feels essential, cut in this order.


References come off first. They don't belong on a resume, federal or not, and you'll provide them when asked. Supervisor names and phone numbers go next: USAJOBS treats supervisor contact as optional and it ranks below your core experience, though a few agencies still look for it, so trim it for room and glance at the announcement first. Salary sits in the same bucket, since your GS grade already tells the reviewer your pay band.


Then combine. Fold your pay plan, series, and grade into your job title line rather than listing them separately, so "Contract Specialist" becomes "Contract Specialist (GS-1102-12)" on one line. Trim any bullet that repeats across two jobs down to a single appearance under the more relevant role. Cut entire positions that don't support your case for this announcement, leaving a one-line placeholder only if dropping them would create a confusing gap.


What you don't cut: hours per week, month-and-year dates, and the duty detail that proves your specialized experience. Those are the load-bearing walls. If you're tempted to thin your skills into a graphic sidebar to save room, don't; those tricks tend to break how the resume gets read. Our take on[where a skills section actually belongs](https://www.hiration.com/blog/skills-section-on-resume/) covers why.


## It's still a federal resume, not a private one


The most common overcorrection is treating the two-page cap as permission to submit a private-sector resume to a federal job. It is not. The change is length, not document type.


A polished[corporate one-pager](https://www.hiration.com/blog/one-page-resume/) , the kind you'd send a startup, will often fail a federal qualification screen because it leaves out the fields the rating depends on: your hours, your exact dates, your series and grade, the specialized-experience detail. You can be perfectly qualified and still rated ineligible because your resume didn't say the things federal HR is required to check. The federal resume kept its DNA; it just lost its length.


Think of it this way. You are writing a federal resume with all its required precision, then squeezing it into the space a private-sector resume uses. Both disciplines at once. That's the actual assignment, and it's why a thoughtful two-page federal resume is harder to write than either of the things it's caught between.


## The CV and medical-research exception


There is a real carve-out, and if it applies to you it changes the whole calculation. For positions that require a curriculum vitae, typically in medical and research fields, agencies may ask you to submit your CV, with the specific information required, as an "other document."A physician's publication list or a scientist's grant history genuinely can't compress to two pages, and the rule accounts for that through the CV path rather than the two-page resume.


Some announcements outside Title 5, along with certain judicial and legislative-branch postings, can also accept a longer resume if the announcement includes instructions for submitting it. And in the early rollout, some agencies and specific hiring authorities handled the transition on their own timelines.


The safe move is the same one federal applicants have always needed: read the announcement. The "How to Apply" and "Required Documents" sections tell you the page requirement and any exception for that specific job. Don't assume the general rule when the announcement is sitting right there telling you the specific one.


## Coming from the military


A large share of federal applicants come out of the military, and the two-page cap lands hard on them, because a full career of assignments, deployments, schools, awards, and collateral duties does not want to fit in two pages.


The discipline is the same one you'd use anyway, now under a firm ceiling: translate, then condense. Turn your MOS, AFSC, or rating and your rank into plain civilian language a reviewer recognizes, keep the assignments that map to the announcement's specialized experience, and let your SF-50s carry the formal record of what your resume can only summarize. Our full walkthrough on[translating military experience into a civilian resume](https://www.hiration.com/blog/military-to-civilian-resume/) gets into the specifics; think of this two-page rule as the container that work now has to fit inside. Translation is what gets a civilian reviewer to understand what you did, and condensing is what gets the whole thing under the ceiling, so you're doing both jobs at once.


## What to do right now


If you're going to apply for a federal job in the near future, don't wait until you've found the perfect posting to deal with this. Do the housekeeping now.


Open your USAJOBS profile and look at the resume stored there. If it's a leftover longform version from a previous job hunt, it's over the limit and it needs to come down to two pages before you apply to anything. If you had a searchable resume in the Agency Talent Portal, it was pulled and won't resurface until you post a compliant one. Check every version the system might hold: the profile resume, any file you uploaded, the builder resume, the searchable one.


Then build your tailored two-page version around a specific announcement rather than in the abstract, because the announcement tells you which experience to lead with. Keep your old long master resume saved offline as a reference so you're not rewriting your history from scratch each time; you just pull from it and trim to fit. Before you submit, one honest read against the announcement's required qualifications, top to bottom, to make sure the two pages still prove you're qualified. If you want a second set of eyes on whether it does, that's what a review is for.


## Frequently Asked Questions


- When did the USAJOBS two-page resume limit start?


September 27, 2025. That's the date USAJOBS began restricting resumes to two pages across profiles, uploads, builder resumes, and the Agency Talent Portal, per OPM's guidance. It sits under the Merit Hiring Plan issued May 29, 2025. Policy can shift, so if you're reading this well after 2026, confirm the current rule on USAJOBS before you rely on it.


- What happens if my federal resume is over two pages?


You're rated ineligible for that job. OPM's guidance says if the only resume an applicant submits is longer than two pages, that applicant is out of consideration, and the rejection notice cites the page requirement. It isn't trimmed to fit or read anyway. The count knocks you out before your qualifications get looked at, which is why this is the highest-stakes formatting rule in federal hiring right now.


- Does the limit apply to the USAJOBS resume builder too?


Yes. The cap covers builder resumes, uploaded files, the resume in your profile, and searchable resumes in the Agency Talent Portal. The catch is that the builder's own spacing and fonts run long, so a few jobs can push you past two pages on their own. If your builder resume looks bloated, that's the formatting, not your experience. Draft tight, then check the page count before you make it searchable.


- Do I still need to list hours worked per week?


Yes, and don't cut it to save space. Hours are how federal HR verifies you have the twelve months of specialized experience a grade requires. Part-time work prorates, so even at a standard forty you have to write forty. Drop the hours and a reviewer can't confirm your qualifying time, which can sink an otherwise strong resume. It's one of the fields that has to survive the trim.


- Can I just submit my regular private-sector resume instead?


No. The change is length, not document type. A polished corporate one-pager usually leaves out the fields federal rating depends on: hours, exact month-and-year dates, series and grade, specialized-experience detail. You can be fully qualified and still rated ineligible because the resume didn't say what HR is required to check. Write a federal resume with all its precision, then compress it into two pages.


- What should I cut first to get under two pages?


References first; they don't belong on any resume and you'll give them when asked. Then supervisor contact and salary, which are lower priority and usually trimmable for space since your GS grade already implies your pay, though it's worth a check against your announcement first. Then compress older, unrelated jobs to a single line. Keep hours, month-and-year dates, and the duty detail that proves your specialized experience. Those are the walls you don't knock down.


- Are supervisor contact and salary still required?


Guidance is mixed, so treat them as lower priority rather than mandatory. USAJOBS marks supervisor contact as optional, and your GS grade already signals your pay band, so both are among the first things to trim when you're over two pages. That said, some agencies still look for supervisor info and some announcements ask for specific fields, so read your announcement before you strip them. The fields you never cut are hours per week, month-and-year dates, and the duty detail that proves your experience.


- Are there any exceptions to the two-page rule?


A few. Positions that require a CV, typically in medical and research fields, can submit the CV as an "other document." Some non-Title 5, judicial, and legislative-branch postings can accept a longer resume if the announcement gives instructions. The way to know is to read the specific announcement; its "How to Apply" and "Required Documents" sections state the page requirement and any exception for that job.


- How do I fit twenty years of experience into two pages?


Prioritize. Give your most recent and most relevant roles real detail, three to five results-focused bullets that prove the specialized experience. Compress older or off-target jobs to a line so there's no unexplained gap. Cut objectives, repeated bullets, and unrelated positions. Mirror the announcement's exact language in your top bullets. The goal isn't to shrink everything evenly; it's to spend your two pages on what qualifies you.


- I already have a resume saved in USAJOBS. Do I need to fix it?


Probably, yes. The limit reaches your stored profile resume, not just what you submit at apply time. A leftover longform resume from a past search is over the cap and needs to come down to two pages before you apply again. If you had a searchable resume in the Agency Talent Portal, it was removed and won't return until you post a compliant one. Check every version the system holds.
