# The Video Cover Letter: How to Handle One (and When It's OK to Refuse)

## What is a video cover letter, and do you have to make one?


A video cover letter is one short clip, usually 60 to 90 seconds, where you introduce yourself and say why you fit a specific role. Sometimes you choose to record it as a differentiator. Sometimes an application field asks for it. It is not a one-way video interview (that's timed, scored questions), and it's not a full video resume. For most jobs a strong written letter still carries more weight. The video helps in client-facing roles, is skippable when it's optional, and is a fair thing to decline when a no-name employer demands it.


Here's the sentence that ends a lot of applications before they start: "Please record a short video telling us about yourself." For plenty of good candidates, that request lands somewhere between annoying and disqualifying. They write well. They interview fine once a human is on the other end. But a webcam and a script? No thanks.


So let's be practical about it. This is a guide to the single, short, self-recorded intro video, the thing people call a video cover letter: when it's worth making, how to make one that doesn't make you cringe, and the times when declining is the smart move rather than the scared one.


## A video cover letter is not a one-way video interview (draw this line first)


Almost every argument about "video cover letters" online is really two different things wearing the same coat, so untangle them before you decide anything.


A **video cover letter** is one clip. You talk to the camera for a minute, maybe ninety seconds, and cover who you are and why this role. There's usually a written cover letter as the alternative, and you control the whole thing: script, takes, when you hit record. It's a companion to your application, not a test.


A **one-way video interview** is a different animal. A platform feeds you several set questions, gives you a timer per answer, sometimes a limited number of takes, and often runs the recording through automated scoring. That's an interview stage, not an intro. If what you're staring at is five prompts with a countdown clock, you're not being asked for a cover letter, you're being asked to sit a screening interview. We've written that up separately: how to handle a[one-way video interview](https://www.hiration.com/blog/one-way-video-interview/) , and the harder question of[whether to refuse a one-way video interview](https://www.hiration.com/blog/refuse-one-way-video-interview/) at all. If your situation is timed questions, go there instead. This post stays with the short single clip.


One more cousin to keep separate. A[video resume](https://www.hiration.com/blog/video-resume/) is a longer produced piece that tries to replace your whole document. A video cover letter is smaller and humbler, a hello with a point. Don't confuse the two, because the production bar and the risks are very different.


## The two ways this shows up: you choose it, or the field demands it


Video cover letters reach you through one of two doors, and which door it is changes almost everything about how you should respond.


**Door one: you decide to make one.** Nobody asked. You're applying to a small marketing agency or a sales team, and you think a thirty-second face-and-voice could set you apart from the identical PDFs in the pile. This is the differentiator play. It's optional, it's yours, and it only makes sense in certain roles (more on which ones below).


**Door two: the application asks for it.** There's a field that says "record a 60-second introduction," or a staffing agency emails you a link, or an applicant portal won't let you submit without a clip attached. Now it's not a flourish, it's a requirement someone put in your path, and your real question becomes whether to clear the hurdle or walk away from it.


The two doors feel similar and they are not. Behind door one, the downside of skipping is zero, because you were never obligated. Behind door two, skipping might cost you the application. So the first thing to figure out, before you write a word of script, is which situation you're actually in and how badly you want this particular job. The same logic that governs[when a cover letter actually earns its place](https://www.hiration.com/blog/when-to-write-a-cover-letter/) applies to the video version: effort follows how much the specific role justifies it.


## Does a video cover letter actually help? It depends on the job


The honest answer nobody wants is "it depends," so here's what it depends on.


The video earns its keep when the job itself is about being on camera, on the phone, or in front of a client. If you're applying for sales, customer success, account management, marketing, PR, media, on-camera work, or a customer-facing startup role, a short clip does something your resume can't: it shows you can hold a room. Recruiters for those roles will tell you they get a read on client-interaction skill almost immediately. For remote jobs especially, being comfortable and clear on camera is part of the actual work, so a candidate who's easy to watch has quietly answered a real question.


Then there's the other half of the job market. Backend engineering, data analysis, accounting, finance, legal, research, academia, most heads-down individual-contributor work. For these, a video adds close to nothing and can actively hurt. The skill being hired for doesn't live on camera, and forcing a naturally quiet, analytical person to perform enthusiasm on video tests something the role will never require. Plenty of excellent hires in these fields would rather translate a paragraph than smile at a lens, and a good recruiter for a quant role knows that a stiff sixty seconds tells them nothing about whether the person can do the work.


Here's the part that gets left out of the hype. When a video is genuinely optional, a lot of recruiters don't watch it. They're buried in an applicant tracking system, a resume takes twenty seconds to skim, and a video takes minutes they don't have. So an optional clip has a low ceiling on its upside: it can help a bit in the right role, and it can be quietly ignored in the wrong one. That asymmetry is worth remembering when you're deciding whether it's worth an evening of takes.

## What to actually say (say what the paper can't)


The single biggest mistake is treating the video like a teleprompter for your resume. If you read your bullet points aloud, you've spent your one on-camera minute repeating information the reader already has, which is worse than saying nothing.


The video's job is to deliver the things paper can't carry: how you sound, whether you seem like someone people want on a call, and genuine, specific interest in this role. That last word matters. Specific. A recruiter can smell a clip that was recorded once and blasted to forty companies, because it never mentions any of them by name.


So give the camera the stuff a document flattens. Warmth. A real sentence about why this company and not the company across the street. One story that shows rather than lists. The written[cover letter that reads like a human email](https://www.hiration.com/blog/cover-letter-like-an-email/) follows the same instinct, and it's a good reference point for the tone you want on video too: direct, warm, like a message to a person rather than a statement to a committee.


## The 60-to-90-second script spine


You want a skeleton, not a word-for-word script you'll read like a hostage. Memorize the beats, then talk. Four beats fit comfortably inside ninety seconds:


1. **Hook (10 seconds).** Your name, what you do, and one line that isn't boilerplate. Not "my name is X and I'm applying for the Y role." They know that. Try "I'm a customer success lead who's spent four years turning angry renewals into references."
2. **Why this role, this company (20 seconds).** One genuine, specific reason you're here. Name the company. Reference the product, the team, the mission, something you actually looked at.
3. **One or two proof points (30 to 40 seconds).** Not your whole history. Pick the one or two accomplishments most relevant to this job and tell them as short stories with an outcome. This is where a light STAR structure helps: situation, what you did, what happened.
4. **Warm close (10 seconds).** Say you'd love to talk more, thank them, done. No fade-to-black music. No "please pick me."


Ninety seconds sounds short until you time yourself. It's roughly 200 to 230 spoken words. That constraint is a gift, because it forces you to cut everything that isn't the point. If your first take runs three minutes, you're not being thorough, you're rambling, and the person watching will feel every extra second. The written version follows the same rule about[how long a cover letter should be](https://www.hiration.com/blog/how-long-should-a-cover-letter-be/) : shorter and sharper almost always beats longer and complete.


## Setup: how to not look like a hostage video


You don't need a studio. You need to be seen and heard clearly, and you need to look like a person, not a robot reading a card. The technical bar is low and the payoff is high, because a clip with garbled audio gets closed in three seconds no matter how good the content is.


A few things matter more than they should:


- **Audio first.** This is the one people skip and the one that sinks them. Muffled or echoey sound gets a video closed faster than dim lighting ever will, so record in a quiet room, sit close to the mic, and use earbuds if you own a pair.
- **Light on your face, not behind it.** Sit facing a window or a lamp. When the bright thing is behind you, the camera turns you into a shadow, and the viewer spends the whole minute squinting at a silhouette instead of listening.
- **Talk to the lens.** The instinct is to watch yourself on screen, which quietly breaks eye contact with whoever's watching later. A sticky note with a little arrow, stuck right next to the camera, fixes it in one shot.
- **Keep the background boring.** A blank wall works. A tidy shelf works. Nobody is scoring your décor, but a cluttered frame drags the eye off your face, and your face is the point.
- **Do a few takes, then quit.** Three or four is plenty. Keep the one where you sound most like a person and walk away, because the hunt for a flawless take is exactly what makes you stiff.


None of this is about looking polished. It's about looking at ease. A slightly rough clip where you sound relaxed will always beat a glossy one where you sound like a hostage reading a demand note.


## The mistakes that sink most video cover letters


Almost every bad video cover letter fails in one of a handful of familiar ways, and knowing them is most of the fix.


**Too long.** A three, four, or five-minute clip reads as poor judgment before you've said anything worth hearing, because the person watching has a queue and you just added yourself to the bottom of it.


**Reading a script stiffly.** Word-for-word delivery flattens your voice and throws away the only edge video had over paper, which was that you sounded human. Learn the beats and talk; don't recite.


**Bad audio.** Echo, a fan in the background, sitting three feet from the mic. All of it is fixable in about thirty seconds, and all of it is fatal if you don't bother.


**Lip-syncing the resume.** When you recite your own bullet points on camera, you've spent the format's one advantage on nothing. If a glance at your document would have told the reader the same thing, the video was pointless.


**Overproduction.** Title cards, a music bed, jump cuts, a spinning logo. All of it reads as trying too hard, and worse, it buries the one thing the clip existed to show, which is you.


And the single worst one: the generic reusable clip. A video with no company-specific detail, obviously shot once and fired at everyone, quietly cancels the only reason the format works. Make it for that job, or don't make it at all.


## When it's fine to skip an optional one


If the field is genuinely optional, treat it as optional. You are allowed to skip it, and often nothing bad happens, because a lot of optional clips never get watched.


Skip it without guilt when the role is heads-down and analytical, where a video adds nothing to the case for you. Skip it when you're deep in a long application day and the marginal clip isn't worth the hour of takes. Skip it when you're camera-averse and the job has nothing to do with being on camera, because forcing an awkward performance can hurt you more than the absence of a video ever would.


The judgment call is simply: is this a job where being watchable is part of the work, and do I want it enough to spend the evening? If both are yes, record. If either is no, a strong written letter and resume carry you fine. There's no rule that says the applicant who filmed themselves wins. In most piles, they don't.


## Accessibility, and asking for an alternative


If recording a video is a barrier because of a disability, a speech difference, anxiety that rises to a condition, or any accessibility need, you can ask for a different way to complete the step. A phone screen. A written statement. A live call instead of a recording.


In the United States, the application process is supposed to be accessible, and asking for a reasonable accommodation is a recognized right rather than a favor you're begging for. You don't have to lay out the medical details behind the request either. Treat what follows as general information, not legal advice, because the specifics shift with where you live and how big the employer is, so check your region's current rules if this matters to your situation. The practical move, though, is low-drama. Email the recruiter, tell them the video format is a barrier for you, and ask whether there's another way to share the same information. A serious employer will have one ready. An employer that stonewalls a reasonable ask has just told you something useful about itself, for free.


## The part employers don't say out loud: bias


There's a reason the instinct to distrust video requests is so widespread, and it isn't just camera shyness. A video shows your face, your age, your race, your gender, your accent, whether you have a visible disability. That's a lot of protected-class information handed over before anyone has evaluated whether you can do the job.


This isn't a fringe worry. It's the same logic that's kept photos off American resumes for decades. Employment guidance in the US steers employers away from asking applicants for photographs before an offer, precisely because a photo reveals characteristics that discrimination law protects, and having that information early creates legal exposure if a rejected candidate later claims appearance drove the decision. A video hands over everything a photo would and then some, at the earliest possible stage. Recruiters on the hiring side know this too. Plenty of them consider a mandatory front-of-funnel video a discrimination case waiting to happen, all risk and little reward, which is one reason careful employers don't use them.


None of this makes every video request sinister. But it does mean your discomfort is grounded in something real, not paranoia, and it's a legitimate factor to weigh when the request is mandatory and the role has nothing to do with being on camera.


## The requests that are genuine red flags


Most companies asking for a video are just using a tool a vendor sold them. A few requests, though, are worth backing away from, and they tend to share a look.


Be wary when a no-name outfit demands a lot of on-camera work up front, especially for low pay. Recording a polished intro for a minimum-wage role that hasn't so much as spoken to you is a mismatch of effort, and it's often a sign the "job" is a funnel for something else. Be wary when the "video cover letter" turns out to be a full timed multi-question interview in disguise, complete with "resumes without the video will not be considered." That's not an intro, that's an unpaid screening stage they've relabeled to sound friendlier.


Be wary, too, of vague terms about what happens to your recording. Your face and voice are yours. It's reasonable to ask what the footage is used for, who sees it, and whether it's stored or reused, and it's fair to hesitate when the answer is fuzzy or the platform's terms are hard to find. This is a privacy question, not an accusation against any particular tool, and asking it is not rude.


When a request pattern-matches to these, declining costs you an application you probably didn't want. That's a trade worth taking.


## The whole decision in one place


Put it together and the map is simple.


If the video is **optional** and the role is client-facing and you want it, make a good one: short, specific, human, made for that job. If it's optional and the role is heads-down or you're not feeling it, skip it and let your written materials do the work, because they usually do.


If the video is **required** , weigh how much you want the job against how the request feels. For a role you genuinely want at a real company, playing along is often the price of entry, the same trade you'd make on any other screening step. For a lukewarm application, a no-name employer, a low-paid gig demanding on-camera effort, or a "cover letter" that's secretly a full interview, refusing is a reasonable choice, not a self-sabotage. Refusing a mandatory step will usually end that application, and it's honest to say so, but ending a bad application early is not the same as losing.


Underneath all of it, keep the proportion straight. The written cover letter and the resume are still the load-bearing parts of nearly every application, and even the debate over[whether cover letters are necessary](https://www.hiration.com/blog/are-cover-letters-necessary/) at all still lands on the paper, not the camera. If you want to understand where a video even fits in the sequence, our walkthrough of[how hiring actually works](https://www.hiration.com/blog/how-hiring-actually-works/) lays out the stages. The video is a differentiator in the right roles, a shrug in most, and a fair thing to decline in a few. Decide which one you're looking at, and act like it.


## Frequently Asked Questions


- How long should a video cover letter be?


Sixty to ninety seconds. Push past two minutes and the person watching starts to notice the clock, which is never the impression you want. Ninety seconds is only about 220 spoken words, so you have room for a hook, one reason you want the job, a proof point, and a goodbye. When a draft runs long, it's usually because you smuggled in things that already live on your resume.


- Is a video cover letter the same as a one-way video interview?


No, and mixing them up is where people go wrong. The cover letter is one clip you control, with a written letter as the fallback. A one-way interview throws several set questions at you on a timer, sometimes with an algorithm scoring your face and voice afterward. That second thing is a screening stage in disguise. Facing prompts on a countdown? Read our guides on handling a[one-way video interview](https://www.hiration.com/blog/one-way-video-interview/) and[whether to refuse one](https://www.hiration.com/blog/refuse-one-way-video-interview/) instead.


- Can I refuse to record one?


You can. An optional field costs nothing to skip. A required one is where it gets real, because declining usually ends that application, so the honest math is how much you want the job weighed against how the request feels. Walking away makes sense for a lukewarm role, a no-name employer, or a "cover letter" that's secretly a full interview. For a company you actually want, recording is often just the toll you pay to keep going.


- What should I actually say?


Not your resume, read aloud. That's the trap. Spend the minute on the things paper can't carry: your voice, one genuine reason you picked this company over the one next door, and a single short story that shows what you can do instead of listing it. Specific beats polished. A clip that could have been mailed to forty employers because it names none of them is worse than sending nothing.


- Do recruiters even watch these?


Frequently they don't, especially the optional ones. Picture the inbox: hundreds of applicants in a tracking system, a resume that takes twenty seconds, and a video that eats several minutes nobody has. So an optional clip carries a low ceiling. It can nudge things in a client-facing role and get quietly closed everywhere else. Worth knowing before you burn an evening on retakes.


- Which jobs is it worth making one for?


Anything where being watchable is part of the actual work. Sales. Customer success. Marketing, PR, media, on-camera roles, a customer-facing seat at an early startup. In those, a good clip proves a thing your document can't. Now flip it: backend engineering, data, finance, legal, research, most heads-down individual work. There, a video adds almost nothing and can backfire by testing a skill the job will never ask you to use.


- What if I have a disability or the format is a barrier?


Ask for another way to do the step. A phone screen. A written statement. In the US, the application process is supposed to be accessible, so requesting a reasonable accommodation is a right rather than a favor you owe an explanation for, and you don't have to share the medical reasons behind it. Treat this as general information, not legal advice, since the rules shift by region and employer size. If it matters to your case, check what applies where you are.


- Is it fair that a video lets employers see my race, age, and gender?


It's a fair thing to be uneasy about. There's a reason photos have stayed off American resumes for years: a picture reveals protected characteristics before anyone has judged whether you can do the job, and employment guidance steers employers away from collecting exactly that. A video hands over even more, even earlier. Plenty of recruiters see a mandatory front-of-funnel clip as legal risk, not an asset, which tells you the worry runs both ways.


- How do I record one without expensive gear?


The phone in your pocket is enough. Get the audio right first, because that's what makes people close a tab: quiet room, close to the mic, earbuds if you have them. Then face a window so the light hits your face rather than your back, keep whatever's behind you plain, and talk to the lens, not to your own reflection on the screen. Shoot it a few times, keep the take where you sound most like yourself, and quit before you stiffen up.


- Does a video cover letter replace the written one?


Almost never, so don't assume it. On nearly every application, the written letter and the resume still do the heavy lifting, and the clip is a supplement at most. If a role wants both, they're doing separate jobs, the letter arguing your case in detail and the video showing the person behind it. Short on energy for both?
