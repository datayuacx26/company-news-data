# How to Advise Students on Freelance and Gig Work?

## How should career advisors talk to students about freelance and gig work?


Career advisors should discuss freelance, contract, gig, and portfolio work as legitimate work models without presenting every opportunity as a good choice. The advising conversation should clarify why the student is considering the arrangement, what the work is expected to provide, whether the opportunity has clear scope and payment terms, and how it supports the student’s longer-term direction. Advisors should help students compare purpose, income predictability, supervision, skill development, working conditions, legal or classification concerns, work authorization issues, and future value. The goal is to move beyond full-time-or-nothing framing while still helping students avoid vague, unpaid, unstable, or low-value arrangements.


If students ask about freelancing, contract work, or combining several income streams, career advisors need language that treats the option seriously without presenting every gig as a good opportunity.


Students may be considering this type of work because it is common in their field, offers flexibility, creates access to clients, supports another professional goal, or provides a practical way to build experience.


Others may be considering it because a full-time search has stalled and they are unsure what else to do.


This guide explains how advisors can discuss gig work as a legitimate work model, clarify what the student is choosing, assess the quality of a specific opportunity, and help the student connect short-term projects to a deliberate career direction.


## Why Should Career Advisors Move Beyond Full-Time-or-Nothing Framing?


Career advisors should present full-time employment, contract work, freelancing, entrepreneurship, and portfolio careers asdifferent work arrangements with different requirements . The appropriate choice depends on the student’s field, goals, financial needs, work authorization, support system, and tolerance for uncertainty.


This framing is consistent with how graduate outcomes are already reported.[NACE’s First-Destination Standards](https://www.naceweb.org/job-market/graduate-outcomes/first-destination/first-destination-standards-and-protocols-key-questions-and-answers/) include entrepreneurship, temporary or contract employment, freelancing, and fellowships or internships within positive career outcomes.


Alternative work arrangements also represent a substantial part of the labor market.


The[U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/pdf/conemp.pdf) reported that 11.9 million people worked as independent contractors in their sole or main job in July 2023, representing 7.4% of total employment. The BLS category includes independent consultants and freelance workers.


However, these figures do not make gig work suitable for every student. They show why advisors need a more precise conversation than “find a full-time job first and freelance only if necessary.”


## What Does Gig Work Mean in an Advising Conversation?


“Gig work” can describe several arrangements with different levels of independence, structure, stability, and professional value. Advisors should identify the actual arrangement before discussing whether it fits the student.


Work arrangement What the student may be doing What the advisor should clarify


Freelance project Completing a defined deliverable directly for a client Scope, fee, payment schedule, ownership, revisions, and client expectations


Fixed-term contract Working in a defined role for an employer, agency, or client for a specified period Duration, supervision, work hours, benefits, renewal potential, and employment classification


Platform-based gig Finding individual tasks or customers through an online marketplace or app Platform fees, demand, safety, payment protection, and relevance to the student’s goals


Portfolio career Combining several professional roles or income streams How the pieces fit together, which stream provides stability, and how the workload will be managed


Independent consulting Advising clients using specialized knowledge or experience Credibility, business development, pricing, contracts, and the limits of the student’s expertise


The label alone provides little information about opportunity quality. A three-month contract with strong supervision and meaningful responsibilities may offer more development than an unstructured permanent role.


A freelance project may provide useful evidence of ability, or it may involve vague expectations, unpaid revisions, and little professional value.


The conversation should focus on the conditions of the work.


## How Should Advisors Introduce Gig Work Without Downgrading It?


Advisors should introduce gig work as one possible work model and connect it directly to the student’s reason for considering it.


A useful opening could be:


*“Freelance and contract work can play different roles in a career. Let’s identify what you want this work to provide and then assess whether the specific arrangement can deliver it.”*


This language gives the option legitimacy while leaving room for careful evaluation.


Avoid language that positions gig work as consolation:


*“You can always freelance until you find a real job.”*


A stronger version would be:


*“Project-based work could help you build paid experience, test a field, develop client relationships, or become part of a longer-term portfolio career. Which of those outcomes are you looking for?”*


Similarly, replace:


*“At least it gives you something to put on your resume.”*


With:


*“What will you produce, learn, demonstrate, or become qualified to do through this work?”*


The second question makes the student evaluate the opportunity’s professional value instead of accepting activity as progress.


## What Questions Reveal Whether the Student Is Choosing or Settling?


Students should be able to explain why they are considering gig work and what role it will play in their broader plan. Uncertainty is acceptable. A vague answer such as “I guess it is better than nothing” signals that the student needs help separating an intentional short-term decision from pressure to accept any available work.


Advisors can ask:


1. What do you want this arrangement to provide: income, flexibility, experience, industry access, autonomy, or a test of a possible field?
2. Would you still consider this type of work if a full-time offer were available?
3. What would make the opportunity worthwhile three or six months from now?
4. What income level and payment predictability do you need?
5. How much responsibility are you comfortable taking for finding clients, managing deadlines, and handling administrative work?
6. What support, feedback, supervision, or professional community will be available?
7. When will you review the arrangement and decide whether to continue, expand, or change direction?


[The University of Utah’s guidance](https://attheu.utah.edu/facultystaff/tips-for-starting-your-freelance-career/) for students considering freelance careers recommends evaluating short-term commissions and opportunities against longer-term goals.


It suggests asking whether the work belongs in the freelancer’s future portfolio, opens useful connections, and fits the professional identity the person wants to build.


That long-term perspective can help advisors move the conversation beyond immediate availability.


## How Can Advisors Discuss the Trade-Offs Without Promoting or Dismissing the Path?


Advisors should use a structured[career comparison framework](https://www.hiration.com/blog/students-career-comparison-framework-higher-ed-advisors/) to evaluate gig work across five practical dimensions: purpose, economics, development, working conditions, and future options.


### Purpose


Clarify what function the work serves. A freelance project may allow a student to test an occupation before committing to it.


Contract work may provide entry into an industry that relies heavily on temporary hiring. A portfolio arrangement may allow someone to combine several professional interests. A platform-based gig may serve mainly as a source of short-term income.


Each purpose requires a different standard for success.


### Economics


Help the student calculate more than the advertised hourly or project rate. The student may need to account for unpaid prospecting, revisions, administrative work, software, equipment, taxes, insurance, payment delays, and periods without assignments.


[Students should compare usable income and financial predictability](https://www.hiration.com/blog/pay-transparency-student-career-advising-higher-ed/) rather than comparing a freelance fee directly with an employee’s hourly wage.


### Development


Identify what the student will learn and who will help them improve. Some independent work develops negotiation, client communication, project management, pricing, and self-direction.


Other work offers limited feedback and repeated low-skill tasks. Advisors should ask what capabilities the student will be able to demonstrate after completing the assignment.


### Working Conditions


Discuss autonomy alongside workload, boundaries, support, and responsibility. A student attracted by schedule flexibility may still face demanding clients, irregular deadlines, unpaid coordination, or pressure to remain constantly available.


Flexibility has value when the student can exercise meaningful control over the work.


### Future Options


Determine what the experience makes possible next. A useful project might lead to a repeat client, referral, stronger work sample, new industry knowledge, or evidence needed for a future position.


An arrangement with no learning, relationship development, usable output, or adequate compensation may offer little future value.


## How Should Advisors Evaluate a Specific Freelance or Contract Opportunity?


A legitimate work model can still contain poor opportunities. Advisors should help students examine the actual terms before accepting. The review should cover:


- **Scope and deliverables:** What will the student produce? How many revisions are included? What counts as completion?
- **Compensation:** Is the amount clear? When will the student be paid? Are deposits, milestones, expenses, cancellations, and late payments addressed?
- **Control and supervision:** Who determines the schedule, process, tools, and location? Who reviews the work? How available is that person?
- **Ownership and confidentiality:** Can the student show the completed work? Who owns the underlying materials? Is the student being asked to sign confidentiality, noncompete, or intellectual-property terms?
- **Duration and exit:** Is the work tied to one deliverable, a fixed period, or an open-ended relationship? How can either party end it?
- **Professional value:** Will the work develop relevant skills, create evidence, expand the student’s network, or improve access to future opportunities?


Worker classification also deserves attention. The[IRS explains](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee) that classification depends on the full relationship, including behavioral control, financial control, contracts, benefits, continuity, and the role the work plays in the business.


A contract that calls someone an independent contractor does not settle the classification question by itself.


Career advisors should help students identify questions and concerns. Legal, tax, or classification determinations should be directed to the appropriate qualified campus or external resource.


## When Does Gig Work Become a Portfolio Career?


A portfolio career exists when someone deliberately combines several professional roles or revenue streams into a workable career structure. It may include freelance clients, part-time employment, teaching, consulting, creative work, entrepreneurship, or fixed-term projects.


Several unrelated jobs do not automatically form a portfolio career. The student should understand the purpose of each component and how the combined arrangement supports income, professional identity, learning, and future opportunities.


For example, a graduate might combine:


- Two days of paid design work for a recurring client
- Part-time communications work for a nonprofit
- Occasional illustration commissions
- Teaching one continuing-education course


[Michigan State University describes](https://careernetwork.msu.edu/blog/2022/01/04/ask-an-expert-building-a-portfolio-career/) portfolio careers as multiple revenue “slices” that are built deliberately around a person’s interests, skills, values, and income needs.


Its career guidance on portfolio careers encourages students to research how professionals combine revenue streams, gain experience across the relevant fields, develop a professional community, and learn small-business practices.


Advisors can ask students to define the role of each component:


- Which part provides dependable income?
- Which part develops the strongest professional evidence?
- Which part creates relationships or future opportunities?
- Which part is personally important but financially unpredictable?
- How many simultaneous responsibilities can the student manage well?


This makes the portfolio visible as a designed system instead of an accumulation of disconnected tasks.


## How Should Students Describe Gig Work to Employers?


Students should describe freelance and contract work through the same evidence expected from other professional experiences: the problem, their responsibility, the work completed, and the result.


Avoid descriptions such as:


*“Did freelance social media work while searching for a job.”*


A stronger version would be:


*“Managed social content for three local businesses, created monthly publishing calendars, and increased average post engagement by 28% across a four-month client engagement.”*


The student should be prepared to explain:


- How the client or assignment was secured
- What expectations were agreed upon
- How the work was planned and delivered
- How feedback, deadlines, or scope changes were managed
- What result the work produced
- What the student would improve in the next engagement


The[USC Career Center advises](https://careers.usc.edu/blog/2021/02/26/the-gig-economy-what-it-is-and-how-to-get-started/) prospective gig workers to define the value they can offer by reviewing previous employment, team projects, resumes, and work samples for contributions that can transfer to client assignments.


Its gig-economy guidance also highlights the need to communicate those strongest assets clearly to potential clients.


## What Guardrails Should Advisors Apply?


A supportive conversation should still identify arrangements that expose the student to avoidable financial, legal, academic, or personal risk.


Career centers may need referral or escalation procedures when:


- The client refuses to define the scope or compensation in writing.
- Payment depends entirely on exposure, future business, or an unspecified possibility of employment.
- The student is expected to complete extensive unpaid test work.
- The client requests sensitive personal or financial information before establishing a legitimate relationship.
- The role appears to function like regular employment while denying the student corresponding protections or benefits.
- The work conflicts with scholarship, financial-aid, benefit, academic, or institutional requirements.
- The student lacks the financial margin to manage irregular income or delayed payment.
- The arrangement may affect immigration or work authorization.


[International students require particular care](https://www.hiration.com/blog/international-students-career-advising-higher-ed/) because freelance work is generally treated as self-employment in the United States.


[Auburn University’s international-student career guidance](https://career.auburn.edu/blog/2025/06/03/can-i-freelance-as-an-international-student/) recommends consulting the institution’s international student services office before beginning freelance work because authorization depends on the student’s status and circumstances.


Career advisors should route these questions to designated immigration-support staff instead of giving informal authorization advice.


## What Should Students Leave the Conversation Understanding?


Students should leave with a clearer reason for considering gig work, a method for evaluating the arrangement, and a defined point at which they will reassess the decision.


A useful closing statement is:


***“This is a valid work model to consider. Its value depends on the specific opportunity, what you need from it, and how it contributes to your next decision. Let’s define those conditions before you commit.”***


That message gives freelance, contract, and portfolio-career paths appropriate legitimacy. It also keeps the advisor focused on quality, affordability, student agency, and long-term direction.
