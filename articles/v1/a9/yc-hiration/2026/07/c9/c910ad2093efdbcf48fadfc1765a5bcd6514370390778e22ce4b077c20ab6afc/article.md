# When Should Career Centers Evaluate New Technology?

## When should career centers evaluate new technology?


Career centers should begin evaluating new technology in the fall semester if they want a realistic fall rollout the following academic year. Fall should be used to review the current system, document workflow problems, identify renewal and cancellation deadlines, and decide whether a formal evaluation is needed. Before winter break, the team should define the problem, affected users, required capabilities, budget timing, project owner, and approval path. Early spring is usually the most practical window for product evaluation because it leaves time for accessibility, cybersecurity, privacy, procurement, legal review, contracting, configuration, training, testing, and summer implementation preparation before students receive access.


If you lead a career center and want new technology ready for the next academic year, the hardest question may be when to begin.


Starting in summer can leave too little time for budget approval, contract review, accessibility, cybersecurity, procurement, configuration, training, and testing.


While starting without a defined institutional need can send the team into months of demonstrations without a clear decision path.


This guide provides a practical academic-year calendar for career center directors and operations leads planning a technology evaluation. It explains what to complete during fall, why early spring is usually the most workable evaluation window for a fall rollout, what must be approved before summer, and when a delayed or narrower launch is the better choice.


## When Should Career Centers Begin Evaluating New Technology?


For a fall rollout, the internal review should begin during the preceding fall semester.


Use fall to collect evidence, late fall to define the need, early spring to evaluate options, late spring to complete approvals, and summer to prepare the selected system for use.


Academic-year period Decision to complete Recommended activity Output needed before moving forward


August to October Does the current system require review? Record recurring problems, renewal dates, staff concerns, and student-use patterns. A documented reason to evaluate, renew, reconfigure, or stop.


November to December What does the center need to address? Define the use case, intended users, budget route, campus stakeholders, and launch term. A short evaluation brief.


January to March Which option should advance? Review a shortlist, schedule equivalent demonstrations, and submit required documentation. A preferred option and approval plan.


March to May Can the institution approve the decision? Complete budget, procurement, legal, accessibility, privacy, and security processes. An approved agreement or a decision to delay.


June to July Is the center prepared to launch? Configure, test, train staff, prepare communications, and confirm ownership. A documented launch-readiness decision.


This timeline serves as a general planning framework rather than a universal higher education calendar.


Institutions with formal RFP processes, complex integrations, campus-wide implementations, or extended contract termination timelines may need to begin planning earlier to accommodate these requirements.


## What Should Career Centers Complete During the Fall?


Fall should be used to determine whether a formal evaluation is necessary.


The team needs enough evidence to explain what is no longer working, which groups are affected, and why the issue cannot be addressed through a simpler workflow or configuration change.


Document:


- Contract renewal and cancellation-notice dates
- Student and staff groups using the current system
- Recurring workflow or support problems
- Capabilities that are unavailable or underused
- Manual work created by the current setup
- Institutional requirements that have changed
- Decisions that must be made before the next budget cycle


The fall review should end with one of four decisions: retain the current system, reconfigure it, begin a formal evaluation, or investigate a wider technology problem.


If the review reveals overlapping tools, disconnected data, or unclear ownership across multiple systems, an advisor oriented[technology stack guide](https://www.hiration.com/blog/career-center-tech-stack-guide-higher-ed/) can help the team map the existing environment before adding or replacing another platform.


## What Should Be Decided Before Winter Break?


Before winter break, the career center should define the decision it expects to make during spring.


A short evaluation brief gives staff, leadership, campus reviewers, and potential providers the same understanding of the problem and the intended timeline.


The brief should state:


- The problem requiring attention
- The students and staff affected
- The workflow the center wants to improve
- The capabilities required
- The intended evaluation period
- The proposed pilot or rollout term
- The contract renewal or cancellation deadline
- The likely funding source
- The internal project owner
- The campus offices that must participate


Budget timing belongs in this stage.


The center should know when requests are due, which funding source may apply, and whether the proposed purchase belongs in the current or following fiscal year.


Once the need and timing are clear, a proper[budget plan](https://www.hiration.com/blog/budget-planning-template-career-center-higher-ed/) can help translate the proposed investment into line items, expected outcomes, KPIs, and a leadership-ready funding request.


## Why Is Early Spring a Practical Evaluation Window?


January through March often provides enough evidence from the fall term while preserving time for institutional review before summer.


Beginning during this window also gives the center room to delay, narrow, or discontinue the project without forcing an unfinished implementation into August.


Campus approval often takes longer than the product comparison itself.


[UCLA](https://dts.ucla.edu/digital-accessibility/buying-software) , for example, says its purchasing-triage process requires at least six to eight weeks and recommends allowing 12 weeks because accessibility, security, and privacy reviews must be completed before procurement.


The[University of Oregon](https://service.uoregon.edu/TDClient/2030/Portal/KB/Article/140716/Accessibility-Reviews-of-Technology-Purchases) notes that accessibility reviews for commercial technology typically take at least two weeks and can take longer when suppliers are slow to provide documentation. It recommends addressing accessibility during the early stages of procurement.


Timelines will vary by institution, but these examples show why the evaluation calendar should be built around campus review lead times, not only the time needed to compare products.


## What Should Happen During the Spring Evaluation?


The spring evaluation should move a defined decision through a controlled sequence.


The team should compare a limited shortlist, schedule equivalent workflow demonstrations, collect required documentation, involve campus reviewers, and establish a final decision deadline.


A practical spring sequence is:


- Confirm the evaluation brief and decision owner.
- Select a limited number of viable options.
- Ask each provider to demonstrate the same priority workflow.
- Send required documentation to campus reviewers.
- Record unresolved approval or implementation dependencies.
- Complete the decision before the institution’s purchasing deadline.


Once the shortlist is ready, apply the same[evaluation questions](https://www.hiration.com/blog/due-diligence-tech-platform-career-centers/) to every demonstration, documentation review, and final comparison so each option is assessed on equal terms.


[EDUCAUSE](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2025/10/2026-educause-top-10-6-measured-approaches-to-new-technologies) also identifies “measured approaches to new technologies” as a 2026 higher education priority, emphasizing clearer evaluation of cost, potential value, implementation impact, and existing systems before institutions make new investments.


## What Must Be Completed Before Summer?


For a fall rollout, the career center should aim to finish selection, institutional review, funding approval, and contracting before summer preparation begins.


Unresolved purchasing decisions can consume the time that staff need for configuration, training, testing, and communication.


Confirm that the institution has completed or formally scheduled:


- Budget approval
- Procurement review
- Contract and legal review
- Privacy and cybersecurity review
- Accessibility review
- Integration confirmation
- Implementation ownership
- Staff preparation dates
- Intended launch group
- Final go-live decision


Fiscal-year deadlines may move the decision point earlier.


The University of Washington’s 2026[procurement calendar](https://finance.uw.edu/ps/tools-reconciling/fiscal-year-end?) , for example, requires major sourcing events tied to expiring funds to be fully approved and submitted by May 15.


Non-catalog purchase requisitions must be fully approved by May 29. Institutional dates differ, but the example shows why a June decision may miss the usable purchasing window.


## What Should Summer Be Reserved For?


Summer should be reserved for implementation preparation. Product selection, institutional approval, and contract negotiation should not consume the full period intended for configuration and testing.


Depending on the system, preparation may include:


- Configuring institutional settings and permissions
- Preparing student groups or cohorts
- Completing integrations and single sign-on
- Testing student and administrator workflows
- Training advisors and support staff
- Documenting internal ownership
- Preparing student communications
- Confirming support and escalation routes
- Setting the first review date


The team should leave enough time to complete these activities before students are expected to use the system. A technically available platform is not necessarily ready for a broad student


## When Is It Too Late for a Broad Fall Rollout?


A broad fall rollout should be reconsidered when approvals remain incomplete, the agreement is unsigned, staff ownership is unclear, or the center lacks enough time to configure, test, train, and communicate before students receive access.


Situation near the proposed launch More practical decision


Required campus reviews remain incomplete Move the launch until approval is received


The contract or funding source is unresolved Retain the current arrangement temporarily


Configuration or integrations remain unfinished Continue testing before student access begins


Staff training cannot be completed Narrow the initial audience or delay the launch


The team still needs evidence of workflow fit Use a limited fall test rather than a broad rollout


The semester begins within a few weeks Move the planned rollout to spring


A smaller or later rollout may create fewer operational problems than introducing unfinished technology during orientation, career fairs, recruiting activity, and peak appointment periods.


## How Should the Calendar Change for a Spring Rollout?


A spring rollout moves the evaluation into the preceding summer and fall. Selection and institutional approval should be substantially complete before winter break so December and early January can be used for preparation.


Period Spring-rollout activity


April to June Review current performance and contract dates


July to August Define the need, budget route, and campus stakeholders


August to October Evaluate options and begin institutional reviews


October to November Complete selection, approval, and contracting


November to December Configure, test, train staff, and prepare communications


January Begin the rollout


Winter break should not become the primary procurement window. Campus closures, staff leave, and limited reviewer availability can reduce the time available for unresolved decisions.


## Which Dates Belong on the Annual Technology Calendar?


The annual technology calendar should include every institutional date capable of delaying or stopping a proposed purchase. Review the calendar once each semester so upcoming renewals and approval deadlines remain visible before a decision becomes urgent.


Include:


- Contract renewal dates
- Cancellation-notice deadlines
- Departmental budget submission dates
- Fiscal-year purchasing deadlines
- Technology-intake periods
- Privacy and security review lead times
- Accessibility-review lead times
- Legal and contract-review periods
- Integration and data-preparation timelines
- Academic breaks and campus closures
- Staff training availability
- Intended student launch dates


Building the evaluation schedule backward from the intended launch term using these dates can really help to strategize the dates.


## Wrapping Up


A workable technology calendar gives the career center enough time to establish the need, compare suitable options, complete institutional reviews, and prepare staff before students receive access.


For many fall rollouts, that means reviewing the current setup during fall, evaluating during early spring, completing approvals by late spring, and reserving summer for preparation.
