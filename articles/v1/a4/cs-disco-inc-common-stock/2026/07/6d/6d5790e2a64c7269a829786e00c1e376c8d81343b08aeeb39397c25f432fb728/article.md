# Best Practices for Redactions in Document Review Workflows

Most review teams think of redactions as a task. The best review teams think of them as a system — one that combines workflow discipline, technology, and expert judgment to protect what matters most.


The difference between those two mindsets is often the difference between smooth, defensible productions and high-stress rework.


In this article, I’m sharing **redaction best practices drawn from**[DISCO Managed Review](https://csdisco.com/offerings/professional-services/document-review) **’s proven approach in high-stakes matters** , so your team can work faster, stay consistent, and deliver productions that hold up under scrutiny.


## **What is redaction? (and why it matters)**


[Redaction](https://support.csdisco.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046056451-Redactions-Feature-Spotlight) is the process of removing information that cannot or should not be disclosed in discovery — typically privileged communications, attorney work product, PII, PHI, trade secrets, and other highly sensitive business data.


In some matters, protective orders can reduce what needs to be redacted, but **privileged material must always be protected** .


Getting this right matters because the downside of a missed redaction is so high. An error can compromise privilege, expose confidential business information, or disclose personal data that triggers regulatory or reputational risk.


Courts and opposing counsel expect productions to be accurate and appropriately redacted; when they aren’t, parties invite disputes, challenges to their credibility, and in some cases, sanctions.


## **Common redaction pitfalls**


Even experienced review teams can run into issues when redactions aren’t approached with the right structure and attention. Here are some common mistakes to avoid.


### **Redacting-on-the-fly**


Redacting-on-the-fly — applying final redactions during first-pass review — may feel efficient, but it almost always leads to inconsistency.


When reviewers place redactions before families, threads, and near-duplicates are reviewed together, similar documents are often treated differently. Reviewers may miss variations of the same sensitive content or redact documents that ultimately won’t be produced.


This approach also makes quality control far more difficult. Without a deliberate handoff and review stage, it’s harder to compare decisions across reviewers or ensure consistent treatment of document families, threads, and near-duplicates.


### **Lack of clear protocols or instructions**


Without well-defined redaction criteria, reviewers are left to make judgment calls that vary from person to person.[Clear protocols](https://csdisco.com/blog/esi-review-protocols-genai) help avoid inconsistent decisions and reduce the time teams spend reworking documents later.

### **Skipping QC**


Contemporaneous quality control is essential to catching missed or improper redactions, especially in email threads or near-duplicate documents where sensitive content may appear multiple times. Waiting until the end of a redaction project will only add time and expense.


### **Metadata blind spots**


PII, PHI, and other sensitive information often lives in metadata, not just the visible text. Overlooking metadata can result in disclosures that were otherwise carefully protected in the body of the document.


### **Over-redaction**


Redacting more than necessary can impede the receiving party’s understanding and invite challenges or disputes. Precision matters — both in what you redact and what you leave visible.

## **Redactions best practices**


Redactions work best when you approach them with a clear sense of purpose. At their core, they are about protecting information that should not be disclosed — whether that’s privileged communications, personal data, or sensitive business material.


To do that well, you need a workflow built for consistency, efficiency, and accuracy.


That’s why redactions should not be tacked onto the end of review. They should be[part of the workflow](https://support.csdisco.com/hc/en-us/articles/360041157912-Review-Workflows-Webinar) from day one, so every decision is traceable, consistent, and supported by the record.


At the same time, redactions live in the real world of tight timelines, rising data volumes, and developing regulations. Review teams have to move quickly without sacrificing precision to hit hard deadlines.


The challenge is finding the balance: protecting sensitive information thoroughly while keeping the workflow efficient enough to scale. When reviewers keep that balance in mind — defensibility, data protection, and efficiency — the rest of the redaction process becomes far more focused and predictable.


Let’s look at some best practices for achieving this balance.


### **Start early**


Early planning and training help reviewers understand what they’re looking for and ensure privilege, PII, PHI, and confidential business information are consistently identified throughout the dataset.


**Key point** : Redactions are like the old adage regarding voting — they should happen “early and often” to avoid last-minute bottlenecks.


### **Avoid context switching**


Reviewers make more accurate decisions when they apply the same analytical mindset to every document. Instead of toggling between “responsive review” and “redaction review,” reviewers should be in a redaction workflow mindset when applying redactions.


This reduces errors and prevents sensitive data from slipping through simply because a document was initially coded non-responsive.


### **Group documents by family and thread**


Redactions must be applied consistently across email chains, family groups, and near-duplicate documents.


When the same reviewer handles all related documents, they can more easily recognize repeated content, track how sensitive information appears across a conversation, and apply redactions uniformly from the start of a thread to the end.


### **Apply redactions systematically**


It can help to review email threads from oldest to newest. This makes it easier to spot patterns and maintain consistency.


This old-to-new approach is especially valuable when privileged content or sensitive information appears repeatedly across versions of the same message. A consistent order also reduces the chance of missed redactions, which means fewer gaps to chase down later and less rework before production.


### **QC as a safeguard**


Quality control is what keeps redactions consistent and court-ready. It’s where teams catch missed sensitive information, confirm that duplicates and threads were handled the same way, and ensure redactions appear everywhere they should.


QC matters even more when the same language repeats across documents — like a privileged phrase, a personal identifier, or a highly confidential, commercially sensitive chemical formula.

### **Accuracy, consistency, and evidentiary integrity**


Modern platforms track every redaction applied, creating **audit trails that demonstrate intent and process** if questions arise later. When privileged or confidential business information appears repeatedly across **families** , **email threads** , or **near-duplicate documents** , technology helps reviewers spot those overlaps and ensure the same content is redacted the same way in each instance.


Sensitive information isn’t always limited to what appears on the page. It can live in file properties and other embedded fields. Technology also **strengthens clawback arguments** . If a privileged phrase appears ten times but slips through once, the workflow and audit trail show it was an isolated mistake — not an intentional waiver.


In this way, technology reinforces reviewer judgment, helping teams apply redactions with the precision and consistency courts expect.


### **Scalability and flexibility**


Modern platforms enable review teams to **handle massive email collections, mixed data types** , and multi-layered review workstreams. Bulk redactions, mass edits, and structured workflows allow experts to quickly apply decisions across wide swaths of material.


Cloud-based infrastructures add another layer of flexibility. Because reviewers can **work securely from anywhere,** teams can expand rapidly when deadlines demand it, bring in additional resources across time zones, and continue redaction work around the clock without compromising security or oversight.

### **Continued advancements**


Technology continues to push redactions forward, especially in areas that traditionally required painstaking manual effort.


One of the most anticipated advancements is **one-to-many redactions** — the ability to redact a privileged paragraph once and have that redaction propagate automatically to every instance of the same content across a dataset, **even when it appears later as quoted or forwarded text inside other documents** .


This capability will be especially powerful in email threads, where privileged language often appears in multiple back-and-forths, or in recurring reports or financial summaries.


Other emerging improvements include **better handling of handwritten content** , more **advanced pattern recognition** , and **AI-assisted spotting** of repeated sensitive material.


While human QC will always be essential, no algorithm can yet match a reviewer’s judgment on privilege, yet automation will continue reducing the rote work that slows teams.


The future of redactions is technology handling the repetitive mechanics while reviewers focus on the decisions that require expertise.
