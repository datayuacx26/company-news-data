# Document Review Process & Best Practices for Legal Teams

In modern litigation, a team's first major hurdle is a tidal wave of[electronically stored information (ESI)](https://csdisco.com/blog/what-is-ediscovery) . When faced with hundreds of thousands of unorganized emails, chat logs, and corporate files, it’s difficult to see the forest for the trees. Document review transforms that mountain of raw, collected data into the clear, actionable insights needed to build a winning case strategy.


In this article, we will explore the seven stages of the legal document review process, common challenges, and the definitive best practices that keep your productions accurate, consistent, and legally sound.


## **What is legal document review?**


**Legal document review** is the ediscovery phase where legal teams systematically examine a data collection to determine its relevance to a matter, analyze it for legal privilege, and identify sensitive information that requires protection.


It is a cornerstone of the litigation process, enabling teams to:


- Uncover key facts for case development
- Fulfill legal discovery obligations
- Protect confidential data


## **The legal document review process**


A successful document review requires a highly structured, repeatable workflow to move data safely from collection to production. Dedicated **linear teams** — review managers and contract attorneys who systematically move through data one document at a time — walk through seven distinct phases to filter out noise and isolate key evidence.

Document review begins with[locating and gathering potential sources of ESI from custodians](https://csdisco.com/blog/plan-defensible-data-collections) . ### **Phase 2: Processing and organization**


Once collected, raw data must be processed into a reviewable format. This step includes[deduplication](https://support.csdisco.com/hc/en-us/articles/206184710-Deduplication-within-DISCO) (removing duplicate files) and[indexing](https://support.csdisco.com/hc/en-us/articles/5966615063565-Topic-clustering-with-automatic-indexing) the text for search. Data is organized logically, often grouped by custodian or date range, to make the subsequent review as efficient as possible.


### **Phase 3: First pass document review and redaction flagging**


During initial review, teams focus on evaluating document responsiveness against the matter’s specific criteria. To maintain momentum and accuracy, reviewers should avoid applying actual[redactions](https://support.csdisco.com/hc/en-us/articles/360046056451-Redactions-Feature-Spotlight) on the fly. Instead, as they determine relevance, they simply apply a uniform "needing redaction" tag to any file or document family containing sensitive data, safely isolating those files for specialized truncation passes later.


### **Phase 4: Thread aggregation and programmatic QC**


Once the first pass is complete, senior teams perform quality control (QC) by systematically structuring the flagged data rather than reviewing files at random. Instead of relying on manual oversight alone, the workflow programmatically groups identical duplicates, near-duplicates, and email threads together.

### **Phase 5: Privilege review and logging**


Any document flagged for legal protection undergoes rigorous evaluation. Legal teams must verify whether a document qualifies as **attorney-client communication** or **work product** (information assembled specifically in anticipation of or during litigation). Documents withheld or redacted for privilege are then formally cataloged on a privilege log provided to opposing counsel.


### **Phase 6: Production**


After validation, responsive, non-privileged documents are finalized for production. The documents are exported in the format agreed upon by both parties (such as native files or static images), along with their corresponding metadata and cleanly applied redaction labels.


### **Phase 7: Ongoing and post-production review**


Review continues beyond production. Teams must analyze incoming data produced by opposing counsel to build depositions, refine fact development, and adjust case strategies as new evidence comes to light.

## **Legal document review considerations**


To conduct a successful review, linear teams must weigh several substantive questions for every file they handle.


### **Is this document relevant?**


Reviewers must determine if the text or metadata explicitly relates to the legal issues or requests for production defined in the matter.


### **Does privilege apply?**


Teams must identify protected attorney-client communications and work product. Work product is easily overlooked because files created purely to support litigation — like internal research or draft strategies — frequently lack an attorney’s name in the metadata or text.


### **What requires protection vs. what can be managed via protective orders?**


Legal privilege is sacrosanct and always requires physical redaction prior to production, but other sensitive data can be handled less restrictively. Personally Identifiable Information (PII), Protected Health Information (PHI), and corporate trade secrets can often be managed early in a case through court-approved Attorney’s Eyes Only (AEO) designations.


### **Does this response address production requests?**


Every document must be cross-referenced against the specific parameters of the case's discovery demands to ensure exact compliance without over-producing or under-producing.


### **Are we adhering to coding protocols?**


Reviewers must strictly follow the defined instructions for tagging and metadata coding to maintain database integrity and enable reliable downstream QC searches.

## **Legal document review challenges**


Even the most seasoned legal teams face systemic hurdles that can derail timelines and compromise data security if left unchecked.


### **Volume of ESI**


Modern litigation frequently deals with hundreds of thousands of files. Managing massive terabytes of data manually makes it an uphill battle to catch every piece of sensitive information.


### **Discovery deadlines**


Courts enforce rigid production schedules. When deadlines loom, teams frequently run out of time, making hurried, last-minute review a high-risk operational environment.


### **Cost management**


Extended timelines, massive data hosting fees, and hundreds of billable human hours spent on highly repetitive manual tasks can quickly overrun corporate litigation budgets.


### [Read the case study.](https://csdisco.com/resource/exigent-and-disco-drive-40-cost-reduction-for-leighton-asia) **** This company reviewed 14 terabytes of data across four continents in just six weeks — while lowering costs by 40%.


### **Consistency across reviewers**


### When large teams handle data, inconsistencies naturally arise. One reviewer might spot a sensitive chemical formula or corporate trade secret, while another reviewer misses it entirely in a related email thread.


### **Data security and confidentiality**


Failing to secure PII or PHI can violate strict compliance requirements. Teams need to understand the risks associated with tools that aren’t designed for ediscovery. For example, redactions performed in PDF tools can be easily reversed, leading to disastrous data leaks.


### **Finding qualified reviewers**


Document review requires specialized legal professionals fluent in complex litigation contexts, strict confidentiality guidelines, and modern software protocols. Sourcing, vetting, and onboarding this highly technical talent on short notice can create a massive operational bottleneck. Without an on-demand, specialized talent pool ready to deploy instantly, teams may struggle to scale up efficiently when a sudden influx of data hits the docket.


### **Inefficient workflows**


Traditional protocols can lead to flat-file reviews that fail to group related data. This fragmented approach routes near-duplicates to different reviewers, scatters email threads out of chronological order, and forces managers to waste hours manually reconciling conflicting coding decisions across identical files.


### **Outdated technology**


Legacy ediscovery systems were built for basic emails and static files. They lack the processing power and native flexibility to ingest, index, and organize complex modern data types like[Slack channels](https://csdisco.com/blog/slack-ediscovery) ,[Microsoft Teams logs](https://csdisco.com/blog/ediscovery-for-ms-teams-chat) , and[ephemeral messaging apps](https://csdisco.com/blog/mastering-ephemeral-data-for-ediscovery) . This rigidity creates immediate data-ingestion bottlenecks, forcing review teams to rely on clumsy, manual workarounds that delay timelines before the review even begins.


## **6 best practices for effective legal document review**


To mitigate risk, control costs, and ensure legal compliance, your team should anchor their strategy around proven industry best practices.


### **1. Define the scope and objectives**


Establish clear discovery boundaries at the beginning of the matter. It’s important to know exactly what data fields, custodians, and date ranges are legally required to prevent costly scope creep.


### **2. Develop a review protocol from the start**


A well-defined protocol acts as the ultimate playbook for the matter, ensuring the entire team reviews documents with total consistency. It provides guidelines for:


- Mapping privilege
- Standardizing tagging logic
- Protecting sensitive data


### **3. Assemble the right team**


Deploy a balanced mix of specialized review managers and linear reviewers. For high-pressure matters, leveraging global resourcing models — such as utilizing integrated teams across different time zones — can effectively double operational output and ensure around-the-clock progress.


[Read the case study.](https://csdisco.com/resource/complex-lit-case-study) **** DISCO’s Professional Services team delivered a 71% reduction in first-pass review time when they partnered with a leading Am Law 200 firm struggling to meet evolving client demand.


### **4. Leverage advanced search and filtering**


Ditch basic keyword hunting. Implement advanced search parameters and regular expressions (RegEx) to find structured strings like Social Security or financial numbers.


Activating these rules produces persistent, case-specific lists that flag protected text and automatically draw reviewers' eyes to sensitive terms. This ensures no critical data is missed, even when scanning thousands of pages a day.


### **5. Implement quality control measures**


A robust QC workflow must group identical duplicates, near-duplicates, and email threads together. Organizing emails sequentially from oldest to newest allows a single redactor to trace the exact pattern of a conversation, ensuring that information that drops off or reappears inside a thread is captured uniformly.


\[Oldest Email\] ──> Tagged for PII / Flagged for Redaction


│


\[Reply Email\] ──> QC ensures the historic thread content is also redacted


│


\[Newest Email\] ──> Consistent application protects data across the family


### **6. Document for defensibility**


A documented, rigorous review process is a team’s primary defense against discovery challenges, motions to compel, and the inadvertent production of privileged data. Accountability can be strengthened by:


- Maintaining clear audit trails
- Establishing consistent feedback loops with counsel
- Conducting proactive statistical sampling to demonstrate that your process was legally reasonable
