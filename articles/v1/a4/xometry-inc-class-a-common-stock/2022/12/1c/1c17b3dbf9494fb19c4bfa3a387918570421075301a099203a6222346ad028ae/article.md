# TPR vs. PVC: Material Differences and Comparisons

Thermoplastic Rubber (TPR) is a synthetic elastomer that combines the processing advantages of plastics with the elasticity of rubber. It can be repeatedly melted and reformed, making it ideal for injection molding and extrusion. TPR is commonly used in applications requiring flexibility, durability, and slip resistance, such as shoe soles, grips, soft-touch handles, and seals.


Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC) is a widely used engineering thermoplastic known for its chemical resistance and versatility. It is available in two forms: rigid (uPVC) and flexible (plasticized PVC). Rigid PVC is extensively used in the construction industry for pipes, profiles, and fittings, while flexible PVC is found in protective equipment, flexible tubing, and insulation. Both types can be processed using extrusion, injection molding, or blow molding.


This article compares TPR and PVC in terms of properties, processing methods, applications, and cost considerations to help guide material selection based on performance and end-use requirements.


## What Is TPR?


TPR refers to a class of synthetic elastomers that can be melt-processed using conventional thermoplastic manufacturing methods such as injection molding, compression molding, and blow molding. These materials exhibit rubber-like elasticity at room temperature but soften and flow when heated, allowing for efficient shaping and reshaping without permanent chemical changes. In contrast, traditional rubbers, both natural and synthetic (e.g., butyl rubber), are typically thermoset materials. They do not melt upon heating and will undergo thermal degradation if overheated due to their cross-linked molecular structure. TPR is used in applications that demand a flexible, durable, and high-grip material, especially when the part geometry is complex and requires processing through thermoplastic techniques. It offers a balance of properties, including good toughness, chemical resistance, and fatigue resistance, making it suitable for grips, seals, soft-touch components, and overmolded parts.

TPR is made up of Styrene Butadiene-Styrene (SBS), which is a type of block copolymer. TPR is synthesized using a process called living anionic polymerization. This is a chain polymerization process that polymerizes two monomers, namely butadiene and styrene, into SBS. This creates a polymer structure with hard, crystalline regions and soft, amorphous regions. The hard regions contain styrene, and the soft regions contain butadiene. The hard regions provide the thermoplastic properties, whereas the amorphous regions supply the elastomeric properties.


## What Is PVC?


PVC is an engineering thermoplastic with excellent resistance to moisture ingress and UV (ultraviolet light). Its good abrasion resistance makes it a popular choice for piping and construction materials. PVC is generally available in two forms, namely plasticized (PVC-P) and unplasticized (PVC-U). Unplasticized PVC is used in parts that require rigidity, like pipes and fittings. Plasticized PVC is used in applications that require flexibility, like cables, tubes, and PPE (personal protective equipment). Modified grades of PVC are available, which have various additives to improve PVC properties, such as overall strength. These grades are referred to as PVC-M (Modified). PVC can be melt-processed using injection molding, extrusion, and blow molding. Additionally, PVC pipes and fittings can be bonded with glue.
