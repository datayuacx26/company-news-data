# PETG 3D Printing Filament: Materials, Properties, and Uses

### What Type of Polymer is PETG?


PETG is a glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate classified as an amorphous thermoplastic polyester. PETG is synthesized by modifying the polymerization of PET with glycol (typically CHDM) to prevent crystallinity and enhance processing that disrupts crystallinity, reduces internal stress during cooling, and stabilize melt behavior during fused deposition modeling. Transparency, moderate chemical resistance, impact toughness, and ductility make PETG suitable for functional 3D printed parts that are required to withstand harsh environments and maintain dimensional consistency. PETG offers advantages for 3D printing through strong layer adhesion, relatively low shrinkage compared to ABS, and improved resistance to brittle fracture under mechanical load. PETG differs from Polylactic Acid (PLA) through higher impact resistance and improved thermal stability, while PLA emphasizes stiffness, dimensional accuracy, and ease of extrusion. ABS emphasizes higher heat resistance and rigidity, while PETG presents lower warping behavior and lower odor and particulate emissions during printing. PETG occupies a middle ground among common 3D printing polymers by balancing strength, toughness, and print reliability for applications including mechanical housings, protective enclosures, and outdoor components as a[Polymer Material](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/what-is-polymer/) .


### What is PETG Filament Made Of?


PETG is made by modifying polyethylene terephthalate (PET) with glycol, forming an amorphous thermoplastic polyester. with reduced crystallinity and improved melt stability. PETG Filament consists of long polymer chains built from terephthalic acid, ethylene glycol, and glycol modifiers that interrupt regular molecular packing and limit crystalline growth during cooling. The filament exhibits a predominantly amorphous polymer structure with limited crystallinity that supports consistent extrusion, strong interlayer bonding, and good dimensional stability during fused deposition modeling. PETG Filament demonstrates balanced 3D printing behavior through controlled melt flow, reduced internal stress during cooling, and improved resistance to brittle fracture under mechanical loading. Polylactic Acid relies on aliphatic polyester chains derived from lactic acid and emphasizes stiffness, surface quality, and low processing temperature. Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene (ABS) relies on a rubber-toughened petroleum-based polymer structure that emphasizes heat resistance and rigidity while introducing higher shrinkage and warping tendencies. PETG Filament occupies a middle position between Polylactic Acid and Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene, combining toughness, moderate chemical resistance, and reliable print performance for functional parts under UV and temperature exposure.


#### Is PETG Considered a Plastic?


Yes, PETG is considered a thermoplastic polymer used in manufacturing and additive manufacturing. PETG belongs to the copolyester family and forms through copolymerization of polyethylene terephthalate with glycol modifiers, producing a melt-processable material with reduced crystallinity and stable melt behavior. PETG appears in 3D printing, packaging, and industrial applications because the material offers toughness, moderate chemical resistance, and dimensional stability under mechanical stress and controlled environmental exposure as a[plastic material](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/what-is-plastic/) .


## What are the Properties of PETG Filament?


The properties of PETG Filament are listed below.


- **Melting Point** : PETG Filament does not present a sharp melting point because glycol modification reduces crystallinity, producing a broad softening range that supports smooth extrusion during fused deposition modeling.
- **Glass Transition Temperature** : PETG Filament reaches glass transition in the range of 75 to 85 degrees Celsius, defining the temperature range where the material transitions from rigid behavior to rubber-like deformation under load.
- **Temperature Resistance** : PETG Filament maintains structural integrity at moderate service temperatures and resists deformation better than low-temperature polymers such as PLA under sustained heat exposure.
- **Flexibility** : PETG Filament demonstrates moderate flexibility through ductile deformation rather than brittle fracture, supporting impact resistance and load-bearing performance in functional parts.
- **UV Resistance** : PETG Filament offers moderate resistance to ultraviolet exposure, supporting limited outdoor use with gradual degradation unless UV stabilizers or protective coatings are applied.
- **Hygroscopicity** : PETG Filament absorbs moisture from the environment at a moderate rate, requiring dry storage conditions to preserve surface finish and extrusion consistency during printing.
- **Heat Resistance** : PETG Filament resists thermal softening better than PLA under continuous mechanical stress while remaining below the heat tolerance of high-temperature engineering plastics.


### What is the density of PETG?


The density of PETG is approximately 1.27 grams per cubic centimeter or 1270 kilograms per cubic meter. PETG density reflects a relatively compact polymer structure that contributes to part weight and dimensional stability, while resistance to deformation depends primarily on mechanical properties rather than density alone. PETG density influences printed component mass, producing parts heavier than PLA, while improved impact resistance and structural reliability result from polymer toughness and ductile deformation in functional applications.


### What is the glass transition temperature of PETG?


The glass transition temperature of PETG is 80 degrees Celsius. PETG reaches glass transition near that temperature range where the polymer transitions from rigid solid behavior to rubber-like deformation, representing an approximate upper limit for dimensional stability under sustained load. PETG glass transition temperature supports strong layer adhesion by allowing interlayer diffusion when deposited above glass transition, while shape retention during cooling results from gradual solidification as the material cools below that threshold.


### What is the heat resistance of PETG?


The heat resistance of PETG under continuous mechanical load ranges up to 60 to 70 degrees Celsius before noticeable softening and creep occur, depending on part geometry and load conditions. PETG maintains limited structural stability below its glass transition temperature, supporting functional parts exposed to moderate heat, while deformation increases rapidly as temperatures approach glass transition. PETG shows higher heat resistance than Polylactic Acid at 55 to 60 degrees Celsius, while remaining below Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene, which tolerates continuous service temperatures near 80 to 85 degrees Celsius.


#### Does PETG Shrink?


Yes, PETG shrinks during cooling, although shrinkage remains low compared to thermoplastics used in fused deposition modeling. PETG shrinkage results from thermal contraction as extruded material transitions from melt to solid, introducing limited dimensional change and reduced warping risk. PETG maintains print stability through controlled cooling, consistent bed adhesion, moderate build plate temperatures, and reduced internal stress resulting from glycol modification.


#### Is PETG Waterproof?


Yes, PETG is considered waterproof in practical use because the polymer exhibits very low water absorption and does not dissolve or chemically degrade when exposed to moisture. PETG forms polymer chains with low permeability that limit water penetration, while strong interlayer adhesion during printing supports waterproof parts when extrusion parameters achieve adequate layer fusion. PETG suits applications involving liquid contact and limited outdoor exposure, while prolonged immersion or insufficient layer bonding introduces seepage risk through interlayer gaps rather than through the polymer material itself.


#### Is PETG Hygroscopic?


Yes, PETG is hygroscopic and absorbs moisture from the surrounding environment over time. PETG moisture absorption occurs at a lower rate than nylon and also lower than Polylactic Acid (PLA), explaining sensitivity to ambient humidity during storage. PETG moisture content affects print quality through surface roughness, stringing, inconsistent extrusion, and reduced layer adhesion, supporting dry storage and pre-drying practices to preserve material performance.


## What is the difference between PET vs PETG?


The difference between PET and PETG lies in polymer structure, processability, and application suitability. PET is a semi‑crystalline or amorphous, but industrial PET used in bottles and packaging is often semi‑crystalline for strength and clarity. Polyethylene terephthalate polymer is used in injection molding, blow molding, and thermoforming, while PETG is a glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate copolyester with reduced crystallinity. PETG incorporates glycol units through copolymerization with polyethylene terephthalate, disrupting molecular packing, lowering internal stress, and improving melt stability. PET exhibits higher stiffness, higher chemical resistance, and greater thermal resistance in finished products, while PET processing requires controlled industrial conditions due to crystallization behavior and dimensional shrinkage during cooling. PETG supports stable extrusion, strong layer adhesion, and low warping behavior, explaining widespread use in fused deposition modeling and functional 3D printing. PET appears frequently in bottles, food packaging, and industrial films, while PETG appears in 3D printed enclosures, protective components, medical housings, and transparent parts where impact resistance and dimensional consistency are required.


### Which filament is better between ABS vs PETG?


PETG is generally better for casual and desktop 3D printing due to easier processing and lower shrinkage; ABS may outperform in heat resistance and stiffness where those are required. PETG provides strong impact resistance and ductile behavior while maintaining lower warping risk and reduced internal stress during cooling compared to ABS. It prints at moderate nozzle temperatures and adheres well to build surfaces without requiring fully enclosed chambers, supporting consistent print quality and improved dimensional stability under typical desktop printing conditions. ABS offers higher heat resistance and rigidity than PETG, which suits applications exposed to elevated temperatures. ABS printing involves higher nozzle and bed temperatures, greater shrinkage, and increased warping risk, complicating print reliability and dimensional control. PETG is tough and ductile, but not flexible in the way TPU or flexible filaments are, while ABS suits structural components that require higher heat resistance and rigid performance in[ABS vs. PETG](https://www.xometry.com/resources/3d-printing/abs-vs-petg-3d-printing/) comparisons.
