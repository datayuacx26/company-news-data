# Iron (Fe): Element, Symbol, Atomic Number and Uses

The four main types of iron differ primarily by carbon content, with higher carbon generally increasing hardness and brittleness while reducing ductility. Pig iron, produced in blast furnaces from iron ore and coke, serves as the raw material from which cast iron and steel are derived through further refining. Wrought iron, historically produced by puddling or hammering, contains the lowest carbon among the four and behaves more like a pure metal. Steel, with carbon content from 0.008% to 2.14%, represents the most widely produced and applied form of iron worldwide.


### 1. Pig Iron


Pig iron is the intermediate product of iron ore smelting in a blast furnace, containing carbon concentrations from 3.5% to 4.5% by weight, along with residual amounts of silicon (0.5% to 1%), manganese (0.25% to 2.5%), sulfur (below 0.05%), and phosphorus (below 0.12%). The high carbon content makes pig iron extremely brittle and unsuitable for direct use as a structural material, limiting its mechanical applications. The name "pig iron" derives from the traditional casting process, where molten iron was poured into a central channel (the "sow") feeding smaller lateral molds ("pigs"), resembling a sow nursing piglets. Pig iron serves primarily as a raw feedstock for steelmaking in basic oxygen furnaces (BOF) and BOF steelmaking reduces carbon and impurities; EAF steelmaking is often scrap-based and may use pig iron/hot metal as an input. It is also remelted to produce cast iron by adjusting the composition and adding alloying elements. Xometry's manufacturing network processes iron-based raw materials, including sourcing from pig iron feedstocks, for casting and machining production runs.


### 2. Cast Iron


Cast iron is an iron-carbon alloy with carbon content from 2% to 4% by weight, a range that exceeds the maximum carbon solubility in austenite (2.14%) and results in the presence of graphite or cementite in the microstructure. The high carbon content gives cast iron excellent fluidity in the molten state, making it highly castable into complex geometries with fine surface detail. Cast iron's compressive strength is high, ranging from 570 MPa to 1,380 MPa depending on grade, but tensile strength is significantly lower due to the brittle nature of graphite or carbide inclusions. The four primary grades are gray iron (graphite flakes), white iron (cementite), ductile iron (nodular graphite), and malleable iron (tempered carbon clusters), each with distinct mechanical behavior. Engine blocks, machine tool bases, pipe fittings, and cookware are frequent applications due to the material's wear resistance and damping capacity. Xometry's custom casting services cover cast iron components across gray iron, ductile iron, and white iron grades.


### 3. Wrought Iron


Wrought iron is a nearly pure iron material with a carbon content below 0.08% by weight, distinguished by the presence of slag inclusions (iron silicate fibers) distributed throughout the metal matrix in a fibrous pattern. The slag content, from 1% to 3% by weight, is not a contaminant but rather a structural component that improves wrought iron's resistance to fatigue and corrosion relative to pure iron. Wrought iron exhibits high ductility (elongation up to 35%), excellent malleability, and a tensile strength from 310 MPa to 500 MPa. The material is produced historically through the puddling process or, more recently, through direct reduction methods. Wrought iron's low carbon content makes it easy to weld, forge, and shape at temperatures below its melting point, a property exploited in historical architectural ironwork, anchor chains, and decorative gates. Modern use of **** wrought iron is limited due to steel's superior mechanical properties and cost efficiency, but it remains relevant in restoration and decorative metalwork.


### 4. Steel


Steel is an iron-carbon alloy with carbon content from 0.008% to 2.14% by weight, a range that encompasses thousands of grades differentiated by carbon level, alloying elements, and heat treatment condition. The combination of iron's abundance with carbon's ability to strengthen the lattice makes steel the most produced metal globally, with annual production exceeding 1.8 billion metric tons. Steel's mechanical properties span a wide range: tensile strength from 400 MPa in low-carbon mild steel to over 2,000 MPa in high-strength alloy steels. Alloying elements (chromium, nickel, molybdenum, vanadium, and manganese) expand steel's capability by improving corrosion resistance, toughness, wear resistance, and heat resistance. Carbon steel, alloy steel, stainless steel, and tool[steel](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/steel/) represent the major categories, each serving distinct industrial functions. Xometry provides CNC machining, fabrication, and custom parts production across a broad range of steel grades and specifications.


## What Is Mild Steel in Terms of Iron (Fe) Composition?


Mild steel, in terms of iron composition, is a low-carbon iron alloy with a carbon content from 0.05% to 0.25% by weight, placing it at the lower end of the carbon steel spectrum. The low carbon concentration limits the formation of carbide phases, resulting in a predominantly ferritic and pearlitic microstructure that is soft, ductile, and highly workable. Mild steel exhibits a tensile strength from 400 MPa to 550 MPa, a yield strength from 250 MPa to 350 MPa, and an elongation at fracture of approximately 20% to 25%, reflecting its plastic deformation capacity. The material is easily welded using standard arc, MIG, and TIG welding processes often without requiring pre-heat in most applications, making it a preferred choice for structural frames, automotive body panels, pipes, and general fabrication. Xometry's manufacturing platform supports mild steel fabrication across laser cutting, CNC machining, and sheet metal forming, with[mild steel](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/mild-steel/) being one of the most frequently ordered material categories.


### Why Is Mild Steel Considered a low-carbon iron (Fe) Alloy?


Mild steel is considered a low-carbon iron alloy because its carbon content falls from 0.05% to 0.25% by weight, a range that keeps the microstructure predominantly ferritic without forming the extensive carbide networks characteristic of medium and high-carbon steels. The quantity of cementite (Fe₃C) precipitated during cooling is insufficient to significantly impair ductility or weldability at carbon levels below 0.25%. The ferritic matrix dominates the microstructure, contributing to mild steel's characteristic softness and formability. Low carbon content also reduces hardenability, meaning mild steel does not harden significantly through quenching, a limitation that simultaneously makes it predictable and easy to process. The "low carbon" designation aligns with classifications in ASTM A108 (standard specification for steel bar), where grades with below 0.25% carbon qualify as low-carbon steel.


#### Does Mild Steel Contain Less Than 0.25% Carbon in Iron (Fe) Structure?


Yes, mild steel contains less than 0.25% carbon in its iron structure, a compositional limit that distinguishes it from medium-carbon steel (0.25% to 0.60%) and high-carbon steel (0.60% to 1.4%). The carbon ceiling of 0.25% is not arbitrary; it marks the threshold above which weldability begins to decline due to increased hardenability and susceptibility to heat-affected zone (HAZ) cracking during welding. Below 0.25% carbon, mild steel maintains adequate ductility for cold forming operations and sufficient toughness for structural use at ambient temperatures. Grades such as ASTM A36 (structural steel, carbon up to 0.26%) and SAE 1018 (carbon 0.15% to 0.20%) are representative examples of mild steel with carbon content below 0.25%.


## What Is 430 Stainless Steel in Relation to Iron (Fe)?


430 stainless steel in relation to iron is an iron-based alloy belonging to the ferritic stainless steel family, characterized by a chromium content from 16% to 18% by weight and a carbon content below 0.12%. The iron matrix of 430 stainless steel constitutes the majority of the alloy's composition, with chromium as the primary alloying element responsible for corrosion resistance. Chromium at concentrations above 10.5% forms a passive chromium oxide (Cr₂O₃) layer on the steel surface, protecting the underlying iron from oxidation and atmospheric corrosion. Grade 430 is non-hardenable by heat treatment due to its ferritic crystal structure, which remains BCC across all temperatures up to the melting range. It offers moderate corrosion resistance, good formability, and resistance to oxidation at elevated temperatures up to 870°C. Xometry's material catalog includes[430 stainless steel](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/430-stainless-steel/) for parts requiring corrosion resistance without the cost of austenitic grades like 304 or 316.


### Why Is 430 Stainless Steel Classified as an Iron-Based Ferritic Stainless Steel?


430 stainless steel is classified as an iron-base ferritic stainless steel due to its BCC crystal structure, maintained across all temperatures from ambient to near-melting, and its composition of iron, chromium (16% to 18%), and low carbon (below 0.12%) without sufficient nickel to stabilize the austenitic (FCC) phase. Ferritic stainless steels form when the chromium-equivalent exceeds the nickel-equivalent in the alloy's composition, a balance quantified by the Schaeffler diagram used in welding metallurgy. Grade 430 contains no significant nickel addition, distinguishing it from austenitic grades (304, 316) that require 8% to 12% nickel to maintain FCC structure. The ferritic classification means 430 stainless steel is weakly ferromagnetic, not hardenable through quenching, and limited in toughness at sub-zero temperatures. The iron-chromium base and BCC structure define ferritic stainless steels, including grade 430.


#### Does 430 Stainless Steel Rely on Chromium Instead of High Carbon in Its Iron Matrix?


Yes, 430 stainless steel relies on chromium from 16% to 18% instead of high carbon content to achieve its defining properties of corrosion resistance and oxidation resistance. Carbon in 430 stainless steel is kept below 0.12% intentionally, because higher carbon levels would promote the formation of chromium carbides (Cr₂₃C₆) at grain boundaries during welding or slow cooling, a phenomenon called sensitization, which depletes chromium from the matrix and reduces corrosion resistance. The chromium content provides the passive oxide layer that prevents iron oxidation without requiring the carbon strengthening mechanism used in tool steels or high-carbon grades. The low-carbon, high-chromium composition of grade 430 makes it weldable (with limitations), formable, and resistant to oxidizing environments, with chromium as the primary functional alloying element.


## What Are the Main Uses of Iron?


The main uses of Iron are listed below.


- **Construction and Infrastructure** : Iron, primarily in the form of steel, is the dominant structural material in buildings, bridges, dams, and roads. Reinforcement bars (rebar) embedded in concrete are typically made from carbon steel with iron as the base, providing tensile strength to otherwise compression-limited concrete structures.
- **Steel Production** : Iron ore processed in blast furnaces produces pig iron, which is refined into steel through oxygen steelmaking or electric arc furnace processes. Steel production accounts for the largest single consumption of iron globally, at over 1.8 billion metric tons annually.
- **Transportation (Vehicles, Trains, Ships)** : Iron-based alloys form the structural frames, engine components, axles, and body panels of automobiles, locomotives, freight railcars, and marine vessels. High-strength low-alloy (HSLA) steels derived from iron reduce vehicle weight while maintaining structural integrity.
- **Tools and Machinery** : Iron alloys are used in cutting tools, dies, molds, gears, shafts, and machine frames where hardness, wear resistance, and strength are required. Tool steels and cast-iron machine bases are standard in precision manufacturing equipment.
- **Household and Everyday Items:** Cast iron cookware, wrought iron furniture, steel appliances, and iron-containing consumer goods are direct domestic applications. Cast iron cookware retains heat effectively due to its high volumetric heat capacity and mass.
- **Magnets and Electrical Applications** : Iron's ferromagnetic properties make it the core material in electromagnets, transformers, electric motors, and generators. Silicon-iron alloys (electrical steel) with silicon content from 2% to 4% reduce core losses in transformers.
- **Chemical and Industrial Uses** : Iron catalyzes the Haber process for ammonia synthesis, a feedstock for iron-based pigments (Fe₂O₃ for red pigment), and a raw material in chemical manufacturing. Iron compounds are used in water treatment to precipitate phosphates and clarify drinking water.


### 1. Iron for Construction and Infrastructure


Iron, in the form of structural steel, reinforcement bar (rebar), and cast iron pipe, forms the foundational material layer of modern construction and civil infrastructure. Structural steel sections (I-beams, H-piles, wide-flange sections) carry compressive and tensile loads in building frames, with yield strengths from 250 MPa to 690 MPa depending on grade. Rebar embedded in reinforced concrete supplies tensile capacity to concrete slabs, columns, and beams, addressing concrete's inherent weakness in tension. Grade 60 rebar (ASTM A615), one of the most widely used construction iron products, has a minimum yield strength of 414 MPa. Cast iron and ductile iron pipes transport water, sewage, and gas in municipal infrastructure systems; ductile iron pipes carry internal pressures from 1,380 kPa to 2,400 kPa without failure. Iron's cost efficiency relative to its mechanical performance makes it the default structural material for projects ranging from residential framing to high-rise towers and suspension bridges.
