# Sulfur (S): Formula, Element and Uses

### What Is the Chemical Formula of Sulfur (S)?


The chemical formula of Sulfur is represented by the symbol S as a single element, but in its most stable naturally occurring molecular form, it exists as S₈, a crown-shaped ring of eight covalently bonded sulfur atoms. The S₈ molecule, known as cyclooctasulfur or orthorhombic sulfur, is the thermodynamically stable allotrope of sulfur at room temperature and standard pressure. Each sulfur atom in the S₈ ring forms two covalent bonds with adjacent sulfur atoms, completing its valence shell without forming double bonds in the ring structure. Sulfur also exists in other allotropic forms: S₆ (rhombohedral sulfur), S₁₂, and polymeric sulfur (plastic sulfur), a chain-like amorphous form produced by quenching molten sulfur in cold water. In chemical compounds, sulfur appears in formulas including H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid), H₂S (hydrogen sulfide), SO₂ (sulfur dioxide), and Na₂SO₄ (sodium sulfate), each reflecting sulfur's variable oxidation states from -2 to +6.


### What Is Sulfur (S) as a Chemical Element?


Sulfur is a nonmetallic chemical element in Group 16 (chalcogens) of the periodic table, Period 3, with the atomic number 16 and the standard atomic weight of 32.06 u. The element has an electronegativity of 2.58 on the Pauling scale, a value reflecting its moderate tendency to attract electrons in covalent bonds, placing it below oxygen (3.44) and above carbon (2.55). Sulfur has four naturally occurring stable isotopes: sulfur-32 (94.93%), sulfur-33 (0.76%), sulfur-34 (4.29%), and sulfur-36 (0.02%), with sulfur-32 dominating natural abundance. The electron configuration of sulfur is \[Ne\] 3s² 3p⁴, providing six valence electrons that drive its chemical bonding in compounds ranging from sulfides (-2 oxidation state) to sulfates (+6 oxidation state). Sulfur melts at 115.2°C and boils at 444.6°C, properties that make it processable at relatively low temperatures compared to metals, supporting its use in chemical manufacturing and vulcanization processes.


#### Is Sulfur a Metal or Nonmetal?


Sulfur is a nonmetal, and its physical and chemical properties confirm the classification across every relevant criterion. At room temperature, sulfur is a brittle, yellow solid that fractures when struck, lacking the malleability and ductility characteristic of metals. Sulfur's electrical conductivity is extremely low at approximately 10⁻¹⁵ S/m, making it an electrical insulator rather than a conductor. Thermal conductivity measures 0.205 W/(m·K), far below the values of metals (iron: 80 W/(m·K), copper: 400 W/(m·K)). Sulfur does not exhibit metallic luster; its surface is dull and matte yellow. Chemically, sulfur gains electrons (acts as an oxidizing agent) instead of donating them, a behavior opposite to metals. The combination of brittleness, non-conductivity, low thermal conductivity, and electron-gaining behavior classifies sulfur unambiguously as a nonmetal.


## How Is Sulfuric Acid Anodizing Connected to Sulfur Chemistry?


Sulfuric acid anodizing is connected to sulfur-based compounds, specifically sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), as the electrolyte solution that drives the electrochemical formation of a protective aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) layer on aluminum surfaces, connecting sulfur chemistry directly to metal surface engineering. In the anodizing process, aluminum parts are immersed in a sulfuric acid bath with concentrations from 15% to 20% H₂SO₄ by weight, at temperatures from 18°C to 22°C, and a direct current is applied at voltage levels from 12 V to 21 V. The sulfuric acid provides the ionic conductivity needed to sustain the electrochemical reaction, where oxygen ions generated at the anode react with the aluminum surface to grow a porous Al₂O₃ film from 5 µm to 25 µm thick. Sulfur incorporates into the oxide film as sulfate ions, typically comprising 12% to 15% of the layer by weight, and is the chemically active component of the electrolyte that governs film growth rate, porosity, and thickness. ### How Is Sulfuric Acid Used in Anodizing Processes?


Sulfuric acid is used in anodizing processes as the primary electrolyte in Type II (standard) and Type III (hard coat), providing the ionic medium through which electrical current flows and aluminum oxide grows on the workpiece surface. A 15% to 20% H₂SO₄ solution at 18°C to 22°C and 12 V to 21 V produces an oxide layer from 5 µm to 25 µm thick with moderate porosity in Type II anodizing, suitable for dyeing and corrosion protection in architectural and consumer applications. Higher H₂SO₄ concentrations (up to 25%) combined with lower temperatures (0°C to 5°C) and higher voltages (25 V to 100 V) produce denser oxide layers from 25 µm to 100 µm thick in Type III hard coat anodizing, with hardness from 400 HV to 600 HV, suitable for wear-resistant military, aerospace, and industrial components. The sulfuric acid electrolyte simultaneously drives oxide growth at the barrier layer and partially dissolves the outer porous layer, controlling the equilibrium between oxide formation and dissolution that determines final film thickness and structure.


#### Is Sulfuric Acid Anodizing Used to Improve Metal Corrosion Resistance?


Yes, sulfuric acid anodizing is used to improve metal corrosion resistance by converting the aluminum surface into a thick, stable aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) layer that acts as a physical barrier preventing moisture, oxygen, and corrosive agents from reaching the underlying metal. The anodized Al₂O₃ layer produced in sulfuric acid electrolyte is integral to the aluminum surface, growing both inward and outward from the original metal surface, and does not peel or delaminate like applied coatings. Salt spray testing (ASTM B117) confirms that Type II anodized aluminum withstands 336 hours to 500 hours of salt fog without corrosion failure, while Type III hard coat anodized surfaces exceed 1,000 hours. Sealing the anodized layer in hot water (96°C to 100°C) or nickel acetate solution closes the pores, further improving corrosion resistance by preventing electrolyte penetration into the oxide structure.


## How Does Sulfur Interact With Steel Types and Grades?


Sulfur interacts with steel types and grades through its role as an impurity that affects the steel’s properties. Sulfur is added in controlled amounts to specific steel grades to improve machinability through the formation of manganese sulfide (MnS) inclusions, soft non-metallic particles that act as internal lubricants and chip breakers during cutting operations, reducing tool wear and improving surface finish. Sulfur content ranges from 0.08% to 0.35% by weight in free-machining steels, well above the residual sulfur levels below 0.05% maintained in structural and high-strength steel grades. MnS inclusions form when manganese reacts with sulfur during solidification, precipitating as elongated particles oriented in the rolling direction of the steel bar or plate. The elongated MnS particles disrupt the continuity of the steel matrix in the machining zone, promoting chip breakage and reducing cutting forces by 15% to 30% compared to equivalent low-sulfur grades. Excess sulfur above the specified range reduces transverse toughness, ductility, and weldability, requiring careful composition control in free-machining[steel types and grades](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/steel-types-grades/) .


### How Does Sulfur Affect 4140 Alloy Steel?


Sulfur affects 4140 alloy steel, improving machinability by forming MnS inclusions that act as chip breakers during turning, milling, and drilling operations, reducing cutting forces and extending tool life in high-volume machining environments. Standard 4140 steel (AISI/SAE specification) contains sulfur as a residual element limited to a maximum of 0.040% by weight, a level low enough to preserve toughness, fatigue resistance, and weldability while permitting minor machinability benefits. Free-machining variants of 4140 incorporate sulfur from 0.08% to 0.13%, producing a measurable increase in machinability rating from approximately 55% to 70% of the AISI 1212 baseline. The tradeoff of elevated sulfur in 4140 is a reduction in Charpy impact toughness in the transverse direction, where MnS inclusions act as crack initiation sites. ### How Does Sulfur Affect 1020 Carbon Steel?


Sulfur affects 1020 carbon steel, improving machinability through MnS inclusion formation, with standard 1020 (AISI/SAE) containing sulfur limited to a maximum of 0.050% and free-machining 1215 steel (a sulfur-enhanced low-carbon grade) containing 0.26% to 0.35% sulfur for maximum cutting efficiency. The machinability rating of low-carbon steel increases significantly; 12L14 (leaded, sulfurized) reaches a machinability rating of 170% relative to the AISI 1212 baseline at sulfur levels from 0.26% to 0.35%, while 1215 (sulfurized, non-leaded) achieves 136%. The MnS inclusions in sulfurized 1020-class steels reduce cutting tool temperature by acting as a solid lubricant at the tool-chip interface, lowering crater wear rates on carbide and high-speed steel tooling. Elevated sulfur in low-carbon grades reduces weldability and cold-forming performance, limiting free-machining sulfurized steels to machined-only applications where subsequent welding or forming is not required, especially in[1020 carbon steel](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/1020-carbon-steel/) .


### How Does Sulfur Affect Stainless Steel?


Sulfur affects stainless steel, improving machinability but reducing corrosion resistance, requiring precise composition control to balance cutting performance against the corrosion protection that defines the material's primary function. Standard austenitic grades (304, 316) limit sulfur to a maximum of 0.030% to preserve the integrity of the passive Cr₂O₃ film; MnS inclusions at higher sulfur levels act as initiation sites for pitting corrosion in chloride environments, where sulfide inclusions dissolve preferentially and create corrosion pits in the surrounding matrix. Grade 303 stainless steel is the primary free-machining austenitic variant, containing 0.15% minimum sulfur, a level that dramatically improves machinability (approximately 78% of the AISI 1212 baseline, versus 45% for 304) at the cost of reduced pitting and crevice corrosion resistance relative to 304. The balance from machinability to corrosion resistance in sulfur-controlled[stainless steel](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/what-is-stainless-steel/) grades is governed by the intended service environment and the severity of corrosive exposure.


#### Does Sulfur Improve Machinability in Different Steel Grades?


Yes, sulfur improves machinability in different steel grades by forming manganese sulfide (MnS) inclusions that reduce cutting forces, promote chip breakage, and decrease tool wear during turning, milling, drilling, and threading operations. The machinability improvement is quantified by the AISI machinability rating system, where 1212 steel (0.70% to 1.00% Mn, 0.16% to 0.23% S ) serves as the 100% baseline. Grades with increasing sulfur content show proportional machinability gains: 1215 (0.26% to 0.35% S) achieves 136%, 12L14 (sulfur plus lead) reaches 170%, and 303 stainless (0.15% min S) achieves 78% versus 304's 45%. The machinability benefit of sulfur applies across low-carbon, medium-carbon, alloy, and stainless steel categories, making sulfur the most cost-effective machinability additive in steel composition design.


## What Is the Role of Sulfur in Nickel Alloys?


The role of Sulfur in nickel alloys is added in controlled amounts to specific free-machining grades to improve machinability through sulfide inclusion formation, while in standard structural nickel alloys, sulfur is treated as a harmful impurity limited to trace levels below 0.010% due to its severe embrittling effect on nickel at elevated temperatures. Nickel is highly susceptible to sulfur embrittlement; sulfur forms nickel sulfide (Ni₃S₂) at grain boundaries in nickel-rich alloys, a compound that melts at 637°C and creates liquid-phase grain boundary films that cause hot cracking during welding and hot working at temperatures above 600°C. Sulfur is balanced against manganese content to promote MnS formation rather than Ni₃S₂ in free-machining nickel alloy grades, directing sulfur into benign nickel-copper sulfide inclusions that aid chip breakage without causing grain boundary embrittlement. ### How Is Sulfur Related to Monel 405?


Sulfur is related to Monel 405 at levels from 0.025% to 0.060% by weight to improve machinability, distinguishing grade 405 from Monel 400, which contains residual sulfur below 0.024% and offers superior corrosion resistance but lower machinability. Monel 405 is a nickel-copper alloy (approximately 63% to 70% Ni, 28% to 34% Cu) where the sulfur addition promotes sulfide inclusion formation that aids chip formation during turning, drilling, and threading on automatic screw machines. The controlled sulfur content in Monel 405 raises its machinability rating to approximately 30% of the AISI 1212 baseline, compared to Monel 400's 22%, a meaningful improvement for high-volume machined parts. Sulfur content in Monel 405 is kept within the 0.025% to 0.060% range to ensure nickel-copper sulfide inclusions form preferentially over nickel sulfides, avoiding grain boundary embrittlement while delivering the machinability benefit that defines the grade.


### How Is Sulfur Related to Post Transition Metal Alloys?


Sulfur in post-transition metal alloys (alloys based on elements including aluminum, lead, tin, bismuth, and indium) acts as an impurity or a deliberate additive that influences machinability and mechanical properties, depending on the specific alloy system and sulfur concentration. In aluminum alloys, sulfur is not a common additive; it appears as trace contamination at levels below 0.001%, with negligible effect on mechanical properties at the concentrations encountered in standard production. Sulfur interacts with lead and tin to form sulfide phases that influence solidification microstructure and casting surface quality in lead-based free-machining alloys and tin-bronze casting alloys. The effects of sulfur in post-transition **metal alloys** are alloy-system specific, requiring composition-dependent assessment rather than a universal characterization, as sulfur's role shifts from beneficial machinability additive to harmful impurity depending on the base metal's affinity for sulfide formation.


#### Is Monel 405 a Nickel-Copper Alloy That Contains Controlled Sulfur Levels?


Yes, Monel 405 is a nickel-copper alloy that contains controlled sulfur levels from 0.025% to 0.060% by weight, a compositional feature that defines the grade's identity as the free-machining variant of the Monel 400 family. The base composition of Monel 405 is nickel from 63% to 70% and copper from 28% to 34%, with the sulfur addition as the primary differentiating variable from Monel 400. Controlled sulfur in Monel 405 produces sulfide inclusions that break chips during automatic screw machine operations, enabling the manufacture of fittings, valve stems, and precision fasteners at production rates impractical with standard Monel 400. ## What Are the Uses of Sulfur?


The uses of Sulfur are listed below.


- **Production of Sulfuric Acid** : Sulfur is the primary raw material for sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) synthesis via the Contact process, with over 270 million metric tons of H₂SO₄ produced globally each year.
- **Fertilizer Manufacturing** : Sulfur and sulfuric acid are used to produce ammonium sulfate, superphosphate, and other sulfur-containing fertilizers that supply essential nutrients to crops.
- **Rubber Vulcanization** : Sulfur crosslinks polymer chains in natural and synthetic rubber during vulcanization, converting soft, tacky rubber into durable, elastic material with tensile strength from 15 MPa to 30 MPa.
- **Matches, Fireworks, and Gunpowder** : Sulfur serves as a fuel component in black powder (10% sulfur) and as an ignition component in match heads and pyrotechnic compositions.
- **Pesticides and Fungicides** : Elemental sulfur and sulfur compounds are applied as fungicides and acaricides in agriculture, effective against powdery mildew, rust, and spider mites at application rates from 1 kg/ha to 5 kg/ha.
- **Pharmaceuticals and Medicines** : Sulfur compounds appear in antibiotics (sulfonamides), antifungal treatments (selenium sulfide), and topical acne medications containing sulfur from 3% to 10% concentration.
- **Petroleum Refining** : Hydrodesulfurization (HDS) removes sulfur compounds from crude oil fractions, producing recovered elemental sulfur as a byproduct while reducing sulfur in fuels to below 10 ppm to meet emission standards.
- **Detergents and Chemical Products** : Sulfonation reactions using SO₃ or H₂SO₄ produce sulfonate surfactants (linear alkylbenzene sulfonate, sodium lauryl sulfate) that form the active cleaning agents in laundry detergents, shampoos, and industrial cleaners.
