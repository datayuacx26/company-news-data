# Magnesium: Definition, Composition, Properties, and Applications

## What Are the Applications of Magnesium?


Magnesium has so many different uses and is commonly found in many applications, a few of which we’ve listed below.


### Pyrotechnics


When burned, magnesium produces an intense bright, white light, so it’s often used in fireworks and flares. It’s an incredible way to enhance the visual effects of pyrotechnic displays.


### Luggage


Magnesium’s great strength-to-weight ratio has made it a popular go-to for the manufacturing of luggage, with many high-end brands using magnesium alloys for their lightweight yet durable suitcase frames.


### Sports and Recreation Equipment


You’ll often see sports equipment like tennis rackets, golf clubs, and bicycle frames made from magnesium alloys. That’s because its ability to provide durability and stiffness despite its lightweight makes it very appealing to athletes and sports companies alike.


### Cameras


Cameras, especially high-end modular cameras, can get very heavy and require careful handling because of their sensitive nature. That’s why magnesium is often used in the production of camera bodies, to keep them as light as possible, while making them more durable.


### Power Tools


Power tools are often made from magnesium in order to keep them light and resistant to heavy impact. Another impressive feature of magnesium is that it can actually dampen vibrations, making it ideal for use in power tools.


### Car Seats


Especially important in sports cars, car seats need to be durable but not weigh the car down with unnecessary weight. That’s where magnesium comes in handy—often used by car manufacturers for the construction of seat frames.


### Laptops


Depending on how you’re reading this article, you may be holding some magnesium right now. Laptop casings are often made using magnesium, especially high-end models, because of important benefits like its strength, weight, and heat dissipation properties.


### Why Magnesium Is Used in Stainless Steel?


Magnesium is a crucial component in the production of steel, primarily due to its role in desulfurization. Sulfur is typically considered an unwanted impurity in steel rather than an intentional alloying element. Sulfur, when present in steel, can have detrimental effects on its mechanical properties. It is also regarded as detrimental to both the surface and internal quality of steel. With concentrations exceeding 0.05%, sulfur can lead to increased brittleness and reduced weldability of steel. Besides making steel more brittle, it plays a role in reducing its melting point and diminishing its intergranular strength and cohesion. Also, in the presence of elemental sulfur, steel can experience both general and localized forms of corrosion. However, intentionally adding small amounts of sulfur, typically ranging from 0.10% to 0.30%, can actually enhance the steel's machinability.


Magnesium can reduce the sulfur content of steel to extremely low levels, as little as 0.0002%.


### Why Is Magnesium Not Used in the Building Industry?


Magnesium is not used in the building industry because magnesium and its alloys are prone to galvanic corrosion, particularly when exposed to moisture and corrosive agents prevalent in construction environments. This susceptibility to corrosion raises durability concerns for long-term structural integrity. Moreover, the cost of producing magnesium and its alloys can be relatively high compared to established building materials like steel or aluminum.


#### Can Magnesium Be Used in Die Casting?


Yes, magnesium can be used in die casting. Pure magnesium is not used, but magnesium alloys such as AZ91D are very popular die casting material choices. This alloy has castability and toughness, rendering it a good choice for crafting lightweight components with sufficient ductility that demand lasting performance. Other common magnesium die casting alloys include: AM60B, AM20, and AM50A.


Magnesium die casting designs can incorporate thin walls in regions where strength isn't a critical factor, and sturdier walls in zones necessitating heightened strength. The high-pressure die casting process enables the production of thinner walls with magnesium (ranging from 1 to 1.5 mm) than with aluminum alloys (typically around 2 to 2.5 mm).
