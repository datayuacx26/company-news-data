# 316 Stainless Steel: Composition and Density

316 Stainless Steel is an austenitic stainless steel alloy recognized for its exceptional corrosion resistance, mechanical strength, and long-term durability across demanding industrial environments. 316 Stainless Steel belongs to the 300 series of stainless steels, distinguished by its inclusion of molybdenum, which sets it apart from standard grades. The alloy delivers reliable performance in marine, chemical processing, and medical applications where exposure to moisture, acids, and harsh chemicals is constant.


Stainless steel, as a material category, earns its widespread adoption through a combination of structural strength, aesthetic appeal, and oxidation resistance. The 316 grade modifies the standard austenitic stainless steel composition through elemental addition, including 16% to 18% chromium, 10% to 14% nickel, and 2% to 3% molybdenum. The density of 316 stainless steel measures approximately 7,990 kg/m³ (0.289 lb/in³), making it a reliable and structurally sound material for load-bearing and precision components. The mechanical integrity and chemical stability of the alloy support its reputation as a premier grade within the[stainless steel](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/what-is-stainless-steel/) family, referenced extensively in manufacturing platforms such as Xometry for sourcing and machining applications.


## What Is 316 Stainless Steel?


316 stainless steel is an austenitic stainless steel alloy recognized for its superior corrosion resistance, mechanical durability, and stable performance under chemically aggressive conditions. The alloy falls within the austenitic family of stainless steels, characterized by a face-centered cubic crystal structure that contributes to its non-magnetic nature and excellent ductility. Chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), and molybdenum (Mo) form the three primary alloying elements that define the grade's performance profile. Chromium content ranging from 16% to 18% forms a passive oxide layer on the surface, preventing oxidation and rust formation. Nickel content (10% to 14%) stabilizes the austenitic structure and improves formability, while molybdenum (2% to 3%) significantly enhances resistance to pitting and crevice corrosion in chloride-rich environments. Marine engineering, chemical processing, and medical device manufacturing rely on 316 stainless steel for components exposed to saltwater, acidic solutions, and sterilization cycles. The grade withstands temperatures up to 870°C (1598°F) in intermittent service and up to 925°C (1697°F) in continuous service, reinforcing its suitability for high-temperature industrial processes.


### Why Is Stainless Steel Widely Used?


Stainless steel is widely used and earns widespread adoption across industries due to its inherent resistance to corrosion, structural strength, and low maintenance requirements. The passive chromium oxide layer that forms on the surface acts as a self-repairing barrier against rust, moisture, and chemical attack, reducing the need for coatings or treatments. A tensile strength ranging from 515 MPa to 620 MPa (74,700 psi to 89,900 psi) makes stainless steel suitable for load-bearing and high-stress applications in construction, aerospace, and manufacturing. The material maintains its mechanical integrity across a wide temperature range, from cryogenic conditions to elevated heat environments exceeding 800°C (1472°F). Food processing, medical equipment, and architectural projects benefit from its smooth, non-porous surface that resists bacterial growth and meets hygiene standards. The material's aesthetic finish, available in brushed, mirror-polished, or matte forms, meets both functional and visual requirements in consumer and commercial products. A long service life paired with minimal upkeep costs positions stainless steel as a cost-effective material choice across construction, energy, and manufacturing sectors.


### What Does 316 Stainless Steel Mean in Material Grading?


316 Stainless Steel means in material grading a high-performance, corrosion-resistant alloy, primarily composed of chromium, nickel, and molybdenum. The designation "316" refers to a specific classification within the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) and American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI) stainless steel grading system. The 300 series classification identifies a group of austenitic stainless steels alloyed primarily with chromium and nickel, and the number 316 specifies a distinct composition within that group. The defining characteristic of the 316 grade is the intentional addition of molybdenum at a concentration from 2% to 3%, which elevates the alloy's resistance to pitting corrosion in chloride and sulfide-rich environments. The grading system communicates composition standards, mechanical expectations, and processing requirements that manufacturers and engineers rely on when selecting materials for specific applications. Standards bodies, including ASTM International (ASTM A240) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO 15510), formally recognize and define the 316 grade, ensuring consistency in material properties across global supply chains. The grade belongs to the austenitic stainless steel family, which accounts for the largest share of stainless steel production globally.


#### Is 316 Stainless Steel Considered a Corrosion Resistant Alloy?


Yes, 316 stainless steel is considered a corrosion-resistant alloy due to its elemental composition that actively inhibits oxidation and chemical degradation. The chromium content (16% to 18%) forms a stable, self-renewing passive oxide film on the surface, preventing rust formation even when exposed to moisture and atmospheric oxygen. Molybdenum (2% to 3%) enhances the alloy's resistance to pitting and crevice corrosion, particularly in environments containing chloride ions, such as seawater and deicing salts. The nickel content (10% to 14%) stabilizes the austenitic phase and contributes to the alloy's general resistance to acidic and alkaline solutions. Testing under ASTM G48 (Standard Test Methods for Pitting and Crevice Corrosion Resistance) confirms 316 stainless steel's superior performance compared to non-molybdenum grades in aggressive chemical environments. Marine hardware, chemical storage tanks, and pharmaceutical equipment take advantage of the alloy's resistance properties in applications where an[alloy metal](https://www.xometry.com/resources/materials/what-is-an-alloy/) with reliable anti-corrosion characteristics is required.


## What Is the Chemical Composition of 316 Stainless Steel?


The chemical composition of 316 stainless steel is of iron (Fe) as its base element, supplemented by chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), and molybdenum (Mo) as the principal alloying elements that define its corrosion resistance and mechanical performance. Chromium content (16% to 18%) generates a passive oxide surface layer that acts as a chemical barrier against rust and oxidation, while molybdenum (2% to 3%) provides targeted protection against pitting and crevice corrosion in chloride environments. Nickel (10% to 14%) stabilizes the austenitic crystal structure and improves ductility and toughness across a wide temperature range. Secondary elements, including carbon (C), manganese (Mn), silicon (Si), phosphorus (P), and sulfur (S), appear in controlled trace quantities that refine the alloy's machinability, weldability, and structural consistency. Carbon content is kept at or below 0.08% to minimize carbide precipitation during welding, a property critical to maintaining corrosion resistance in heat-affected zones. The combined elemental balance ensures 316 stainless steel meets the compositional requirements defined under ASTM A240 and AISI standards, confirming its suitability for high-performance industrial use.
