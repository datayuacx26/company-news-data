# College Athletics Is Quietly Becoming a Media Business


Tomorrow a rule change takes effect that most people outside compliance offices will never read, and it is going to reshape what athletic departments spend their time making.


On June 23 the College Sports Commission sent Division I schools and conferences a memo updating how NIL deals get reviewed. Starting July 1, most associated NIL deals worth between 600 and 15,000 dollars will skip range of compensation review entirely, unless an athlete passes 50,000 dollars in associated deals across the academic year. The previous framework, in place only since April, exempted deals up to 2,500 dollars and capped out at 15,000. The commission is also moving its compensation model from confidence intervals to prediction intervals, which is a technical way of saying it will tolerate a wider spread of what a deal is allowed to be worth.


## Why a paperwork change matters


Review friction is the thing that has been holding back small deals. A local restaurant offering an athlete 1,200 dollars was, until now, signing up for a process that cost more in effort than the deal was worth. Raising the exemption ceiling to 15,000 dollars removes that calculation for the overwhelming majority of transactions.


The result is going to be volume. The NIL economy across college sports is already estimated at roughly 2.7 billion dollars in 2026, with about 1.9 billion of that reaching athletes through school payments, collectives and brand arrangements. Separately, schools can now share revenue directly with athletes, capped near 20.5 million dollars per institution. The money has arrived. What changes tomorrow is the number of individual agreements it flows through.


## Small deals are paid for in content


A 1,200 dollar deal is almost never an appearance fee. It is a post. A story. A reel with the sponsor's product in frame and the athlete's best play from Saturday behind it. Every one of those deliverables needs footage, and the only entity that holds the footage is the athletic department.


That puts departments in an unfamiliar position. A Power Four school might sponsor several hundred athletes across two dozen sports, most of which get no broadcast coverage worth speaking of. Serving all of them means producing individual, athlete-specific clips at a rate no communications staff was ever built to sustain. Football and men's basketball already get this treatment. Nobody has the staffing to extend it to rowing.
