## Is a massage gun HSA and FSA eligible?


For qualifying customers, yes. A massage gun can be an HSA/FSA-eligible expense, and in practice most percussive massage devices clear at checkout without extra documentation.


The reason sits in the Internal Revenue Code. Section 213(d) defines medical care as amounts paid for "the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or for the purpose of affecting any structure or function of the body." A percussive device used for muscle pain, spasm, or restricted movement may fall within that definition, depending on how it is used and how your plan treats it.


Two separate things have to line up, and it helps to keep them apart in your head.


**Substantiation** is what happens at the moment you pay. Many HSA/FSA-eligible products are flagged in merchant systems so the card transaction clears on its own. The industry term is auto-substantiation, and it runs on merchant inventory data (IIAS/SIGIS), not on a ruling from the IRS. When a product is classified that way, there is nothing to submit at the point of sale, though you should still keep the receipt.


**Eligibility** is what your plan administrator concludes if they ever review the expense. Auto-substantiation makes the payment go through smoothly; it is a payment-processing step, not a final determination. Your administrator always has the final say. An expense later found to be non-qualified becomes taxable income, generally with an additional 20% tax in the case of an HSA, though some exceptions apply. That is why keeping records is worth the ten seconds it costs, even on a purchase that sailed through.


One important caveat about how classification works: it is resolved **per product** , not per brand. Two devices from the same company can land differently, and accessories are almost always assessed on their own. Flex shows the current classification for an item at checkout. Classification can change, and your plan administrator makes the final determination, so treat the product page as the source rather than any list in a blog post, including this one.


### Do you always need a Letter of Medical Necessity to buy a massage gun with your HSA/FSA?


Usually not, and this is where massage guns differ from a lot of the wellness category.


A Letter of Medical Necessity (LMN) is a short note from a licensed clinician confirming that a product supports treatment or mitigation of a diagnosed condition. It exists for **dual-purpose** products, meaning things that are ordinary household purchases for most people but genuinely medical for some. Saunas, red light therapy devices, and air purifiers are classic examples: nothing about the product itself signals a medical use, so a clinician has to make that connection explicit.


Massage guns generally sit on the other side of that line. Their common use is muscle pain and recovery, which is why most are classified for auto-substantiation instead of case-by-case documentation.


A small number of listings are not classified for auto-substantiation. The product page shows the current classification for the item you are buying, so check it before you assume. Where a letter is required, Flex connects you asynchronously with a licensed clinician in your state, who reviews your consultation and decides whether to issue it. There are no appointments to schedule and no waiting rooms. For a qualifying customer, an LMN is usually all it takes, though whether a letter is issued is always the clinician's medical judgment rather than a foregone conclusion, and your plan administrator still makes the final call on the expense.


## Types of medical conditions that qualify a massage gun to be HSA and FSA eligible


Because most massage guns substantiate automatically, you generally will not be asked to document a condition at all. It is still worth understanding why the category qualifies, partly because it tells you whether your own use case is on solid ground, and partly because it matters if you end up with a device that needs a letter, or if your administrator reviews the expense later.


These are the conditions people most commonly have in mind:


**Chronic back and neck pain.** Persistent lower-back pain, cervical pain, and the muscular tension that accompanies long hours at a desk are among the reasons people reach for a percussive device. Where a licensed clinician has diagnosed a chronic pain condition and recommends at-home soft-tissue work between appointments, the connection to treatment is direct.


**Myofascial pain syndrome and trigger points.** Localized knots in the fascia that refer pain elsewhere in the body are commonly treated with targeted pressure. Percussive therapy is a self-administered version of what a licensed clinician does by hand.


**Muscle spasms and spasticity.** Involuntary contractions, whether from injury, overuse, or a neurological condition, are sometimes managed in part through mechanical stimulation of the affected muscle. For people managing spasticity as part of a longer-term condition, some clinicians recommend a home device alongside in-clinic sessions.


**Post-surgical and post-injury rehabilitation.** After an orthopedic procedure or a soft-tissue injury, regaining range of motion is usually a slow, daily process. Massage guns are used as an adjunct to a formal physical therapy program, on a licensed clinician's guidance about which areas to work and which to avoid.


**Limited range of motion and joint stiffness.** Stiffness in the shoulder, hip, or knee, whether from arthritis, from immobilization after an injury, or from scar tissue, is often addressed through soft-tissue mobilization before stretching or strengthening work.


**Fibromyalgia and widespread pain conditions.** People managing widespread musculoskeletal pain sometimes use gentle percussive therapy as one component of a broader plan. Intensity settings matter here, so it is worth discussing the approach with your clinician first.


**Delayed-onset muscle soreness and training recovery.** The most common reason people buy one. Worth being straight about: recovery from ordinary exercise is closer to general wellness than to medical treatment, and it is the use case an administrator is least likely to view as medical care if the expense is ever questioned. For a device that auto-substantiates this makes no practical difference to your purchase, but it is a weak foundation if you are seeking an LMN.


**Poor circulation.** Reduced peripheral circulation, sometimes secondary to another diagnosed condition, is another reason clinicians suggest mechanical stimulation of the limbs. Anyone with a circulatory or clotting condition should confirm with their clinician that percussive therapy is appropriate before using one.


## How to buy a massage gun using your HSA or FSA directly or through reimbursement


There are two routes. Paying directly is faster and involves no forms; reimbursement is the fallback when you have already paid out of pocket or bought somewhere that does not accept HSA/FSA cards.


### 1. Pay with your HSA/FSA directly at checkout


This is the path most people should take, and for a device that auto-substantiates it takes no longer than an ordinary online purchase.


1. **Choose your device from a store that offers Flex at checkout.** The brands in the next section all sit in the Flex marketplace.
2. **Check the eligibility shown on the product page.** It tells you whether the device substantiates automatically or requires a Letter of Medical Necessity. Flex shows the current classification for that item; classification can change, so the product page governs.
3. **Select Flex at checkout and pay with your HSA or FSA card.** For an auto-substantiating device, the transaction clears without further steps.
4. **If the product requires an LMN, complete the short consultation.** You answer a few questions about your health, a licensed clinician in your state reviews your responses, and if they agree your condition warrants it they issue the letter. It happens in the same checkout flow rather than as a separate appointment. Review times vary, and the clinician decides whether a letter is issued.
5. **Keep your records.** Flex stores your receipt, and your LMN where one was issued, so both are in one place if your administrator asks later.


### 2. Make a reimbursement claim


If you already bought a massage gun with a personal card, or bought from a retailer that does not accept HSA/FSA payment, you can ask your administrator to reimburse you from your account.


1. **Find your receipt.** It needs to show the merchant, the date, the item, and the amount. A credit card statement line usually will not be enough on its own, since administrators generally want an itemized receipt.
2. **Check the timing rules before you file.** FSA expenses generally must be incurred during the plan year they are claimed against. HSA expenses must be incurred after your HSA was established, so a purchase predating the account cannot be reimbursed from it, no matter how well documented.
3. **Get a Letter of Medical Necessity if your administrator asks for one.** Many administrators want the letter to be dated so that it covers the period when the expense was incurred, so a letter obtained long after the fact may not be accepted. If you need one, a licensed clinician can assess whether your condition supports it.
4. **Submit the claim** through your administrator's portal or app, attaching the receipt and any supporting documentation.
5. **Wait for the determination.** Your administrator decides whether to reimburse, and approval is not automatic. If it is approved, funds typically arrive by direct deposit or check.


Paying directly avoids all of this, which is the practical argument for buying through a store that supports HSA/FSA payment in the first place.
