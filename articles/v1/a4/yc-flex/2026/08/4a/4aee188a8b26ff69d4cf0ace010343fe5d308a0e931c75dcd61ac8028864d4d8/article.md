# Are Male Fertility Tests & Supplements HSA/FSA Eligible?

Male factor contributes to a substantial share of infertility cases, and yet the testing and treatment side of that equation gets a fraction of the attention. If you and your partner are trying to conceive, a sperm test is a low-friction place to start, and for qualifying customers, your HSA or FSA can go toward it, along with the supplements a clinician may recommend afterwards.


Here is what this guide covers:


- **Is male fertility testing and treatment HSA and FSA eligible?** Why at-home tests and monitors are treated differently from supplements at checkout.
- **Types of medical conditions that qualify male fertility products to be HSA and FSA eligible.** The diagnoses that support a Letter of Medical Necessity.
- **How to buy male fertility products using your HSA or FSA directly or through reimbursement.** Both routes, with the documentation each one needs.
- **4 male fertility products you can shop with Flex.** Tests, monitors, and supplements from brands in the Flex marketplace.


The split between tests and supplements drives almost everything else in this guide, so that is where we'll start.


## Is male fertility testing and treatment HSA and FSA eligible?


For qualifying customers, yes, and the practical answer depends on which kind of product you are buying.


**At-home sperm tests and fertility monitors generally clear on their own.** A semen analysis kit is a diagnostic test. Nothing about it has a plausible non-medical use, so it carries its purpose on its face. Products in this group are typically classified for auto-substantiation, which means the transaction goes through when you pay with your HSA or FSA card, with no letter, no consultation, and no form to submit afterwards.


**Fertility supplements generally require a Letter of Medical Necessity.** A capsule containing CoQ10, zinc, or omega-3s looks identical whether it is being taken to support sperm motility under a clinician's guidance or as a general daily vitamin. That ambiguity is what makes supplements **dual-purpose** products, and it is why a clinician has to connect the product to a diagnosed condition before an administrator will treat the expense as medical care.


Section 213(d) of the Internal Revenue Code defines medical care as amounts paid for "the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or for the purpose of affecting any structure or function of the body." Male factor infertility is a diagnosable medical condition, and both testing for it and supporting its treatment can fall within that definition. What differs between a test and a supplement is not whether the statute reaches it, but how much documentation an administrator needs in order to see the medical purpose.


Two terms worth keeping separate, because people conflate them constantly.


**Substantiation** is what happens at the moment you pay. Certain products are flagged in merchant systems so the card transaction clears automatically. It is a payment-processing step running on merchant inventory data, not a ruling from the IRS.


**Eligibility** is what your plan administrator concludes if the expense is ever reviewed. Your administrator always has the final say. An expense later determined to be non-qualified becomes taxable income, generally with an additional 20% tax in the case of an HSA, though some exceptions apply. That is why keeping your receipt matters even on a purchase that cleared without friction.


One more thing worth knowing: classification is resolved **per product** , not per brand. The same company can sell a test that auto-substantiates and a supplement that requires a letter, which is the case for at least one brand in the Flex marketplace. Flex shows the current classification for an item at checkout, and classification can change, so treat the product page as the source.


### Do you always need a Letter of Medical Necessity to buy male fertility products with your HSA/FSA?


No, and knowing which side of the line a product sits on saves you a step.


A Letter of Medical Necessity (LMN) is a short note from a licensed clinician confirming that a product supports treatment or mitigation of a diagnosed condition. You will generally **not** need one for at-home semen analysis and sperm test kits, or for fertility and hormone monitors including male fertility testing kits. These are diagnostics, and they typically substantiate on their own.


You generally **will** need one for male fertility supplements, including motility and sperm-quality formulations. Where a supplement is classified that way, the product page will say so before you pay. Because classification is set per item, check the page rather than assuming a whole category behaves one way.


If a product needs a letter, Flex connects you asynchronously with a licensed clinician in your state, who reviews your consultation and decides whether to issue it. There is no appointment to book and no waiting room. For a qualifying customer, an LMN is usually all it takes, though whether a letter is issued is always the clinician's medical judgment rather than a foregone conclusion, and your plan administrator still makes the final call on the expense.


A useful sequence: test first, supplement second. A semen analysis usually clears without documentation, and the results are what a clinician will want to see anyway when assessing whether a supplement regimen is warranted.


## Types of medical conditions that qualify male fertility products to be HSA and FSA eligible


Where a Letter of Medical Necessity is required, it has to connect the product to a diagnosed condition. These are the diagnoses that most commonly support male fertility products:


**Male factor infertility.** The umbrella diagnosis, applied when testing identifies a male contribution to a couple's difficulty conceiving. It is the most direct basis for a letter covering a fertility supplement, and it is typically established through semen analysis rather than assumed.


**Abnormal spermatozoa.** Findings of atypical sperm morphology, meaning the shape and structure of the cells, identified on a semen analysis. Morphology is one of the standard parameters a lab reports, alongside count and motility.


**Reduced sperm motility.** Sperm that do not move effectively have difficulty reaching and fertilizing an egg, regardless of how many are present. Motility is a parameter many fertility supplement formulations are aimed at, which is why it often appears as the documented basis for one.


**Azoospermia and arrest of spermatogenesis.** The absence of measurable sperm in the ejaculate, or a halt in sperm production at some stage of development. These are significant clinical findings that warrant a specialist workup, and any product recommendation would sit inside a broader treatment plan.


**Unexplained infertility.** Where a couple has been trying to conceive without success and standard testing has not identified a cause. A clinician may still connect a product to it, though administrators vary in how they treat this.


**Hormonal imbalance.** Testosterone, FSH, and LH levels outside expected ranges are associated with changes in sperm production. Where bloodwork documents an imbalance, the connection between treatment and reproductive function is easier to establish.


**Obesity as it relates to fertility.** Elevated BMI is associated with changes in hormone levels and sperm parameters, and clinicians managing fertility often address it as part of the plan. Where a clinician documents that link, it can support the specific product the clinician names.


Two things to be realistic about. First, these are conditions a clinician diagnoses; a self-assessment that something feels off is not the same thing, which is part of why testing comes first. Second, buying a supplement as general wellness support, with no diagnosis and no clinician involvement, is the case an administrator is least likely to treat as medical care. That is what dual-purpose means in practice: the everyday use is a personal expense, and a documented medical need is what changes the treatment.


## How to buy male fertility products using your HSA or FSA directly or through reimbursement


Two routes. Paying directly is faster and involves no forms; reimbursement is the fallback when you have already paid out of pocket.


### 1. Pay with your HSA/FSA directly at checkout


1. **Choose your product from a store that offers Flex at checkout.** The brands in the next section are all in the Flex marketplace.
2. **Check the eligibility shown on the product page.** It tells you whether the item substantiates automatically or requires a Letter of Medical Necessity, which is the difference between a two-minute checkout and one that includes a short consultation.
3. **For a test or monitor, pay with your HSA or FSA card.** For products classified for auto-substantiation, the transaction clears without further steps.
4. **For a supplement, complete the short consultation.** You answer a few questions about your health and any testing you have had. A licensed clinician in your state reviews your responses and, if they agree your condition warrants it, issues the Letter of Medical Necessity. It happens in the same checkout flow rather than as a separate appointment. Review times vary, and the decision is the clinician's.
5. **Keep your records.** Flex stores your receipt, and your LMN where one was issued, so both are in one place if your administrator asks. Letters commonly specify a duration, which matters for a supplement you plan to reorder.


### 2. Make a reimbursement claim


If you have already bought a test or a supplement with a personal card, you can ask your administrator to reimburse you.


1. **Find an itemized receipt** showing the merchant, date, item, and amount. A card statement line alone is usually not enough.
2. **Check the timing rules before you file.** FSA expenses generally must be incurred during the plan year they are claimed against. HSA expenses must be incurred after your HSA was established, so a purchase predating the account cannot be reimbursed from it.
3. **Understand how letters obtained after the fact are treated.** Many administrators require the LMN to be dated so that it covers the period when the expense was incurred. A letter obtained months later to justify an earlier purchase may not be accepted, so confirm with your administrator rather than assuming.
4. **Obtain the Letter of Medical Necessity** if the product needs one and you do not already have one covering the purchase.
5. **Submit the claim** through your administrator's portal or app, attaching the receipt and any supporting documentation.
6. **Wait for the determination.** Approval is not automatic. If approved, funds usually arrive by direct deposit or check.


Because supplement regimens typically run for several months and get reordered, paying directly is worth setting up properly the first time rather than filing a claim every cycle.
