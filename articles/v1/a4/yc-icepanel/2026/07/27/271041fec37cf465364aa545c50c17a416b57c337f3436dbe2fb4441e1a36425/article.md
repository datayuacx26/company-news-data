# Our journey to 3.5 mil

I was reminded that this time last year I wrote a[post](https://icepanel.io/blog/2025-07-23-icepanels-journey) about hitting USD $2 mil ARR. It seemed to be well received, and it’s nice to look up, step back, and see where you’ve come.


I posted on socials about[hitting $3 mil](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jacob-shadbolt-design_i-was-under-the-impression-the-first-1m-share-7425238646103957504-kfom/?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop&rcm=ACoAABWWbl0BS-j7AuaFO6kDhhmq_kc7FpqzVGs) earlier this year. In that post, I discussed how things continue to be hard, mostly due to market expectations. We continue to grow steadily as a company, and though not as headline worthy as startups often crave to be, we’re still having fun and get to focus on why we started and what matters to us - a kick ass product we’re proud of.


I thought I would write more about what things we’ve learned since last year’s post, as well as core events that happened.


# Timeline


As a recap:


- 6 months $0 → $1
- 2.5 years: $1 → $1m ARR
- 1 year: $1m ARR → $2m ARR
- 1 year: $2m ARR → $3m ARR
- 6 months: $3m ARR → $3.5m ARR


We’re adding ~$83k ARR each month. Something we’re always trying to accelerate.

Reliant on AI to do their job with limited deep technical knowledge, causing a lot of issues with the team’s progress. Sadly, it doesn’t work out.

We now ban agentic building during the work trial.

# What did we learn this last year?


## Market shift.


This is likely not a surprise to anyone building software… but it’s clear that this year, more than before, the market has shifted to automating everything.


Before it felt like a gimmick, but now it’s the expectation that you support automation/agentic workflows.


The hardest part for us as builders is that this is despite the data showing limited ROI (or just ROI uncertainties).


## Hiring.


### Hire people > don’t just fill a role.


Good people figure out what’s needed and where to add value.


e.g. First customer success hire ended up becoming our lead designer. The second customer success hire ended up pushing sales and expansions, increasing our revenue dramatically (whilst owning customer success too).


### Collect people


Hiring is a long and painful journey, but as a lean team, finding the right person through those pains is worth it to me. We have a high bar of expectation, which paired with hiring in person in Vancouver and unable to match VC funded companies who are happy to throw unholy amounts of money at people, make it a lot harder… That said, outsourcing our hiring hasn’t helped find the person we want any faster - it’s just hard.


“Collecting” people before a role is ready for them has turned out to help us greatly in the long run. (4 of the team knew us before we hired them). I’ve spoken to many people who I believe are great candidates - just not exactly what we’re looking for right now.


To point out the elephant in the room - yes, we’re all men. This is not by design, we just sadly get far fewer non-male applicants (we’re hiring btw…).


### Natural end


Things and people that work well at a specific stage and time of your journey, might not work at other stages.


This is a hard pill to swallow, but as a business and team, you need everyone invested in the journey of where you’re heading. Anything holding the company back is bad for everyone trying to push forward.


### AI has ruined applications but also made it better.


I do all our hiring myself, from headhunting to application reviews and all first interviews.


Over 75% of our applicants used AI for the application. This is easy to sniff out - it reads as boring, soulless and vanilla as you can get. Even if AI added flavour in your application, the first time we meet will likely feel like I’m talking to a completely different person - you’ll likely not get the job either way and have just wasted both of our time.


This is however amazing for those who put real effort into an application they’re actually excited about. I used to hate the AI applications as it felt a waste of time reviewing plagiarized work (let’s call it what it is…), but they benchmark what mediocre looks like, so the exceptional people float to the top. We also ask engineers to bring in work their proud of. Again, things built completely with agents (to the point people have outright told us they’ve never looked at the code… given this is for a senior engineering position…) might look cool on the surface, but when you dig deeper, they crumble and there’s rarely value beneath.


## Product


### Building the right thing


Building a product that provides value continuously, is genuinely hard.


“What got us here, won’t get us there” feels more relevant than ever right now - we’re always learning, which is fun!


Dig deeper than the surface level always - We’ve learned that customers have these wild ideas that seem crazy to us … but there’s usually a reason deeper that explains why. Don’t take what they say at face value, but meet them there to dig deeper


We’re still figuring out our growth engine.


## Scale what works


### Conferences


We started going to a couple to see if this works, so now we’re scaling up to attend more (within reason). Meeting people in person, seeing their instant response and jamming on ideas with people new to the tool has been an awesome experience. BUT - it has to be the right type of conference. We attended CANSEC this year, and it’s hard to standout as a tool for software architects when you’re stood next to an actual tank… We plan to continuing attending targeted and highly relevant conferences.


### Training


Services continues to be a huge value add for our higher tier customers, and we now have 2 trainers who help us scale our training programme to more teams across the globe, especially in timezones that mean we don’t need to do 5am calls anymore.


# Comparison


It’s 6 years since Victor and I started IcePanel, which is the longest job I’ve ever had, and completely flown by…


Comparing to other founders used to make me feel inadequate. We didn’t raise tens of millions, hyper scale a team, spend tens of millions on marketing, shout from the rooftops about how we’re “killing it”, demand the team works 80 hours a week etc.


But none of that was why we started - so why should it matter now?


We started because we wanted to learn how to build a product people cared about for a problem we saw in our own day jobs.


In this time we have:


- Learned how to talk to customers and understand their problems. (the original reason we started)
- Moved country and had 12 offices.
- Made our first dollar
- Got into YC - realized the VC path wasn’t for us
- Hired an awesome, lean and autonomous team.
- Grown to 900 customers and $3.5mil ARR.
- Have fun building.


All that matters to us is what our customers think, what our employees think and what our mum’s think. That’s all the validation we need.


# Where now?


We continue focusing on building the coolest tool for software architecture design so teams can focus on what matters most - their customers. We’re all playing a new game with the current market, which brings a whole new set of challenges.


I don’t tend to like posting about how we’re making money whilst having fun building because it feels like cheating. It also feels “icky” to me, as it comes across as boasting, which is not a very British thing to do… I feel incredibly lucky to be able to build a really cool thing for our really cool customers. These posts are more for me to share and highlight the alternate path that a lot of people starting to build a product or company don’t hear about, because it’s not sexy or headline worthy. Bootstrapping your own company and making sure your customers are happy and that you make more than you spend, is just good math in my head. The path you get to control is the one that’s more “you” as a founder and gives you enjoyment as well as being financially beneficial.


Thank you to all our supportive customers, old and new. We’re here because of you and are all incredibly grateful for your support and feedback.


Of course the same issues from my last post apply:


- The arguments and wrong turns.
- The continued sacrifices.
- The features nobody cares about.
- The “I can solve all your problems” messages in my inbox every hour.
- Pricing and contract negotiations lost from something out of our control.
- Big companies taking advantage of a smaller companies.
- and so on…


I’m proud of all the work we do as a team and love that we have fun solving problems every day. It’s still hard - but I’m starting to think that is why it’s fun.
