# How to Build a Product People Want to Show Other People

## Marketing Trends for July 17th, 2026: Product Led Brand Marketing


When a customer receives a surprisingly good result from a product, they take a screenshot, send it to a colleague, and explain what happened in Slack. Someone asks which tool created it. Another person requests access. By the end of the week, the product has entered three conversations the marketing team did not initiate, pay for, or observe directly.


This is the distribution advantage marketers tend to overlook when separating product from brand. A useful product solves a problem. A showable product also gives the customer something worth carrying into the world: a result, an identity, a ritual, an image, a story, or an artifact that becomes easier to share than to explain.


Consumer companies understand this instinctively. Packaging appears in photographs. Products become accessories. Unboxing becomes content. People display the object on desks, kitchen counters, bathroom shelves, gym bags, and social feeds because the item communicates something beyond its functional use.


B2B products create social objects too. They produce reports, dashboards, benchmarks, assessments, certifications, visualizations, workflows, and results that customers can show to a manager, team, client, board, or professional community.


The product does not need to become viral in the consumer-internet sense. It needs to create moments people feel motivated to repeat.


## Products are becoming part of the media system


The boundary between product and marketing has always been porous.


A beautiful object appears in a photograph. A useful template gets forwarded to a colleague. A customer wears branded merchandise because it signals membership. A product-generated report enters a quarterly meeting and becomes the company’s most persuasive sales asset.


Current consumer-brand trends make this relationship unusually visible. Recent *Vogue Business* reporting described food, beverage, and wellness companies borrowing the visual language of fashion and beauty. Products such as premium water, protein snacks, and herbal teas are being designed as lifestyle objects that look appropriate in a creator’s feed or a carefully composed photograph. Founders interviewed for the article emphasized that product, packaging, imagery, retail, partnerships, and campaigns should feel as though they belong to the same world.


The packaging does not merely contain the product. It becomes a distribution surface.


McKinsey has separately identified “emotion-first products” as an emerging business model in which anticipation, identity, affinity, and community are designed into the commercial proposition from the beginning. Emotion becomes part of the economic system rather than a benefit marketing tries to add after the product is finished.


That idea applies far beyond consumer goods. A B2B company may never produce a photogenic bottle. It can still build a product experience that gives users a result they want other people to see.


## Showability begins with a real product truth


A shareable moment cannot be sustained through decoration alone.


A visually distinctive product may earn an initial post. A surprising campaign may generate curiosity. Customers continue sharing when the product gives them something meaningful: a better result, an easier routine, a useful insight, a sense of progress, or a way to express who they are.


The recent interest in design-led consumer products carries an important caveat. Aesthetic packaging can increase desire, but the product still has to justify repeat use. The wellness and supplement categories are increasingly crowded with visually sophisticated brands, while consumers are also asking harder questions about efficacy, convenience, scientific support, and actual product performance.


Marketing teams should therefore begin with the truth the product can reliably create.


For Surface, that truth might be the moment a marketing or sales team sees the complete history behind a lead rather than receiving a form submission without context. A rep who can see which articles, case studies, integrations, and pricing pages a buyer reviewed receives something immediately useful and demonstrable.


The[full lead journey](https://withsurface.com/blog/lead-user-journey) is both a capability and an artifact. It gives the user information they can bring into a sales conversation, team review, or performance report. The product creates something that is easier to show than a generic claim about “better marketing intelligence.”


## The five kinds of product moments people share


Products become showable in several different ways. Companies should decide which type fits the customer and use case rather than forcing every product into a social-media loop.


### The result


Customers share an outcome they are proud of.


This may be revenue growth, time saved, an improved conversion rate, a finished design, a solved technical problem, a cleaner forecast, or a personal transformation. The product gives the customer a before-and-after story.


Results travel when they are specific, credible, and easy to communicate. A statement such as “we improved efficiency” contains little social value. A report showing that a team reduced lead-response time from four hours to forty seconds gives the customer a concrete achievement to present.


### The artifact


The product produces something that can leave the interface.


Examples include a benchmark report, visual analysis, certificate, scorecard, strategy map, generated image, presentation, project plan, audit, or customer-facing deliverable.


The artifact should preserve enough product identity to create attribution without becoming an advertisement. A useful report that subtly identifies the system which produced it can travel naturally. A report covered in promotional banners makes the customer feel as though they are distributing company collateral.


### The ritual


The product becomes part of a repeated behavior.


Morning fitness summaries, annual listening reports, weekly team scorecards, project retrospectives, and monthly business reviews all create recurring opportunities for customers to discuss the product.


Rituals help brands accumulate meaning. A one-time campaign asks people to remember. A repeated product behavior gives them another reason to encounter and use the brand.


### The identity signal


The product communicates something about the user.


A person may share a tool because it makes them appear informed, creative, efficient, discerning, technically capable, environmentally conscious, or part of a particular community.


Identity can be subtle. A developer sharing an elegant technical workflow signals expertise. A marketing leader distributing a clear attribution framework signals operational sophistication. The product helps the customer tell a story about themselves.


### The discovery


The product reveals something the customer did not know.


Unexpected insights are naturally conversational. A user shares the finding because it changes an assumption, exposes an opportunity, or gives the team language for a problem it had previously struggled to describe.


Products with analytics, research, classification, or recommendation features are especially capable of creating discovery moments. The interface should make the insight clear enough that users can understand and explain it without reconstructing the analysis manually.


## Consumer products turn use into culture


The strongest consumer examples show how utility, design, ritual, and community reinforce each other.


Vaseline offers an unusual case because its core product is more than 150 years old. Unilever CMO Leandro Barreto recently described how creators and customers have expanded the cultural life of the product by demonstrating unconventional uses, including applying it to dog noses or using it inside shoes. The company gained relevance by observing and embracing how communities were reinterpreting a familiar object.


The marketing did not invent every use from headquarters. Customers created material around the product because its flexibility invited experimentation.


This suggests a different approach to product-led marketing. The company does not need to control every interpretation. It needs to provide enough product integrity, recognizability, and openness for customers to discover uses worth discussing.


Marketing teams can then document, organize, validate, and amplify the most useful behavior. Customer creativity becomes research as well as distribution.


## B2B products need shareable outcomes, not consumer gimmicks


B2B teams often misunderstand product virality by copying visible consumer mechanics.


They add badges, celebration animations, social-sharing buttons, points, leaderboards, and decorative summaries without asking whether the customer gains anything by sharing them. The mechanism exists. The motivation does not.


A professional user usually shares a product output because it helps them perform their role.


They may want to:


-


Demonstrate progress to a manager


-


Explain a decision to another department


-


Give a client a useful deliverable


-


Build consensus around a strategy


-


Prove that an investment worked


-


Warn the team about a risk


-


Establish personal credibility


-


Teach colleagues a better process


-


Compare performance against a benchmark


-


Request additional budget or support


The showable B2B product should make one of those jobs easier.


A marketing dashboard becomes shareable when it clearly explains what changed and what the team should do next. A security report becomes shareable when it helps an executive understand risk. A lead-operations product becomes shareable when sales can see why a prospect was prioritized and what the buyer researched before converting.


Surface’s broader[content marketing operating model](https://withsurface.com/blog/content-marketing-guide-2026) separates visibility, trust, conversion, distribution, and measurement. Product-generated artifacts can connect all five. They provide useful material, demonstrate the company’s method, travel between people, and create a path toward commercial action.


## Design the handoff between product and conversation


The shareable moment usually occurs at a boundary.


A user finishes a task and needs approval. A marketer discovers a trend and needs to inform leadership. A sales representative receives a lead and needs to prepare for outreach. A customer completes an assessment and needs to discuss the result.


Products should identify these boundaries and make the handoff easier.


Ask:


-


What has the user just accomplished?


-


Who needs to see the result next?


-


What context will that person require?


-


Which format makes the information easiest to understand?


-


What portion can be shared safely?


-


How should the recipient return to the product?


-


What product or brand identity should remain attached?


The answers may lead to exportable reports, collaborative links, presentations, email summaries, CRM records, annotated screenshots, public profiles, embeddable modules, or client-ready PDFs.


A “share” button is the final interface element. The product strategy begins with understanding why the user would press it.


## Make the product legible in ten seconds


Showable products create clear stories.


The customer should be able to point to the output and say:


-


Here is what changed.


-


Here is what the product discovered.


-


Here is why the result matters.


-


Here is what we should do next.


Dense interfaces may contain important detail, but the first layer needs a legible conclusion. Surface’s[storytelling work](https://withsurface.com/blog/the-storyteller-role-in-marketing-kpis-craft-and-brand-strategy) applies inside the product as much as it applies to campaigns. Hierarchy, tension, evidence, sequence, and explanation determine whether a user can turn data into a story.


This is especially important for AI-generated results. A product that produces an answer without visible reasoning, sources, caveats, or next steps may create temporary surprise. A product that helps the user understand and defend the result creates an artifact they can use with confidence.


## Let the customer receive the social value


Companies often overbrand the shareable output.


They place a giant logo above a small customer result. They force recipients through an acquisition form. They require public posting. They make the user’s achievement feel like an advertisement for the vendor.


The better question is who should look impressive.


In most cases, the customer should.


The report should help the customer appear informed. The visualization should help them communicate clearly. The assessment should help them lead a better conversation. The benchmark should give them useful context. Product attribution can remain visible without consuming the artifact.


This approach creates a healthier exchange. The customer receives professional or personal value. The company receives distribution and association with the value created.


## Build a recognizable product world


Showability becomes more powerful when the product, brand, content, and campaigns feel connected.


The recent design-led CPG trend is useful because those companies are building coherent worlds. The product, packaging, photography, retail environment, creator partnerships, and storytelling share a recognizable logic.


B2B companies can create the same coherence without copying consumer aesthetics.


A product-generated report can use the same terminology as the company’s research. A dashboard can reflect the hierarchy used in sales conversations. A customer assessment can connect to educational content. A benchmark can become an annual editorial asset. A product insight can become the starting point for an executive discussion or customer story.


The product then participates in the brand instead of sitting behind the marketing layer as a separate visual and linguistic system.


Teams using[AI without losing their brand voice](https://withsurface.com/blog/ai-content-strategy-brand-voice) should apply the same discipline to generated product outputs. Default AI language can make every insight sound as though it came from the same anonymous consultant. Product voice should remain clear, useful, and recognizable.


## Measure whether customers carry the product forward


Product-led brand effects can be measured, although the evidence will be distributed.


Useful signals include:


-


Export and share rates


-


Collaborative invitations


-


Public links created


-


Screenshots or product images mentioned online


-


Referral traffic from shared artifacts


-


New users entering through collaboration


-


Repeat use of reports and assessments


-


Customer references during sales conversations


-


Branded search following major product moments


-


Template or artifact reuse


-


Creator and community adoption


-


Lead quality from shared-product journeys


-


Retention among users who share or collaborate


Teams should also review the qualitative evidence. Why did someone share? Who received it? What did the artifact help the user accomplish? Which part of the product did the recipient remember?


[Lead journey tracking](https://withsurface.com/blog/lead-user-journey) can reveal what happens when a recipient enters the owned experience. The company can see whether shared-product visitors inspect use cases, read comparisons, view pricing, and eventually convert.


The objective is not to claim that every share caused revenue. It is to understand whether the product is creating its own useful paths into the market.


## A practical product-showability workshop


Bring together product, marketing, customer success, design, and sales. Choose one high-value customer workflow and answer the following questions.


### What does the customer accomplish?


Describe the outcome in the customer’s language rather than the product’s feature vocabulary.


### Who needs to see it?


Identify the colleague, manager, client, community, or external stakeholder who benefits from the result.


### Why would the customer share?


Define the professional, emotional, or social value the customer receives.


### What should travel?


Choose the result, evidence, context, and next action the recipient needs.


### What should remain attached?


Preserve appropriate product identity, source information, methodology, and a route back into the experience.


### What could create discomfort?


Review privacy, confidentiality, competitive sensitivity, personal data, and the risk of accidental disclosure.


### How will the company learn?


Track use, ask customers about the behavior, and use the findings to improve the product and marketing system.


The exercise should produce a real feature or workflow hypothesis. “Make it more viral” is not a product requirement.


## The product should give marketing something true to amplify


Marketing teams often search for a story while the product experience remains generic.


They develop campaign concepts, commission research, hire creators, and build visual systems because the product itself provides few distinctive moments. Those efforts can still create value. They become considerably stronger when the product gives them something real.


A powerful result, elegant ritual, recognizable artifact, unexpected discovery, or customer-created use case gives marketing durable material. The team can explain it, document it, distribute it, and connect it to a larger point of view.


The company no longer has to ask customers to believe that the product is interesting.


Customers are already showing one another why.
