# Do Muslim Parents Accept Marriage Outside Their Ethnicity? We Asked Six Elders.

American Muslims are the most racially and ethnically diverse religious community in the United States: no single racial or ethnic group forms a majority, and only 35% worship in congregations where most people share their background. When Muzz asked six elders whether they would accept a child marrying outside their ethnicity, all six said yes — and every one of them named shared faith, not ethnicity, as the condition.

### **How ethnically diverse are American Muslims?**


More than any other religious community in the country — and it is not close.


Pew Research Center’s 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study, based on more than 36,000 respondents, found that no single racial or ethnic group forms a majority among U.S. Muslims. Roughly 30% are White (a category that currently includes Arab and MENA respondents under Census methodology), 30% Asian, 20% Black, 11% Hispanic, and 8% identify with another race or with more than one. Nearly six in ten Muslim adults in the U.S. (59%) – were born outside the country.


The Institute for Social Policy and Understanding reached the same conclusion from a different direction: of every religious and non-religious group it surveyed for its American Muslim Poll, Muslims were the only one without a majority from a single racial or ethnic background.


American Muslims are also young. Pew found roughly 35% are between 18 and 29 – a far larger share of the community sitting squarely in the marriage-search years than in most religious groups in the country.


### **Look at where American Muslims actually pray**


This is the number that explains why the question in our video comes up at all.


Pew found that two-thirds of Americans who attend religious services worship alongside congregants who mostly share their own race or ethnicity. Among Muslim Americans, only 35% do. Twenty-six percent attend congregations where most worshippers are a *different* race than they are, and 39% attend congregations where no single racial group holds a majority.


Translation: the average American mosque is one of the most ethnically mixed rooms in American religious life. The person standing beside you in the saff on Friday is statistically unlikely to share your background.


Marrying “outside your ethnicity” is not an edge case for this community. Given where we pray, where we study, and where we work, it is close to the default condition.


### **How common is interethnic marriage in the U.S.?**


Common, and rising sharply. Pew’s most comprehensive analysis of Census Bureau data found that 17% of U.S. newlyweds in 2015 — one in six — married someone of a different race or ethnicity. In 1967, the year *Loving v. Virginia* was decided, that figure was 3%. A more than fivefold increase in under fifty years.


The detail that matters most for our community is what happens across generations. Intermarriage rates among U.S. born adults run dramatically higher than among the foreign-born. In the same analysis, 46% of U.S. born Asian newlyweds and 39% of U.S. born Hispanic newlyweds married someone of a different race or ethnicity.


Set that against the 59% of American Muslim adults who were born abroad, and the shape of the thing becomes clear. Many of our parents arrived from countries where marrying within the community was simply how it worked. Their children were raised in mixed mosques, mixed schools and mixed cities. The gap in the video is not a “values” gap. It is a generational and geographic one — and the data says it closes.


Location matters too. Pew’s regional analysis found intermarriage highest in the West, where 22% of newlyweds married across racial or ethnic lines, compared with 14% in the South, 13% in the Northeast and 11% in the Midwest.


### **So why do some families still hesitate?**


Because our elders answered a question in principle, and families live in practice — and the gap between those two things is where most of the pain in this conversation sits.


When a parent hesitates, the objection is usually not about faith at all.


It is closer to:


- **Language.** Will my grandchildren be able to talk to me?
- **Continuity.** We carried this culture across an ocean. Does it stop here?
- **The extended family.** Not “what do I think,” but “what will they say” — the aunt back home, the cousins, the community that still has an opinion from six thousand miles away.
- **The unfamiliar.** A wedding they do not know how to host. In-laws they do not know how to talk to. Customs and expectations they cannot predict.
- **Colorism.** Which deserves to be named plainly, because it is present in many of our communities and it causes real damage.


Almost none of those are religious concerns. Most of them are fear of the unfamiliar — and fear responds to exposure and time far better than it responds to debate.


Knowing that before the conversation changes what you are actually arguing about.


### **How to talk to your parents about marrying outside your culture**


Six things that tend to work better than the speech you have been rehearsing:


1. **Lead with character, faith and family.** Open with how they practise, how they treat their own parents, what they want their life to look like. That is the criterion the elders in our video named without prompting.
2. **Find out what the actual objection is.** “Is this about their deen, or about how it’ll feel for the family?” Both are worth taking seriously. They are not the same conversation and they do not have the same answer.
3. **Let them meet the family, not just the person.** Most of what parents fear is the unknown extended family rather than the individual in front of them. Meeting resolves more than arguing does.
4. **Bring in a trusted elder early.** Someone your parents already respect changes the temperature of the room more than any argument you make yourself.
5. **Address the practical fears directly.** Language, holidays, where you’ll live, how the wedding works. Vague reassurance does not land; specifics do.
6. **Give it time and repetition.** A first no is very often a first reaction. The elders in our video were not persuaded on camera — they already held that view. Plenty of others get there slowly.


### **Frequently asked questions**


**How ethnically diverse are Muslims in America?**
More diverse than any other U.S. religious group. Pew’s 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study found no single racial or ethnic group forms a majority among U.S. Muslims: roughly 30% White, 30% Asian, 20% Black, 11% Hispanic and 8% another or multiple races. ISPU found Muslims were the only group it surveyed without a majority from one racial or ethnic background.


**Do Muslim parents accept marriage outside their ethnicity?**
Attitudes vary by family and community, and no survey speaks for every household. In the six elder interviews Muzz filmed, all six said yes — and each named shared faith rather than ethnicity as their condition.


**How common is interracial marriage in the United States?**
Pew’s analysis of Census data found 17% of U.S. newlyweds in 2015 married someone of a different race or ethnicity, up from 3% in 1967. Rates are substantially higher among the U.S.-born: 46% of U.S.-born Asian newlyweds and 39% of U.S.-born Hispanic newlyweds.


**Do American Muslims mostly pray alongside people of their own background?**
No. Pew found only 35% of Muslim Americans worship in congregations where most people share their race or ethnicity, compared with two-thirds of Americans overall. Thirty-nine percent attend congregations where no racial group is a majority.


**Why do some Muslim families object to interethnic marriage?**
The concerns families raise are usually cultural rather than religious — language, continuity, extended-family opinion, unfamiliarity with different wedding and in-law customs, and in some communities colourism. Attitudes on interracial marriage have shifted substantially across American society within a single generation.


**Is marrying outside your ethnicity permitted in Islam?**
Questions about religious rulings are best taken to a qualified scholar or your local imam, who can address your specific situation — Muzz does not issue religious guidance.
