# What Should You Discuss Before Getting Married? A Muslim Couple’s Checklist

**Before getting married, Muslim couples should agree on six things: children and parenting, money and provision, family and in-laws, roles at home, how they handle conflict, and where they see the next five to ten years.**

You’ve discussed the wedding colours. You’ve discussed whether his cousin can be trusted with the guest list. You have not discussed how you’re raising your children.


This is normal. It’s also how people end up three years in, mid-argument about whose mother is visiting for how long, realising this was always going to happen.


### **Children: do you want them, and how are you raising them?**


Easy part first – do you both want kids, roughly how many.


Harder part: how.


Because whatever your parents did is what you’ll default to at 2am when the baby won’t sleep and nobody has the energy to be intentional. If one of you grew up with the slipper flying across the room when you misbehave and the other grew up with a family meeting, that’s going to come up.


And deen isn’t automatic. Nobody absorbs it through the walls. Islam is taught, on purpose, by two people who agreed on how – or it’s taught inconsistently by two people who didn’t.


You can both want three kids and still want completely different childhoods for them.


### **Worth asking**


- Kids – yes/no, how many, when?
- How were each of us disciplined? Are we repeating it or actively not?
- Islamic school, public school, weekend madrasah, some combination?
- What does tarbiyah actually look like on a normal day?
- What if we can’t have children?


### **Money: who provides, and what happens to the rest?**


This is the one everyone skips because it feels haram to bring up before the nikah. It’s also the one the research is most brutal about.


A study of 4,574 couples using data from the National Survey of Families and Households found financial disagreements were the strongest disagreement type predicting divorce. Sonya Britt, the Kansas State researcher who led it, didn’t hedge: money beats children, intimacy and in-laws as a predictor. For husbands and for wives.


Here’s the part that should genuinely alarm you. They controlled for income, debt and net worth. Made no difference. You cannot out-earn this problem. Couples arguing about money early were at higher risk whether they had a lot of it or none.


Newer data agrees. A 2021 study found finances were the biggest source of conflict in 40% of disagreements among people in long-term relationships. Fidelity’s 2024 Couples & Money study found a quarter of couples named money as their single biggest relationship challenge, and nearly half argue about it at least sometimes.


Megan McCoy, a financial therapist and professor at Kansas State, explains why money fights hit differently: they run longer, resolve less, and leak into everything else. The actual issue is usually that two people have inherited opposite beliefs about what money is *for* . One of you grew up watching money mean safety. The other watched it mean generosity, or status, or freedom. Nobody told you this about yourselves. You just have it.


Which is exactly why it’s a pre-nikah conversation.


### **Worth asking**


- Who provides, and what does that mean in practice?
- Savers or spenders? What did money look like in each of our houses?
- Joint account, separate, both?
- How are we handling zakat and sadaqah?
- Is there debt the other person doesn’t know about? (Ask. Now.)
- What are we saving for – house, Hajj, business, school?


### **Family and in-laws: where are you living, and what are the boundaries?**


In our communities this question quietly decides how your first two years go.


Where are you living – your own place, his family’s house, ten minutes from hers? Who’s expected at which gathering, and how often is “expected” actually “mandatory”? What happens when someone’s parents need care?


Boundaries aren’t disrespectful. They’re the thing that lets you stay married *and* stay close to your families. The couples who struggle usually don’t have villains for in-laws. They just never agreed what the arrangement was, so every single occasion becomes a negotiation neither of them prepared for.


Loving each other shouldn’t cost you your family. It also shouldn’t be the thing you give up to keep the peace.


### **Worth asking**


- Where are we living year one? Does that change?
- How much time with each side, and who decides that?
- What if a parent needs to move in?
- What stays between us and doesn’t get relayed to family?
- If a parent objects to something we decided together — then what?


### **Roles at home: who’s actually doing what?**


Cooking, cleaning, bills, school runs, and the invisible job of being the person who remembers any of it needs doing.


Most couples never have this conversation. They assume. Then the assumptions don’t match, and by the time anyone says anything out loud it’s not a conversation any more — it’s a list someone’s been keeping.


Be specific. “We’ll share things fairly” is not a plan, it’s a vibe. Who’s cooking on a Wednesday when you both got in at seven? Who does the admin — the appointments, the forms, the calls nobody wants to make? What happens in a week when one of you is travelling or sick or drowning at work?


Sort it now, before resentment sorts it for you.


### **Worth asking**


- What did the split look like in each of our houses growing up?
- Are we both working? Does that change after kids?
- Who’s handling bills, admin, appointments?
- What does a regular weekday evening look like?
- What are we each quietly expecting that we haven’t said?


### **Conflict: how do you argue, and how do you come back?**


You’re going to argue. The question is what happens after.


Some people need space. Some can’t sleep until it’s resolved. Some raise their voice and consider the matter closed within the hour while the other person is still thinking about it in July. None of these is wrong by itself. The mismatch is what does the damage.


Ask outright whether you’d both go to counselling if it came to it. A lot of Muslim couples treat counselling as proof something’s already broken, which means it gets suggested at the exact point it’s least likely to work. Agreeing now that it’s on the table takes the shame out of it later.


Arguments don’t end marriages. Ego does — specifically, both people waiting for the other one to apologise first.


### **Worth asking**


- How did each of our families handle conflict?
- Do you need space when you’re upset, or resolution?
- Would you go to counselling if we needed it?
- Who do we talk to when it’s hard — and who do we not?
- What does an actual apology look like to you?


### **The future: are you walking the same direction?**


Five, ten years out. Career, deen, family, city, the thing you want that you haven’t told anyone.


Is someone planning to move abroad? Go back to study? Start a business that eats the savings for three years? Does one of you picture a big loud house full of kids while the other pictures two and a lot of quiet? Is one of you staying near your parents forever while the other has been mentally packing since 2019?


Ambition isn’t the problem. Undisclosed ambition is.


Two people walking in different directions can’t build one home.


### **Worth asking**


- Where do you want to be living in ten years?
- What do you want to have done by then?
- What would you need me to back, even when it’s inconvenient?
- What’s genuinely non-negotiable for you?
- What would make you feel this marriage went well?

### **Frequently asked questions**


**What should you discuss before getting married in Islam?**
Six areas cover most of it: children and parenting approach, money and provision, family and in-law boundaries, division of work at home, how you handle conflict, and your five-to-ten-year plans. These are practical compatibility questions, alongside the character and deen questions couples usually cover first.


**What is the most important thing to agree on before marriage?**
Research points to money. A study of 4,574 couples found financial disagreements were the strongest predictor of divorce — stronger than disagreements about children, intimacy or in-laws — and the finding held regardless of the couple’s income, debt or net worth.


**How many questions should you ask before nikah?**
There’s no set number, and quality matters more than volume. Cover the six areas above rather than working through a long list. One honest conversation about money or in-laws is worth more than fifty surface-level questions.


**Is premarital counselling encouraged for Muslim couples?**
Many imams and Muslim counsellors offer premarital sessions, and plenty of couples find them useful for surfacing exactly these conversations. Whether you pursue it is personal — but agreeing in advance that counselling is acceptable makes it far more likely to help if you ever need it.


**What are red flags to look for before marriage?**
Refusing to discuss money, deflecting questions about family expectations, treating your ambitions as negotiable while treating theirs as fixed, or shutting down conversations by insisting it’ll work itself out. Avoidance during the talking stage rarely improves after the nikah.


**How do I bring up difficult topics before marriage?**
Ask about their family rather than about them. “How did your parents handle money?” is easier to answer than “are you a spender?” — and usually more revealing. Involving a wali or a trusted elder can also make the harder conversations more comfortable.
