**SACRAMENTO, Calif. (KCRA) —**


Two of California's biggest railroads showed off their latest safety and training equipment in Sacramento on Tuesday and said they are ready if an oil train accident happens in the state.


[Watch report: How safe are freight trains carrying oil?](https://www.kcra.com/news/how-safe-are-freight-trains-carrying-oil/31461538)


Advertisement


Fire officials from Sacramento, Roseville and other communities toured several large pieces of training and safety equipment in Old Sacramento.


Union Pacific railroad, for example, has converted a tanker car into a classroom with examples of the various valves firefighters might find when responding to a leak.


Visitors also saw an example of a tanker car equipped with shielding designed to prevent punctures and leaks in the event of a derailment.


UP and BNSF organized and paid for the event.


"I think it's something we owe not only to our customers, but to our employees and to the communities in which we operate," said Lena Kent, of BNSF.


The issue of rail safety has received increased attention following several high profile accidents involving trains carrying crude oil.


Last week, a 109-car CSX train was traveling through West Virginia when it derailed and exploded.


The accident caused a house to catch fire and two towns to be evacuated.


According to statistics from the California Energy Commission, the amount of oil imported to California via rail began to dramatically increase in 2013.


In 2014, those imports totaled 5.7 million barrels, mostly from Canada, North Dakota and New Mexico.


UP and BNSF said they are using the equipment on display in Old Sacramento to train thousands of firefighters each year.


Recently, the Association of American Railroads unveiled a phone app that allows emergency responders to use a rail car's identification number to learn its contents.


A new law, AB 380, requires rail companies to update the state every three months on where their oil trains have been running.


However, state emergency officials said they would still like access to real-time information about oil trains' locations.


UP and BNSF said they are willing to share that information on one condition.


"It will be part of a process where we will introduce them to the database. And once a confidentiality agreement has been signed, then they will have access to that database," said Aaron Hunt of UP.


The railroads said such secrecy is necessary to prevent the oil trains from becoming possible terrorism targets.


However, the state has refused to sign such confidentiality agreements.


"Under right to know information, the public's right to know, we'll have to share that information at some point," said Kim Zagaris, fire chief for the California Office of Emergency Services.


Zagaris said he is confident that eventually the state and the railroads will reach an agreement to share the information.


So far, the stalemate has shown no signs of breaking.


Gov. Jerry Brown has proposed taxing oil train shipments as a way to pay for more training and equipment.


A similar proposal failed to pass last year in the state legislature.
