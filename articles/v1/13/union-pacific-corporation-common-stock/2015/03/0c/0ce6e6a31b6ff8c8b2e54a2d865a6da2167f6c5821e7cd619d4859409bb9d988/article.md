# UP Works Hard to Prevent Derailments

Four trains hauling crude oil have derailed in the U.S. and Canada since mid-February, rupturing tank cars, spilling their contents, polluting waterways and igniting fires that burned for days.


Although none of those incidents occurred in Idaho, there is certainly the potential for such an accident here. Union Pacific transports between 400 and 600 carloads of crude oil through the state on a monthly basis, said Francisco Castillo, director of corporate relations and media for the railroad.


None of that is Bakken crude, which some government tests have shown to be more volatile, even though the American Petroleum Institute says it is no more volatile than other light, sweet crudes.


Either way, crude oil is considered to be a hazardous material, and a train that derails while hauling it can cause problems. That’s why UP officials work hard to prevent such accidents from occurring in the first place, and why local law enforcement and fire department officials include hazardous materials training in their emergency preparedness efforts.


“Crude oil is classified by the government as a hazardous material and while there are certain risks involved in any form of transportation, railroads deliver hazardous material safely 99.997 percent of the time,” Castillo said. “That’s why we follow the strictest safety practices, and are working tirelessly to achieve our goal of zero derailments.”


UP has been able to decrease derailments by 38 percent over the last decade, he said.


Between 2005 and 2014, the railroad invested about $31 billion in strengthening its network and infrastructure, he said, adding that it plans to spend another $4.3 billion this year.


In addition, UP uses lasers and ultrasound to identify rail imperfections, and can forecast many potential problems before they happen by tracking the acoustic vibration of the wheels, Castillo said.


“We perform a real-time analysis of every rail car moving on our system each time it passes a trackside sensor, equaling 20 million car evaluations per day,” he said.


But even with all of those precautions, things can still go wrong, That’s why UP also has emergency procedures in place to deal with such accidents.


“We conduct comprehensive, ongoing employee safety training and have also invested in several fire-fighting trailers with fire-fighting foam,” Castillo said. “We have developed a reserve of foam and have strategically positioned trailers along our route for deployment as needed.”


Union Pacific also works with fire departments and emergency responders to offer comprehensive training in communities where the railroad operates, Castillo said.


“Union Pacific annually trains approximately 2,500 local, state and federal first-responders on ways to minimize the impact of a derailment in their communities. Union Pacific has trained nearly 38,000 public responders and almost 7,500 private responders (shippers and contractors) since 2003,” he said. “This includes classroom and hands-on training.”


Both Bannock County Sheriff Lorin Nielsen and Pocatello Fire Chief David Gates said they work with the railroad when it comes to their emergency preparations.


“We have done drills together and have practiced various scenarios,” Gates said.


Although they don’t have a step-by-step plan for every potential disaster — likely an impossible task — the fire department does have an emergency operations plan that covers a broad range of scenarios, including hazardous material spills, and the railroad provides a copy of its emergency operations plan as well, Gates said.


“In emergency response, we take a generalist approach,” he said, adding that that works because their responses to a variety of emergency situations are similar with just a few minor differences that can be adjusted as needed.


Nielsen agrees.


For instance, whether they are dealing with a fire or a hazardous material spill, they need to have a way to evacuate people and get them to a safe shelter while they work to contain and mitigate the problem, he said.


Nielsen said Bannock County has an emergency operations plan that is updated regularly, and the sheriff’s office meets with fire and ambulance and health officials every other month to plan and address any issues that come up.


Although the sheriff’s office and fire department train for many different scenarios, a hazardous material incident is certainly one of them. Especially since there is a railroad, factories, a nuclear reactor and two interstates in the area.


Nielsen said they work with the railroad, factories and others so that they know what types of hazardous materials are being transported through or stored in town, and those organizations have been up front in providing that data.


Gates said they also try to make sure they know who to contact in the event of an emergency so they can get any additional information they need to deal with the situation at hand.


Since there are many hazardous materials being transported through or stored in town, Nielsen said it’s important to know how to safely deal with them, especially since many of those materials play important roles and are necessary.


Oil is one of them.


Last year, UP shipped approximately 141,000 carloads of crude oil, and handles an average of three to five loaded unit trains every day, Castillo said.


“North America is now the biggest producer of crude oil in the world, partly as a result of fracking in North Dakota and other Western states. This energy renaissance is strengthening the U.S. economy, creating jobs, and increasing America’s energy independence,” Castillo said.


“Union Pacific’s role in this energy renaissance is to transport crude from oil fields to refineries as safely and efficiently as possible,” he said, adding that railroads provide the infrastructure, flexible network and efficiency needed to move large volumes of crude oil from remote locations where it is being recovered to destinations where it is highly valued. “Without America’s rail network, the current energy renaissance would not be nearly as robust.”


Still, there are dangers that come with transporting crude oil more frequently.


The federal government predicts that trains hauling crude oil or ethanol will derail an average of 10 times a year over the next two decades, causing more than $4 billion in damage and possibly killing hundreds of people if an accident happens in a densely populated part of the U.S.


The projection comes from a previously unreported analysis by the Department of Transportation that reviewed the risks of moving vast quantities of both fuels across the nation and through major cities.


The most recent spate of accidents began Feb. 14 when a 100-car Canadian National Railway train hauling crude oil and petroleum distillates derailed in a remote part of Ontario, Canada. Less than 48 hours later, a 109-car CSX oil train derailed and caught fire near Mount Carbon, West Virginia, leaking oil into a Kanawha River tributary and burning a house to its foundation.


On Thursday, 21 cars of a 105-car Burlington Northern-Santa Fe train hauling oil from the Bakken region of North Dakota derailed about 3 miles outside Galena, Illinois, a town of about 3,000 in the state's northwest corner. On Saturday, a 94-car Canadian National Railway crude oil train derailed about 3 miles outside the Northern Ontario town of Gogama and destroyed a bridge. The accident was only 23 miles from the Feb. 14th derailment.


The worst such accident in recent years involved a runaway train derailed in Lac-Megantic, Quebec, just across the U.S.-Canada border from Maine, on July 6, 2013. The resulting explosions and fire killed 47 people and destroyed the town's business district.
