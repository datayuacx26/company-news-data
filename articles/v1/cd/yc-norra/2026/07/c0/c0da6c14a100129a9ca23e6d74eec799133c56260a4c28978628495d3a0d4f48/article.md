A therapist has a resident booked for gait training at ten. The plan calls for a portable e-stim unit and a gait belt. The unit was in the gym yesterday. This morning it is gone: a restorative aide carried it to a resident's room for a bedside session and left it there. The therapist burns fifteen minutes of a booked slot walking the halls to find it. In a skilled nursing facility, that is a normal Tuesday, and it costs the building money in ways that never land as a single invoice line.

## Why therapy equipment goes missing in a nursing home


Rehab equipment in a skilled nursing facility splits into two groups, and they fail in different ways.


The first is the fixed gym gear: parallel bars, hi-lo mat tables, therapy stairs, recumbent cross trainers, treadmills. Too big to walk off. It does not disappear so much as sit idle or break, and when it breaks a whole modality goes dark until someone notices.


The second is where the money leaks: the portable, shareable pieces. E-stim and ultrasound units, pedal exercisers, arm bikes, hand weights, therabands, gait belts, restorative kits. Small, valuable, constantly in motion, and they leave the gym for two legitimate reasons nobody logs. One is bedside therapy: not every resident can come to the gym, so the therapist brings the equipment to the room. Two is restorative nursing: floor aides pull pedal exercisers and weights from the gym for scheduled programs and leave them in a room or supply closet when the round ends. Both are correct care. Neither writes down where the equipment went, so by afternoon the gym is short on gear it owns and the therapist starts the scavenger hunt.


One more thing makes this class harder: many buildings contract therapy to an outside company, which blurs ownership. When the provider changes, what belongs to the facility versus the departing contractor turns into a scramble, and undocumented gear walks out with the old vendor.

Then comes the rental trap. When a portable unit cannot be found or has broken, therapy does not pause a plan of care. It rents a replacement, or the building buys a duplicate because "we don't have one," when in truth it owns three, hidden in rooms across two floors. The rental arrives, gets used, then parks in a corner and keeps billing long after the need passed, because the return trigger is a person remembering, and nobody owns that memory.


## What missing and idle therapy equipment costs


The costs stack here, and they are bigger than they look.


The first is equipment dollars. A typical 110-bed skilled nursing facility wastes $155,000 to $500,000 a year on equipment: duplicate rentals, lost items, and retail replacements. Rehab modality units and specialty devices are not cheap, so every needless rental or duplicate here lands hard. Set that against a median SNF operating margin of 1.8%, roughly $200,000 of profit on a 100-bed building, and equipment waste alone can equal 77% to 150% of annual profit. The full model is in[how to cut equipment spending at a skilled nursing facility](https://www.norra.io/blog/cut-equipment-spending-skilled-nursing-facility) .


The second cost does not print on an invoice: lost therapy time. Nurses and therapy staff lose 30 to 60 minutes per shift hunting for equipment, and in a rehab department that comes straight out of billable, care-planned minutes. A session that starts late or gets cut short is a frustrated resident and a slower recovery. Under the way skilled nursing therapy is paid and surveyed, delivered minutes are the number, and equipment you cannot find erodes them.


A third cost is the absence of information: utilization you cannot see. Nobody can tell which pieces get used, so a gym ends up with an expensive unit gathering dust in one corner while a duplicate bills as a rental in another. Without data on what moves, you cannot right-size the gym, justify a capital request, or cancel a rental you are paying for but not using.


## How to fix it operationally


You can tighten this up without buying a new machine, and the discipline is worth putting in place regardless of vendor.


- **Tag both groups, portable pieces first.** Put a durable ID on the fixed gym gear and, more important, on every portable piece: modality units, pedal exercisers, weights, gait belts, restorative kits. The portable items are the ones that walk.
- **Keep a location history, not a snapshot.** You need where a unit is now and where it has been, so you can find it today and see which pieces never leave a corner.
- **Give everything a home base and sweep at end of day.** Anything that leaves the gym for a session belongs back at its base by shift's end. Build the sweep into the closing routine.
- **Settle ownership at every contract therapy transition.** When a provider comes or goes, inventory against a documented list so the facility's gear does not walk out with the old vendor.
- **Run rent-versus-own, and check utilization before you buy a duplicate.** Total what you have paid in rent against the purchase price, and return anything that has billed past what buying costs. Before approving a duplicate, confirm you actually lack one rather than failing to find it.


The catch is that every step depends on someone updating a log at each move, and the moves happen mid-session, when no one stops to type. That is the wall barcode and spreadsheet systems hit: they are only as current as the last person who remembered to scan.
