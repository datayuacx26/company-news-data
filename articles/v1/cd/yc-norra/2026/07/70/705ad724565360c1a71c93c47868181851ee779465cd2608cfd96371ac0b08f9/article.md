# Broda and Geri-Chair Tracking in Skilled Nursing Facilities

A resident's Broda chair is fitted to her: the tilt, the recline, the seat width, the positioning her care plan calls for. It travels with her to the dining room at noon, to therapy at two, and back to her room by four. Somewhere in that day it gets parked in a hallway, rolled into a shower room, or pushed into the soiled utility while housekeeping works. In a busy skilled nursing facility, a chair that expensive should be the easiest asset to account for. It is usually the hardest.


Broda chairs and geri-chairs are high-value positioning and comfort chairs, and they behave less like furniture than like the residents who sit in them. They move constantly, they get assigned and reassigned, and when a resident discharges, their chair tends to disappear into a back room instead of going back into rotation. This guide covers why that happens, what it costs your building, and how to track every chair down to the room it is in right now.


## Why Broda and geri-chairs disappear


A geri-chair is not a wheelchair you grab from a pool. It is set up for one resident: a specific recline for pressure relief, a specific tilt for a fall-risk positioning need, a width that fits. That individual fitting is exactly what makes the chair hard to keep track of, because it is always attached to a person who is always moving.


Follow a single chair through a day. It goes to the dining room and gets left there after the meal. It goes to therapy and stays in the gym. It gets swapped out for cleaning and a different chair comes back. A CNA on another unit borrows it because a resident needs a chair now and the nearest one is here. None of these moves are wrong. Care does not stop so someone can write down where a chair went. But by the end of a shift, the chair that belongs to room 214 could be on any of three floors, and nobody logged a single hop.


Then a resident discharges, and the failure mode gets expensive. The chair was theirs. Now it is nobody's. It gets rolled into a storage room, a shower room, or the end of a hallway, and it sits. Two weeks later a new admission needs a positioning chair, maintenance cannot find a free one in ten minutes, and the building calls the rental company. You now rent a chair you already own, parked forty feet away behind a laundry cart.


That is the rental trap for this equipment class, and it is worse for chairs than for most items. Broda and geri-chairs are expensive enough that facilities rent them by default instead of buying, so a single unaccounted chair turns into a rental line that bills every day. The rental keeps billing after the resident who needed it discharged, because the return trigger is a person remembering, and nobody owns that memory. Multiply it across a chain: each building rents its own chairs because corporate cannot see the three idle ones sitting at a sister facility across town.


## What a lost or idle chair actually costs


Put numbers on it. A typical 110-bed skilled nursing facility wastes $155,000 to $500,000 a year on equipment: duplicate rentals, lost items, retail replacements, and rentals that keep billing after the resident is gone. High-value positioning chairs sit at the top of that list, because a new one can run several thousand dollars, and a rental that bills unnoticed for six months can quietly cost more than buying the chair outright.


Set that waste against the margin it comes out of. The median skilled nursing facility runs a 1.8% operating margin, roughly $200,000 of profit on a 100-bed building. Equipment waste of $155,000 to $500,000 equals 77% to 150% of a facility's entire annual profit. A handful of orphaned chairs and forgotten rentals is not a rounding error. It is a real bite out of the number your operator reports. The cost is not only dollars. Nurses and aides lose 30 to 60 minutes per shift hunting for equipment, time pulled straight off the floor and away from residents. And a resident whose fitted chair cannot be found is a care problem: staff improvise with whatever chair is free, positioning slips, and on survey day you cannot show where a care-planned item is. Across the roughly 15,000 skilled nursing facilities in the country, this same pattern repeats building by building.


## How to get control of your chairs


You can tighten this up without buying anything, and the discipline is worth putting in place regardless of vendor.


- **Tag and inventory every chair.** Give each Broda and geri-chair a durable ID and a master list: model, positioning configuration, own or rent, and rental start date for anything you are billed for. You cannot manage what you have not counted.
- **Keep a location history, not a snapshot.** Knowing where a chair was last month is useless. You need where it is now and where it has been, so you can find it today and see which chairs never leave a storage room.
- **Make discharge trigger reassignment.** The moment a resident discharges, their chair goes back into the pool or back to the rental company. Build it into the discharge checklist so a freed chair never becomes an orphaned one.
- **Run rent-versus-own on every rental chair.** Total what you have paid in rental fees against the purchase price. When cumulative rent passes what the chair costs to buy, return it and buy one. Audit rental invoices monthly so nothing bills past a discharge.


The catch is that all of this depends on someone updating a log at every move, and the moves happen during care, when nobody stops to log. That is the wall barcode and spreadsheet systems hit: they are only as current as the last person who remembered to scan or type.
