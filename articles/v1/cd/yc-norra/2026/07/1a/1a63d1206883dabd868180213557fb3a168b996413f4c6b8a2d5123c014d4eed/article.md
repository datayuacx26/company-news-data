# Shower Chair and Commode Tracking in Nursing Homes

Shower chairs and commodes are the equipment a skilled nursing facility treats as beneath tracking. Each one is cheap, there are dozens in the building, and they get bought by the case and forgotten on the floor. That is why they bleed money. A nursing home does not lose these units in one event. It loses them a few at a time, until materials management orders another case to cover a shortage that is not real. The gear is already in the building. Nobody can see it.


This guide covers why shower chairs and commodes disappear faster than their price tag suggests, what the slow leak costs a nursing home against a thin margin, how to fix it operationally, and how Norra tracks every unit to the room without anyone scanning a thing.


## Why shower chairs and commodes disappear in a nursing home


The problem starts with the price. A standard bedside commode or shower chair costs little enough that no one guards it the way they guard a lift or a bariatric bed. No sign-out sheet, no home base, no return rule. It is pool gear that belongs to no one, which is the first condition for a thing to go missing.


Then the movement scatters them. Four patterns drive the loss:


- **They live in wet, shared rooms.** A shower chair belongs in the shower room and a commode at a bedside, but both migrate: into resident bathrooms, into soiled-utility rooms for cleaning, into the wrong room after a transfer. Each move is a chance for the unit to be parked somewhere and forgotten.
- **They rust out and vanish.** These units live in moisture. Frames corrode, seats crack, hardware seizes. A staffer pulls a degraded unit from service and it goes to a storage room or a dumpster with no record. The fleet shrinks silently, one quiet retirement at a time.
- **Cleaning separates them from service.** A commode goes to soiled utility to be disinfected between residents and does not come back. A unit waiting to be cleaned looks the same as a unit that is lost.
- **Bulk buying masks the whole thing.** You do not order one commode. You order a case, and that case absorbs the units sitting idle down the hall, the ones rusting in storage, and the ones that were never really gone. The reorder hides the loss instead of exposing it.


There is a rental angle too, though it is narrower than with big equipment. A heavier resident admits and needs a bariatric shower commode. Nobody can find the bariatric unit the building already owns, so the facility rents one that then keeps billing long after the resident discharged. For the standard units, the trap is not rental. It is the endless bulk reorder.


## What the slow leak actually costs


A typical skilled nursing facility of around 110 beds loses $155,000 to $500,000 a year to equipment waste: duplicate purchases, lost items, and rentals that outlive their need. Shower chairs and commodes rarely headline that number, and that is the trap. Their damage is death by a thousand cuts: low cost per unit, high volume, high churn, zero visibility. A facility that re-buys two cases a quarter it did not need, retires a dozen units early, and rents a bariatric commode it already owns is spending thousands a year on a category it dismisses as pocket change.


Set that against the economics of the building. The median SNF operating margin is 1.8 percent, roughly $200,000 of profit on a 100-bed facility in a good year. Total equipment waste of $155,000 to $500,000 equals 77 to 150 percent of a typical facility's annual profit. The commode line is a piece of that, and it is the piece nobody defends because each unit is too cheap to argue about. The dollars still leave the building. Add the staff time: nurses and aides lose 30 to 60 minutes per shift hunting for equipment, and a shower chair a resident needs right now for a scheduled bath is on that list.


Then there is the survey and infection-control exposure, which is sharper for this class than for almost any other. Shower chairs and commodes are shared, body-contact items that pass between residents, so they sit directly under infection prevention. A commode you cannot locate is a commode you cannot verify was cleaned, and a degraded unit still in rotation is a hazard. F880 (infection prevention and control) and F689 (accident hazards) under[42 CFR Part 483](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-483) are among the most-cited F-tags in the country. Missing commodes are a compliance problem wearing a cheap price tag.


## How to stop losing them: the operational playbook


You can cut this loss without any technology. The discipline is what matters, and it is worth doing on paper before you buy anything:


1. **Set a par level per unit and count against it.** Decide how many shower chairs and commodes each unit should hold. A number you count against is a number that reveals a shortage is fake before you reorder.
2. **Give every unit a home base and a label.** A commode assigned to a bedside and a shower chair assigned to a shower room is a unit someone will notice is gone. Pool gear with no home drifts by design.
3. **Close the cleaning loop.** When a unit goes to soiled utility to be disinfected, it comes back to its base. A named return step keeps cleaning from turning into disappearance.
4. **Count before you place the bulk order.** No case of commodes gets ordered until someone confirms the building is actually short. In a chain, check the sister buildings before you buy.
5. **Retire on the record.** When a rusted unit comes out of service, log it. A tracked retirement tells you your real fleet size. A silent one makes the next count wrong.


Here is the honest catch: the manual version works, and it works with a clipboard and one focused hour a month. It also decays the first month nobody owns that hour. These units move too fast and cost too little for a static count to stay true, which is why the leak creeps back the moment attention drifts. The durable fix keeps the count current on its own.
