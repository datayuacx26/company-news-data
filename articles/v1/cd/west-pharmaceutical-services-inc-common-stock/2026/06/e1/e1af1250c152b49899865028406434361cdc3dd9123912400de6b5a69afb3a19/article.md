# Considerations in Design and Development of Novel Combination Drug-Delivery Devices


**Developing a novel (as opposed to utilizing a platform technology) drug delivery device is a complex, and highly iterative endeavor. There are several considerations to keep in mind, and West will be publishing a series of blog posts to detail those considerations; the first post in the series will focus on how to successfully land on the right problem definition and ensure appropriate scoping as that’ll have a significant impact on timelines, program cost, and realization of outcomes.**


### Scoping Projects & Problems


Scoping projects and problems in medical engineering is the "Phase 0" or **Initiation** stage where clinical needs are translated into a formal, manageable project structure. This process prevents "scope creep" and ensures the device fulfils a genuine market and regulatory need.


#### 1. Problem Identification & Definition


Before a project begins, the core healthcare challenge must be articulated clearly.


- **Problem Statement:** Draft a concise description of the clinical pain points for patients, caregivers, or clinicians.
- **Clinical Need & Intended Use:** Formally define what the device will do, who will use it, and how it will improve patient outcomes.
- **Unique Selling Point (USP):** Identify the device's key differentiators from existing solutions to confirm market viability.


#### 2. Opportunity & Risk Analysis


Engineers evaluate the project's feasibility to decide if it should proceed to formal development.


- **Market & Competitor Research:** Analyse existing products and potential gaps in the current healthcare landscape.
- **Initial Risk Assessment:** Identify high-level technical, clinical, and safety risks early, often using tools like SWOT or FMEA.
- **Regulatory & IP Scoping:** Determine the device's likely classification (e.g., FDA Class I, II, or III) and investigate the patent landscape to avoid infringement.


#### 3. Defining Project Boundaries


This step sets the "In Frame" and "Out of Frame" parameters to focus resources.


- **Scope Statement:** Create a detailed document outlining specific objectives, deliverables, and—crucially—what is **excluded** from the project.
- **Constraints & Parameters:** Establish non-negotiable limits such as size, weight, budget, and required regulatory standards (e.g., ISO 13485).
- **Deliverables:** List the tangible outputs, such as working prototypes, test reports, and regulatory submission files.


#### 4. Resource & Stakeholder Planning


Scoping involves assembling the right multidisciplinary team and securing support.


- **Stakeholder Identification:** Engage all relevant parties—from corporate administrators to end-user clinicians—early to align expectations.
- **Resource Allocation:** Identify necessary people, materials, equipment, and financial funding.
- **Roles & Responsibilities:** Clearly define who is responsible for specific activities, often visualized through an organizational chart or process map.


#### 5. Finalizing the Project Charter


The phase concludes with a formal agreement to move into design.


- **Estimates & Timelines:** Develop high-level schedules with key milestones and decision "gates".
- **Acceptance Criteria:** Define measurable metrics that will be used to judge the project's success at the end of the lifecycle.
- **Approval:** The Project Manager seeks formal sign-off on a **Scoping Report** or **Project Charter** from the sponsor.
