## **Why 409A valuations matter more than ever in 2026**


Once a compliance checkbox, a poorly executed 409A valuation is now a strategic liability and a complicating factor for fundraising. Closer IRS scrutiny and the rise of secondary sales mean the margin for error is shrinking, and the potential implications of a valuation that cuts corners are more serious. Investors are also applying more due diligence pressure, often requiring up-to-date valuations before committing capital.


Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code establishes acceptable approaches for determining the[fair market value](https://pulley.com/guides/what-is-fair-market-value) (FMV) of a company's common stock. This valuation sets the strike price for employee stock options and supports IRS compliance, including[Form 3921](https://pulley.com/guides/what-is-form-3921) . Companies file Form 3921 when employees exercise Incentive Stock Options (ISOs), enabling the IRS to track and enforce tax liability.


A third-party valuation that follows recognized standards grants safe harbor status — meaning the IRS will accept it unless it can prove the valuation unreasonable. Most private companies use independent appraisals to document FMV and stay compliant.


A weak valuation has real consequences. Strike prices set below FMV expose employees to immediate tax liability and penalties. Auditors who question your methodology can delay your financial close or require a restatement. Investors running due diligence will press on assumptions — and a report that can't withstand that scrutiny can stall a round. For CFOs managing ASC 718 reporting, a poorly performed 409A creates a shaky foundation for every stock-based compensation calculation that follows.


### **The risks of treating 409As like a commodity**


When defining recognized standards, things get murky. Audit firms look closely at who prepared the report, the sources of financial data, and whether it supports the fair market value you report to the IRS.


Valuations based on internal datasets or automated models look credible but may not hold up under scrutiny. Unclear methods and assumptions raise questions during review. Some valuation firms use the Probability Weighted Expected Return Method (PWERM), which weighs exit scenarios like IPO or acquisition. While PWERM is valid, its results depend on assumptions that reflect your company’s actual outlook.


Even small errors in 409A valuations complicate equity planning and create compliance concerns for pre-IPO companies.

## **FAQ: Common Carta 409A valuation questions**


### **How do auditors evaluate 409A reports during financial audits?**


Auditors examine methodology rigor, data source independence, and ASC 718 compliance. They verify that assumptions are defensible, calculations are documented, and the preparer holds recognized credentials (ASA, CFA, or ABV). Outdated data, opaque methodologies, or uncredentialed preparers will draw pushback and delay your audit timeline.


### **What happens if your 409A valuation fails an audit?**


You'll need a revised report, often under time pressure and at additional cost. This delays your financial close, requires restating stock-based compensation expenses, and can trigger tax penalties for employees who exercised options at an incorrect strike price.


### **Can you switch from Carta to another provider mid-year?**


Yes. A new provider conducts a fresh valuation using your current financials and cap table data, typically timed around a material event. Most transitions are straightforward. If audit readiness or pricing transparency is a concern, switch before your next funding round or audit cycle.
