# Kids' Toys Wholesale in Pakistan for Resellers

Kids' toys are one of the easiest categories to resell in Pakistan and one of the easiest to lose money on. Demand runs all year, gifting spikes are predictable, and a good toy sells itself in a ten-second video. The catch is that toys are bulky, breakable and bought by parents who care about safety — so a bad sourcing decision comes back to you as refused parcels.


There are three realistic ways to get toys wholesale here: buy stock from a local wholesale market, import from China, or work from a supplier catalogue and order only after a customer confirms, so you never hold stock at all.


Below: how the three routes compare, which toy categories actually move in Pakistan, what to check before you list a toy, how to price around cash-on-delivery returns, and the months worth planning for.


## What "wholesale" really means for a toy reseller


In the toy trade the word covers two very different things, and beginners mix them up.


The first is **buying at a wholesale rate** . You pay a market or an importer for a carton, you own it, and it sits with you until it sells. The unit price is low. The risk is entirely yours.


The second is **sourcing at a wholesale rate without owning the carton** . You list products from a supplier's catalogue and the order is placed only once a buyer has confirmed. You get catalogue pricing on a single unit and you carry nothing.


For a new reseller the second is the sane starting point. Toys are the worst category to guess on — a design that was in every hand last Eid can be dead by the next one, and nobody takes back a carton of last season's plastic. Learn what sells with someone else's inventory, then buy stock later if the volume justifies it. ## Route 1: Pakistan's wholesale toy markets


Every big city has a general-goods market where toys move by the carton. Karachi's Bolton Market and Jodia Bazar, Lahore's Shah Alam Market, Rawalpindi's Raja Bazaar, Faisalabad's old city bazaars — all of them carry imported toys alongside household goods. Almost all of that stock arrives from China through an importer, passes through a wholesaler, and reaches you a step or two down the chain.


What the markets are genuinely good for:


- **Holding the product.** Toy quality varies wildly between two boxes that look identical in a photo. Thirty seconds with one in your hand tells you more than ten product images.
- **Reading demand.** What a shopkeeper stacks at the front of the shop is what's selling this month. That signal doesn't exist on a screen.
- **Restocking a proven seller.** Once you know an item moves, a local wholesaler is the fastest way to keep it in supply.


What they're bad for is starting. You pay upfront, you buy by the carton, you store it, and a design that doesn't move is money parked on your floor. Toys also eat more shelf space per rupee of value than almost anything else you could stock. ## Toy categories that sell in Pakistan


A toy that works for a reseller usually passes four tests: a parent recognises what it is instantly, it demonstrates in a short video, it survives a courier, and it sits at a price someone will decide on without a family meeting. The same four tests are why our shelf guides to the[best home and kitchen products to resell](https://www.markaz.app/blog-post/best-home-kitchen-products-to-resell-in-pakistan) and the[best gadgets and accessories to dropship](https://www.markaz.app/blog-post/best-gadgets-accessories-to-dropship-in-pakistan) read the way they do — toys just add a safety test on top.


- **Soft toys and plush.** The gifting staple. Light, hard to break, and photographs well. Watch the size — a large plush is mostly air, and couriers charge for the space it takes, not just the weight.
- **Remote-control cars and die-cast vehicles.** The strongest video category there is: the product does the advertising. Be honest about what's in the box — whether batteries are included, and how long a charge actually lasts. Overclaiming here brings the parcel straight back.
- **Building blocks and STEM kits.** Parents buy these on "they're learning something", which is a much easier sell than "they want it". Magnetic tiles, block sets, simple science and robotics kits. Count the pieces against the box claim before you list.
- **Puzzles and board games.** Flat, light, ship beautifully, and they sell to the whole family rather than one child. Strong through Ramadan evenings and winter, when nobody wants to be outdoors.
- **Dolls, kitchen sets and role-play.** Steady year-round demand and an easy photo. Check the plastic quality — the cheapest tier cracks the first week and you'll hear about it.
- **Baby and infant toys.** Rattles, teethers, soft books, activity gyms. Repeat buyers, because babies age out of every stage. This is also the category with the least tolerance for a safety mistake, so read the next section twice before you list here.
- **Outdoor toys and ride-ons.** Bicycles, scooters, tricycles, ride-on cars. The highest ticket value in toys and the hardest logistics — bulky to ship, expensive to return. Sell these once you have a customer base, not on day one.
- **Art, craft and colouring sets.** Clay, colouring kits, sticker books, sketch sets. They pair naturally with the school year, and they're cheap enough to be an add-on to a bigger order. If you sell to parents already, our guide to[the best books for kids by age](https://www.markaz.app/blog-post/best-books-for-kids-by-age-pakistan) covers the neighbouring shelf.
- **Collectibles and blind boxes.** Whatever the playground is talking about this term. Sells fast, dies fast — a good category to test with no stock and a bad one to buy a carton of.


What to leave alone at the start: anything glass or fragile, big ride-ons, and toys carrying a cartoon character or brand you can't verify. If the box says a famous name and the supplier can't tell you where it came from, don't attach your own name to it.


## Safety and quality: check this before you list


Parents forgive a slow delivery. They don't forgive a toy that hurt their child, and one bad story in a neighbourhood WhatsApp group ends a small reselling business. Check every item you're about to put your name on:


- **Small parts.** Anything that fits in a child's mouth is a choking risk. A toy with detachable small pieces is not a toy for a toddler, whatever the box shows.
- **Battery compartments.** The lid should be screwed shut, not clipped. Button cells are the single most dangerous thing in a cheap toy, and a lid a two-year-old can pop open is a reason to skip the product.
- **Edges and plastic quality.** Flex it. Brittle plastic that snaps leaves sharp edges. If it cracks in your hand, it will crack in a child's.
- **Paint and smell.** A strong chemical smell out of the packet is a warning sign. So is paint that marks your fingers.
- **Cords and strings.** Long cords on baby and cot toys are a strangulation risk. Skip them.
- **Age labelling.** Repeat the age range from the box in your own listing, honestly. Selling a 6+ toy to a parent shopping for a two-year-old is how you get a return and a bad review at the same time.
- **Safety markings.** Better toys carry a marking on the box or the packet. Look for it, ask the supplier when it isn't there — and never describe a toy as certified or tested unless you have seen the marking yourself.

## Packing so the toy arrives the way you sold it


A toy is a gift. It arrives at a birthday, and a crushed box is a failed order even when the toy inside is fine.


- Keep the retail box intact and put it inside a shipping carton — box in a box, not the retail box with a label slapped on it.
- Fill the empty space. A toy rattling around in a half-empty carton breaks on the third handling.
- Film a short packing clip for higher-value items. It settles a "it arrived broken" argument in seconds.
- Tell the buyer whether the packaging is gift-ready. Many parents are ordering for a party and will pay for the wrap.


## Pricing toys so cash on delivery doesn't eat the margin


Your selling price is your decision, not the supplier's. Build it in this order:


1. **Start from the listed product price.** That's your cost, and it's the only number you didn't choose.
2. **Add your margin.** Enough that the order is worth the hour it takes to sell, confirm and follow up.
3. **Add a return buffer.** A share of COD orders comes back. Toys refuse more often than boring categories, because a parent who has second thoughts at the door has second thoughts about a want, not a need. Price so the delivered orders still carry the refused ones.
4. **Sanity-check against the market.** Parents cross-check toy prices against the shop near their house. If you're far above it, they'll say so in the comments.
5. **Round to a clean number.** Awkward figures make people ask why.


Two habits protect the margin more than any pricing trick. Bundle where it's natural — a toy plus the batteries it needs, or two puzzles for siblings — so the delivery cost is spread across a bigger order. And never discount to rescue a buyer who is hesitating on trust; answer the question they're actually worried about instead. ## The toy calendar worth planning around


Toys are one of the few categories where you can see demand coming.


- **Both Eids.** The biggest gifting weeks of the year. Eidi buys toys. Post two weeks early — parents decide before the last day, and couriers slow down at the end.
- **Birthdays, all year.** The steady base under everything else. A customer who bought a birthday gift in March has another birthday in the family within months, so save every number.
- **Summer holidays.** Long, hot, and indoors for most of the country. Puzzles, board games, craft kits and anything that fills an afternoon.
- **Ramadan evenings.** Family games and quiet indoor toys move well in the weeks before Eid.
- **Back to school.** Art, craft and stationery-adjacent toys ride the same wave as school supplies.
- **Wedding season.** People buy for other people's children. Gift-ready packaging matters more here than anywhere else.


The point of a calendar when you hold no stock is timing your posts, not timing a purchase. That is the whole advantage of catalogue sourcing in a seasonal category.


## Selling toys with no ad budget


You don't need to spend on ads to sell your first toys. You need to be visible where parents already are.


- **WhatsApp status.** Still the highest-converting channel for a new reseller. Two or three toys a day, not twenty.
- **Parent and area groups.** School parent groups, neighbourhood buy-and-sell groups, apartment and building groups. Read the rules before posting; the sellers who get removed are the ones who post the same photo five times.
- **Short demo videos.** Film the toy working on your own table — your hand in the frame, real light. Don't post videos of other people's children, and don't reuse a supplier's studio clip everyone else is already using.
- **Answer the parent's real question.** Which age, is it safe, does it need batteries, is the box giftable. Answer those in the caption and half the messages disappear.
- **Follow up after delivery.** Ask how the child liked it. That one message is what makes the birthday-gift customer come back next birthday.

## Mistakes that cost toy resellers money


- **Buying a carton in month one.** The market rate looks unbeatable right up until the design stops selling.
- **Ignoring size.** A cheap, huge toy can cost more to move than an expensive small one. Check the box dimensions before you fall in love with the unit price.
- **Skipping the confirmation call.** A voice call before dispatch — the amount, the address, the delivery day — removes most impulse cancellations.
- **Selling on the character, not the toy.** Naming a cartoon brand you can't verify is a risk you don't need to take. Sell what the toy does.
- **Listing fifty products.** Five toys you can answer any question about beat a catalogue you've never held.
- **Going quiet after a complaint.** A broken toy handled well produces a repeat customer. A broken toy ignored produces a group post with your number in it.
