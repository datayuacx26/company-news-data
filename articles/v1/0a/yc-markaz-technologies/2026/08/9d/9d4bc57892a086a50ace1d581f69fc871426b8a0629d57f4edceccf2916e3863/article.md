# How to Write Product Descriptions That Sell

A product description sells when it answers three questions in the first ten seconds: what is this, will it work for me, and what happens if it is wrong. Everything after that is decoration.


The structure that works is short — a one-line promise, the specifications a buyer needs to picture the item in their hand, two or three benefits tied to a real moment of use, an honest block that handles the obvious objections, and one clear next step. Write it in the words your customer uses, not the words on the supplier's packing list.


Selling in Pakistan adds one rule. Most orders are cash on delivery, so the buyer risks time and the awkwardness of turning a rider away, not money. Your description has to settle the doubt before the parcel arrives.


## What a product description is actually for


It is the sales conversation you do not get to have. For every buyer who messages you asking about the fabric, ten scroll past because the caption told them nothing. The description does three jobs, in this order:


- **Identify.** Name the thing plainly. "Stainless steel water bottle, 1 litre" beats "premium hydration companion" every time, because the first one can be pictured and the second one cannot.
- **Convince.** Connect one feature to one moment the buyer recognises. Not "durable material" but "does not dent when it rolls around in a school bag".
- **De-risk.** Say what happens if the size is wrong, the colour is off, or the item does not arrive. Silence on this point reads as bad news.


A description that does all three can be four lines long. Length is not the goal; answered questions are.


## The five-part structure


Use the same skeleton for every product and you stop staring at a blank box. Fill it in this order.


### 1. One line that says what it is and who it is for


This is the line that survives being screenshotted. Product type, the one number or feature that matters most, and the buyer. "Non-stick 24cm frying pan — light enough for a hostel kitchen, deep enough for a family fry." Avoid opening with an adjective. Adjectives are what everyone else opens with, so they carry no information.


### 2. The specs, written as facts


Give the buyer the boring numbers: size in inches or centimetres, weight, capacity, material, what is in the box, what needs batteries, whether it is machine washable. List them, do not bury them in a paragraph. For clothing, add the measurement of the actual garment, not just "Medium" — sizes vary wildly between suppliers, and a size dispute at the door becomes a refused parcel.


If you do not know a spec, ask before you list the product. Guessing a measurement is how you earn a return.


### 3. Two or three benefits tied to a real moment


A benefit is a feature plus a situation. Take each spec and finish the sentence "which means that…".


- 600ml capacity → *which means* it fits a standard bike bottle cage and lasts a full commute.
- Double-stitched seams → *which means* it survives the wash more than a few times.
- Rechargeable, no cable needed → *which means* it still works when the load-shedding hits.


Three of these is plenty. Six is a wall nobody reads.


### 4. The objection block


Write down the three questions customers actually message you about — you already know them — and answer them in the description. Almost always they are some version of: is it the same as the picture, how long will it take to reach me, and what if I do not like it. Answer with what you can genuinely promise: delivery inside Pakistan on Markaz orders usually runs 3–5 days, items sourced from China take roughly 10–17 days, and there is a 7-day return window with buyer protection. Say the real timeline. A buyer who was told five days and waits twelve does not order again.


### 5. One next step


End with a single instruction: "Order now, pay the rider," or "Send me your size and I will confirm stock." One. Two calls to action split the decision and lose both.


## Writing for a cash-on-delivery buyer


COD changes the psychology of the sale. Because no money moves until the door, the buyer's hesitation is not "can I afford this" — it is "will I be embarrassed opening this in front of the rider". That shifts what your description has to carry.


- **Be specific about what arrives.** One unit or a pack of two? Which colour, if the photo shows four? Confusion at the door is a refused parcel and a return cost.
- **Name the price plainly in PKR** , and say whether delivery is included. Hidden charges are the fastest way to a cancellation.
- **Do not over-promise the photo.** If the fabric is lighter than it looks under studio lights, write that. One honest sentence costs you a few orders and saves you many more returns.
- **Give an arrival window, not a date.** "Usually 3–5 days" survives a delayed rider; "arriving Tuesday" does not.


Return rates fall when descriptions are boring and accurate. That is the whole trick, and it is worth more than any clever caption. ## A worked example


Both versions below are written as an illustration, not copied from a live listing.


**Before —** "Amazing quality water bottle! Premium look, best for gym and office. Limited stock, order fast!!!"


Nothing here can be checked. There is no size, no material, no price, no delivery time, and nothing a buyer could not say about any bottle on the internet.


**After —**


*Stainless steel water bottle — 1 litre, keeps water cold through a full shift.*


- Capacity: 1 litre · Height: 26 cm · Weight: 340 g empty
- Double-wall stainless steel, screw cap with a silicone seal
- Fits a standard gym bag side pocket and a car cup holder
- Hand wash only — not for the dishwasher
- In the box: 1 bottle. Colour as shown in the photo.


*Cash on delivery across Pakistan, usually 3–5 days. 7-day return window if it arrives damaged or is not what you ordered. Message your city and I will confirm stock today.*


The second version is longer, but every line answers a question a buyer would otherwise ask — or would leave without asking.


## Words that do the work, words that do not


Cut these on sight: *premium, high quality, amazing, best in market, world class, must have* . Every seller uses them, so they carry zero information and read as filler.


Keep these: measurements, materials, weights, capacities, timings, care instructions, and comparisons to objects the buyer already owns ("about the size of an A4 sheet", "lighter than a phone"). Concrete language is persuasive because it is checkable.


Two habits that lift conversion straight away: write in second person ("you"), and use short sentences. A description read on a phone, one-handed, on mobile data, is skimmed — not studied.


## The same product, three different channels


One product needs more than one description. The skeleton stays; the length and the opening change.


- **WhatsApp.** Six lines, maximum. Photo, one-line promise, three specs as a bullet list, price in PKR, delivery time, and "reply with your address". Nobody scrolls a wall of text in a chat.
- **Facebook post or page.** Lead with the moment, not the product. "School bags getting heavy again?" then the specs. Put the price in the post — hiding it behind "inbox for price" costs you more orders than it saves.
- **TikTok or Reels caption.** The video is the description. The caption carries the one number that makes people stop, plus the next step.
- **Your own store.** A Shopify or WooCommerce listing is the only place where the full description earns its keep, because Google reads it. Use the whole five-part structure, add a short FAQ block, and keep the specs in a list so they can be scanned.


## Writing for search without writing for robots


If your description lives on a website, it is also a page that can rank. A few rules cover most of the value:


- **Put the plain search term in the title.** People search "kids school bag waterproof", not "durable scholastic carryall". Match the words in the search box.
- **Write the first 150 characters as a standalone answer** — that is what tends to appear as the search snippet.
- **Never paste the supplier's copy.** When forty sellers publish the same block of text, none of them ranks. Rewriting it in your own words is the cheapest ranking advantage available.
- **Fill the alt text on your images** with a real description of what is in the photo, and name the file the same way.
- **Answer the questions people ask** in a short FAQ at the bottom. Those questions are search queries.


Keyword stuffing is not part of this. Repeating "water bottle Pakistan" eleven times reads badly to humans and buys nothing from search engines.


## Six mistakes that cost orders


1. **No size or measurement.** The single largest cause of returns in clothing and homeware.
2. **Copy-pasted supplier text.** Duplicate on the web, generic to the reader, invisible to search.
3. **Price missing.** "DM for price" filters out most of the people who would have bought.
4. **Emoji instead of information.** Two are fine. Twelve read as a scam.
5. **Claims you cannot support.** Never invent a warranty, a certification, a discount percentage or a delivery guarantee you do not control.
6. **Everything in capitals.** It does not add urgency; it adds noise.


## A fifteen-minute routine per product


1. **Two minutes:** collect the facts — measurements, material, contents, care, price in PKR.
2. **Three minutes:** write the one-line promise. Rewrite it twice; the second one is usually better.
3. **Three minutes:** turn three specs into "which means…" benefits.
4. **Three minutes:** write the objection block from your three most-asked questions.
5. **Two minutes:** add the next step, and cut every adjective that is not doing a job.
6. **Two minutes:** read it aloud. If you would not say it to a customer in a shop, rewrite the line.


Do this once per product and reuse the skeleton. By the tenth listing it takes five minutes.
