# A More Efficient Path to Modernizing Accommodations for the Public Sector

Public-sector employers often recognize the need to modernize internal systems long before they're able to implement new technology.


‍


Even when an organization has identified a clear operational need, the traditional procurement process can require months of planning, drafting, review, vendor evaluation and approval. For teams managing workplace accommodations, that delay lands directly on employees who are waiting for the support they need to do their jobs.


‍


In certain circumstances, sole-source procurement can provide public agencies with a more efficient path forward.


‍


#### **What sole-source procurement actually means**


[Sole-source procurement](https://www.ojp.gov/sites/g/files/xyckuh241/files/media/document/Sole_Source_FactSheet_C.pdf) isn’t a shortcut around the rules. It’s a recognized pathway within the rules.


‍


Most public procurement frameworks assume competition is the default because competition protects public dollars. But those same frameworks acknowledge that competition isn’t always possible or meaningful. When only one supplier can deliver what an agency needs, running a competitive solicitation doesn’t produce a better outcome. It produces a slower one.


‍


**Federal procurement guidance specifically recognizes patent issues and patented or proprietary systems as grounds for demonstrating that a product or service is available from only one source.** State and local rules generally mirror this logic, though the thresholds, forms and approval chains vary widely.


‍


The practical distinction is this:


- A competitive procurement asks "who can do this?"
- A sole-source justification answers "why can only this vendor do this?


The second question requires documentation, not the absence of it.


‍


#### **Why the traditional path takes so long**


For cities, counties, school districts, and other public-sector organizations, purchasing new technology can be a lengthy process. A traditional competitive procurement may require an organization to:


- Develop detailed specifications and requirements
- Publish a solicitation and hold a vendor question period
- Evaluate multiple proposals against a scoring rubric
- Conduct demos, reference checks and security reviews
- Move through legal, finance and council or board approval


‍


Each step is reasonable on its own. Stacked together, they routinely stretch across two or three budget quarters, and sometimes across a fiscal year boundary, which can mean starting over.


‍


Meanwhile, the accommodations process the agency is trying to fix keeps running the way it always has.


‍

#### **What a sole-source justification usually requires**


Every entity has its own process, but most justifications ask an agency to document some version of the following:


‍


1. **The operational need:** What problem is being solved, and what is the cost of not solving it? For accommodations, this often includes legal exposure, inconsistent decisions across departments and documentation gaps that surface during a charge or audit.
2. **Why the requirement is unique:** This is where the proprietary capability lives. It is not enough to say the product is good. The justification needs to connect the specific functionality to the specific need.
3. **What alternatives were considered:** Agencies are generally expected to show they looked. Documenting why general-purpose case management or HRIS tools do not meet the requirement is part of the record.
4. **Price reasonableness:** Without competing bids, agencies often need a separate basis for concluding the price is fair. Comparable public-sector contracts, published pricing or a cost analysis can serve this purpose.


‍


At Disclo, we’ve supported enough of these to know where they tend to stall, and we’re happy to help teams assemble the documentation rather than leaving them to reverse-engineer it.


‍


#### **Why accommodations are a particularly good fit**


Workplace accommodations are a complex area for public employers. Agencies may be supporting employees across multiple departments, locations, job classifications and collective bargaining environments. Requests can involve sensitive medical information, multiple stakeholders and decisions that must be carefully documented and defended later.


‍


When the process is managed through email, spreadsheets or disconnected systems, consistency is nearly impossible to achieve. Two employees with similar requests in different departments can receive different answers, and no one finds out until it becomes a problem. Employees may also be unsure where to begin, what information is required or what happens after they submit a request.


‍


Technology built **specifically** for accommodations can help public agencies create a more structured and accessible process without forcing HR teams to bend general-purpose tools into a shape they were never designed for.


‍


#### **Common questions from procurement teams**


1. **Does sole-source mean we skip due diligence?** No. It removes the competitive solicitation, not the review. Security assessments, legal review and approvals all still apply.
2. **Will this hold up if challenged?** That depends on the quality of the justification, which is exactly why the documentation matters. A well-supported record built on an issued patent is considerably more defensible than one built on familiarity or convenience.
3. **How much time does it actually save?** It varies by entity. The savings come from eliminating the solicitation, evaluation and scoring phases, which are typically the longest part of the timeline.
