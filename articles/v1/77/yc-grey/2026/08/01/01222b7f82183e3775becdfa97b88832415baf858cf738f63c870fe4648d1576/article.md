# 15 Japanese words and phrases to know before you visit Japan

Japan is one of the most exciting countries to explore, yet knowing a few local words can completely change your experience. You could be ordering ramen, asking for directions or shopping in a neighbourhood convenience store, making the effort to speak even a little Japanese is often met with genuine appreciation and warmer interactions.


The good news is you don't need to become fluent before you travel. A handful of everyday greetings, polite expressions and numbers will help you navigate restaurants, train stations, hotels and tourist attractions with greater confidence.


In this guide, you'll learn 15 useful Japanese words and phrases every traveller should know before arriving. They're easy to remember, practical in everyday situations and will help you enjoy a more comfortable, respectful and rewarding trip across Japan.

## **Japanese greetings every traveller should know**


These simple greetings are used every day in Japan. Learning when to use each one will help you make a positive first impression and show respect for local customs.


- **Konnichiwa:** Meaning **"Hello"** or **"Good afternoon,"** this is the most common greeting you'll hear during the day. It's suitable for shops, restaurants, hotels and meeting people for the first time.
- **Ohayō gozaimasu:** Meaning **"Good morning,"** this polite greeting is used until late morning. It's appropriate when greeting hotel staff, shopkeepers or anyone you meet early in the day.
- **Konbanwa:** Meaning **"Good evening,"** use this greeting after sunset when entering restaurants, hotels or meeting friends in the evening. It's polite, friendly and widely used across Japan.
- **Sayonara:** Meaning **"Goodbye,"** this is best used when parting for a long time. For casual, everyday goodbyes, Japanese speakers often use simpler expressions instead of saying *sayonara* .


## **Polite Japanese phrases every traveller should know**


Politeness is an important part of Japanese culture. Using respectful expressions, even if your pronunciation isn't perfect, shows good manners and is often appreciated more than speaking fluent Japanese without courtesy.


- **Arigatō gozaimasu** : Meaning **"Thank you very much,"** this is the polite way to express gratitude. Use it when a waiter serves your meal, a shop assistant helps you or someone gives you directions.
- **Sumimasen:** One of Japan's most useful phrases, it means **"Excuse me," "Sorry,"** or even **"Thank you"** in certain situations. Use it to get someone's attention, apologise politely or squeeze through a crowd.
- **Onegaishimasu:** Meaning **"Please,"** this phrase is commonly used when making a request, ordering food or asking for assistance. It's more natural than simply saying "please" in many everyday situations.
- **Hai:**Meaning **"Yes,"** *hai* is used to agree, confirm information or show you're listening. You'll hear it frequently in conversations, shops and restaurants throughout Japan.
- **Iie:** Meaning **"No,"** this is the polite way to decline something or disagree. It's soft and respectful, making it suitable for everyday conversations with strangers and service staff.


## **Useful words for trains stations and directions**


Japan's transport system is one of the world's best, but knowing a few direction-related words can make navigating stations, asking for help and finding your way much easier.


- **Eki (Station)** : You'll see and hear this word constantly when travelling by train. It refers to a railway or subway station and appears on maps, station signs and public announcements.
- **Toire (Toilet)** : A useful word whenever you're looking for public restrooms in train stations, shopping centres, restaurants or tourist attractions. Most major stations have clean, well-marked facilities.
- **Doko (Where?)** : Use this when asking for directions, such as "Tokyo Station, doko?" Even with limited Japanese, locals will usually understand you're asking where something is.
- **Migi (Right)** : Helpful when following directions from locals or reading simple navigation signs. You'll often hear it when asking how to reach a station exit or nearby attraction.
- **Hidari (Left)** : The opposite of *migi* , this word helps when navigating stations, crossing large intersections or following walking directions to hotels, restaurants and tourist sites.


## **Japanese dining phrases to use in restaurants**


Eating out is one of the highlights of visiting Japan. Knowing a few restaurant phrases and basic dining etiquette will make ordering food, paying your bill and interacting with staff much more comfortable.


- **Oishii (Delicious)** : Use this to compliment your meal. Saying *oishii* after tasting your food is a simple way to show appreciation and is always welcomed by restaurant staff.
- **Mizu (Water)** : If you need a glass of water, simply ask for *mizu* . Drinking water is usually provided free of charge in many Japanese restaurants.
- **Okaikei (The bill)** : When you're ready to pay, say *okaikei onegaishimasu* to politely ask for the bill. In many restaurants, you pay at the cashier near the entrance rather than at your table.
- **Itadakimasu (Let's eat)** : This expression is traditionally said before beginning a meal as a sign of gratitude for the food. While visitors aren't expected to say it, using it respectfully is appreciated and shows an understanding of Japanese dining culture.


## **Understanding Japanese numbers and money**


Knowing a few numbers and payment words makes shopping, ordering food and paying for everyday purchases much easier in Japan.


### **Numbers one to ten**


The numbers you'll hear most often are **ichi (one), ni (two), san (three), shi/yon (four), go (five), roku (six), shichi/nana (seven), hachi (eight), kyū (nine)** and **jū (ten)** . Learning these helps when reading prices, buying train tickets, counting change or ordering multiple items.


### **En; Japanese yen**


**En** refers to the **Japanese yen (¥)** , Japan's official currency. Prices are almost always displayed in yen, whether you're paying for meals, transport, shopping or attraction tickets, so recognising the term will make everyday spending much easier.


### **Genkin; Cash**


**Genkin** means **cash** . Although Japan has become more card-friendly in recent years, cash is still commonly used in neighbourhood restaurants, local markets, shrines and smaller family-run businesses, so it's worth carrying some yen wherever you travel.


### **Kādo; Card**


**Kādo** means **card** . Visa and Mastercard are widely accepted in cities, hotels and major retailers. ## **Emergency words and phrases you should never travel without**


These expressions can make a big difference if you need urgent assistance or struggle to communicate in an unfamiliar situation.


### **Tasukete (Help)**


If you're in danger or need immediate assistance, tasukete (help) is the word to remember. Whether you've lost your way, feel threatened or witness an emergency, saying it clearly will quickly alert people nearby that you need assistance.


### **Byōin (Hospital)**


Byōin (hospital) is an essential word if you need medical attention. Use it when asking for the nearest hospital, speaking with hotel staff or requesting directions from locals during an illness or medical emergency while travelling.


### **Wakarimasen (I don't understand)**


Don't worry if you can't follow a conversation. Saying wakarimasen (I don't understand) politely lets the other person know to slow down, repeat themselves or communicate in a simpler way, making everyday interactions much less stressful.


### **Tasukete kudasai (Please help me)**


For a stronger request, say tasukete kudasai (please help me). Adding kudasai makes your request polite while making it clear that you urgently need assistance from someone nearby.


### **Eigo o hanasemasu ka? (Do you speak English?)**


If communication becomes difficult, ask eigo o hanasemasu ka? (Do you speak English?). In major cities and tourist areas, you'll often find someone who can help or direct you to an English-speaking staff member.


## **Frequently asked questions**


### **How do you say hello in Japanese?**


The most common way to say hello is konnichiwa, which is used during the daytime and early evening. It's appropriate when greeting shop staff, hotel employees or people you meet for the first time. For mornings and evenings, Japanese speakers usually use more specific greetings instead of *konnichiwa* .


### **How do you say good morning in Japanese?**


You say ohayō gozaimasu, the polite form of "good morning." It's commonly used until late morning when greeting hotel staff, colleagues, restaurant workers or anyone you meet early in the day. Friends sometimes shorten it to ohayō, but visitors should use the polite version in most situations.


### **Is English spoken in Japan?**


English is spoken in many hotels, airports and major tourist attractions, but it's less common in smaller towns, neighbourhood restaurants and local businesses. Learning a few basic Japanese words can make everyday interactions much easier and is often appreciated by local people throughout your visit.


### **Do I need cash in Japan?**


Yes. Although card acceptance has improved significantly, Japan is still a cash-friendly country. Smaller restaurants, local markets, temples and family-run businesses may only accept cash, so carrying some Japanese yen alongside your payment card helps you avoid inconvenience during your travels and makes everyday purchases much easier.


### **Is tipping expected in Japan?**


No. Tipping is not part of Japanese culture and can even cause confusion. Restaurants, hotels, taxis and other service providers include excellent service as part of the experience, so simply saying arigatō gozaimasu is the most appropriate way to show appreciation after receiving good service.


### **Can I use my card in Japan?**


Yes. Visa and Mastercard are widely accepted in cities, hotels, department stores and many restaurants.
