# What is an offshore bank account? A complete guide

Say the words “offshore bank account”, and most people instinctively think of a shadowy villain in a spy movie, casually wiring millions of dollars to a secret Swiss account. But this image is not quite accurate.


An offshore bank account is simply an account held in a country other than the one you live in. People use them to hold foreign currency, receive international payments, and protect savings against local currency swings. Offshore accounts are legal when declared to your tax authority. A multi-currency account offers most of the same benefits online.


So, it is not about popular culture with secrecy, tax evasion, or complex wealth structures reserved for the very rich. And this guide clarifies this by explaining what an offshore bank account actually is, how it works, whether it is legal, and how modern multi-currency accounts have made many of the practical benefits accessible to anyone.


## What is an offshore bank account?


An offshore bank account is simply a bank account held in a country other than the country you live in or are a citizen. The term originally referred to accounts in jurisdictions physically separate from major economies, such as island nations. Today, it is used more broadly for accounts held outside your home country.


That makes offshore banking less mysterious than it often sounds. For example, if you live in the UK and open a bank account in Ireland, that is technically an offshore account. Similarly, if a US freelancer opens a foreign currency account with a UK-regulated fintech to receive payments from European clients, that can also be considered offshore banking.


The idea of offshore accounts as secret bank accounts comes largely from how it has been abused in the past, especially in certain jurisdictions with strict banking secrecy. But with International information-sharing agreements now in play, the secrecy has declined significantly.


Today, offshore accounts are commonly used to hold foreign currencies, manage international income, and access financial services that may not be available in someone's home country.


## How do offshore bank accounts work?


An offshore account works much like a regular bank account, although there are some important differences.


- **Holding foreign currency:** One of the main reasons people use offshore or foreign accounts is to hold money in another currency without converting it immediately. This can be useful if you earn in a foreign currency or expect to spend money in that currency later.
- **Receiving and sending money internationally:** Many offshore accounts provide local banking details for the currency they support. For example, a USD account may provide a US routing number, while a GBP account may provide a UK sort code. Your clients, employers, or family can then send money to you as a local transfer rather than an expensive international wire.
- **Interest and account features:** Some offshore accounts pay interest on your balance, although rates vary by provider and currency. Many also offer features such as debit cards, online banking, and international transfers.
- **Access:** Traditional offshore bank accounts may require in-person verification, minimum deposits, or ongoing maintenance fees. Modern fintech and multi-currency accounts have made the process much easier, with many allowing you to open an account online after completing identity verification.


## Are offshore accounts legal?


Yes, offshore accounts are legal, as long as you follow the tax and reporting rules that apply in your country. Simply keeping money in a foreign account is not illegal in itself. What matters is whether you accurately declare that account and any income earned from it to your relevant tax authority.


Many countries participate in international information-sharing frameworks, including the OECD's Common Reporting Standard (CRS). US citizens and residents are also subject to the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA).


These rules mean foreign banks may be required to report information about foreign account holders to the relevant tax authorities. US citizens and residents with foreign accounts above certain thresholds may also need to file a Foreign Bank Account Report (FBAR) with FinCEN and report foreign income on their tax return.


In other words, the legal issue is usually not having an offshore account. It is failing to declare it when required.


This is general information, not tax or legal advice; consult a qualified tax professional for guidance specific to your situation and jurisdiction.


## Who should consider an offshore or multi-currency account?


An account outside your home country can be useful for several groups of people.


- **Expats and people living abroad:** If you live in one country but earn money, own assets, or plan to retire in another, an offshore or foreign account can make managing money across both countries easier and reduce unnecessary currency conversions.
- **Freelancers and remote workers with foreign clients:** Receiving payments in your client's currency through local banking details can help you avoid international wire fees and repeated currency conversions.
- **Savers dealing with local currency volatility:** If your local currency is losing value quickly, holding some savings in a more stable currency such as USD, GBP, or EUR can help protect your purchasing power.
- **Frequent travellers:** Holding balances in multiple currencies simplifies spending across countries without repeated conversion at unfavourable rates each time.
- **International business owners:** Businesses with customers or suppliers in different countries can use multiple currency accounts to receive and make payments without converting money unnecessarily.


## Offshore account vs foreign bank account: what is the difference?


The terms offshore bank account and foreign bank account are often used interchangeably, although there is a slight difference in how they are viewed.


A foreign bank account is simply an account held outside your country of residence. It is a straightforward and neutral description.


An offshore account can mean the same thing, but the term has historical associations with low-tax or high-secrecy jurisdictions such as the Cayman Islands, Switzerland, and the Channel Islands.


In everyday financial discussions, the two terms are often used to describe the same thing. They both refer to a bank account held outside your home country. The distinction becomes more important in specific legal, tax, and compliance situations.


Whether you need an account as an individual or a small business, the use is essentially the same. They allow you to hold, receive, and spend money in another currency or through a financial system different from that of your country.

## Frequently asked questions about offshore bank account opening


### **Is there a minimum deposit to open an offshore bank account?**


It depends on the provider. Traditional offshore banks, particularly those serving high-net-worth clients, often require minimum deposits of $10,000 or more. Modern multi-currency fintech accounts, including Grey, typically have no minimum deposit, making them more accessible to freelancers, remote workers, and small businesses.


### **Is an offshore bank account worth it?**


If you earn, spend, or save in multiple currencies, an offshore or multi-currency account can be useful. It can reduce the need for repeated currency conversions through a domestic bank, which may become expensive over time. For most individuals, a modern multi-currency account can provide the practical benefits of offshore banking with less cost and complexity.


### **Can anyone open an offshore bank account?**


Most people can open a modern multi-currency account online if they can complete standard identity verification, usually with a government-issued ID and proof of address. Traditional offshore banks may have stricter requirements, including minimum deposits and restrictions based on nationality or country of residence.


### **Is money in an offshore account taxable?**


Yes. Income earned through an offshore or foreign account is generally taxable in your country of tax residence. Depending on your location, you may also need to declare the account even if it does not generate income. For example, US persons may have FBAR reporting requirements. Failing to declare an account when required can lead to significant penalties. This is general information, not tax advice, so speak to a qualified tax professional about your situation.


### **What is the difference between an offshore account and a savings account?**
