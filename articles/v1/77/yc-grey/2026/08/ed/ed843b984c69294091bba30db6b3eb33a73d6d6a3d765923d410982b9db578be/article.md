# What is the currency of Israel? Shekel symbol and code

In the early 1980s, Israel’s old shekel went completely off the rails. Inflation hit nearly 450%, prices changed daily, and people needed bags full of cash just to buy groceries. By 1985, the government had to take more drastic action. So, chopped off three zeros and introduced the new Israeli shekel.


The currency of Israel is the new Israeli shekel (currency code ILS), often shown with the shekel symbol. It is issued by the Bank of Israel and is divided into 100 agorot. One US dollar is worth a few shekels, though the exact rate changes daily and should be checked live before you travel or transfer.


Many decades down the line, many outsiders are still confused by Israel's currency. Is it the shekel, the new shekel, or the Israeli pound? This guide clarifies the terminology, explains the symbols and denominations, and covers what you need to know about the dollar-to-shekel exchange rate before you travel or send money.


## What is the currency of Israel?


Israel's official currency is the new Israeli shekel, commonly shortened to shekel in everyday use. It is issued and managed by[the Bank of Israel](https://www.boi.org.il/) , the country's central bank.


The current shekel was introduced in 1985 following a period of severe inflation in the Israeli economy. It replaced the earlier currency, also called the shekel. And before that, there was the Israeli pound.


In real life, no one actually calls the currency the "new Israeli shekel." Most people and institutions simply call it the shekel.


The Bank of Israel manages monetary policy for the shekel, including setting interest rates and working to maintain the currency’s stability, much like the Federal Reserve does for the US dollar.


## The shekel symbol and code


The shekel's symbol is ₪, a distinctive character that combines the Hebrew letters shin (ש) and het (ח), the first letters of "shekel" and "hadash" (new) in Hebrew.


The ISO 4217 currency code for the shekel is ILS (Israeli new shekel), used in banking, finance, and currency conversion internationally to unambiguously refer to Israel's currency.


The shekel is subdivided into 100 agorot (singular: agora), the Israeli equivalent of cents. In practice, agorot amounts below 10 have become largely obsolete in circulation due to inflation reducing their practical value, and prices are commonly rounded to the nearest 10 agorot or whole shekel in cash transactions.


**How prices are typically written:**


- ₪100 (using the shekel symbol)
- 100 ILS (using the ISO code)

## Dollar to shekel: the exchange rate


The exchange rate between the US dollar and the Israeli shekel is a freely floating market rate. This means the value of the shekel fluctuates continuously throughout the day, driven by supply and demand in the foreign exchange market, rather than being fixed or tightly controlled by the Bank of Israel.


Several factors influence the dollar-to-shekel rate day to day, including:


- The interest rate difference between the US Federal Reserve and the Bank of Israel
- Geopolitical developments in the region
- Israel's trade balance
- Other global factors


Because exchange rates can change throughout the day, any fixed rate can quickly become outdated. If you are planning a trip, budgeting, or sending money, check a reputable, live currency converter for the latest dollar-to-shekel rate.

## Getting and spending shekels as a traveller


Card payments are popular in Israel. Visa and Mastercard are widely accepted in major cities like Tel Aviv, Jerusalem, Haifa, and most tourist areas, including restaurants, shops, hotels, and taxis. You will still want some cash for smaller vendors, local markets such as Mahane Yehuda in Jerusalem and Carmel Market in Tel Aviv, and some smaller towns.


You can find ATMs throughout Israeli cities, and most accept international cards. Your bank may charge a foreign transaction or cash withdrawal fee, while the ATM operator may also charge a fee. Check your bank’s fees for international ATM withdrawals before your trip so you know what to expect.


Tipping is common in Israeli restaurants, with 10% to 15% generally expected. Tipping taxi drivers is less common, although rounding up the fare is appreciated.

## Frequently asked questions about Israel’s currency


### **What is the symbol for the shekel?**


The shekel symbol is ₪. It comes from the Hebrew letters shin and het, representing *shekel hadash* (new shekel). You will see it on price tags, receipts, and other financial documents in Israel. The international currency code is ILS.


### **How much is 1 dollar in shekels?**


The dollar-to-shekel exchange rate changes throughout the day based on market conditions. Instead of relying on a fixed rate, check a live currency converter or financial data source before travelling, budgeting, or sending money. The rate can change even within the same day.


### **Is USD accepted in Israel?**


Some tourist-focused businesses, hotels, and vendors may accept US dollars, but this is not standard practice. The exchange rate offered when paying in dollars may also be less favourable. For most purchases, you will get a more predictable rate by paying in shekels with a card or cash.


### **What are agorot?**


Agorot (singular: agora) are the smaller units of the Israeli shekel, similar to cents for the US dollar. There are 100 agorot in one shekel. Coins worth less than 10 agorot are rarely used today, and cash prices are often rounded.


### **Can I use my card in Israel?**


Yes. Visa and Mastercard are widely accepted at restaurants, shops, hotels, and taxis, particularly in major cities and tourist areas. Cash is still useful for smaller vendors, markets, and less touristy areas. It is also a good idea to let your card issuer know about your travel plans to reduce the risk of transactions being blocked.


### **Should I exchange money before I travel to Israel?**


You generally do not need to exchange a large amount of cash before travelling. Israel has widespread card acceptance and plenty of ATMs. Having a small amount of cash for immediate expenses, such as transport from the airport, can be useful, but paying by card and withdrawing shekels locally will usually be more convenient than exchanging a large amount before your trip.
