# How to Get Your First International Freelance Client | Grey

Most of the time, landing your first international client is the most difficult part of venturing into freelancing. It does not necessarily get easier from there, but your chances of securing another gig are comparatively higher. Learning how to get freelance clients from the US or UK when you are based in Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, or India is a different challenge from finding local clients.


If you are wondering how to get freelance clients internationally, the answer is not only about improving your skills. You also need to build trust with clients in another country, know where to find opportunities, and make it simple for them to pay you.


Getting your first international freelance client requires three things: a portfolio that proves you can do the work, a way to reach clients in your target market, and a payment setup that makes it easy for them to pay you. Most freelancers focus only on the first and miss the second and third.


This guide covers seven approaches that specifically work for international client acquisition, including the parts most articles skip.


## Why getting international clients is different


The opportunity is real and growing. The online gig economy now accounts for up to[12% of the global labour market, with around 435 million workers worldwide](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2023/09/07/demand-for-online-gig-work-rapidly-rising-in-developing-countries?utm_source=chatgpt.com) . Demand for online gig work has grown rapidly, particularly in developing countries, creating more opportunities for freelancers in markets like Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, and India to work with clients in the US, UK, and beyond.


But a freelancer in Lagos or Nairobi trying to land a client in New York or London faces barriers that go beyond possessing the right skills. Understanding these barriers is the first step towards getting past them.


- **Trust issues:** Most US and UK clients have never hired internationally. They might immediately become sceptical about the risks of hiring someone from a different country, even if the work is identical to what a local freelancer would produce. You are not just selling your skill. You are also asking them to trust a process they have not done before.
- **No track record in their market:** Your work history, references, and reputation are largely invisible to a client in a market you have never worked in. A recommendation from a Lagos business means less to a San Francisco startup than a recommendation from someone they know.
- **Payment problems:** Sometimes, hiring goes smoothly until it is time to discuss payment, and you do not have a convenient payment method. That might be the end of the deal. An international client might be discouraged by the stress of figuring out how to initiate a SWIFT wire to an unfamiliar country, incur sending fees, or navigate a payment platform they are not familiar with. They would rather use domestic payment systems such as ACH in the US, Faster Payments in the UK, or SEPA in Europe.
- **Time zone incompatibility:** An eight- to twelve-hour difference requires extra effort to maintain real-time communication and avoid scheduling issues. Clients who have not worked internationally before might assume that significant time zone differences can affect the working relationship and final outcome.


Each of these barriers is solvable, and none of them requires you to be less talented than you are.


If you have been struggling with landing your first international freelance client, here are 7 things you might need to do differently to stand a chance in this competitive gig economy.


## Choose a niche and position yourself clearly


One of the biggest mistakes new freelancers make is trying to appeal to every possible client. International clients are usually looking for someone who understands their industry, audience, and specific business challenges.


Instead of positioning yourself as a general “graphic designer”, “writer”, or “developer”, be specific about who you help and the results you deliver. A profile that says “Email copywriter for e-commerce brands” or “UI designer for SaaS startups” gives clients a clearer reason to choose you over dozens of other freelancers.


Your niche should also be reflected across your portfolio, LinkedIn profile, and proposals. When a US or UK client immediately understands what you do and who you work with, they are more likely to trust that you can solve their problem.


You do not need to pick a niche forever. Your positioning can evolve as you gain experience, but having a clear focus makes it easier to attract your first international clients.


## Build a portfolio that works for international clients


Your portfolio is what a US, EU, or UK client sees before they agree to a call. It needs to do specific things to convert a stranger in a different country into someone willing to pay you.


A strong international portfolio checklist:


- **Be on a platform that international clients trust:** Behance, Dribbble, GitHub, or a simple personal website. Do not host your portfolio only on Instagram or Facebook, as many US and UK clients associate those platforms with consumer use rather than professional services.
- **Contain enough substance:** Include 3 to 5 case studies. Describe the brief, your process, and the outcome. What problem did the client have? What did you do? What changed as a result?
- **Be written in clear, professional English:** This signals that communication will not be a barrier.
- **Include a rate range or starting price:** This prepares clients and saves time for both parties. It also signals confidence and professionalism.
- **Show work that is relevant to your target market:** If you want to work with US e-commerce brands, include work that looks like what those brands need. Clients hire people whose portfolios look like the work they need done.


If you do not have client work to show, create mock projects, such as redesigning a brand you admire or creating a mock campaign for a product you use. The quality of the work matters more than whether there was a real client.


## Use platforms to get your first freelance client


The fastest route to a first international freelance client is a platform that already has those clients. Platforms like Upwork, Fiverr, PeoplePerHour, and Toptal have US and UK clients actively looking for freelancers right now. They have verified payment systems. They handle the trust infrastructure you have not yet built.


A practical strategy for your first 90 days:


1. **Pick one platform and go deep.** A mediocre presence on five platforms is less effective than a strong, complete profile on one.
2. **Accept two or three early projects at a rate slightly below your target.** The goal at this stage is reviews, not income maximisation. One honest review from a US client is worth more than any portfolio piece for your next proposal.
3. **Over-communicate.** Send updates before they are asked for. Deliver before deadlines. Make your first clients feel working with you internationally was easier than working with someone local.
4. **Raise rates after your first five reviews.** Once you have social proof from real clients,[learn how to negotiate your freelance rates](https://grey.co/blog/negotiate-freelance-rates) to move toward the income level that reflects your skill.


The first client is the hardest. The second is noticeably easier because you have proof.


## Reach clients directly on LinkedIn


LinkedIn is where the people who hire freelancers spend their professional time. A well-used LinkedIn profile and outreach strategy can generate inbound interest without competing on a crowded platform.


Practical freelance client outreach on LinkedIn:


- **Optimise your headline with your skill and target market.** “Brand Designer for US Tech Startups” is more effective than simply saying “Graphic Designer”. The more specific you are, the more relevant your profile appears to the right people.
- **Connect with decision-makers, not other freelancers.** Marketing managers, creative directors, founders, and heads of content at companies in your target niche are the people who hire freelancers. Connect with them and engage with their content before pitching your services.
- **Post two to three times per week.** Share work, process insights, lessons from projects, or observations about your field. Consistent activity builds visibility with people who are not yet connected to you.
- **Send short, personalised direct messages.** Reference something specific about their company or role. Ask a relevant question or offer something useful. The pitch should be about their needs, not only about your services.


Consistency over three to four months on LinkedIn generates compounding inbound interest. The first month feels like shouting into the void. By month four, people start coming to you.


## Join communities where your clients spend time


Your next client might be in a Slack channel you have not joined yet. US and UK professionals in design, marketing, technology, and content regularly share freelance opportunities within communities before posting them publicly.


Some communities worth joining:


- **Designer Hangout (Slack):** An active community for UX and product designers with regular job postings.
- **r/forhire (Reddit):** A dedicated subreddit where clients post freelance opportunities across disciplines.
- **Industry-specific Slack workspaces in your niche:** Search for “\[your industry\] Slack community” to find relevant groups.


The approach in every community is the same: contribute before you ask for anything. Answer questions, share useful information, and become a recognisable name. When someone posts a job that matches your skills, you are no longer a stranger.


## Cold pitch small agencies for subcontract work


Large direct clients are difficult to reach. Small agencies are not.


A design agency in Austin or Manchester with six to fifteen people regularly has more work than their permanent team can handle. They subcontract the overflow to freelancers, often internationally. They are experienced in remote work arrangements, know how to brief contractors, and are not afraid to hire someone outside their country.


How to find and pitch them:


1. Search for “\[your service\] agency \[city\]” and look at studios with a few employees.
2. Check their website for a “Work With Us”, “Careers”, or similar page. Many small agencies invite contractor pitches directly.
3. Send a short, specific email: one sentence on who you are, one on what you do, and one asking if they bring in outside contractors when their capacity is full.


The pitch does not need to be long. It needs to be relevant. Do not copy and paste the same message to fifty agencies. Write something that shows you looked at their work specifically.


## Solve the payment problem before your client has to


The payment conversation is where international freelance deals fall apart more often than most people admit. A client who wants to hire you but does not know how to pay you may choose a local freelancer instead. Not because you were not good enough, but because you made the payment question feel like their problem to solve.


You can remove this entirely by having the right setup before your first proposal goes out.


US clients pay by ACH, the US domestic bank transfer system. If you share a US routing number and account number with them, they make a standard domestic payment. No international wire, no SWIFT fees, and no confusion.

## Frequently asked questions


### **How do I find my first freelance client with no experience?**


Start with speculative portfolio work if you have no client work to show. Build three to five strong pieces in the niche you want to work in, host them on Behance or a personal site, and use Fiverr or Upwork to get your first paid review. The first client does not need to pay well. The first client needs to write a review that the second client can read.


### **How do freelancers in Africa find international clients?**


The most effective routes are platforms (Upwork, Fiverr, PeoplePerHour), LinkedIn outreach, and subcontracting for small agencies. The payment setup matters as much as the client acquisition strategy. Many African freelancers lose potential clients because the payment question creates friction. Having a USD or GBP account ready before the conversation starts removes that barrier.


### **What is the best platform to find freelance clients?**


It depends on your discipline and experience. Upwork is the most broadly active across skill categories and works for both beginners and experienced freelancers. Fiverr suits services that can be packaged as fixed-price deliverables. Toptal pays the most but has a rigorous screening process. PeoplePerHour has a strong UK and European client base. Most experienced freelancers eventually develop direct client relationships and use platforms primarily to fill gaps.


### **How do I write a cold pitch to a freelance client?**


Keep it short and specific. One sentence on who you are, one on something relevant you noticed about their business, and one on why you might be useful to them. The goal of a cold pitch is to get a reply, not to close a deal. Do not attach your portfolio unsolicited. Do not use a template you sent to 100 other businesses. Make it clear you looked at their work specifically.


### **How do I get paid by international clients?**


The most reliable and cost-effective method is giving clients an account number in their currency. US clients can pay USD via ACH to a US routing number and account number. UK clients can pay GBP via Faster Payments to a UK sort code and account number. Grey provides both. This removes the need for international wires, which are slower, more expensive for both parties, and involve correspondent bank deductions that reduce what you receive.


### **How long does it take to land a first freelance client?**


It varies significantly. Freelancers who sign up to a platform, submit well-targeted proposals, and have a strong portfolio typically see their first client within four to eight weeks. Those who approach it casually or fail to invest in their profile may wait months. Direct outreach on LinkedIn takes longer to produce results but compounds more effectively over time. A realistic expectation for a focused first-time freelancer is two to three months to a first paying client.
