# Bali Digital Nomad Visa: Working Legally in Indonesia | Grey

Bali has been one of the world's most popular destinations for remote workers for years. The combination of affordable living, established coworking infrastructure, warm climate, and English-friendly environment has made it a default destination for nomads. The visa situation, however, has always been more complicated than the destination's reputation suggests.


Bali does not have its own visa system. Indonesia launched a Digital Nomad Visa (E33G) in 2023, but uptake has been limited. Most remote workers in Bali use a tourist visa or Social Visa, neither of which officially permits remote work. The E33G visa is the only route that explicitly allows working remotely for foreign employers.


This article covers the four realistic visa options for working remotely in Bali, states clearly what each one permits and does not permit, and explains the only route that provides explicit legal authorisation for remote work. The goal is to give you accurate information so you can make an informed decision rather than a comfortable one.

## Does Bali have a digital nomad visa?


Bali is a province of Indonesia, not an independent country. Indonesia's national visa system applies to Bali, Lombok, Java, and every other island. There is no Bali-specific visa.


Indonesia introduced the E33G Remote Worker Visa, its formal digital nomad visa, in 2024. The E33G is a KITAS (Kartu Izin Tinggal Terbatas), a temporary stay permit, classified under the E33G visa category for remote workers employed by companies outside Indonesia.


The E33G is the only visa in Indonesia's system that explicitly authorises remote work for a foreign employer while residing in Indonesia. All other commonly used visas, including the tourist Visa on Arrival, the Social or Cultural Visa (B211A), and standard business visas, do not explicitly authorise remote work.


In practice, large numbers of foreigners have worked remotely in Bali on tourist visas and Social Visas for years. This was widely tolerated. The enforcement landscape changed significantly in 2025 and 2026. Bali immigration formed a dedicated enforcement task force, and deportations increased substantially, with immigration officers monitoring social media, coworking-space activity, and LinkedIn profiles as evidence for enforcement. The "open secret" that tourist visas were fine for remote work is no longer accurate to describe current conditions.

### **Tourist Visa on Arrival (VoA)**


Available to citizens of most countries on arrival at major Indonesian airports and seaports. Valid for 30 days, extendable once for a further 30 days at an immigration office. No advance application required.


The VoA does not permit remote work for a foreign employer. It is a leisure and short-stay visa. Many remote workers use it anyway. Enforcement risk has increased in 2026, with immigration specifically targeting foreigners working on tourist visas.


### **Social/Cultural Visa (B211A)**


Requires an Indonesian sponsor, a cultural institution, language school, or individual, to provide a letter of sponsorship. The B211A allows stays of up to 60 days, extendable multiple times to a maximum of 180 days total. It does not explicitly permit remote work.


B211A enforcement increased in 2025 and 2026, with deportations specifically citing remote workers on social visas. The B211A remains popular among nomads on longer Bali stays, but it carries a higher enforcement risk than before.


### **KITAS (Temporary Stay Permit)**


The general KITAS is a residency permit for people with legitimate reasons to live in Indonesia long-term, including employment with an Indonesian company, marriage to an Indonesian citizen, or investment. An employment KITAS requires an Indonesian employer and work permit, which is not relevant for remote workers employed abroad.


The E33G is technically classified as a KITAS variant, specifically the E33G Remote Worker KITAS. This is the relevant version for digital nomads.


### **E33G Remote Worker Visa**


The only visa in Indonesia's system that explicitly authorises remote work for a foreign employer while residing in Indonesia. Introduced in 2024, it is valid for 12 months, allows multiple entries, and does not require an Indonesian sponsor.


## Indonesia E33G digital nomad visa: requirements


Confirm the current requirements from Indonesia's Directorate General of Immigration at[imigrasi.go.id](http://imigrasi.go.id/) before applying, as they are subject to change.


- **Employment type:** You must be employed by a company registered outside Indonesia. The E33G is designed for remote employees, not freelancers. Freelancers with clients outside Indonesia sit in a grey area; consult a licensed Indonesian immigration agent before applying if your income is freelance-based.
- **Minimum annual income:** $60,000 per year, or the equivalent in foreign currency, from a foreign employer or clients
- **Bank account balance:** Minimum $2,000 USD in a bank account for three consecutive months prior to application
- **Passport validity:** At least 18 months of remaining validity and three blank pages.
- **Employment contract:** A valid employment contract with a non-Indonesian company confirming a remote work arrangement.
- **Health insurance:** Valid health insurance covering Indonesia
- **Criminal record:** Clean background check
- **Restricted nationalities:** Citizens of certain countries are not eligible; verify the current exclusion list on the official Indonesian immigration website


The E33G does not permit any income-generating work for Indonesian companies or clients. Even consulting for a local business, accepting payment from an Indonesian entity, or providing services to someone who pays you from Indonesia violates the visa terms.


## How to apply for the E33G visa: step by step


1. **Confirm eligibility.** Verify your income meets the $60,000 annual threshold, your employer is registered outside Indonesia, and your nationality is not on the restricted list. If you are self-employed or freelance, consult an immigration agent before proceeding.
2. **Gather your documents.** Passport (all pages), employment contract confirming remote work with a non-Indonesian company, three to six months of bank statements demonstrating consistent income above the threshold, health insurance certificate covering Indonesia, and a criminal background check from your home country.
3. **Choose your application route.** Applications can be submitted at an Indonesian embassy or consulate in your home country. Some applicants use licensed Indonesian immigration agents who handle the KITAS processing on arrival. The official fee at the consulate is IDR 7,000,000, approximately $430 USD. Total costs, including KITAS card processing and agent fees, typically run from $600 to $1,000.
4. **Submit the application and pay the fee.** Fees are non-refundable regardless of outcome as of May 2025 enforcement changes.
5. **Wait for processing.** Processing typically takes 7 to 14 working days. Your application may be approved or may require additional documents.
6. **Enter Indonesia and activate the permit.** Enter through an approved port of entry. The E33G sticker is placed in your passport. Complete KITAS card processing at the immigration office in your area of residence in Indonesia, typically within 14 days of arrival. Note: you must enter Indonesia within 90 days of the visa issuance date, or the visa will become void.

## Costs, practical realities and managing money in Bali


- **Visa cost:** IDR 7,000,000 (~$430 USD) official fee, plus an additional $200 to $600 for agent and KITAS card processing. Total first-year cost: $600 to $1,000 depending on how you apply.
- **Cost of living in Bali:** Monthly costs vary significantly by lifestyle and area. A realistic mid-range budget covers $400 to $700 for a one-bedroom villa, $200 to $400 for food (a mix of local warungs and occasional restaurants), $100 to $200 for a coworking membership, and $50 to $100 for transport. A comfortable monthly budget runs $1,000 to $2,000, considerably less than most Western cities.
- **Tax position:** Staying in Indonesia for more than 183 days in a calendar year triggers Indonesian tax residency under domestic law, which makes worldwide income potentially taxable in Indonesia. Foreign-sourced income remitted to Indonesia may also be subject to tax depending on the bilateral tax treaty between Indonesia and your home country. Many E33G holders plan their annual stay to remain below 183 days in a given calendar year to reduce this exposure. The interaction between home country tax obligations and Indonesian tax residency is complex. Consult a cross-border tax adviser before making long-term plans.
- **Internet:** Bali's internet infrastructure is adequate for most remote work in major nomad hubs including Canggu, Seminyak, Ubud, and Sanur. Coworking spaces reliably offer 50 to 100 Mbps. Residential connections are more variable. Most nomads use a combination of home internet and coworking access.

## Frequently asked questions


### **Can I work remotely in Bali on a tourist visa?**


Legally, no. The Tourist Visa on Arrival does not authorise remote work for a foreign employer. Enforcement of this rule increased significantly in 2025 and 2026, with Bali immigration conducting active operations in popular nomad areas, including Canggu and Seminyak, and using social media monitoring and LinkedIn activity as evidence of enforcement. The risk of working on a tourist visa in Bali is meaningfully higher in 2026 than it was in the past.


### **What is the Indonesia E33G digital nomad visa?**


The E33G Remote Worker Visa is a temporary stay permit (KITAS) launched by Indonesia in 2024. It is the only visa in Indonesia's system that explicitly authorises remote work for a foreign employer while residing in Indonesia. It is valid for 12 months, allows multiple entries, and requires a minimum annual income of $60,000 from non-Indonesian sources. It does not permit work for Indonesian companies or clients.


### **How much does it cost to live in Bali as a digital nomad?**


A comfortable mid-range monthly budget runs $1,000 to $2,000 depending on accommodation choice, lifestyle, and how much you eat out at tourist-priced restaurants versus local warungs. Accommodation is the largest variable: a basic room in a guesthouse costs $300 to $500 per month, while a private villa with pool access in Canggu or Seminyak costs $700 to $2,000 depending on size and season.


### **Do I pay tax in Indonesia if I work remotely in Bali?**


Staying in Indonesia for more than 183 days in a calendar year triggers Indonesian tax residency, which makes worldwide income potentially subject to Indonesian tax. Foreign-sourced income that is not remitted to Indonesia is generally not taxed under most circumstances, but the rules depend on the specific bilateral tax treaty between Indonesia and your home country. Consult a qualified tax professional before planning an extended stay.


### **How long can I stay in Bali as a digital nomad?**


For a Tourist Visa on Arrival, the maximum stay is 60 days (a 30-day initial visa plus one 30-day extension). On a B211A Social Visa, up to 180 days total across extensions. On the E33G Remote Worker Visa, 12 months with multiple entries. After 12 months on the E33G visa, the visa is non-extendable, and the holder must leave Indonesia and reapply from outside Indonesia.


### **Can I get internet fast enough to work remotely in Bali?**


Yes, in the major nomad hubs. Coworking spaces in Canggu, Seminyak, Ubud, and Sanur consistently offer 50-100 Mbps connections with backup options. Residential internet is more variable, and older properties in less-developed areas may have slower or less reliable connections. For work requiring stable video calls and cloud uploads, coworking is the most reliable option. Mobile data from Telkomsel or XL Axiata provides a usable backup connection.
