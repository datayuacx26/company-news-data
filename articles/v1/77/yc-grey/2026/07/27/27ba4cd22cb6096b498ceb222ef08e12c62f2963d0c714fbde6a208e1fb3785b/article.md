# Freelance Writing Rates: Per Word and Per Article | Grey

One of the biggest questions new freelancers ask is, "How much should I charge?" Some end up checking what rates are more popular on freelancing platforms or content mills. But like any professional service, freelance writing isn't priced at random. Your rates should reflect your experience, the complexity of the work, the expertise required, and the value you bring to the client.


Freelance writing rates vary widely by niche, experience, and format. Beginners typically charge $0.05 to $0.20 per word or $50 to $150 per article. Mid-level writers charge $0.25 to $0.80 per word or $200 to $800 per article. Specialised writers in finance, SaaS, or healthcare charge $1 to $3 per word or $1,000 to $5,000 per long-form piece. A beginner writing a 500-word wellness blog article shouldn't charge the same as an experienced healthcare writer producing a research-backed white paper or a SaaS specialist writing a high-converting landing page


Professional clients are looking to pay for your expertise, reliability, and results. Understanding what writers in your niche and at your experience level are actually earning is the first step in setting rates that reflect the quality of your work. At the end of this article, you should understand how to arrive at the right pricing for your freelance writing, what pricing structure to opt for, and how to scale your pricing.


## How much to charge for freelance writing


There is no single "standard" freelance writing rate. It is not one-size-fits-all. What you can charge depends on four main factors:


- **Experience level:** Writers with a proven track record, a strong portfolio, and testimonials can charge significantly more than beginners. Clients often pay for reliability as much as for writing skills. An experienced writer usually needs fewer revisions, understands SEO and content strategy, and can produce publish-ready work more efficiently.
- **Your niche:** Some industries require specialised knowledge, which commands higher rates. Writing a travel blog or lifestyle article generally pays less than writing about finance, healthcare, cybersecurity, SaaS, or law, where accuracy, research, and subject expertise are essential. The harder it is to replace your knowledge, the more you can charge.
- **The type of content:** Not all writing requires the same amount of effort. A 1,000-word blog post is very different from a white paper, case study, technical guide, sales page, or email campaign. Content that involves interviews, original research, data analysis, or strategic thinking typically commands higher fees than straightforward blog articles.
- **The type of client:** Who you're writing for often has as much impact on pricing as what you're writing. Content mills and small businesses usually have tighter budgets. In contrast, SaaS companies, agencies, funded startups, and enterprise businesses often pay considerably more because they view content as a revenue-generating investment rather than just another expense. Long-term clients may also offer more stable work, allowing you to negotiate higher overall earnings through retainers or ongoing contracts.


Freelance writers generally use one of three pricing models:


- **Per word:** The most common pricing model for blog posts and articles. It's easy for clients to understand and straightforward to calculate, though it rewards volume more than quality.
- **Per article:** A fixed fee regardless of the final word count. This model allows experienced writers to price based on expertise, research, and value instead of the number of words written.
- **Per hour:** Less common for content writing but useful for editing, content strategy, consulting, research, and other work where the final deliverable is difficult to define in advance.

## How to price a freelance article


Experienced writers get paid more when they price per article instead of charging per word because they get paid for their expertise over the length of the article. Pricing per article allows you to price based on the time you are likely going to spend on research and your expertise. A fixed per-word pricing does not understand the difference between writing 1,000 words on “How artificial intelligence is changing everyday smartphone use” and writing 1,000 words on “Optimising deep neural networks using stochastic gradient descent.”

## When to charge hourly vs per word vs per project


The best pricing model depends on the type of work you're doing, how clearly the project is defined, and how much control you have over the scope. As you gain experience, you'll likely use a mix of all three rather than sticking to one method.


- **Charge per word** for a standard blog post, article, or website content with a clearly agreed-upon word count. This model is easy for clients to understand and makes it straightforward to compare quotes. It works particularly well for SEO content, news articles, and regular blog writing, where the amount of writing closely reflects the amount of work involved. The downside is that it rewards volume rather than efficiency. Two writers may produce equally valuable articles, but the faster, more experienced writer earns less if they simply write fewer words.
- **Charge per article or per project** when the scope is clearly defined, and the value of the work extends beyond word count. This is the preferred model for experienced freelancers because clients pay for the finished result rather than the number of words on the page. Project pricing works especially well for:


- Long-form guides
- White papers
- Case studies
- Landing pages
- Email sequences
- Website copy
- Thought leadership articles


If the work entails in-depth research, opt for project-based pricing rather than per-word rates. A 1,500-word article may take two hours or ten hours, depending on the research required. Charging a fixed project fee allows you to account for planning, interviews, revisions, and expertise instead of billing only for the final word count.
- **Charge hourly** when the scope is difficult to define in advance, or the work involves ongoing collaboration rather than producing a single deliverable. Editing, content strategy, consulting, training, content audits, and meetings are all good candidates for hourly pricing because the amount of work can change throughout the engagement. Experienced editors, consultants, and content strategists commonly charge between $75 and $200 per hour, with specialist consultants often charging even more.

## How to raise your freelance writing rates


Virtually every freelance writer has been in this position, believing they are charging below their worth. Interestingly, most freelance writers wait too long before increasing their prices. As your experience grows, your work becomes faster, your writing improves, and clients need less editing or guidance. Your rates should reflect improvements in quality and value. The primary reason for this hesitation is the fear of losing existing clients without being able to replace them with higher-paying ones.


While it is not a strict rule, it is a good idea to review your pricing every 6 to 12 months. Another sign you might be due for a pricing update is when your schedule is consistently full, and you start turning down gigs.


Here is a guide on how you can raise your freelance writing rates.


### Raise rates for new clients first


The easiest place to introduce higher rates is with new clients. There is no need to announce the change publicly or explain why your prices have increased. Simply quote your current rate as your standard pricing. If prospective clients continue accepting your proposals without hesitation, your rates may still be below what the market is willing to pay.


### Give existing clients advance notice


Existing clients deserve time to adjust their budgets. Giving 30 to 60 days' notice is good enough and helps preserve long-term relationships.


A simple message is enough:


> I wanted to let you know that, effective \[date\], my rate for \[service\] will be \[new rate\]. I'm sharing this in advance so you have plenty of time to plan accordingly. Thank you for your continued trust and support. I've really enjoyed working together and hope we can continue our collaboration.


Avoid lengthy explanations or apologising for the price change. Professional clients understand that rates increase over time.


### Raise rates because your value has increased


Higher prices are easiest to justify when you've developed new expertise or can demonstrate stronger results. Examples include:


- Building a portfolio in a specialist niche like finance or healthcare
- Learning SEO, conversion copywriting, or content strategy
- Consistently producing content that ranks well or generates leads
- Working with recognised brands or publications
- Becoming more efficient while maintaining quality


The more valuable the outcomes you create, the less your clients focus on the writing itself and the more they focus on the business results.


### Know when to walk away


Not every client will accept higher rates, and that's okay. If a long-term client genuinely cannot accommodate the increase, you can agree on a short transition period while you replace the income.


However, keeping your rates artificially low for years often prevents your business from growing. Replacing one low-paying client with a higher-paying one usually creates more room for better opportunities and a healthier workload. And like we commonly say in freelancing, the well-paying client is less likely to stress you as much as the low-paying client.

## How to receive payments as an international freelance writer


Most of the highest-paying content writing clients are US or UK companies. If you are based outside those countries, how you pay your invoice affects how much you actually receive.


When a US content marketing agency pays a $1,500 article fee via international wire to a bank account in Nigeria, Kenya, or India, correspondent banks in the wire's routing chain typically deduct $15 to $30 in handling fees. The receiving bank converts the remaining USD to local currency at its internal rate, usually 2% to 5% below mid-market. The writer might receive about $1,300 to $1,400 from a $1,500 payment.


If that same client pays you via ACH to a US bank account, the full $1,500 arrives without any intermediary bank deductions. You can then convert the money when the exchange rate works in your favour.

## Frequently asked questions


### **What is a good rate for a beginner freelance writer?**


A reasonable starting point is $0.10–$0.20 per word or $100–$200 per article for general content writing. If you're entering a specialised niche, start at the lower end of that niche's typical rates and increase your prices as your portfolio and client reviews grow.


### **Do high-volume clients pay less per word?**


Often, yes. Clients ordering large amounts of content each month may negotiate lower rates in exchange for steady work. Some writers prefer consistency, while others choose to work with fewer clients at higher rates. The right choice depends on your income goals and workload preferences.


### **Should I charge for research time?**


Yes. Research is part of the work and should be included in your pricing. If an article takes three hours to research and two hours to write, price it as a five-hour project. For per-word pricing, make sure your rate reflects the level of research required.


### **Is $100 per article a good rate?**


For a standard 1,000-word blog post, $100 is a reasonable beginner rate, but it shouldn't be your long-term target. As you gain experience, improve your portfolio, and specialise in higher-value niches, aim to increase your rates well beyond this level.


### **How do I justify higher writing rates to clients?**


Focus on the value your writing creates rather than the time it takes to produce it. Content that attracts search traffic, generates leads, improves conversions, or supports sales is worth far more than the number of hours spent writing it. Use examples and measurable results whenever possible.
