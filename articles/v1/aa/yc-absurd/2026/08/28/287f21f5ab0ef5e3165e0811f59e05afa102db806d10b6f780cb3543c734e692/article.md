# Why Membership Organisations Love Umbraco

Membership organisations have some of the most complicated digital ecosystems around. Perhaps that's exactly why Umbraco has become so popular with them.


At first glance, a membership website doesn't sound particularly complicated.


You need some content. A way to join. Somewhere for existing members to log in. Perhaps some events, news and resources.


Then you look under the bonnet.


There's an Association Management System or CRM containing the member record. An events platform. Email marketing. Payments. Learning and CPD. Gated content. Member directories. Authentication. Analytics. Personalisation. Sometimes ecommerce. Often several decades of content.


And somehow, to the member, all of this is supposed to feel like one coherent organisation.


That's where things get difficult.


It's also one of the reasons Umbraco has quietly become such a dominant platform in the membership sector.


According to the MemberWise Digital Excellence Report, Umbraco has been the most popular CMS for membership organisations and associations for five consecutive years.


That's an interesting level of concentration for a sector with some very specific digital requirements.


So why does it work so well?


## The CMS isn't the centre of the universe


This is probably the most important point.


For most membership organisations, the CMS shouldn't be the system that does everything.


Your CRM or AMS should probably remain responsible for the member record.


Your LMS should probably remain responsible for learning.


Your event platform should probably remain responsible for events.


Your payment provider should probably process payments.


And your CMS should be very good at orchestrating those capabilities into an experience that makes sense to a member.


Umbraco fits that model particularly well.


It's API-friendly and deliberately designed to integrate with other systems. Umbraco's own integration ecosystem covers CRM, analytics, email marketing, automation, payments and other services, while its composable architecture is intended to let organisations connect the technologies they actually need rather than buying everything from a single vendor.


That matters in membership.


Because replacing your website shouldn't require replacing your entire organisation.


## Membership organisations already have technology


One of the traps in a digital transformation programme is assuming you're starting with a blank sheet of paper.


You aren't.


A membership organisation might have spent years implementing Microsoft Dynamics, Salesforce, iMIS or another membership platform. It might have a specialist events system that works perfectly well. Its learning platform might contain years of CPD records.


There will be processes built around those systems, teams trained to use them and data that absolutely cannot disappear because somebody has decided it's time for a new website.


The question isn't usually:


What platform can replace all of this?


It's:


How do we make all of this feel like one experience?


Those are very different problems.


Umbraco's composable approach means you can leave specialist systems doing the jobs they're good at while creating a much better experience over the top.


That makes gradual transformation possible too.


You don't have to solve the entire technology estate in one enormous programme. Individual systems can be integrated, improved or eventually replaced without rebuilding the entire digital experience every time something changes.


For organisations with complicated estates and limited appetite for another five-year transformation programme, that's significant.


## A member doesn't care about your technology stack


This sounds obvious, but plenty of digital experiences suggest otherwise. A member doesn't know that events are managed by one department and professional development by another. They don't care that their membership record lives in the CRM while their learning history lives in the LMS and they don't know that the website, event platform and member portal have different suppliers. They just know they're a member.


Yet organisational and technological boundaries have a habit of appearing in the experience.


Different logins.


Different navigation.


Different designs.


Information you already gave the organisation being requested again.


A website that knows you're a member followed by an event booking system that apparently doesn't.


The technical architecture might make perfect sense internally while making absolutely no sense to the person using it.


The role of the digital experience is to hide that complexity, and that's where a flexible CMS becomes particularly valuable. Umbraco can act as the experience layer across multiple underlying services, bringing content and data together without pretending they all need to live in the CMS.


## The website has two very different jobs


Membership websites also have an unusual tension built into them. They need to convince people to become members, and they need to continually convince existing members that membership is worth keeping. Those are completely different jobs.


For a prospective member, the experience is closer to ecommerce or lead generation.


Why should I join?


What do I get?


Is this relevant to me?


What does it cost?


For an existing member, the questions change.


What's happening in my industry?


What events can I attend?


Where are my resources?


What's the status of my CPD?


When does my membership renew?


The same digital platform needs to support acquisition, onboarding, engagement and retention. That means the idea of one generic "website experience" starts to break down fairly quickly. Umbraco gives organisations the flexibility to create different journeys around those needs, while still managing the underlying content centrally.


And increasingly, that opens the door to much more personalised experiences.


## Membership should change the experience


Logging into a membership website shouldn't simply reveal an extra item in the navigation called "Member Area".


The fact that the organisation knows who you are should fundamentally change the experience; a graduate member shouldn't necessarily see the same thing as someone who has been in the profession for 30 years, and someone who has already booked an event doesn't need to keep seeing advertising for it.


A member halfway through a qualification might need different content from someone who has completed it; someone approaching renewal has a very different relationship with the organisation from somebody who joined yesterday.


Once Umbraco is connected to the systems holding that information, those signals can begin shaping the digital experience.


Content can become more relevant, journeys can become shorter, events can be recommended, resources can be surfaced, renewal messaging can appear at the right time, and the website starts behaving less like a publishing platform and more like a membership service.


Umbraco itself is increasingly investing in this area, with personalisation and engagement capabilities designed to use first-party data to tailor digital experiences.


## Your content team still has to use it


There is another reason Umbraco works particularly well in this sector that is considerably less exciting, but just as important - people actually have to use the CMS.


Membership organisations can produce an enormous amount of content.


News.


Policy updates.


Guidance.


Research.


Events.


Training.


Resources.


Campaigns.


Consultations.


Industry updates.


If publishing all of that requires a developer, the technology has failed.


Umbraco has always put a lot of emphasis on the editor experience. Content structures, reusable components, permissions and workflows can be designed around how an organisation actually operates rather than forcing editors into a rigid publishing model. That's important when content is being produced by multiple teams, subject-matter experts and sometimes regional or specialist groups.


The sophisticated bit should happen underneath and publishing a page shouldn't feel sophisticated at all.


## It doesn't force you into someone else's idea of a membership organisation


This might ultimately be Umbraco's biggest advantage.


No two membership organisations really work in the same way; one might revolve around professional qualifications and CPD, another around events and networking, another around lobbying and representation, another around research, standards or accreditation, and another might do all of those things.


The temptation with large digital platforms is to buy an enormous suite and gradually reshape the organisation around the way the software thinks it should operate.


Umbraco takes almost the opposite approach.


The CMS provides the foundation, but the experience can be designed around the organisation. That flexibility is one of the reasons Umbraco is used across organisations including the British Medical Association, the Institution of Engineering and Technology, the Chartered Institute of Marketing and The Law Society of England and Wales.


These aren't identical organisations running identical membership models.


That's the point.


## Open source matters more than it sounds


There is a commercial argument here too.


Membership organisations tend to think in decades rather than funding rounds.


Technology choices have a habit of sticking around. That makes vendor lock-in, escalating licence costs and proprietary technology particularly uncomfortable.


Umbraco's core CMS is open source and built on .NET. Organisations aren't locked into a single agency, hosting provider or proprietary development environment.


That doesn't mean running a sophisticated digital platform is free. It obviously isn't.


But there is a meaningful difference between investing money in the digital experience you actually want and spending a large part of the budget simply for permission to use the software underneath it.


For membership organisations where every pound ultimately comes from members, that distinction matters.


## Complexity underneath. Simplicity on top.


Perhaps the reason Umbraco works so well for membership organisations is that the philosophy of the platform matches the problem.


Membership organisations are complicated.


Their technology estates are complicated.


Their audiences are complicated.


Their content is complicated.


Their relationships with members can last for decades.


Trying to solve that complexity by putting everything into one enormous system rarely makes it disappear. It just moves the complexity somewhere else.


A better approach is to let specialist systems do what they're good at, connect them properly and put a coherent experience over the top. That's what Umbraco is particularly good at.


The member shouldn't need to understand your CRM, LMS, AMS, CMS or events platform; they shouldn't really need to know they exist. They should just feel like the organisation knows who they are, and perhaps that's the simplest explanation for why membership organisations love Umbraco.


It lets the technology underneath be complicated, without forcing the member experience to be.
