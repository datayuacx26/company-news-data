# Sustainable Inventory Management: Why Profit and the Planet Converge at the Forecast

By Elie Dufeu, CTO & Co-Founder, Metreecs. Published 1 July 2026.


Sustainable inventory management means forecasting and buying at the product level so retailers order closer to real demand, cutting both overstock waste and the markdowns that erode margin. It is not a tradeoff between profit and the planet. It is what happens when the forecast stops being wrong by a wide margin.


Here's an uncomfortable fact for anyone still buying from last year's numbers plus a gut-check: as of 19 July 2026, large EU retailers can no longer legally destroy unsold clothing, footwear, and accessories. The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation just turned a sustainability talking point into a compliance deadline. Every buyer who over-ordered a style this spring now owns that inventory in a way they didn't three months ago.


This article is not another lecture on why sustainability matters. You already know that. What you need is a retail sustainability strategy grounded in a mechanism, not a mission statement: how better forecasting reduces the volume of stock that gets discounted, warehoused, or destroyed in the first place, and what that looks like in your buying cycle this season.


### Key Takeaways


- Forecasting closer to the product and location level directly reduces the volume of stock bought speculatively and later marked down or scrapped
- The EU's ESPR destruction ban takes effect 19 July 2026 for large companies, covering unsold apparel, footwear, and clothing accessories
- An estimated 4-9% of unsold textiles in Europe are destroyed before ever being worn, generating roughly 5.6 million tonnes of CO2 a year, comparable to Sweden's total net emissions in 2021 (European Commission)
- Better replenishment cadence reduces reliance on emergency freight, which lowers both stockout risk and the environmental cost of expedited shipping
- Lower days inventory outstanding is both a margin signal and a waste signal: it tracks how much manufactured stock is sitting unsold at any given time


## What does sustainable inventory management actually mean?


Sustainable inventory management is the practice of forecasting and replenishing stock at a granularity fine enough that retailers buy close to actual demand, rather than buying broad and correcting later with discounts or write-offs. The environmental benefit is a byproduct of forecasting accuracy, not a separate initiative bolted on top. Less overbuying means less fabric produced, less freight burned on corrections, and less unsold stock heading to a warehouse, an outlet, or a landfill.


Most retailers still treat sustainability and profitability as competing budget lines: one team optimizes margin, another writes the corporate responsibility report. That split is the actual problem. The two goals converge at exactly one point: the forecast.

The planning vocabulary here matters. Days of inventory on hand (DIO), sell-through rate, and safety stock are usually framed as pure margin metrics. Each one is also a waste metric in disguise.


A lower DIO means less stock sitting in a warehouse waiting for a decision. A higher sell-through rate means fewer units ever reach the markdown rack. Retailers who track these numbers for margin reasons are already tracking their environmental exposure, whether they label it that way or not.


## Why "profit versus planet" is the wrong frame for inventory decisions


A pattern that shows up repeatedly across footwear and apparel buying teams: an order gets placed for a new style based on a category-level forecast that looks reasonable at the time. That single buying decision costs real margin. It also means units of manufactured product were shipped, warehoused, and discounted for no commercial reason. Nobody chose that outcome. It emerged from a forecast that treated an entire store network as one undifferentiated market.

## The three real costs of overstock: margin, waste, and now, regulation


Overstock used to be framed as a margin problem alone. Without sustainable inventory management practices in place, it is now three problems stacked on top of each other.


**Margin.** Every unit bought above real demand gets sold at a discount or not at all. Markdown rates compound across a network: a significant overbuy on a single product rarely stays contained to one store.


**Waste.** Deadstock, inventory that never sells at any markdown level, has to go somewhere. Some gets liquidated, some gets returned to a distribution center at a cost, and industry estimates suggest that in Europe, 4-9% of unsold textiles are destroyed outright before ever being worn, generating close to[5.6 million tonnes of CO2 annually](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/new-eu-rules-stop-destruction-unsold-clothes-and-shoes-2026-02-09_en) .


**Regulation.** The EU's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation removes the destruction option entirely for large companies starting 19 July 2026. A buyer who overstocks a product no longer has a quiet way to make it disappear. It has to be resold, donated, or recycled, all of which cost more than not overbuying it in the first place.


These three costs used to sit in different departments. They now sit on the same line of the same buying decision.


Benchmarks vary by vertical, but the pattern holds broadly. Fashion retailers running weekly, category-level replenishment cycles typically carry higher DIO and higher markdown rates than teams forecasting daily at the product level. Jewelry and accessories retailers face a different version of the same problem: fewer units per product, but each one represents a larger share of margin if it ends up unsold. The lever is the same regardless of vertical: tighter forecasting granularity, not a bigger clearance budget.


## How AI demand forecasting cuts overstock and its environmental footprint at the source


The fix is not a better clearance strategy. It is a forecast that doesn't generate the excess in the first place.

## Five practical steps to align profitability and sustainability this season


You don't need a new platform to start practicing sustainable inventory management. These five moves work with what you already have and compound once you add product-level forecasting.


1. Pull your markdown-to-waste ratio. For the last full season, calculate what percentage of markdown units eventually went to liquidation, donation, or disposal rather than selling at any price. This number is your baseline; most mid-market retailers have never calculated it.
2. Segment safety stock by demand variability, not by category. A high-variance product and a stable replenishment item should never carry the same buffer rule. Applying one rule to both creates overstock on the stable item and stockout risk on the volatile one, at the same time.
3. Shorten your riskiest replenishment cycles first. Identify the two or three products with the highest markdown history and pilot a daily reorder trigger for them before rolling it out network-wide.
4. Audit your open-to-buy against last season's actual sell-through, not last season's plan. Buying plans built on the original forecast repeat the original error. Buying plans built on what actually sold correct it.
5. Model your ESPR exposure now, not in Q3. If your business meets the EU's large-company threshold, map which products are likely to end this season unsold and unsellable through normal channels. Under the new rule, that stock needs a resale, donation, or recycling path arranged in advance, not improvised after the destruction ban takes effect.


## Where circular economy channels fit once forecasting improves


Even with product-level forecasting, some unsold stock is unavoidable. What matters now is what happens to it next.


Before ESPR, unsold stock had a quiet backstop: destruction. A brand could write off a batch of last season's stock without much operational disruption. That backstop disappears for large companies on 19 July 2026, which means every unit forecasting does not prevent now needs an actual second life, not a disposal shortcut.


A pattern that shows up when teams first map end-of-season stock in detail: a meaningful share of a seasonal line turns out to have been neither sold nor formally accounted for, sitting in a third-party warehouse for an extended period with no resale or recycling plan attached. Setting up a resale partnership typically takes a matter of weeks and costs a fraction of the warehousing fees that stock would otherwise keep accumulating.


That is the practical shape of circular inventory management: not a marketing claim, but a defined channel, resale, donation, or recycling, that unsold stock moves into automatically instead of sitting in limbo. Retailers who pair better forecasting with a pre-arranged circular channel cover both sides of the ESPR requirement.


Forecasting reduces the volume of stock generated. The circular channel handles what forecasting cannot prevent. After 19 July 2026, EU retailers no longer have the option to skip either one.


## What sustainable inventory management doesn't solve


It would be dishonest to promise that better forecasting eliminates overstock entirely. It doesn't. Fashion demand is genuinely volatile, trend cycles shift mid-season, and even a highly accurate forecast leaves a margin of error that shows up as some unsold stock every season.


What changes is the scale of the problem. A footwear brand that used to end each season with a high share of units unsold at full price can see that fall meaningfully with product-level forecasting in place, not to zero. The remaining unsold stock still needs a resale or donation channel, especially once destruction stops being an option under ESPR.


There's also a real adoption cost. Moving from category-level Excel planning to product-level AI forecasting takes weeks of integration, not minutes, and it asks planners to trust a model before they've seen a full season of results from it. Retailers who get the most value are the ones who pilot on their highest-volatility products first and expand from there, rather than trying to convert the entire catalog on day one.


### FAQ


**How does inventory management affect a company's environmental impact?**
Every unit ordered above actual demand eventually becomes markdown stock, warehouse overflow, or in the worst case, destroyed inventory. Reducing the gap between forecasted and actual demand reduces overproduction at the source, which is a more direct environmental lever than most sustainability initiatives further down the supply chain.


**Can retailers actually reduce waste without hurting profitability?**
Yes, because the two are driven by the same variable: forecast accuracy. Retailers using product-level demand forecasting reduce both overstock (a margin problem) and the disposal volume that overstock generates (an environmental problem) with the same investment, not two separate ones.


**What is the EU's ban on destroying unsold clothing?**
The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR) prohibits large companies operating in the EU from destroying unsold apparel, footwear, and clothing accessories, effective 19 July 2026. Retailers meeting the size threshold must now resell, donate, or recycle unsold stock instead.


**Does AI demand forecasting really reduce overstock, or is it marketing?**
Forecast accuracy is measurable and auditable against actual sell-through, which is what makes it a credible lever rather than a marketing claim: retailers can track forecast accuracy and inventory on hand against their own baseline performance before and after a change in approach.


**How do I start if my team is still planning in Excel?**
Start with your highest-volatility, highest-markdown products rather than the full catalog. Pull last season's markdown-to-waste ratio, segment safety stock by demand variability, and pilot a shorter replenishment cycle on two or three products before expanding further.


**What is circular inventory management, and is it different from sustainable inventory management?**
Circular inventory management covers what happens to stock after it goes unsold: resale, donation, or recycling instead of disposal. Sustainable inventory management is the broader practice of forecasting accurately enough that less stock reaches that point in the first place. The two work together; forecasting reduces the volume, and circular channels handle what remains.
