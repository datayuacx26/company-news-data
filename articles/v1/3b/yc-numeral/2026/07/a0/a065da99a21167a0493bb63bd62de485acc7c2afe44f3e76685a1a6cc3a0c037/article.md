## Remitting sales tax: who remits sales tax in the us?


The responsibility for charging, collecting, reporting, and remitting sales tax in a sales transaction falls primarily on the seller.


While customers do owe sales tax on most purchases, the seller serves as an agent of the state in gathering this revenue.


Failure to collect tax from buyers does not relieve the seller from owing tax to the state.


### Role of the seller


Sellers must obtain a sales tax permit or license in most states and register with revenue departments wherever they have sufficient physical or economic presence (known as nexus).


With a permit, the seller can legally collect sales tax from customers and is responsible for correctly calculating amounts due on invoices.


Beyond collecting sales tax, the seller must file regular returns and remit all tax revenue to appropriate agencies. Returns are typically submitted monthly, quarterly, or annually, depending on the state and volume of taxable sales.


> Returns report total sales, exempt sales, taxable sales, and the sales tax due. Payment must be remitted along with the return by the due date to avoid penalties and interest. Overall, the seller bears the burden of adequately imposing, collecting, tracking, reporting, and paying sales tax in the jurisdictions where sales occur.


### Role of the customer


Customers are responsible for paying the advertised amount, including any sales tax due at the point of sale. Any issues with tax charges must be addressed directly with the seller.


Customers who qualify for a sales tax exemption must also provide applicable exemption certificates to sellers as proof that sales tax should not be collected.


These certificates indicate how the goods or services will be used and that the customer is tax-exempt.


### Sales tax vs. use tax: what’s the difference?


You may also generally hear two terms: sales tax and use tax. While they seem similar, they are distinct taxes that apply to different transactions.


Sales tax is a percentage tax paid when purchasing tangible products or services depending on the state of sale/purchase. If the seller has a nexus in the state where the sale takes place, they are responsible for collecting sales tax at the time of purchase.


Use tax is a sales tax you pay to use a product or service in a state. Here are some examples:


- Purchases made from out-of-state sellers who don’t collect local sales tax due to lack of nexus
- Items purchased for resale but then used personally
- Taxable goods bought in a state with a lower sales tax rate but brought home for use


Use tax aims to discourage tax evasion from out-of-state sellers and ensure equivalent taxation of goods regardless of origin. The same introductory tax rate applies but is paid directly to the state by the buyer rather than the seller. Retailers are not required to collect use tax.

### When do sales tax and use tax apply, and who collects it?


Whether sales tax or use tax applies depends primarily on whether the seller has a nexus. If the seller has a physical presence or economic nexus in the state where the buyer resides, sales tax applies, and the seller must collect it. Without nexus, the seller has no obligation to collect tax, so the buyer owes use tax instead.


However, larger online sellers may now have to collect sales tax nationwide following the Supreme Court’s[South Dakota v. Wayfair](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/17pdf/17-494_j4el.pdf) ruling.


This established an economic nexus threshold based on sales volume and transactions, which requires more out-of-state retailers to collect. This means sales tax is now charged on more online purchases for consumers.


For businesses, use tax typically comes into play when purchases are made from out-of-state suppliers before reselling items or incorporating them into finished projects. Even with more remote sellers collecting sales tax, use tax liabilities still need to be tracked on exempt inventory later used internally.


## What are the requirements for remitting sales tax?


A business must take steps before legally collecting and remitting sales taxes. Failing to check these boxes off could make you non-compliant.


### Business registered with a seller’s permit


After forming a business, one of the first regulatory tasks is registering for a seller’s permit (sometimes called a sales tax permit, reseller permit, or vendor’s license).


This authorizes your business to collect transaction taxes and is required in most states before applying sales tax. Permits are obtained by submitting a registration application along with any licensing fees to the appropriate state revenue agency.


When granted, the seller will receive a sales tax account number or tax ID used when filing returns. States normally issue permits fairly quickly after verifying a seller’s information. However, if the state cannot verify identity or legal business formation, it may conduct further investigation before approving the permit.


### Physical or economic nexus in the state


The term nexus refers to a seller having sufficient physical or economic transactions in a state or county to be subject to their tax laws and regulations.


Nexus establishes a tax presence obligating the seller to register, collect, file, and remit sales taxes to that jurisdiction.


Historically, nexus was created by physical business locations, employees, inventory, trade shows, or advertising in the area. However, with ecommerce, states lost vast sales tax revenue as online sellers claimed no physical presence.


[South Dakota v. Wayfair](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/17pdf/17-494_j4el.pdf) overturned this precedent by ruling that states can impose taxes based on economic nexus.


Having just one transaction or minor advertising activity likely doesn’t reach nexus. However, exceeding certain sales, transaction, or inventory thresholds can trigger tax obligations without physical presence.

### *What products and services are taxable and exempt*


All but five US states have a statewide sales tax—exceptions are[Alaska](https://www.numeral.com/blog/alaska-sales-tax-guide) ,[Delaware](https://www.numeral.com/blog/delaware-sales-tax-guide) ,[Montana](https://www.numeral.com/blog/montana-sales-tax-guide) ,[New Hampshire](https://www.numeral.com/blog/new-hampshire-sales-tax-guide) , and[Oregon](https://www.numeral.com/blog/oregon-sales-tax-guide) .


But not all goods and services qualify as taxable sales. States typically tax retail sales of tangible personal goods and certain services. General rules of thumb include:


- Physical products are taxable unless specifically exempted
- [Services are generally not taxable](https://www.numeral.com/blog/sales-tax-on-services) unless specifically included
- [Wholesale sales are usually exempt](https://www.numeral.com/blog/wholesale-sales-tax)
- Retail sales are taxable.


However, states differ significantly in what services they tax. For example, only a few states tax professional services, while most impose sales tax on repair and installation services. Other exceptions like groceries, clothing, and prescription medicine also exist.


## The process to remit sales tax


With robust tax collection procedures in place, businesses must tackle the regular process of remitting the sales tax they gather to state authorities. Keeping tax payments timely and reporting fully avoids non-compliance.


### Filing frequency and due dates


Most states require monthly, quarterly, or annual filing cycles depending on seller size and taxable sales volume. Higher sales volumes generally mean more frequent filing is needed. For example, small businesses may only need to file an annual return. However, retailers collecting over $20,000 annually must often file monthly or quarterly returns.


[Due dates vary](https://www.numeral.com/blog/sales-tax-due-dates) but are typically the 20th of the month following the reporting period. For quarterly returns, payment cycles often align with calendar quarters ending March, June, September, and December. Returns and payments for a given quarter are then due a month later.


To avoid interest and penalties, sales tax must be paid by the due date, regardless of when the state receives payment. Have funds withdrawn a few days early or postmark checks on time. Setting calendar reminders for recurring due dates prevents missed filings.


### How to file a sales tax return


Completing and submitting the sales tax return summarizes taxable sales activity and reconciles amounts collected against taxes owed. Required forms, processes for filing, and payment methods vary by state.


Many tax authorities now offer online filing of sales tax returns through revenue department websites or third-party systems. Online filing platforms walk users through the process step-by-step, perform some calculations automatically, and warn about inconsistencies that could trigger an audit.


However, online systems generally don’t support filings across multiple states. Sellers operating in several jurisdictions may have to use separate state systems or rely on spreadsheet templates. This quickly becomes administratively burdensome. Automated sales tax software that handles multi-state filing centrally provides a big advantage.

### Payment methods for remitting sales tax


Acceptable payment methods include checks, money orders, ACH withdrawals, wire transfers, and sometimes credit cards. Start the payment process early enough to ensure the state receives funds by the due date.


If filing online, returns often cannot be submitted until payment is initiated through the website. Mailing paper checks means allowing time for postal delays. Forgetting to sync payment with the return deadline could still result in penalties for lateness.


## Common pitfalls in sales tax remittance


Staying fully compliant with sales tax obligations from registration and collection to filing and payment is no simple task, especially for sellers operating in multiple states. Unfortunately, the complexities involved leave plenty of room for good-faith mistakes and accidental non-compliance.


### Consequences of non-compliance


The penalties for late filings or underpayment can quickly wipe out profits. Consequences may include:


- Assessment of penalties up to 25% of tax due
- Interest charges of 10% or more annually
- Loss of important sales tax exemptions
- Tax liens or levies against the company
- Revocation of sales tax permit
- Mandatory security deposits for future filings
- Tax audit and investigation


In severe cases of repeated non-compliance, owners or executives face potential jail time and dissolution of the business altogether. The risks make compliance an extremely high priority.


### Importance of collecting sales tax exemption certificates


Sales tax exemptions for resale, nonprofit, and[manufacturing](https://www.numeral.com/blog/manufacturing-sales-tax-exemptions) purposes help sellers minimize unnecessary collection. But, failing to charge tax doesn’t negate the seller’s liability if unable to prove valid exemptions during an audit.
