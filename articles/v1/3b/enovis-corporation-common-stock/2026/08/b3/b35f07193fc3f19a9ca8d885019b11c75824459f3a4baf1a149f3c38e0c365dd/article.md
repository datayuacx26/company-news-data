# Summer Falls and Wrist Fractures: How Bracing Can Support Recovery

The summer months bring more time spent outside playing sports and being active. However, with this increase in activity comes an increase in the likelihood of injury, including wrist fractures. Wrist-bracing products exist to prevent and manage wrist fractures, but what evidence exists for their efficacy? Read on as we investigate.


## **What Is a Distal Radius Fracture (DRF)?**


Distal radius fractures (DRFs) occur when the radius, located in the forearm, breaks close to the wrist. DRFs are one of the most common injuries encountered in orthopedic practice, accounting for 8-15% of all bone injuries in adults.1 They are most commonly caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand while the wrist is in dorsiflexion.


The most common candidates for DRF are:


- Children/Adolescents (males): Due to sports participation and partly to a rapidly developing skeletal structure.2
- Young adults (males): Sports participation and car accidents are common causes.2
- Elderly (females): Due to osteoporosis, often caused by ‘reaching out’ to break a fall.2 This may be done purposely by those with good neuromuscular control in an effort to avoid hip or humeral fractures.2


Understanding who is at risk provides important context, but recognizing the specific activities that commonly lead to these fractures can help with both prevention and timely intervention.


## **Summer Activities Most Commonly Associated with Wrist Fractures**


- **Cycling and mountain biking** — slips on steep, wet, or uneven ground can result in a fall onto the hand
- **Skateboarding and roller sports** — frequently involve forward or sideways falls onto the hands
- **Football and basketball** — fractures may occur when a player is knocked off balance, trips while running, collides with another player, or lands awkwardly with the hand extended
- **Hiking, trail running, climbing, and bouldering** — slips on steep, wet, or uneven ground can result in a fall onto the hand
- **Trampolining and outdoor play** — particularly relevant to children and adolescents. Falls from trampolines or climbing equipment commonly produce distal forearm fractures because the child lands with the arm extended


Once a distal radius fracture has occurred, optimal recovery relies on prompt and appropriate treatment.


## **From Immobilization to Rehabilitation: Distal Radius Fracture Recovery**


The goal of distal radius fracture treatment is to restore the mobility and durability of the upper extremity.3


Most patients with non or minimally displaced DRF can be treated non-operatively with short-arm cast (plaster or fiberglass) immobilization alone.4


Traditionally, an immobilization period of 4-6 weeks is chosen. However, due to the risk of secondary fracture displacement being highest in the first 2 weeks after injury, recent literature has challenged this, suggesting that a 3-week immobilization period may be sufficient.4


After plaster removal, patients with distal radius fractures often experience discomfort, especially after prolonged immobilization, with reduced wrist flexibility and grip strength, which may interfere with daily activities. Multimodal, progressive rehabilitation can gradually restore wrist mobility and help patients return to normal life. Supervised rehabilitation under professional guidance may be more effective than self-directed exercises5. The use of assistive devices (e.g., electrical stimulation, whirlpool baths) or smart rehabilitation apps may enhance outcomes.6,7,8


## **How Wrist Bracing Can Support Wrist Fracture Recovery**


While plaster or fiberglass casts are most commonly used for immobilizing stable, non-displaced wrist fractures, this approach carries some negative aspects that can impact recovery.


Neither plaster or fiberglass casts are waterproof, meaning patients must take care during bathing to prevent them getting wet and damaging their structural integrity. Further to this is the propensity for dampness inside the cast and pressure-related complications such as acute carpal tunnel syndrome and skin necrosis.9,10


Orthopedic wrist braces address these issues in different ways depending on their design. Unlike traditional casts, these braces are removable, allowing access to the limb for purposes of examination or hygiene. While many wrist braces are made from a combination of semi-rigid elements and fabric materials, some are constructed from thermoformable materials that allow them to be heated and shaped to fit the forearm for improved comfort. These thermoformable braces are usually also water-resistant, meaning patients can bathe or even swim while wearing them.9,11


The benefits these wrist braces offer can result in higher patient satisfaction in comparison to traditional casting.12 Beyond patient experience, clinical studies have examined whether these braces deliver comparable functional outcomes to traditional immobilization methods.


## **What Does the Clinical Evidence Say About Wrist Braces?**


Recent evidence-based guidelines for distal radius fracture treatment show bracing can be a viable alternative to cast immobilization.13


Sellbrant et al.’s 2021 study compared the quality of recovery between wrist brace and traditional plaster cast immobilization following surgery for DRF. It found that bracing was a feasible option, with the only difference found that patients in the cast group had significantly less pain 24-hours post-surgery.14


Conversely, a 2023 study of 50 patients found no disadvantages to using wrist braces instead of traditional casting. While it found braces to be equal to plaster casts in terms of patient satisfaction, stability, physical function, and quality of life, it found them superior in hygiene, adaptability, and application.15


There is also evidence to suggest the effectiveness of wrist braces in treating children with distal radius fractures. A 2018 study of 40 pediatric patients found that treating DRF with a removable wrist brace without clinical or radiographic follow up resulted in nearly universal good patient outcomes and parent satisfaction. It also noted that no special training is required to use these devices, allowing them to be implemented quickly on a wide scale.16


Regarding thermoformable wrist braces, a study by White et al. (2021) found that they demonstrated markedly less water absorption when compared with traditional plaster and fibercast liners. For clinicians, this may provide cost savings and reduced resources when addressing moisture-related splinting issues for distal radius fracture.9
