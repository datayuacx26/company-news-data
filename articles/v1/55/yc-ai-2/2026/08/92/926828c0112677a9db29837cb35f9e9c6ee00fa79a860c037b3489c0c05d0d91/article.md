An illustrative $99 patent-search quote and a $25,000 freedom-to-operate opinion can both be fair for their stated scopes. They buy different work. The word “search” hides differences in the question, sources, claim analysis, jurisdiction, and attorney review.

## Patent search prices at a glance


Start with the decision the search must support. A patentability search looks for prior art that may affect a proposed invention’s novelty or nonobviousness. An invalidity search looks for prior art against one or more issued claims.


A freedom-to-operate (FTO) search looks for enforceable claims that may cover a planned product or activity. A landscape maps a broader technology field.


The same patent database may appear in all four workflows. The documents selected, date rules, legal-status work, and final deliverable are different.

## The USPTO application search fee is different from a private search


The “patent search fee” on a United States Patent and Trademark Office invoice is a government application search fee paid in connection with filing. It is not a standalone patent-search service price and does not purchase a pre-filing report from a searcher or attorney. The agency uses the fee as part of examining the application after filing.


The current[USPTO fee schedule](https://www.uspto.gov/learning-and-resources/fees-and-payment/uspto-fee-schedule) lists the following domestic application search fees under 37 C.F.R. § 1.16(k) through (m). They apply only to the stated original utility, design or design continuing prosecution application (CPA), and plant categories:


Domestic application category Large entity Small entity Micro entity


Original utility under § 1.16(k) $770 $308 $154


Design or design CPA under § 1.16(l) $300 $120 $60


Plant under § 1.16(m) $485 $194 $97


For an application in one of these § 1.16 categories, the search fee is due in addition to the applicable basic filing fee and examination fee. A provisional application has a filing fee but no separate § 1.16 search or examination fee.


The table does not cover every nonprovisional filing path. Patent Cooperation Treaty national-stage applications, for example, use a separate fee framework under 37 C.F.R. § 1.492. Reissue applications have a separate search-fee category under § 1.16(n). These distinctions explain why a search quote from counsel can appear alongside a second “search fee” on the government-fee estimate without being a duplicate charge.


## What you get at each price tier


### Free DIY search: database access costs nothing, judgment takes time


USPTO Patent Public Search, Google Patents, Espacenet, and the World Intellectual Property Organization’s (WIPO) PATENTSCOPE provide free access to large patent collections. The USPTO’s own[six-step search strategy](https://www.uspto.gov/patents/search/patent-search-strategy) begins with brainstorming terms and running keyword searches. It then calls for reviewing found documents in depth, expanding through relevant Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) classes, reviewing cited and citing references, and broadening the search to foreign patents, non-patent literature, or a patent professional’s search. That is much more work than typing a product name into one search box.


A preliminary DIY search is useful for finding a clear knockout reference and learning the vocabulary used in the field. Its cash price is $0, but its true cost is the time required to build queries, read the specification and claims, group patent families, and change direction as new terminology appears.


It also has unavoidable limits. U.S. prior art can include patents, published applications, printed publications, public use, sales, and other public availability under[35 U.S.C. § 102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/35/102) . Patent-only searching leaves part of that universe untouched.


Most U.S. nonprovisional utility and plant applications are published around 18 months from the earliest filing date for which a benefit is sought, subject to the exceptions in 35 U.S.C. § 122(b)(2). Applications that remain unpublished create an[unpublished-application blind spot](https://www.uspto.gov/web/offices/pac/mpep/s1120.html) that a paid search cannot remove either.


For a claim governed by the first-inventor-to-file provisions of the Leahy-Smith America Invents Act (AIA), a U.S. patent document that publishes or issues later may still qualify as § 102(a)(2) prior art. It must name another inventor, have been effectively filed before that claim’s effective filing date for the relied-on subject matter, and fall outside the § 102(b)(2) exceptions.

### Patentability search cost: $400–$1,400, or $1,500–$2,300 with an opinion


Published search-only examples span $400–$1,400.[Planet Patent’s average-price guide](https://www.planetpatent.com/patent-search-types-prices/) lists $400–$900 for mechanical, electrical, consumer, business-method, electronic, and software novelty searches, and $700–$1,400 for biotech, chemical, genetic, or medical work. These figures are indicative published averages, and Planet Patent provides a firm quote only after reviewing a request.


PRH separately charges a €1,265 base fee for its fixed-price novelty search plus a mandatory €6.50 invoicing charge. Both charges are subject to 25.5% VAT under its[current fee schedule](https://www.prh.fi/en/price-lists/pathakmaks/tutkimuspalvelut_12.html) . Neither source establishes a typical global rate.


Published attorney packages provide another useful anchor. Axenfeld Law lists $1,500–$2,000 for a patent search, detailed patentability opinion, and discussion of the result in its[service fee](https://axenfeldlaw.com/patent-search-patentability-opinion-services/) . Simpson & Simpson lists $2,200 for most inventions and $2,300 for software and business methods in its[fixed-fee example](https://www.idealawyers.com/patent-faqs) .


Those sources do not establish a standard search-only deliverable. In a quote, ask whether the report identifies the closest references, explains their relevance, and states the databases, countries, date range, and non-patent sources covered.


The cited attorney examples establish that their stated fees include an opinion. Axenfeld also includes a discussion of the result and options for moving forward. They do not show that added fees necessarily produce more search hits or any particular number of results.


Search-only patentability research does not require a registered patent practitioner. A patentability opinion or other legal advice about work contemplated before the USPTO should come from a registered patent attorney or patent agent authorized for that work.


The boundary matters.[37 C.F.R. § 11.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/11.5) limits USPTO registration to patent matters before the Office and services reasonably necessary and incident to authorized applications or proceedings. Registration does not make a patent agent a licensed attorney.


FTO, noninfringement, standalone validity, and litigation opinions outside that scope should be handled by an appropriately licensed attorney. Representing another person or entity in court generally requires counsel admitted to that court.


An individual party’s ability to appear pro se depends on applicable[law and court rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1654) . USPTO registration alone does not authorize court representation.


Read the engagement scope closely. “Patentability analysis” on a low-cost automated report is not necessarily a legal opinion. A qualified practitioner’s analysis also remains limited to the art found. It cannot guarantee that the USPTO will allow a claim or that another reference will not surface later.


### Invalidity search: $1,900–$10,000 in published pricing


An invalidity search starts from an issued patent and selected claims. The searcher must identify the governing version of § 102, determine the effective filing date for each challenged claim, trace the relevant priority chains, and map each reference to what it discloses.


For patent- or publication-based anticipation, a single enabling disclosure must disclose every claim limitation arranged as claimed, either expressly or inherently. Other § 102 categories, including public use, on-sale activity, and other public availability, can require different proof. An obviousness theory under § 103 may combine references, which adds another layer of technical and legal analysis.


A publication-date cutoff does not capture every qualifying reference. For a challenged claim governed by the AIA’s first-inventor-to-file provisions, a U.S. patent, U.S. application publication, or qualifying WIPO publication may qualify under § 102(a)(2) even if it issues or publishes after the challenged claim’s effective filing date.


The document must name another inventor, have been effectively filed before the challenged claim’s effective filing date for the relied-on subject matter, and fall outside the § 102(b)(2) exceptions. The USPTO states these rules in[Manual of Patent Examining Procedure (MPEP) § 2154](https://www.uspto.gov/web/offices/pac/mpep/s2154.html) . Search scope should therefore capture publication and issue dates as well as inventive entities, actual filing, benefit, priority, and subject-matter-support data.


Planet Patent publishes a $1,900–$10,000 average range for validity and invalidity searches in its[average-price guide](https://www.planetpatent.com/patent-search-types-prices/) . Treat that as an indicative published average range, not a defined package price. The number of asserted claims, disputed priority issues, technical density, non-patent literature, product or system art, foreign-language material, and need for a claim chart can push a matter beyond it.


A formal invalidity opinion, inter partes review petition, expert declaration, or litigation brief is separate work. Authorized practitioners can handle work within USPTO proceedings. Standalone litigation opinions require appropriately licensed attorneys, while court filings and appearances must follow the applicable court’s admission and pro se rules. USPTO registration alone is insufficient.


The most valuable deliverable is rarely the longest reference list. It is a small set of strong references with dates, provenance, pinpoint citations, limitation mappings, and an auditable search trail. A coverage matrix also exposes gaps before counsel spends time polishing a weak reference.


This is where AI changes the economics. It makes repeated search passes and early mappings cheap enough to run in-house, while attorneys retain control over priority, claim construction, evidentiary support, and the final invalidity theory. Our guide to[AI prior art search tools](https://www.tryandai.com/blog/best-ai-prior-art-search-tools) explains the litigation-specific requirements in more depth.


### FTO search: $1,500–$3,000 for screening, with higher fees for opinions


An FTO search answers a different question from patentability. Patentability asks what qualifying prior art may prevent you from patenting an invention. FTO asks whether making, using, selling, offering to sell, or importing a product in a specified country may fall within another party’s enforceable claims.


Published search-only examples include a $1,500–$3,000 average range for infringement and FTO or clearance searches in[Planet Patent’s guide](https://www.planetpatent.com/patent-search-types-prices/) . PRH charges a €2,670 base fee plus a mandatory €6.50 invoicing charge for its FTO service. Both charges are subject to 25.5% VAT, and PRH shows €3,359 as the total for its[standard scope](https://www.prh.fi/en/intellectualpropertyrights/patentit/theabcofpatenting/servicesanddatabases/searchservices/freedomtooperatereview.html) .


That standard scope includes an initial meeting, about two days of examiner search work, a search report with legal-status information, and copies of the ten most relevant publications. PRH expressly does not take a position on possible infringement. Planet Patent’s figure is an indicative published average, while PRH’s total applies to its defined standard service.


Published figures for higher-scope FTO work describe different purchases. Morse’s[2017 law-firm guidance](https://www.morse.law/news/freedom-to-operate-prevention-is-the-best-medicine/?output=pdf) places a formal FTO opinion at $10,000–$30,000.


ipCapital Group’s[2026 cost analysis](https://www.ipcg.com/answers/how-much-does-a-freedom-to-operate-search-and-opinion-cost) gives $10,000–$50,000 and beyond for a combined engagement spanning the search, analysis, and legal opinion, with the search and opinion treated as separate phases. The first figure is for the formal opinion; the second estimates the end-to-end engagement.


The combined work can include searching, product-feature mapping, live-claim and legal-status analysis, and counsel’s written opinion. Analysts and patent agents can support search and mapping.


When that support contributes to a legal opinion, it should be performed under the supervising licensed counsel responsible for the opinion. A U.S. FTO or noninfringement opinion should be issued by an appropriately licensed attorney authorized to provide that advice.


USPTO registration is a separate credential for practice before the Office and is not always required for an FTO opinion. Qualified local counsel should be involved when other jurisdictions are in scope.


The country and product boundaries must be explicit. Patent rights are territorial, legal status changes, and pending claims can change before issuance. A patent on your own improvement also does not confer permission to commercialize it. As WIPO explains in its[freedom-to-operate guidance](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/ip-and-business-launching-a-new-product-freedom-to-operate-34956) , a broader third-party patent may still cover the activity.


### Patent landscape: $800–$1,200 for a basic search, $5,000–$50,000+ for analysis


A landscape is priced by the size of the field and the decision it needs to support. Planet Patent lists an $800–$1,200 average range for a broad-overview landscape search in its[published guide](https://www.planetpatent.com/patent-search-types-prices/) . That basic output differs from a decision-focused analysis. ipCapital Group says scoped projects commonly run from about $5,000 for a narrow, single-technology review to $50,000 or more for a broad, multi-jurisdiction competitive landscape in its[2026 cost range](https://www.ipcg.com/answers/how-much-does-a-patent-landscape-analysis-cost) .


The higher-priced study may clean thousands of patent families, normalize assignees, read claims, categorize technologies, compare jurisdictions, and draw research and development or competitive conclusions. The two published ranges describe different deliverables rather than a single continuous market rate.


A cheap landscape can still be useful when the question is narrow. It becomes expensive when the buyer expects high recall across a loosely defined field and human interpretation of every relevant family. Ask for the estimated document and family counts, inclusion rules, categorization depth, and update date before comparing landscape quotes.


## Why patent search costs vary


Seven variables explain most price differences:


1. **The question.** A quick novelty screen, a written patentability assessment, an invalidity theory, and an FTO opinion carry different stakes and analysis.
2. **The unit of work.** One invention concept costs less than ten claims or a product with dozens of independently searchable features.
3. **Technical complexity.** Chemical structures, biological sequences, standards, source code, and fast-changing terminology require specialized sources and search methods.
4. **Geographic scope.** More countries add collections, languages, family consolidation, and legal-status review.
5. **Source coverage.** Patents are only one source. Journals, standards, manuals, catalogs, theses, videos, archived web pages, and product evidence can require separate systems and verification.
6. **The deliverable.** A list of publication numbers is cheaper than a ranked report with excerpts, claim mappings, pinpoint citations, search history, and attorney conclusions.
7. **Iteration and urgency.** Extra search passes, newly identified claim constructions, and rush deadlines add cost. AI and in-house tools reduce the marginal cost of iteration most sharply.


Technology labels alone are poor pricing proxies. A tightly scoped software claim can be easier than a deceptively simple mechanical assembly with decades of crowded art. The quote should describe the work, not merely name the field.


## Compare the total cost of using the results


The lowest search invoice can create the highest matter cost. Use this formula:


**Total search cost = external or platform fee + internal review time + follow-on analysis or charting + paid reruns**


Suppose a $500 report returns a largely unranked set that takes a lawyer eight hours to screen. At a hypothetical $450 blended rate, the usable result costs $4,100 before any charting. A $2,000 report that reduces review to two hours costs $2,900 on the same assumptions.


The arithmetic is illustrative, but the buying rule is real. Ask how the output reduces the next person’s work.


For litigation, that usually means limitation-level mappings, pinpoint citations, and direct export into a[review-ready claim chart](https://www.tryandai.com/blog/submission-ready-claim-charts-with-ai) . For prosecution, it means a concise set of close references that counsel can use when deciding whether and how to draft claims. For FTO, it means a defensible shortlist with current legal status and product-feature mappings.


## What every patent search quote should specify


Use the same scope sheet for every bidder. A useful quote answers all of these questions:


- **Question:** Is the job a preliminary novelty check, patentability search, invalidity search, FTO analysis, infringement search, or landscape?
- **Subject:** Which invention disclosure, claims, product versions, features, or technology categories are included?
- **Dates:** Which version of § 102 governs, what effective filing date applies to each challenged claim, and who confirms the challenged and reference priority chains, public-availability dates, § 102(a)(2) effective-filing dates, inventive-entity differences, and applicable exceptions?
- **Jurisdictions:** Which patent offices and commercial markets are included?
- **Sources:** Are foreign patents, non-patent literature, standards, products, public-use evidence, and archived web materials in scope?
- **Methods:** Will the search use keywords, classifications, semantic search, citations, assignees, inventors, images, structures, or sequences?
- **Deliverable:** Will you receive raw references, relevance notes, excerpts, pinpoint citations, claim mappings, family data, legal status, and the query log?
- **Review:** Who performs quality control? Search-only patentability research does not require a registered practitioner. If the deliverable includes a patentability opinion or other legal advice about a USPTO matter, which registered patent attorney or agent is responsible? For an FTO, noninfringement, standalone validity, or court-litigation opinion, which licensed attorney is responsible?
- **Iteration:** How many feedback rounds or reruns are included, and what triggers an added fee?
- **Operations:** What are the turnaround time, confidentiality terms, data-retention terms, and total fixed or capped fee?


If a provider cannot state what its price excludes, the quote is not ready to compare.


## Choose the budget that matches the decision


Spend in stages when the stakes permit it:


1. **Run inexpensive triage.** Use free public databases or an AI search to find obvious prior art and improve the vocabulary of the request.
2. **Pay for focused human review.** Give the searcher or counsel a clear disclosure, known references, claims, dates, and markets. Better inputs prevent paid time from going toward basic scoping.
3. **Escalate only the strongest candidates.** Commission a legal opinion, claim chart, expert work, or expanded jurisdictional search after the first pass shows where risk or value concentrates.
4. **Use a second method for high-stakes matters.** For consequential litigation or product clearance, combine AI breadth and iteration with an independent expert search or counsel review.


This staged model keeps a $50 screening question from turning into a $20,000 engagement too early. It also prevents a $50 report from being mistaken for the answer to a $20,000 legal question.


## Frequently asked questions


### Is a prior art search legally required before filing a patent application?


No. The USPTO’s[MPEP § 410](https://www.uspto.gov/web/offices/pac/mpep/s410.html) states that an applicant has no duty to conduct a prior art search before filing.


The disclosure duty is separate. Under[37 C.F.R. § 1.56](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/1.56) , it applies to each inventor named in the application, each attorney or agent who prepares or prosecutes it, and every other person who is substantively involved in its preparation or prosecution and has the association specified in the rule. The rule does not categorically place that individual duty on applicant organizations. Whether a voluntary search is worth the cost depends on the invention, existing knowledge of the field, filing strategy, and budget.


### Can I do my own patent search?


Yes. Public patent databases are free, and a DIY search is a sensible first screen. Use keywords and synonyms, CPC or International Patent Classification codes, backward and forward citations, foreign collections, and relevant non-patent literature. Treat the result as research, rather than a guarantee of patentability, validity, or freedom to operate.


### Can AI do a patent search?


Yes. AI can search by technical concept, rank references, traverse citations, and map passages or figures to claim limitations. Its useful role is retrieval, triage, and drafting analysis for human review. A low-cost AI report is not automatically a legal opinion, and high-stakes conclusions remain with qualified counsel.


### How long does a patent search take?


Most users of[our Prior Art Search](https://www.tryandai.com/product/prior-art-search) find strong candidate references within 30–60 minutes. That benchmark covers candidate discovery and excludes attorney review or a legal opinion.


For its own novelty-search service, PRH says the search work typically takes one or a couple of working days. Its[published delivery estimates](https://www.prh.fi/en/intellectualpropertyrights/patentit/theabcofpatenting/servicesanddatabases/searchservices/noveltysearch.html) are about two weeks for fixed-price work and three weeks for hourly work.


WIPO’s[2015 guidelines](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/pubdocs/en/wipo_pub_946.pdf) reproduce a historical palm-oil landscape project schedule that assigned four weeks to Phase I, four weeks after comments to Phase II, and two weeks after comments to Phase III. That 2015 example is not a current WIPO service benchmark. Each timing applies only to its stated service, scope, or historical project.


### Does a patent search guarantee that no conflicting patent or prior art exists?


No. Search terminology, classification, source access, translations, unpublished applications, and public evidence outside patent databases all create gaps. A well-scoped search reduces uncertainty and creates a better decision record. It cannot eliminate uncertainty.
