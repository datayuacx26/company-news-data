## **Essential Forms for Medical Record Release**


Law-firm requests commonly require a valid patient authorization unless the request is being made under a separate patient-access, representative, subpoena, court-order, or other legally recognized disclosure pathway.


### **Key Elements of a Valid Authorization**


[California Civil Code § 56.11](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-1/part-2-6/chapter-2/section-56-11/) sets out detailed requirements that go beyond a generic form. An authorization should:


-


Appear clearly separate from other language and meet the applicable type-size requirement


-


Identify the disclosing provider and the authorized recipient, meaning your firm's name and contact information


-


Specify the uses and limitations required by the provider form and authorization law, rather than a canned phrase like "legal representation"


-


Describe the records sought, including date ranges and types such as treatment notes, billing, and imaging


-


Contain an expiration date or event


-


Carry the patient's signature and date, original or compliant electronic


-


Advise the signer of the right to receive a copy


Check § 56.11 and the receiving provider's own form rather than treating an abbreviated checklist as complete.

## **Cost Considerations**


For copies furnished under California's patient-access statute, physicians may generally charge up to $0.25 per page, or $0.50 for microfilm, plus permitted clerical costs. Reasonable costs not exceeding actual costs may apply to copies of X-rays or similar materials. Facility fees vary, so confirm the amount with each provider rather than assuming a statewide ceiling applies to every third-party transaction.


California provides no-cost copies for specified public-benefit and related claims under § 123110(d), subject to statutory restrictions. **This matters for PI firms** : the provision generally does not apply where a private attorney is paying claim-related costs pending the outcome.

## **Frequently Asked Questions**


### **How long do California providers have to release medical records?**


For qualifying patient-access requests, California law generally requires physicians to provide copies within 15 days. Different rules apply when records are disclosed directly to an attorney, and the Medical Board notes that § 123110 does not cover a request to transfer records to an attorney. Separately, HIPAA generally requires covered entities to act on an individual's access request within 30 calendar days, with one additional 30-day period on timely written notice.


### **What's the difference between a HIPAA authorization and a general release form?**


A HIPAA authorization meets federal standards for disclosure of protected health information, including elements such as an expiration date and a description of the information. California's CMIA adds requirements under Civil Code § 56.11. Generic release forms often lack elements that cause rejections. Using facility-specific forms, or[AI validation from](https://www.getcodeshealth.com/) Codes Health, helps satisfy both frameworks.
