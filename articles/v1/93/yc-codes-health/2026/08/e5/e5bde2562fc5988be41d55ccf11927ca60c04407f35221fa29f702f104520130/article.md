## **1. Understanding Medical Records Release Forms in Sacramento**


### **What Is a HIPAA Authorization Form?**


A HIPAA authorization permits a covered entity to release protected health information to a third party, in this case your firm. HIPAA generally permits disclosure pursuant to a valid written authorization, alongside disclosures otherwise permitted by the Privacy Rule. California's Confidentiality of Medical Information Act adds its own requirements, and the two are not identical.


One clarification worth making early: for records held by the Department of Health Care Services, including Medi-Cal information, use the applicable DHCS form. For hospital or physician records, use a HIPAA and CMIA-compliant authorization or the provider's own release form. Evidence Code § 1158 contains a statutory form for qualifying attorney requests. The DHCS form is not a universal authorization every Sacramento hospital must accept.


### **Key Elements of a Valid Release Form**


[Under HIPAA (45 C.F.R. § 164.508)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.508) , an authorization needs all core elements and required statements:


-


Patient identification sufficient for the provider to find the correct record, commonly full name and date of birth. A Social Security number is not a HIPAA requirement, though providers may ask for more identifiers


-


Specific description of the information disclosed, such as treatment notes, diagnostic reports, labs, imaging, billing, and prescriptions


-


Provider identification and recipient identification, meaning who discloses and your firm's name, address, and contact information


-


Expiration date or event


-


Patient signature and date


-


Required statements on the right to revoke, conditioning of treatment or payment, and potential redisclosure


[Under California Civil Code § 56.11](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-1/part-2-6/chapter-2/section-56-11/) , additional validity requirements apply, including separate authorization language in handwriting or minimum 14-point type, signature and date, specific use limitations, and recipient and provider identification. Signatures may be electronic or handwritten. The statute also requires an expiration date or event, generally limited to one year or less unless the signer requests a later date or another exception applies.


### **Avoiding Common Authorization Errors**


Incomplete or defective authorizations are a common cause of rejection and delay. A defect forces resubmission, which may mean the processing period only begins once a valid request is received. Common triggers:


-


Signature missing a date


-


Sensitive-record categories not properly addressed, including mental health, substance use, HIV-related, and genetic testing records


-


Vague date ranges, such as "all records" with no dates


-


Missing firm contact information, or an authorization already past its expiration


-


Missing identity documentation where the channel requires it. Sutter's online process requires a photo ID upload, though neither HIPAA nor California law makes that universal

-

-


Set follow-up reminders rather than waiting for a status update


### **Consistent Follow-Up**


The cadence below is an internal practice recommendation, not a statutory timetable. Which deadline applies depends on whether the request is a § 123110 access request, a qualifying § 1158 attorney request, or something else.


**Day 7** : call each provider that has not confirmed receipt and resubmit immediately if a defect is identified.


**Day 12** : written follow-up citing the statute that actually governs your request.


**Day 15** : escalate to the facility compliance officer or patient advocate. A demand letter is an optional internal step, not a legal requirement, and counsel should determine the appropriate remedy based on the request mechanism.


### **Verifying Completeness**


On receipt, check that all requested date ranges are covered without gaps, every record type is present including treatment notes, billing, imaging, and labs, pages are legible, the patient name is correct throughout, and imaging is enclosed rather than just radiology reports.

## **8. Cost-Effective Retrieval: Balancing Speed and Expense**


### **California Fee Rules**


For patient-access requests governed by § 123110, providers may charge up to $0.25 per paper page, or $0.50 per page copied from microfilm. The same statute addresses electronic copy costs, including media costs, and imaging is subject to reasonable cost not exceeding actual cost. Different cost rules can apply to attorney requests under Evidence Code § 1158.


Certification fees vary by provider and request type, so confirm the amount first. Total cost varies by provider, record volume, format, imaging, and which fee rules apply, which is why a per-provider budget figure is not worth relying on.


### **No-Fee Exceptions**


Section 123110(d) provides no-cost copies for specified public-benefit and immigration-related claims, currently including Medi-Cal, CalWORKs, SSI and SSP, Social Security disability, In-Home Supportive Services, veterans disability benefits, CalFresh, certain assistance for aged, blind, and disabled legal immigrants, housing subsidies, U status, and VAWA relief.


**Two qualifications matter.** The subdivision does not apply where the patient is represented by a private attorney paying costs related to the claim pending its outcome, with private attorney defined as one not employed by a nonprofit legal services entity. And qualifying no-cost requests carry a 30-day transmission requirement rather than 15.

Request electronic format where supported, since it may reduce mailing delays and simplify tracking. Include prepayment where required, and specify each facility even within a centralized system.
