# Good Faith Estimates for Healthcare Practices | Fuse

Federal price transparency expectations and No Surprises Act (NSA) enforcement have made the **good faith estimate** one of the most visible parts of **No Surprises Act compliance** for front-line teams. Under the NSA **GFE requirements** , **convening providers** and **convening facilities** that are not enrolled in Medicare or Medicaid must furnish an estimate to **uninsured and self-pay patients** when items or services are scheduled, and when those patients request one. The obligation is not only about sending a PDF on time. It is about whether the numbers reflect the care plan you expect to deliver.


Auditors and patients experience **good faith estimate healthcare** obligations differently. An auditor asks whether a document went out inside the delivery window. A patient asks whether the number on the page matched the number on the statement. When those two experiences diverge, the practice still spent staff time on paperwork, yet it did not buy the trust or collections stability that motivated the rule in the first place.


This **good faith estimate healthcare** guide covers who needs **NSA GFE** documents, what they must contain, how timing rules change with the scheduling window, and why **[CPT-level verification](https://www.fuseinsight.com/cpt-level-benefits-verification/)** is the operational difference between a compliant file and a **patient cost estimate** patients can rely on. ## **Most GFE Failures Are Accuracy Problems, Not Paperwork Problems**


Most practices understand that a **good faith estimate** must exist for qualifying patients. The harder problem is that many **GFE requirements** are satisfied on paper with figures that do not survive contact with the claim. Teams pull a benefit tier from a portal, drop it into a template, and move on. When the claim posts, the patient sees a different copay, an unmet deductible segment, or a denied add-on code. If the final bill is at least **$400** above what the **NSA GFE** showed for the same episode, the patient can pursue **patient-provider dispute resolution** through HHS. That is why accuracy matters as much as timestamps.


The gap is not usually neglect. It is structural. Standard workflows answer, “Is this member active on this plan?” **Good faith estimate healthcare** workflows must answer, “What will this member owe for these procedure codes on this date of service at this place of care?” Those are different questions. Closing the gap is what separates a symbolic estimate from a dependable **patient cost estimate** .


Practices that already collect deposits or card-on-file authorizations feel this tension first. A **patient cost estimate** that was rounded from a brochure copay creates downstream refunds, payment plan rework, and phone volume your team cannot schedule away. Treating the **NSA GFE** as a revenue-cycle artifact rather than a patient communication tool is how teams end up compliant on delivery timestamps and exposed on dollar variance.


## **Generic Eligibility Checks Do Not Return the Data GFEs Require**


Front desk teams are trained to run eligibility checks early. Those checks are necessary, but they are rarely sufficient for **GFE requirements** . A typical payer portal or 270/271 eligibility transaction confirms identifiers, plan selection, and that coverage appears active. It may summarize deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums at a plan level. It generally does **not** tell you the member’s cost share for the exact CPT or HCPCS codes on the schedule, whether those codes require prior authorization, or whether a carve-out applies to the rendering provider.


For a scheduled knee injection, for example, the plan may show a primary care copay online while the specialist office visit applies under a different benefit. Imaging bundled with surgery may process under another rule set entirely. **No Surprises Act compliance** for **NSA GFE** documents expects an itemized expected charge picture tied to the items and services reasonably expected to be furnished. Plan-level snapshots rarely reach that depth without an extra verification pass.


Think of it as a checklist your schedulers can use when judging whether an eligibility screen is “good enough” for a **good faith estimate** :


• **What standard checks usually confirm:** Member identity, plan name, effective dates, and sometimes aggregate deductible or out-of-pocket totals.


• **What GFE-accurate checks still need:** How the listed CPT or HCPCS codes map to benefit categories for that plan, what cost sharing applies after deductible credit order is applied, whether authorization is in place for those codes, and whether any provider- or site-specific limitations change the allowed amount.


If your team cannot point to where each line item on the **NSA GFE** came from in the payer’s own materials, you are still in the first column.


## **CPT-Level Verification Gives You the Benefits Detail Every GFE Depends On**

Done well, it surfaces procedure-specific cost sharing, remaining deductible balances that will apply to the claim, coinsurance percentages that attach to the service category, prior authorization requirements, and coverage limitations that portals omit. When payer portals stop short, **direct payer calls** fill in the blanks and create an auditable trail.

## **Who Must Provide a Good Faith Estimate?**


**Convening providers** and **convening facilities** that are not federal health program participants in Medicare or Medicaid must provide **NSA GFE** documents to **uninsured and self-pay patients** when they schedule a primary item or service, or upon request. Uninsured patients lack applicable coverage for the visit. Self-pay patients are those who choose not to have a claim submitted to their plan for that encounter. Emergency services sit outside the standard advance estimate pathway, and other exclusions can apply depending on how the patient is classified and how care is delivered.


The “who” question also has a practical corollary for operations: someone on your team must own the convening role for each episode. In independent practices, that is often the scheduling site where the patient first books. In facility-based care, the hospital or ASC registration desk may convene while professional charges still require coordination. Map that ownership explicitly so **GFE requirements** do not fall between departments.


State laws can layer on additional estimate duties, as they do in several jurisdictions. For the federal baseline and how it interacts with facility-based care, keep the[No Surprises Act compliance hub](https://www.fuseinsight.com/blog/no-surprises-act/) handy when you train staff.


## **What Must a Compliant GFE Include?**


A compliant estimate is more than a single dollar total. Federal **GFE requirements** call for patient identifiers, provider identifiers such as NPI and TIN, an itemized list of expected charges, applicable diagnosis and service codes such as ICD and CPT or HCPCS, expected dates of service and service locations, and co-provider or co-facility details when others are expected to bill separately. Patients must also receive information about their rights if billed substantially above the estimate, including the **patient-provider dispute resolution** pathway.


Itemization is where **CPT-level verification** pays off twice: first when you build the estimate, and again when the patient compares the statement line by line. A lump-sum total without code-level backing is difficult to defend if a single add-on code posts differently than expected. Listing each anticipated charge with its code also helps patients understand facility fees, supply charges, and professional components that otherwise read as duplicate billing.


When care is recurring, a single **good faith estimate** can cover repeated items or services for up to twelve months if the expected scope stays consistent. That is useful for therapy series or infusion schedules, but it still assumes the underlying clinical plan and codes remain stable.


### **GFE Requirements for Multi-Provider and Surgical Cases**


Surgical episodes and imaging bundles are where **NSA GFE** coordination becomes operationally heavy. The **convening provider** —the entity that receives the scheduling request for the primary service—must request expected charge information from co-providers and co-facilities **within one business day** of scheduling (or of the estimate request). Co-providers and co-facilities are expected to respond quickly enough for the convening provider to assemble a consolidated estimate. When everyone returns data on time, **good faith reliance** protections limit how much liability falls on the convening provider for another party’s late or incomplete numbers. When responses lag, the convening provider still faces clock pressure. That is why surgical and multi-specialty cases benefit from automated benefit pulls and documented payer contacts rather than ad hoc email chains alone.


## **GFE Timing Rules: How Long You Have Based on When the Visit Is Scheduled**


Timing is measured in **business days** from the day the service is scheduled or requested, not calendar days from the clinical event. Missing the window is an easy-to-audit failure mode, which is why scheduling software flags and EMR tasks matter as much as template content.


### **How the Scheduling Window Determines Your Deadline**


Use a simple grid with scheduling staff:


Days between scheduling and expected date of service GFE delivery requirement


At least 10 business days Provide the GFE within **3 business days** of scheduling


Between 3 and 9 business days Provide the GFE within **1 business day** of scheduling


Fewer than 3 business days No advance GFE is required **before** the service under the standard scheduling rule


Patient requests an estimate without scheduling Provide the GFE within **3 business days** of the request


If the date of service or scope of care shifts materially, treat it as a trigger to refresh documentation. An outdated **good faith estimate** undermines trust and reopens **PPDR** exposure if the bill diverges from what the patient was told.


## **The $400 Variance That Triggers a Patient-Provider Dispute**


When actual billed charges exceed the total expected amount on the **NSA GFE** by **$400 or more** , the patient may initiate **patient-provider dispute resolution** with HHS within **120 calendar days** of receiving the bill. The process is designed to be patient-navigable, which means your practice may receive a formal dispute even when clinical care was appropriate. Staff time spent assembling records, revisiting eligibility, and reconciling ledgers is unbillable overhead, and payment timelines can stretch while the dispute runs.


The preventive strategy is not to lowball estimates. It is to build estimates from **CPT-level verification** and documented payer responses so the expected total matches how the claim will actually process. Fewer surprises mean fewer disputes and cleaner **patient cost estimates** at the point of collection.
