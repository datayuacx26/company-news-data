# Navigating EPR Legislation in the U.S.: How Adhesives and Packaging Can Meet Regulations

The U.S. recycling system has long relied on taxpayers and municipalities to cover the costs of waste collection and processing.[Although this model is underperforming, with only about 21% of recyclable material being recycled and most losses occurring at the household level.](https://recyclingpartnership.org/wp-content/uploads/dlm_uploads/2024/05/SORR_Methodology-1-1.pdf) To address these gaps, policymakers are increasingly adopting Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) laws on a state-by-state level.


EPR is already established in regions like the EU and Canada. Now, U.S. states are adopting similar laws to improve collection, recycling rates, reduce waste, and promote sustainable packaging design. For brands and packaging producers, this shift presents both a challenge and an opportunity.


### Overview of EPR Legislation for Packaging in the U.S.


EPR is a policy approach that requires producers to fund and, in some cases, manage the end-of-life of their packaging, including collection, sorting, and recycling, while being incentivized to design packaging that is more easily recyclable and sustainable. This transfers the financial responsibility for packaging waste from local governments to the companies that place packaging on the market.


Producers are typically required to join a Producer Responsibility Organization (PRO), also referred to as Stewardship Organizations (SOs), such as the[Circular Action Alliance (CAA)](https://circularactionalliance.org/) , which creates the program plan and manages compliance as well as fee collection. The EPR program plan outlines how fees are assessed and charged to producers. These fees are typically based on the amount and packaging material placed on the market and are eco-modulated, meaning that they vary according to the sustainability attributes of the packaging, such as recyclability, use of recycled content, and design choices.


Now at the beginning of 2026, seven states —[Oregon, Colorado, California, Maine, Minnesota, Maryland, and Washington](https://epr.sustainablepackaging.org/) — have already adopted EPR packaging laws. Additional states, including New York, New Jersey, Illinois, and Massachusetts, are actively considering similar measures. Each law varies, but the core goal is the same: hold producers accountable for packaging waste.


Each law defines “producer” differently, but in most cases, responsibility lies with the company putting the product on the market such as the brand owner, manufacturer, importer, or distributor. If your company sells packaged products in these states, it is essential to determine whether you are a producer under EPR legislation.


Products included are defined by legislation or producer responsibility plans and specify packaging types, such as paper products, plastic packaging, e-commerce and food service ware. Some states also include industrial B2B packaging. Definitions vary by state and typically indicate format and material type.


Failure to comply can result in penalties, higher costs, and reputational risks. On the other hand, proactive companies can benefit from reduced compliance risks, access to eco-modulated fee incentives, and stronger sustainability credentials.


### Adhesives: An Important Factor in EPR Packaging Compliance


Adhesives and coatings play a critical role in packaging recyclability and are essential to EPR compliance. While they are not recycled, they must be compatible with recycling processes, either removable or integrable without compromising material quality and recycling yields.
