# What Intestacy Means for Your Estate in July 2026

Intestacy is the legal condition that arises when someone dies without a valid will. State law steps in to decide who inherits, in what order, and how the estate is divided. The rules vary by state and can produce very different outcomes. In this article, we'll highlight key aspects of intestacy and how they impact the settlement process.


**Key Takeaways:**


- Intestacy is the legal condition that arises when someone dies without a valid will; state law then determines who inherits and in what order.
- Intestate succession follows a fixed priority list: spouse and children first, then parents, siblings, and extended relatives.
- Life insurance, retirement accounts, joint tenancy property, and trust assets pass outside intestacy rules entirely and are not governed by state succession statutes.

## How Intestacy Affects the Estate: The Probate Process


When someone dies without a will, their estate goes through probate under[intestacy laws](https://www.elayne.com/resources/what-happens-when-someone-dies-without-a-will-intestate-succession-explained) . Probate is the court-supervised process that validates the distribution of assets, and intestacy rules determine exactly who receives what.


The[probate court](https://www.elayne.com/resources/what-is-probate-court) appoints an administrator (not an[executor](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/information-for-executors) named in a will) to manage the estate. That administrator locates assets, pays outstanding debts, and distributes what remains according to the state's intestacy statute.


A few key points to note:


- Probate under intestacy can take longer than probate with a will, because the court must first determine which relatives qualify as heirs under state law before any distribution begins.
- Creditors and tax obligations are settled before any heir receives a share, regardless of what the deceased may have intended.
- Assets held jointly or with named beneficiaries, such as life insurance or retirement accounts, pass outside probate entirely and are not affected by intestacy rules.

## The Intestate Succession Order: Who Inherits


When someone dies without a will, state law steps in with a fixed priority list that determines who inherits. This order is called the intestate succession order, and it follows bloodline relationships from closest to most distant.


The sequence generally runs as follows, and you can see how each state applies this order in[FindLaw's state-by-state intestate succession guide](https://www.findlaw.com/estate/planning-an-estate/intestate-succession-laws-by-state.html) :


- Spouse and children come first. In most states, a surviving spouse and children share the estate, though the exact split depends on whether the children are also the spouse's children.
- If there is no surviving spouse, children inherit everything equally.
- Parents inherit if there is no spouse or children.
- Siblings inherit if neither parents nor a spouse or children survive the decedent.
- Extended relatives such as grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins may inherit if no closer relatives exist.
- The state itself takes the estate if no qualifying relatives can be found, through a process called escheatment.


## Which Assets Are Subject to Intestacy Laws


Not every asset a person owns gets sorted by intestacy rules when they die without a will. The rules only apply to probate assets: property that would have passed through a will had one existed.


Assets with their own built-in transfer mechanisms pass outside of probate entirely. These include:


- Accounts with named beneficiaries, such as life insurance policies, IRAs, and 401(k)s, which transfer directly to whoever is listed on the beneficiary designation form.
- Property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship, which passes automatically to the surviving co-owner.
- Assets held in a revocable living trust, which distribute according to the trust document, not state intestacy rules.
- Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death accounts, which move to the named recipient without going through probate at all.


What remains after these transfers are accounted for is the probate estate, and that is the portion intestacy law governs.


## What Adjudication of Intestacy Means


A petition for formal adjudication of intestacy is a court filing that asks a judge to make two official determinations: that the person died without a valid will, and who the legal heirs are under state law.


In some states, once a court has formally adjudicated intestacy and identified the heirs,[real estate without a will](https://www.elayne.com/resources/what-happens-to-real-estate-without-a-will) can be transferred using that order without appointing a full personal representative, which can shorten the overall process considerably.


If this term appears in estate paperwork, it typically means the estate requires a court's official sign-off on heirship before any transfers can proceed. The adjudication itself does not distribute assets, but it sets the legal foundation for what comes next.

## How Intestacy Laws Vary by State


Intestacy laws follow a shared framework across the United States, but the details vary considerably from state to state. Where you live at the time of death determines which rules apply, and those rules can produce very different outcomes for surviving family members.


Here are a few ways states diverge in practice:


- In Texas, the intestacy statutes reflect the state's community property system, meaning a surviving spouse may receive a different share of separately owned property than community property, depending on whether the deceased had children from another relationship.
- California also operates under community property rules, so a surviving spouse automatically retains their half of community assets, with the deceased's half distributed under intestacy only if no will directs otherwise.
- Michigan and Connecticut follow common law property rules, which generally give a surviving spouse a set share of the estate, with the remainder divided among children or other relatives according to each state's specific statutory formula.
- New Jersey's law of intestacy follows the Uniform Probate Code framework closely, giving the surviving spouse the entire estate when all children are shared, but splitting the estate differently when a spouse has children from a prior relationship.

## FAQ


### What is intestacy?


Intestacy is the legal condition that arises when a person dies without a valid will, leaving state law to govern who inherits and in what order. When intestacy applies, the deceased person's wishes carry no legal standing; the state's default succession rules govern the entire distribution.


### What is partial intestacy and when does it apply to an estate?


Partial intestacy occurs when a will exists but doesn't cover certain assets. The will governs what it covers, and state intestacy law applies to everything else.


### What is a petition for formal adjudication of intestacy, and does it transfer assets?


A petition for formal adjudication of intestacy is a court filing asking a judge to make two official determinations: that the person died without a valid will, and who the legal heirs are under state law. The adjudication itself does not distribute assets, but it sets the legal foundation for what comes next. In some states, that court order allows real property to transfer without appointing a full personal representative, which can shorten the overall process considerably.


### What does "issue" mean in intestacy law?


"Issue" is the legal term for direct lineal descendants: children, grandchildren, and any further descendants in the bloodline. When a statute distributes an estate to a decedent's issue, it means blood descendants only, not siblings, parents, or other relatives.


### What happens if there are no living relatives?


The estate escheats to the state. Courts conduct a search for qualifying heirs first, working through the succession order from closest relatives outward. Only if no one qualifies does the state government take the assets.


### Does jointly owned property go through intestacy?


No. Property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship passes automatically to the surviving co-owner, completely outside probate. Intestacy rules have no bearing on it.


### How long does intestate probate typically last?


Intestate estates generally take longer to settle than estates with a will, because the court must confirm heirship before any distribution can begin. Timelines vary by state, estate complexity, and whether any heirship disputes arise, but most intestate estates move through probate over the course of several months to more than a year.


### What happens to a house when someone dies without a will?


Real property passes through the intestate succession order just like other probate assets. The court identifies the legal heirs, and title transfers according to state law. In some states, a formal adjudication of intestacy allows real estate to transfer using the court order directly, without a full probate administration.


### What is escheatment?


Escheatment is the process by which a deceased person's estate passes to the state government when no qualifying relatives can be found. Courts work through the full succession order first, from closest relatives outward. Only when no eligible heir exists does the state claim the assets.
