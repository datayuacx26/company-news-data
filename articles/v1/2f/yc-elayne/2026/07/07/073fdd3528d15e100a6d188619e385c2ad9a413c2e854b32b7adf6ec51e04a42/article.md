# Transfer on Death Deed in California: July 2026 Guide

A California transfer on death deed lets a property owner pass real estate directly to a named beneficiary at death, with no probate required. It costs far less upfront than a living trust, and the form is widely available. However, the deed only works for certain property types, comes with a creditor liability window, and has certain tax implications. In this article, we'll cover how a California TOD deed works, which property types qualify, recording requirements, how beneficiaries claim property after the owner dies, and how a TOD deed compares to a living trust.


**Key Takeaways:**


- A California TOD deed transfers property directly to named beneficiaries at death, with no court involvement required.
- The deed must be signed, notarized, and recorded with the county recorder within 60 days of notarization to be legally valid.
- Beneficiaries face a 120-day waiting period and a 3-year title insurance delay before they can sell or refinance the property.
- Only specific property types qualify, including residential property with one to four units, condominium units, and single-family residences on agricultural land of 40 acres or less.
- The deed is only available to owners who hold title as a sole owner or tenant in common; joint tenancy and community property with right of survivorship already pass outside probate.


## What Is a Transfer on Death Deed in California?


A[transfer on death deed](https://www.elayne.com/resources/what-is-a-transfer-on-death-deed-how-tod-deeds-help-you-skip-probate) in California is a legal document that lets a property owner name one or more beneficiaries to receive real estate automatically at death, without going through probate. The property stays fully under the owner's control during their lifetime. They can sell it, refinance it, or revoke the deed at any time.


California's version is called the revocable transfer on death deed, sometimes written as the[TOD deed](https://saclaw.org/resource_library/californias-transfer-on-death-deed-one-option-to-avoid-probate-on-your-home/) or TODD. It was made permanent under California Probate Code sections 5610 through 5696 after a pilot program that began in 2016.


The deed must be signed, notarized, and recorded with the county recorder's office before the owner dies. Recording is what activates the beneficiary designation. A deed that is signed but never recorded has no legal effect.


### Who Can Use a TOD Deed in California


Not every property qualifies. The law limits TOD deeds to specific property types:


- Residential property with one to four units, where the owner occupies or intends to occupy at least one unit
- A condominium unit
- A single-family residence on agricultural land of 40 acres or less


The owner must hold title as a sole owner or as a[tenant in common](https://www.elayne.com/resources/joint-tenancy-tenants-in-common-executors) . Property held in joint tenancy or community property with right of survivorship already passes outside probate through survivorship, so a TOD deed is generally not needed for those arrangements.

## What Property Qualifies for a California TOD Deed


Not all real property in California qualifies for transfer by TOD deed. The California Probate Code limits eligible property to real property located within the state, which covers most residential homes, condominiums, and vacant land. Commercial real estate and rental properties can also qualify, provided they meet the ownership structure requirements.


The key restriction involves how title is held. A TOD deed works only when the transferor holds title as a sole owner or as a tenant in common. Property held in joint tenancy or community property with right of survivorship already passes automatically to the surviving co-owner, making a TOD deed unnecessary and, in practice, ineffective for those ownership types.


There are a few additional property types that fall outside the scope of what a TOD deed can transfer:


- Property already held in a living trust cannot be transferred by TOD deed, since the trust itself governs how that property passes.
- Real property owned by a business entity, such as an LLC or corporation, is not eligible because the individual does not hold title directly.
- Property located outside California requires the laws of that state, which may or may not recognize TOD deeds.


## How to Create a Valid California TOD Deed


California law sets out specific requirements a TOD deed must meet to be legally valid. Getting any of these wrong can render the deed ineffective, leaving the property subject to probate.


The deed must be in writing and signed by the property owner in front of a notary public. It must clearly identify the property using its legal description, name at least one beneficiary, and include the statutory language indicating the transfer takes effect at death. Once signed and notarized, the deed must be recorded with the county recorder's office in the county where the property is located within 60 days of notarization.


Here are the steps in sequence:


- Complete the deed form using the statutory template, which includes the required declaration language. Forms are available through county recorder offices in Los Angeles, Alameda, San Diego, and other counties, or through the California Courts self-help website.
- Sign the deed before a notary public. The notarization must occur before recording, and the 60-day recording window begins on the date of notarization, not the date the deed is drafted.
- File the deed with the county recorder's office in the county where the property sits. Recording fees vary by county but generally fall in the range of $15 to $24 for the first page, with additional per-page fees after that.
- Complete a Preliminary Change of Ownership Report (PCOR) and submit it alongside the deed at recording. Most county recorder offices require this form at the time of filing.


The deed does not need to be delivered to the beneficiary, and the beneficiary does not need to sign or even know about the deed for it to be valid. The property owner retains full ownership and control during their lifetime.

## How a Beneficiary Claims Property After the Owner Dies


After the owner dies, the beneficiary named in a California transfer on death deed can claim the property by filing a few key documents with the county recorder's office where the property is located.


The process generally follows these steps:


- The beneficiary obtains a certified copy of the owner's death certificate, which serves as the official record that the transfer condition has been met.
- The beneficiary completes and signs an affidavit of survivorship (sometimes called an affidavit of death of transferor), confirming that the named beneficiary is alive and accepting the property.
- Both the death certificate and the signed affidavit are recorded with the county recorder's office.
- Once recorded, title passes to the beneficiary without any court involvement, and the beneficiary can take possession, sell, or refinance the property as the new owner.


The recording is typically completed within days of submitting the documents, though timelines can vary by county. Los Angeles County, San Diego County, and Alameda County each have their own recorder's office procedures, and fees for recording generally fall in the range of $15 to $25 per page.


One important note: if the deceased owner had outstanding debts, creditors may still have claims against the property even after the transfer completes. California law gives creditors a window to file claims following a death, and a beneficiary who receives property through a TOD deed can remain responsible for those obligations up to the value of the property received.

## FAQ


### What is a transfer on death deed in California and how does it work?


A California transfer on death deed (also called a revocable TOD deed or TODD) is a legal document that lets a property owner name one or more beneficiaries to receive real estate automatically at death, without probate court involvement. The owner keeps full control of the property during their lifetime and can revoke or update the deed at any time. The deed takes effect only after it is signed, notarized, and recorded with the county recorder's office where the property is located.


### What are the most common problems with transfer on death deeds in California?


The three problems families encounter most often are the three-year title insurance delay (beneficiaries generally cannot sell or refinance until that window closes under Probate Code Section 5696), personal creditor liability up to the value of the property received, and deed failure when the named beneficiary dies before the owner and no alternate is on record.


### How do I get the Los Angeles County transfer on death deed form?


The Los Angeles County transfer on death deed form is available through the LA County Recorder's official website or in person at their offices. San Diego County, Alameda County, and most other California counties provide the same revocable transfer on death deed form through their respective recorder's office websites or self-help counters. The California Courts self-help website also offers a version of the form with basic filing instructions. Once you have the form, it must be signed before a notary public and recorded within 60 days of notarization to be legally valid.


### Does a TOD deed avoid probate in California?


Yes. When the property owner dies, the named beneficiary can claim the property directly by recording an affidavit and[a certified death certificate](https://www.usa.gov/death-certificate) with the county recorder. No court involvement is required.


### Can a TOD deed be revoked?


A California TOD deed is fully revocable at any time before death. The owner can record a revocation form or a new TOD deed to replace or cancel the prior one.


### Does the beneficiary have to accept the property?


No. A beneficiary can disclaim the property within nine months of the owner's death. If they disclaim, the property passes as if they had predeceased the owner.


### What happens if the beneficiary dies before the owner?


If no alternate beneficiary is named and the primary beneficiary dies first, the TOD deed fails and the property goes through probate.


### Are there income or estate tax consequences?


The property receives a stepped-up cost basis at the owner's death, which may reduce[real estate inheritance tax](https://www.elayne.com/resources/real-estate-inheritance-tax-101-everything-you-need-to-know-after-someone-dies) liability if the beneficiary later sells. California has no state estate tax, though federal estate tax rules may apply depending on the estate's size.


### Does a TOD deed protect against the owner's creditors?


No. Creditors of the deceased owner can still make claims against the property after death. A TOD deed does not shield the property from outstanding debts or Medi-Cal recovery claims.
