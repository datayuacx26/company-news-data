# Trade-Line Search in Probate: Attorney Guide (August 2026)

A credit bureau trade-line search is one of the first tools a personal representative should have in hand during probate administration. The decedent's credit file can help surface open accounts, unpaid balances, and creditor relationships that may not appear anywhere in the decedent's own records. Without it, the estate's liability picture may be incomplete before creditor notice deadlines begin running.


[Creditor notice deadlines](https://www.elayne.com/resources/notice-to-creditors-requirements) start running from the date of appointment or publication, depending on state law. A trade-line search run before those deadlines close can give legal teams documented, organized evidence of the estate's debt obligations. It can also be helpful in satisfying the personal representative's diligent-search obligation.

## What a Credit Bureau Trade-Line Search Reveals in Probate


A credit bureau trade-line search pulls every open account, unpaid balance, collection item, and recent inquiry tied to the decedent into one structured record. The report informs the estate inventory directly: creditors that don't appear in the decedent's files may appear here.


A trade-line report typically returns accounts across several categories: revolving credit (credit cards and lines of credit), installment loans (auto, personal, student), mortgage obligations, and collection items. Each entry shows the creditor's name, account balance, payment history, and account status.


For the estate inventory, what the trade-line search adds is a structured liability picture. The personal representative has a fiduciary duty to identify the estate's debts, not just its assets. A creditor that does not file a timely claim after proper notice may lose the right to collect, but that outcome depends on the creditor receiving proper notice in the first place. A trade-line search run early in administration helps give the personal representative documented evidence of which creditors existed at the time of death, and supports the notice and diligent search record.


Joint accounts and authorized-user accounts also appear on a trade-line search. A joint account carries obligations differently than a solely held account. When a joint account appears, the estate may have continued liability for the balance depending on state law and the structure of the obligation. An authorized-user relationship, by contrast, generally does not create personal liability for the estate.


Secured debt surfaces on the report as well. A mortgage, a vehicle loan, or a line of credit secured against real property will appear with the current balance and the creditor's identity. This information connects directly to the asset side of the estate: a mortgage on the report can be cross-referenced against property records to confirm the asset is in the inventory and the lien is accounted for.

## Who Has Legal Authority to Request a Deceased Person's Credit Report


Not every family member can pull a deceased person's credit report. Under FCRA rules and each bureau's own requirements, recognized standing belongs to the surviving spouse, a court-appointed[executor](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/information-for-executors) or administrator, and anyone holding[Letters Testamentary](https://www.elayne.com/resources/what-are-letters-testamentary) or Letters of Administration.


The exact documentation each bureau requires varies, so confirming current requirements directly with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion before submitting is helpful. For an attorney, one of the key tasks at this stage is confirming that the personal representative's authority is in place and properly documented before the request goes out.


## Documents Required Before Submitting a Credit Bureau Request


Before contacting any bureau, the attorney's office should have four items ready: a certified copy of the death certificate, Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration, government-issued ID for the requesting party, and the decedent's full legal name, Social Security number, date of birth, and last known residence.

## What Happens After a Bureau Processes a Death Notification


Once a bureau processes the death notification, it places a deceased indicator on the credit file. From that point, the file is effectively frozen: credit scoring stops, and new credit cannot be opened in the decedent's name. TransUnion, for example, typically updates the file within five business days of receiving the notification letter and sends a confirmation to the spouse or authorized party who submitted the request. The confirmation should be retained in the estate record.

## Disputing Errors and Fraudulent Trade Lines Under the FCRA


Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, consumer reporting agencies must maintain accurate files, and this is an obligation that covers deceased consumer records as well. When a fraudulent account, post-death inquiry, or incorrect balance appears on a trade-line search, the personal representative has standing to file a written dispute with the relevant bureau.

## FAQ


### How do you get a credit report for a deceased person, and who is legally allowed to request one?


A surviving spouse, court-appointed executor, or anyone holding Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration has recognized standing under FCRA rules to request a deceased person's credit report. Each bureau requires[a certified death certificate](https://www.usa.gov/death-certificate) , government-issued ID for the requesting party, and the decedent's full legal name, Social Security number, date of birth, and last known residence.


### Experian vs TransUnion vs Equifax for reporting a death: do I need to contact all three?


Yes, each bureau maintains a separate credit file and processes death notifications independently. Equifax and Experian accept both mail and online submissions; TransUnion processes these requests by mail only. A confirmed deceased indicator on one bureau's file does not automatically update the others.


### **What does a deceased person's credit report show in probate?**


A deceased person's credit report lists open accounts, outstanding balances, collection items, recent credit inquiries, and the creditors associated with each account. In probate, this record may surface creditor relationships that do not appear anywhere in the decedent's own files, giving the personal representative a more complete picture of the estate's liabilities before creditor notice deadlines run.


### **What is a deceased indicator, and what does it mean for the estate?**


A deceased indicator is a notation a credit bureau places on a credit file once it has processed a death notification. Once that flag is in place, credit scoring on the file stops and new credit accounts cannot be opened in the decedent's name. The indicator does not close or transfer any existing accounts; those steps require separate action with each creditor.


### **How does a trade-line search support the personal representative's diligent search obligation?**


A trade-line search produces a documented, source-linked record of the decedent's known creditor relationships at a point in time. Where state law requires the personal representative to make a diligent search for creditors before closing the estate, that documented search record can help demonstrate that the estate's liability picture was examined through a recognized source. The scope of the diligent search obligation varies by jurisdiction.
