# Breaking down neuroscience through deep biological interpretation

## You don't need to read minds to understand your neuroscience data – you need to read the biology.


### From data generation to biological interpretation


Advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) have reshaped research on neurodevelopmental disorders. Whole-genome sequencing, RNA sequencing, single-cell transcriptomics and spatial transcriptomics now allow researchers to examine the developing nervous system at extraordinary resolution.


These new, detailed datasets have created new opportunities – instead of just checking which genes show increased or decreased expression, researchers can decode the biological meaning of these changes. The only problem is the scale.


A single RNA-seq study may identify hundreds or thousands of differentially expressed genes. A genome sequencing project may uncover many rare variants, including variants of uncertain significance. Single-cell studies can reveal cell type-specific changes across progenitors, neurons and glial populations. Each result is valuable, but the larger scientific task is connecting those results to plausible mechanisms.


### Why interpretation matters


Neurological disorders such as autism spectrum disorder, Rett syndrome, fragile X syndrome, epilepsy-associated developmental disorders and intellectual disability rarely point to one simple biological explanation. Instead, diverse genetic and molecular findings often converge on shared processes, including synaptic function, neuronal development, immune signaling, chromatin regulation and cell fate decisions.

This is why biological interpretation is becoming essential in neuroscience bioinformatics. It helps researchers move from lists of genes or variants toward questions that are more useful for discovery:


- Which pathways appear disrupted?
- Which upstream regulators may explain the data?
- Do independent datasets point toward related mechanisms?
- Which findings are supported by existing biological evidence?
- Which hypotheses are strong enough to prioritize for validation?


### Case in point: Autism research beyond gene lists


Recent autism research illustrates this challenge well. In a 2023 Nature study1, researchers combined cerebral organoids, CRISPR-based perturbation and single-cell transcriptomics to examine high-risk autism genes. The study identified vulnerable developmental cell states and gene regulatory modules associated with autism risk, showing how single-cell data can help connect genetic perturbations to cell type-specific biological effects.


That type of study produces rich, multilayered data – and to interpret this data properly, researchers need to understand how altered genes relate to developmental timing, cell identity, molecular pathways and prior evidence from the literature.


Other studies have also highlighted convergent biology in autism, including links between synaptic dysfunction, immune responses and neurotransmission-related processes2,3. Together, these findings reinforce a key point: Discovery depends not only on generating data but also on interpreting them within a broader biological framework.


### Connecting discovery to biological understanding


As multiomic approaches become more common, the importance of interpretation will only grow. Genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics and spatial biology each provide a different view of disease biology. The opportunity lies in connecting them.
