## What Are Interest Rates?


Interest rates are the cost of borrowing money or the return you earn on savings. Central banks, such as the Bank of England, set benchmark rates that influence borrowing and lending across the economy. Commercial banks then adjust their own rates based on this benchmark.


When interest rates rise, borrowing becomes more expensive and saving becomes more rewarding. When rates fall, loans become cheaper and spending often increases. These changes influence investment flows and currency demand around the world.


Interest rates also reflect economic conditions. Central banks often increase rates to control inflation and reduce excess spending. They lower rates to stimulate growth during economic slowdowns.

## What Are Exchange Rates?


Exchange rates show how much one currency is worth compared to another. They determine how many euros you receive for pounds or how many dollars you get for your local currency. Rates change constantly based on global supply and demand.


If demand for a currency increases, its value usually rises. If demand falls, the currency often weakens. These shifts affect travellers, businesses, and families who send money internationally. Understanding[what drives exchange rates](https://www.westernunion.com/blog/en/gb/currency-fluctuation-what-drives-exchange-rates/) helps you plan your transfers more effectively. Rates respond to economic signals, investor behaviour, and global events.


## How Interest Rates Influence Exchange Rates


Interest rates influence exchange rates by shaping global investment decisions. Investors often move money to countries offering higher returns on savings and bonds. This movement increases demand for that country’s currency.


Higher demand typically strengthens the currency. Lower interest rates can reduce foreign investment and weaken the currency. This relationship answers the common question: how do interest rates affect exchange rates?


### Higher Interest Rates and Currency Strength


When a country raises interest rates, it may attract international investors seeking better returns. Investors must buy that country’s currency to invest there. This increases demand and can push the exchange rate higher.


### Lower Interest Rates and Currency Weakness


When interest rates fall, returns on investments may become less attractive. Investors may move funds to countries with better yields. This reduces demand for the original currency.


Lower demand can weaken the exchange rate. If you[send money abroad](https://www.westernunion.com/gb/en/send-money-abroad.html) during this period, your recipient may receive less in foreign currency. These shifts highlight how changes in interest rates affect exchange rates in practical terms.


## The Economic Mechanism Behind the Link


The connection between interest rates and exchange rates follows a clear economic process. Central bank decisions influence borrowing costs and savings returns. These decisions affect capital flows across borders.


When rates rise, foreign investors seek higher returns. They exchange their currency for the higher-yielding currency, increasing demand. This demand strengthens the exchange rate. When rates fall, capital may flow outward. Investors convert funds into other currencies offering stronger returns. This reduces demand and can lower the currency’s value.


Inflation expectations also play a role. If rate increases successfully control inflation, investor confidence may improve further. Confidence strengthens currency demand even more.


## Other Factors That Affect Exchange Rates


Interest rates are important, but they are not the only driver of currency movements. Exchange rates respond to a range of economic and political influences. Understanding these factors gives you a fuller picture of what drives exchange rates.


### Inflation Rates


Higher inflation can reduce a currency’s purchasing power. Central banks often raise interest rates to control inflation. Persistent inflation may weaken a currency over time.


### Political Stability and Elections


Stable governments tend to attract investment. Political uncertainty can reduce investor confidence and weaken a currency. Elections and policy changes often create short-term volatility.


### Trade Balance


A country that exports more than it imports often sees strong demand for its currency. A trade deficit can reduce demand and weaken the currency. Trade flows directly affect foreign exchange markets.


### Speculation and Market Sentiment


Currency traders react to news, forecasts, and economic data. Positive sentiment can strengthen a currency quickly. Negative expectations may cause rapid declines.


### Global Events


Events such as financial crises, pandemics, or geopolitical tensions can shift currency markets. Investors may move funds to stable economies during uncertainty. These flows can significantly affect exchange rates.
