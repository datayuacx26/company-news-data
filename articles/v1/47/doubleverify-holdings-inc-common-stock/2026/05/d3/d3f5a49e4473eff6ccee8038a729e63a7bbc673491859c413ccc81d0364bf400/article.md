# Maximizing Team Engagement With Storytelling Techniques

*Written By:*[Luna Erica](https://www.linkedin.com/in/luna-erica-23a675187/)


Imagine we’re in a meeting together. We’re starting a new project to improve our scraper bot, and I, the PM, start telling you about this thing called robots.txt. I could go with the facts and tell you the following:


*“Robots.txt dictates how a crawler bot — that’s an automated program that systematically collects publicly available data from websites by simulating user browsing and then saving the information in a structured format, such as a spreadsheet or database — should behave in the context of a specific site. Robots.txt files are owned and edited by publishers.”*


Sure, you’re a professional, you’re used to technical terminology and can parse this. But what if instead, I would describe it like this:


*“A robots.txt file is an additional wall of defense maintained by a publisher. The wall is in place to stop crawler bots from crawling their way through a publisher’s web environment and saving the information they come across. The publisher can play with these files to specify which bots can and cannot access the site.”*


We’re dumbing down the language a little, maybe, but wouldn’t you remember that second option better?


There are ample disconnects between PMs and technical employees that demonstrate the need for improved communication on both sides. Sadly, because engineers, analysts and PMs alike are all incredibly busy, it’s harder to make information — especially very complex technical information — stick. What’s needed is a clear structure, a hook, and, if you can get one, a story.


At the time of writing, I’m new to this industry. Hell, I’m new as a PM! And that fact has given me a lot of food for thought, especially about communications within a big, multidisciplinary tech company like DV. What I’m not so new at is linguistics. The field of linguistics — the study of language — includes significant research on the cognitive effects of different ways of communication. This blog post taps into linguistic definitions of storytelling, explains why it is so effective, and gives you a few hands-on ways to use it in simple, casual conversation. Let’s dive in.


### What Storytelling Actually Is


If you’re not 100% convinced of this method yet, bear with me. Storytelling doesn’t have to come in the form of a 500-page novel about dwarves and hobbits and orcs. It doesn’t even have to come in the form of a ‘story’ as we understand it from a young age. Storytelling is an interaction between a storyteller and their audience. It conveys aspects of people or things through context, either real or imagined.


Metaphors are a great example. This type of phrasing has the additional benefit of extending an olive branch to those colleagues on the call who may not be as familiar with technical jargon.


I’m not saying we shouldn’t use jargon. Jargon exists to specify what we’re talking about in a specialized field of knowledge. I’m talking about packaging your jargon into a neater package for improved understanding. That very sentence, “packaging your jargon into a neater package,” is an example of storytelling through a metaphor. I’ll go over metaphors and other easy applications of storytelling in this article.


### Why Storytelling Is So Effective


People respond well to stories. Time and time again, cognitive linguistic research has shown that wiring storytelling into your explanations enhances learning, personal connections, and even empathy. Yeah, you read that right — Brockington, in 2021, for example, found that storytelling increases oxytocin and positive emotions in hospitalized children. Many other studies across different demographics have similar results: When you hear a story, your brain creates a mental image that leads to mental stimulation, bringing pleasure to the brain. These emotions then focus the brain’s goal-seeking activity, making the audience *want* to understand what you’re saying.


But let’s focus less on the brainwavey background of storytelling for a second and instead hone in on the direct implications of our throwing around a metaphor here and there. I think we’ve all been in situations where we find a certain form of communication just sticks better in our brains. This can be the word order someone used, the gestures, or a specific turn of phrase. All of these aspects form the context for an interaction.


Storytelling is a way to spice up an interaction so it sticks better in someone’s mind. On top of that, it also helps nourish social relationships: The hormone I mentioned before, oxytocin, increases empathy. This works for both the storyteller and the audience, so even if you’re not encouraging the people around you to tell stories, you still have a solid chance of improving your relationships with your colleagues if you share an anecdote a few times a month. Now I’m not saying you’ll be best friends with all your coworkers immediately, but throwing in a fun word every once in a while can’t hurt the office bonding.


So the question remains: How do you actually use storytelling techniques in your meetings without sounding like a pompous jerk?


### How To Actually Employ Storytelling in Your Daily Life


Using storytelling can be as straightforward as you want it to be. Here, it’s important to remember: to each their own. Not every one of these techniques will feel natural to everyone, but using even just one of these tools can go a long way in engaging your peers.


#### 1. Metaphors and similes


Consider the robots.txt example from earlier. Remember how I phrased it as “a wall of defense”? That’s a metaphor in action. At the risk of dragging you back to high school English class, here’s a brief overview of metaphors and similes:


- **Metaphors** say one thing *is* another thing. Saying “The classification pipeline is the beating heart of our product” likens the system to a heart that pumps classifications to clients.
- A **simile** compares one thing to another using ‘like’ or ‘as’. For example, we can say that mapping DV brand safety segments from our technical tools into the DV Pinnacle platform is *like* inserting a plug into a socket. We ‘plug’ our segments into Pinnacle to let the classifications flow through like electricity.


When considering how to translate technical jargon across teams, ask yourself: What everyday object or process does this remind me of?


#### 2. AI prompts


Let’s be honest. Sometimes you’re too deep in the weeds to come up with a good metaphor for a meeting or Slack message. AI can back you up there. Try using the following prompt on an AI tool that’s been approved for DV-internal use and see what it returns:


```text
"I'm a [YOUR ROLE] speaking to [YOUR AUDIENCE], and I need to define what [YOUR TECHNICAL CONCEPT OR PROJECT] is.   Create 3 simple metaphors or analogies that compare this concept   to everyday objects or experiences that this audience would be   familiar with. Make them vivid and memorable. Keep it simple, and don't make the audience feel like I'm talking down to them."
```


Fill in the brackets with your role, audience and terminology. For example:


User:


*“I’m a classification PM speaking to pre-bid PMs, and I need to explain how OLTP tables power our classifications.”*


Example AI response:


*“OLTP tables are like the menu that defines what’s available. When a classification system categorizes content, it’s constantly checking: Does a particular category exist? What’s its identifier? Does it have severity levels? The categories table is that menu — it tells every downstream system what’s available. If the menu’s outdated, everyone’s working with the wrong information.”*


#### 3. Other storytelling tools


Beyond metaphors, there’s a whole toolkit of storytelling devices that bring narrative into your explanations to make them more engaging:


Use a **before/after format** to show the impact of your work:


- *Instead of* : ** “We need this dashboard because PMs spend too many hours gathering data.”
- *Use:* “Before we implemented this dashboard, PMs were spending three hours gathering data. After? 30 seconds.”
- Effect *:* This creates a mini-narrative arc with tension and resolution.


Ask a **rhetorical question** to get your audience engaged:


- *Instead of:* “ct_impressions in VT table 871203972_categories_2 trend downwards on Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays.”
- *Use:* “Ever wonder why assignment rates go down on the weekends?” before diving into peak usage patterns.
- Effect: Questions engage your listeners’ curiosity.


Phrase your suggestion as a **personal anecdote** :


- *Instead of* : “We can decrease errors by 90% on happymoms.com by changing the CPU we use, using a similar method to the one in the release that went out last week.”
- *Use* : “Last week, I was debugging a CPU issue and realized we could use the same technique to decrease errors on happymoms.com by 90%!”
- Effect: Personal stories are easier to follow and make abstract concepts concrete.


Use **adjectives** beyond “good” and “bad” to make your statements more memorable:


- *Instead of:* “The segment offering was bad.”
- *Use:* “The segment offering was sparse and, for clients who want to use our product to the fullest, frustrating.”
- Effect: Vivid, specific adjectives dramatically increase engagement and retention.


The key here is subtlety. If you use all of these at once, you’ll sound like you’re trying too hard. It can help to start with one tool, prompt AI to give suggestions or even ask for direct feedback from coworkers to see if your explanation would be clearer if you phrased it differently using one of these tools.


### First Steps Toward Storytelling


Ask yourself two things: Which of these methods would be the most natural for you to use in a conversation? And when’s your next meeting with a colleague who’s prone to zoning out when they hear technical jargon?


Ahead of that meeting, try to prepare just one fun turn of phrase to engage them. Once you’ve done this a few times, it’ll become a habit, and your technical communication will become more powerful and visual with every meeting. And who knows, maybe the research proves right, and you’ll make some friends along the way!
