# How Simplify helped Jessica land her first internship!

In 2022, Jessica Lopez was a first-generation community-college student searching for a remote, accessible first internship. She used Simplify to reduce the repetitive work in online applications and ultimately landed a 12-week rotational internship at Alation. Her experience is a case study in defining real constraints, finding suitable roles, and protecting time for the parts of an application that require judgment.


**Editorial note:** This is a historical user case study based on Jessica’s original 2022 interview and her account of interning at Alation. The 2026 refresh reorganizes those existing sources; it is not a new interview or an update on her current life. Jessica’s result is specific to her experience, and another applicant’s result may differ.


## Jessica’s Starting Point


At the time of the interview, Jessica was studying Business, Economics, and Communications at Coastline College. She also served as vice president of the college’s associated student government. As a first-generation college student who had just completed her first year, she said she knew little about navigating higher education or beginning a professional search.


She wanted to spend her first summer in an internship, but she faced a narrow set of realistic options. Jessica explained that a physical disability and chronic illness made remote learning and work the environments in which she had been most successful. She therefore needed a fully remote opportunity that was open to a community-college student rather than restricted to upperclassmen.


Those requirements did not make the search less ambitious. She explored marketing, finance, public policy, product management, and corporate social responsibility. The challenge was finding roles that fit both her interests and the conditions in which she could participate successfully.


## Turning Constraints Into Search Criteria


Jessica searched across several job sites and through general web searches. In her original account, she said many listings felt irrelevant, inactive, inaccurate, or spammy. That made the discovery stage exhausting before an application had even begun.


After hours of searching, she identified 38 internships that seemed like a strong fit. That number referred to a target list, not 38 completed applications or interviews. The roles crossed several fields because she was still learning which kind of work she wanted to pursue.


The useful part of this approach was the filter. Jessica was not looking for every internship. She was looking for roles that were remote, accessible to an early college student, and connected to subjects she wanted to test through real work.


## The Application Bottleneck Was Also an Accessibility Barrier


Finding suitable internships exposed the next problem. Online portals frequently asked Jessica to re-enter information already included in her resume, such as prior experience, descriptions, and cover-letter content. She said some forms disabled copying and pasting, which forced her to enter the same information manually.


> “Even when copying and pasting, each application would take me over 45 minutes to fill in.”


The original interview makes an important distinction between convenience and access. A repetitive form can be frustrating for any applicant. Jessica explained that the same design can make an application inaccessible for someone with a mobility or dexterity disability. Faster form completion was therefore not just about submitting more applications. It reduced a barrier that was consuming time and physical effort.


Jessica wanted to spend that time adapting her resume and cover letter to the role. Those tasks required her to decide which experience was relevant and how to explain her interest. Re-entering identical contact and work-history fields did not improve that judgment.


## How Jessica Used Simplify in Her 2022 Search


Jessica described Simplify as a place where she could discover internships and use autofill to complete repetitive fields. She had previously used a spreadsheet to record applications and links. Simplify’s application tracker gave her a way to see when she applied and the status she had recorded without rebuilding the same process by hand.


In the original interview, Jessica said Simplify changed her pace to **“ten applications per hour instead of two.”** That comparison reflected her experience at the time. It was not a promise about how quickly another person will apply, and it did not replace tailoring or checking each submission.


The workflow separated repeatable information from role-specific work. Autofill handled fields that appeared across portals. Jessica could then focus on whether the internship fit her criteria and on adapting her materials to the description. Tracking helped her keep the search organized after a submission.


## The Documented Alation Result


Jessica found Alation while browsing technology internships through Simplify. The listing described a rotational internship, which interested her because she was considering several areas of work. She tailored her resume and cover letter to the description even though she said she initially knew little about the company.


She received the offer and completed a 12-week internship at Alation. In her original accounts, Jessica described working on more than 15 projects, including webpages and marketing emails connected to product launches. The rotation gave her exposure to marketing-related work and opportunities to collaborate across the company.


The internship also expanded her professional network. Jessica wrote about meeting other interns and employees, learning from people across the business, and taking on work that helped her understand marketing as a possible career direction. These were the documented outcomes of her 2022 internship, not claims about her current work or plans.

## What Other Internship Seekers Can Learn From the Process


Jessica’s story does not provide a formula for receiving an offer. It does show how an applicant can turn a broad goal into a manageable process. The steps below are general guidance for a current search; they do not describe additional actions Jessica took beyond the 2022 sources.


### 1. Write down your real constraints


Start with conditions that determine whether you can participate and succeed. These may include eligibility, work location, schedule, transportation, technology, or an accommodation process. Separate non-negotiable constraints from preferences. That keeps you from spending time on an opportunity that cannot work while leaving room to consider unfamiliar roles.


Jessica’s target was specific enough to guide her search: fully remote, open to someone early in college, and connected to fields she wanted to explore. Your filters may be different, but they should be explicit.


### 2. Use a current recruiting calendar


Internship timelines change, and different employers open applications at different points in the cycle. Use the[Summer Internship Timeline](https://simplify.jobs/blog/summer-2027-internship-timeline?ref=simplify.jobs) to plan when to begin searching and when to check more often. Treat dates as a planning tool rather than a guarantee that a particular company will open on schedule.


### 3. Build a qualified role list before applying


A target list is useful only when each role fits the basic filters. Check the location, term, eligibility, and core responsibilities before adding an internship. For a current starting point, browse[Top Summer Internships](https://simplify.jobs/l/Top-Summer-Internships-2026?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) and verify every posting before investing time in an application.


Record why each role belongs on the list. A short note such as “remote, first-year eligible, marketing rotation” makes it easier to prioritize and later explain your interest.


### 4. Prepare truthful materials before speeding up submission


Autofill can reduce repeated typing, but it cannot decide which experience best supports a role. Create a clear base resume and then adjust relevant skills, projects, and wording without inventing qualifications. The[ATS Resume Guide](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-write-ats-resume-2026?ref=simplify.jobs) explains how to keep a resume readable while reflecting the language of a real job description.


Review every autofilled field before submitting. A saved profile may contain an old date, a formatting error, or a response that does not fit the question. Application speed is useful only when the submitted information remains accurate.


### 5. Track decisions, not just submissions


For each application, save the role, employer, date, status, link, and the version of your materials. Add a next step when one exists. Tracking prevents duplicate applications and gives you a record when a recruiter follows up after the listing changes or disappears.


A tracker can also reveal whether the search criteria need work. If many target roles fail at eligibility review, adjust the discovery filters. If applications produce interviews but interviews stall, shift time toward preparation rather than simply increasing volume.


### 6. Prepare first-person interview evidence


After applying, identify examples that show how you learn, collaborate, solve problems, and adapt. Use the[Behavioral Interview Guide](https://simplify.jobs/blog/prepare-behavioral-interview-without-sounding-rehearsed?ref=simplify.jobs) to organize those examples without memorizing a script. Keep the focus on what happened, what you did, and what changed.


Jessica’s Alation account shows why role research matters as well. She described studying the company before a later interview round because she initially had only a vague understanding of its work. Before an interview, learn what the company does and connect that context to the role.


### 7. Build a support system that fits you


In her original interview, Jessica encouraged job seekers with disabilities to find community, learn from disabled professionals, consider volunteer experience when practical, and use available mentorship or counseling resources. She also emphasized that disclosure is a personal choice tied to comfort and support. No applicant owes personal information that is not required for the process.


A support system can include peers who review materials, mentors who explain unfamiliar recruiting norms, or people who help you evaluate whether an opportunity meets your needs. The goal is not to copy someone else’s path. It is to make your own decisions with better information.


## Accessibility Is Part of Recruiting Quality


Jessica’s account shows why employers should look beyond whether an application technically loads. Repetitive fields, disabled copy-and-paste functions, and inflexible tools can impose very different costs on different applicants. An accessible process gives qualified people a workable way to provide the information needed for a hiring decision.


For candidates, the lesson is not to accept every barrier as a personal failure. Identify the environment and process you need, look for a clear accommodation route when relevant, and focus effort on opportunities that meet the essential conditions. If a portal creates a problem, document it and use the employer’s stated support channel when one is available.


## What Recruiting Teams Can Audit


Jessica’s account also gives employers a practical place to begin. The following steps are general recommendations based on the application barrier she described; they are not additional claims about her search or about Alation.


- **Complete the application as a candidate would.** Count how many times the form requests information already contained in a resume. Record how long the process takes and whether progress can be saved.
- **Question restrictions on basic input tools.** If copying and pasting is disabled, identify the specific purpose and consider whether another control can address it without forcing every applicant to retype long responses.
- **Test more than the happy path.** Review what happens when a field produces an error, a session expires, or an applicant needs to return later. Confirm that instructions explain how to recover without restarting the entire form.
- **Make help easy to find.** Give candidates a clear contact for technical access problems and accommodation requests. Explain what information the hiring team needs to move the request forward.
- **Ask disabled candidates and accessibility specialists to review the process.** A form may be technically available while still creating avoidable work. Treat that feedback as product evidence and assign someone to fix recurring barriers.


An employer does not need to know a candidate’s diagnosis to improve a repetitive process. Removing unnecessary re-entry can help every applicant while addressing a barrier that, as Jessica explained, affects some people much more severely.


## A Source-Faithful View of the Result


This case study supports a narrow conclusion. In Jessica’s 2022 search, repetitive applications created a concrete accessibility problem. Simplify helped her reduce that manual work, keep applications organized, and discover the Alation opportunity. She then tailored her materials, completed the company’s interview process, and had a 12-week internship with more than 15 projects and broader professional exposure.


It does not establish that submitting more applications guarantees an internship. Jessica’s outcome also involved selecting a suitable role, adapting her materials, researching the company, interviewing, and building relationships during the internship. The transferable value lies in the process: define the constraints, remove avoidable repetition, preserve the work that needs judgment, build support around the search, and keep the system accountable to the people using it.
