# How to Put Expected Graduation Date on a Resume

**Put your expected graduation date in the Education section, beside or directly below your degree.** Use a clear month-and-year label, such as **B.S. in Accounting — Expected May 2027** .

## What's the correct format for an expected graduation date on a resume?


The clearest format is **Expected Month Year** . “Anticipated Month Year” is also understandable, but “Expected” is shorter and matches the wording many application forms use.


I see students spend far too long polishing this five-word line. The goal is not to find a magic phrase. It is to give a recruiter an honest, easy-to-scan estimate of when you expect to finish your degree.


Here are three copyable examples:


- **B.S. in Accounting — Expected May 2027**
- **Bachelor of Science in Mechanical Engineering** | University of Michigan | Expected May 2027
- **B.A. in Economics (Anticipated December 2026)** | University of Texas at Austin


Use the month your school or program currently expects you to complete the degree. You do not need the exact commencement day. If you know only the term, “Expected Fall 2027” is a reasonable temporary label until you can confirm the month.

## Where does the expected graduation date go on a resume?


Keep it inside the Education section, close to the degree it describes. A recruiter should not have to search your header or summary to figure out whether you are still enrolled.


A practical order is:


1. **Degree and field of study.** Example: Bachelor of Science in Accounting.
2. **School and location.** Include the city and state only if the location adds useful context.
3. **Expected graduation date.** Use month and year when available.
4. **Optional details.** GPA, honors, coursework, or activities only when they help with the role.


Students and recent graduates often place Education near the top because the degree, coursework, and graduation timing are central qualifications. If your relevant experience is stronger than your school history, Experience can come first. There is no universal two-year switch; choose the order that makes the strongest evidence easiest to find.


## How should you format dates so resume software can read them?


Consistency is the useful rule. If your work history uses “June 2024 – Present,” write “Expected May 2027” rather than changing to “05/2027” for school. Month names are easy for people to interpret and reduce ambiguity between U.S. and international numeric date formats.


Applicant tracking systems vary by vendor and by employer configuration. A simple text label placed next to the correct degree is a low-risk choice, but no single phrase guarantees a particular parser result. ## Should you include your GPA next to the expected date?


Include a GPA when the posting or employer asks for it. Otherwise, treat it as optional evidence. A strong GPA can help a student with limited experience, but there is no universal 3.5 cutoff that applies to every employer, industry, or program.


- **Follow the job requirements.** If an employer sets a minimum or requests a transcript, answer accurately rather than relying on a general resume rule.
- **Show the scale.** Write “GPA: 3.7/4.0” so the number has context.
- **Use your current, earned GPA.** Do not list a projected final number.
- **Label a major GPA clearly.** If it is relevant and your school calculates it, write “Major GPA: 3.8/4.0.” Do not present it as your cumulative GPA.


If you have little paid experience, your Education section may need to carry more of the story. ## What if you don't know your exact graduation date?


Use the most accurate estimate you can support today, and update it when your academic plan changes. Real timelines move because of transfers, co-ops, leaves, part-time enrollment, prerequisite changes, and program requirements.


- **You know the term but not the month.** Use “Expected Fall 2027,” then replace it with a month after checking your academic calendar or asking your registrar.
- **Your timeline changed.** Update both your resume and active applications where the employer allows edits. You do not need to explain a routine one-term shift on the resume.
- **You transferred schools.** Put the expected date under the institution that will award the degree. List the earlier school separately only if it adds useful coursework, credentials, or context.
- **You do not have a target term.** “Degree in progress” is more honest than inventing a date. Some roles require graduation within a stated window, so check eligibility before applying.
- **You left without completing the degree.** Do not imply that it was awarded. Depending on relevance, list the school with dates attended or describe completed coursework accurately.


Some employers verify education during a background check, while others do not. Because policies and the scope of checks vary, the useful rule is simple: keep the school, degree status, and timing truthful and current.


Application portals also vary. Workday, Greenhouse, and other systems can be configured differently by each employer. If a form requires an end date for an in-progress degree, enter your current expected month and year and follow any employer-specific instructions rather than trying to force the resume's typography into the field.


## When should you update the Education section?


After you graduate, remove “Expected” and show the actual completion month and year. If graduation is delayed, update the date before your next application. An old estimate is more confusing than a current one.


Coursework is also contextual. Keep a short, relevant selection when it helps prove knowledge you have not yet shown through projects or work. Remove or shorten it once stronger evidence fills the page, not automatically on a fixed anniversary. The same principle applies to a[resume summary](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-write-a-resume-summary-in-2024-10-examples?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) : include one when it adds context, not because every resume needs the same sections.

## What should you check before sending the resume?


- The degree status and expected date are accurate today.
- The month-and-year style matches the other dates on the page.
- The date sits with the correct school and degree.
- Any GPA is current, labeled, and shown with its scale.
- Coursework earns its space for this particular role.
- The exported PDF contains selectable text in a sensible reading order.


That last check takes less than a minute: select all the text in the exported file, copy it, and paste it into a plain-text editor. It is not a perfect simulation of every ATS, but it can reveal missing text or a scrambled reading order before you apply.

## Frequently asked questions


### Can I write “Expected 2027” if I don't know the month?


Yes, if 2027 is the most precise truthful estimate you have. A month and year are clearer when available, and a term such as “Expected Fall 2027” gives more context than a bare year. Confirm the timing with your school when you can.


### Do I remove the expected graduation date after I graduate?


Yes. Replace the estimate with the actual completion month and year, such as “May 2027.” If your degree was not completed, do not present the date as a graduation date.


### Should the online application use the same date?


Use the same underlying expected month and year, but follow the form's required format. Employer rules and portal fields differ, so read the label carefully and answer any eligibility question exactly as asked.


### How much relevant coursework should I include?


Include only courses that help show readiness for the target role and are not already obvious from your degree. A short line is usually enough. If projects, internships, or work experience make the same point more convincingly, use the space for that stronger evidence.


### Can I abbreviate the month?


Yes. “Sep 2027” and “September 2027” are both understandable. Pick one style and use it consistently across Education and Experience; avoid ambiguous numeric dates when your resume may be read internationally.
