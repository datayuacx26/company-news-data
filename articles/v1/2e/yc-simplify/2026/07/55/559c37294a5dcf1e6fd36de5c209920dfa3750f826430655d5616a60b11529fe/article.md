# How Many Bullet Points Per Job on a Resume? We Counted 11,819 Jobs

Three to five bullet points per job, for most jobs. Give your current role, or the one closest to the job you are applying to, five or six. Give old, junior, or unrelated roles one or two. Past six bullets on a single job you are usually padding.


That is also the advice every career site gives, and for once the data backs it up. What the advice leaves out is the part that matters when you actually sit down to edit: how to split bullets between the job you had last year and the job you had in 2019. So we counted ours.


## What 11,819 real jobs look like


Most answers to this question are one writer's guess at a round number. More than two million people use Simplify, so we went and counted. Across 11,819 individual jobs listed on resumes edited on Simplify in the twelve months to July 2026:


Bullets on the job Share of jobs


1 6.2%


2 14.6%


3 24.4%


4 19.4%


5 13.5%


6 6.8%


7 4.4%


8 or more 10.5%


(0.2% listed a job with no bullets at all.) Median: 4. Mean: 4.35. The 3 to 5 band covers 57.3% of jobs, and 3 on its own is the most common single choice.


So the conventional answer holds, which is worth saying out loud, because most resume advice does not survive being checked. But the middle of a distribution is the boring part. About 21% of jobs carry two bullets or fewer. Another 22% carry six or more, including the 10.5% at eight or more. Two in five jobs on real resumes sit outside the range everyone recommends, and they miss it in opposite directions. Work out which tail you are in before you count anything.


One honest caveat: nobody counts your bullets. No recruiter has rejected a resume for having six on one job and two on the next. What a reader does notice is a page too dense to skim, or a job you held for three years summed up in a single line. Bullet count is a proxy for those problems, not a rule of its own.


## How to spend your bullet budget


Across the same resumes, the median one carries 16 bullets in total across its entire work history, and most sit between 11 and 24. Treat that as a budget rather than a per-job quota. How you spend it tells the reader where to look.


Job seekers already front-load a little without being told to. In the same data, the first job listed on a resume has a median of 5 bullets. The second has 4. Everything from the third job down has 3. The instinct is right. Most people just do not push it far enough.


Spend bullets on relevance, not on seniority or how long you were there.


- **Current role, or the one closest to the job you want.** Five to six bullets. This is where a reader decides whether to keep going.
- **An earlier role in the same line of work.** Three to four.
- **Old, junior, or unrelated roles.** One or two, or just the title, company, and dates with nothing underneath. A bullet-free line is a legitimate choice and it reads as deliberate.


A concrete budget for someone with three jobs applying to a growth marketing role:


- Growth Marketing Manager, 2024 to present: 6 bullets
- Marketing Associate, 2022 to 2024: 3 bullets
- Barista, 2020 to 2022: no bullets, one line


Ten bullets, and a recruiter knows in two seconds what you want to be hired for. The flat version, four bullets each, spends twelve bullets giving the coffee shop the same visual weight as the job that qualifies you. Every bullet you spend on 2020 is one you did not spend on last year.


Each bullet still has to earn its line. The[XYZ format](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-use-the-xyz-resume-format?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) is the fastest way to check whether one does.


## The two ways this goes wrong


**Too few.** About 21% of jobs use two bullets or fewer. Sometimes that is correct, on a job from eight years ago in a different field. On a recent, relevant job it reads like nothing happened there.


- *Before, 1 bullet:* "Responsible for managing the company's social media accounts."
- *After, 3 bullets:* "Ran organic social across four channels for a 40-person B2B software company." / "Grew LinkedIn following from 2,000 to 11,000 in 14 months." / "Built the content calendar and brief template the marketing team still uses."


Same job, same facts, nothing inflated. The one-bullet version confirms the job existed. The three-bullet version says what happened in it. If you genuinely cannot find three things to say about a recent role, that is information about the job rather than about your writing, and our guide to[writing a resume with no experience](https://simplify.jobs/blog/writing-a-resume-with-no-experience?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) covers what else can carry the page.


**Too many.** Six or more bullets shows up on 22% of jobs, eight or more on 10.5%. Past six, a reader stops reading bullets and starts skimming past them, so your best one gets skipped alongside your worst.


- *Before, 8 bullets, abridged:* "Attended daily standups with the engineering team." / "Collaborated with cross-functional partners on product requirements." / "Supported QA during release cycles." / "Participated in code review." / "Developed new features for the customer web app." / "Fixed bugs reported by users." / "Wrote documentation." / "Assisted with onboarding new engineers."
- *After, 4 bullets:* "Shipped 12 features in React and TypeScript to a web app used by 30,000 people a month." / "Cut p95 dashboard load time from 3.1s to 900ms by rewriting its data layer." / "Owned release QA for a biweekly deploy cycle spanning three engineering teams." / "Wrote the onboarding docs that took new engineer ramp-up from three weeks to one."


The eight-bullet version describes a job. The four-bullet version describes what one person did in it. Look at what got cut: everything that described attendance rather than output. The fifteen most common opening verbs in our data are developed, designed, led, collaborated, managed, built, engineered, conducted, implemented, spearheaded, executed, supported, delivered, created, and enhanced. Two of them, collaborated and supported, mostly describe being in the room while something happened. Those are the bullets to fold into a stronger one or cut.


## Common questions


**How many bullet points should my current job have?**
Five or six, and more than any other job on the page. In our data the first job listed on a resume already gets a median of 5 bullets versus 3 for everything from the third job down. The exception: your current job is not the relevant one. Then cut it back and give the space to the job that is.


**Should I use bullet points or paragraphs on a resume?**
Bullets, for work experience. Recruiters skim, and a paragraph makes them read the whole thing to find the one number they care about. Paragraphs are fine at the top of the page, in a summary, which is a different section doing a different job. (We have a[guide to writing a resume summary](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-write-a-resume-summary-in-2024-10-examples?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) , plus a companion post on[whether that summary should be in first person](https://simplify.jobs/blog/should-a-resume-summary-be-in-first-person?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) .)


**How long should each resume bullet point be?**
One line, two at the outside. A bullet that wraps onto a third line is usually doing two jobs and should be split, or it can lose its first four words. "Responsible for managing the" is often exactly those four words.


**Do internships need bullet points?**
Yes, if the internship is recent or relevant, and two or three is plenty. An internship listed with nothing under it looks like something you are hiding. Once you have a few years of full-time work behind you, internships can drop to a single line, then off the resume.
