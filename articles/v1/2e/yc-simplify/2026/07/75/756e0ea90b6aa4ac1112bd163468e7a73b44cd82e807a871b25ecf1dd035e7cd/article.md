# Should a Resume Summary Be in First Person? We Checked 23,000 Resumes

No. A resume summary should not use "I," "me," or "my." Write it in **implied first person** : you are still describing yourself, but you drop the pronouns. Instead of "I'm a product designer with six years of experience," write "Product designer with six years of experience."


That's the whole answer. The rest of this post covers what implied first person looks like at different career stages, the one place where this rule flips, and some data on how job seekers actually write summaries, because we checked.


## What 23,000 real resume summaries look like


Most advice on this question is one career coach's opinion. More than two million people use Simplify, so we pulled our own data instead. Out of 23,191 resume summaries edited on Simplify in the past twelve months:


- 61% contain no personal pronouns at all
- 37% use a first-person pronoun somewhere ("I," "my," or "me")
- Fewer than 1% are written in third person ("she," "he," "they")
- The median summary is 64 words, roughly three to four sentences


Two things stand out. First, implied first person is already the majority convention. Recruiters see pronoun-free summaries most of the time, which is exactly why a summary built from "I" statements reads as the exception. Cut the pronouns and you move from the 37% to the majority that writes like it knows the genre. Second, almost nobody writes in third person, and the few who do stand out in a bad way. Referring to yourself as "he" or "she" on your own resume reads like your resume was written about you, not by you.


One honest caveat: no recruiter throws out a resume because it contains the word "I." This is a polish signal, not a dealbreaker. But summaries sit at the top of the page, recruiters skim them in seconds, and small conventions are how they sort people who know the genre from people who don't.


## What implied first person looks like


The fix is not just deleting pronouns. Deleting them forces a better sentence structure: you lead with the noun that describes you and follow with the evidence.


**New grad.**


- *With pronouns:* "I am a recent computer science graduate and I am looking for a software engineering role where I can apply my skills."
- *Implied first person:* "Recent Georgia Tech computer science graduate with two internships shipping production Python. Built a course-scheduling tool used by 4,000 students."


The first version spends its words on wanting a job, which every applicant does. The second spends them on proof. (If you're starting from zero experience, we wrote a separate guide to[writing a resume with no experience](https://simplify.jobs/blog/writing-a-resume-with-no-experience?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) .)


**Career changer.**


- *With pronouns:* "I spent eight years in retail management and I'm now transitioning into human resources."
- *Implied first person:* "Retail manager moving into HR after eight years hiring, scheduling, and coaching teams of 20+. Completed SHRM-CP certification in 2026."


**Mid-career.**


- *With pronouns:* "I'm a data analyst with five years of experience and my specialty is marketing analytics."
- *Implied first person:* "Data analyst with five years in marketing analytics. Built the attribution model that reallocated $2M in annual ad spend at a DTC brand."


**Senior / management.**


- *With pronouns:* "I lead engineering teams and I've managed budgets of over $10M."
- *Implied first person:* "Engineering director who has scaled teams from 12 to 45 across three product lines while owning a $10M+ budget."


Notice the pattern. Every rewrite starts with a role noun ("graduate," "manager," "analyst," "director") and gets to a number within the first two sentences. The pronoun rule is really a forcing function for that structure.


## Why the convention exists


Three practical reasons, none of them mystical:


1. **Space.** The median summary is 64 words. "I am a" spends three of them announcing something the document already announces: a resume is about you. Implied first person gives those words back.
2. **Rhythm.** When every sentence starts with "I," the summary reads like a list of claims. When sentences start with roles and results, it reads like evidence.
3. **Genre.** Recruiters read hundreds of resumes. Matching the standard register is like showing up to the interview dressed appropriately. It doesn't win the job, but it never costs you anything.


And to head off a common worry: applicant tracking systems do not penalize pronouns. Software doesn't care. This convention exists for the humans who read the summary after the software passes it along.


## Where the rule flips


The implied-first-person rule applies to your resume. It does not apply everywhere, and copying your resume summary into other places is one of the most common mistakes we see.


- **LinkedIn.** Your LinkedIn About section should use full first person. "I build data pipelines for healthcare companies" sounds natural there; "Data engineer with six years of experience" sounds like a resume pasted into a social profile, because it is. We have[15 LinkedIn summary examples](https://simplify.jobs/blog/15-linkedin-summary-examples?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) if you want to see the difference in practice.
- **Cover letters.** Full first person, always. A cover letter without "I" reads like a hostage note.
- **Portfolio and personal sites.** Your call. First person is the safer default.
- **Third person.** Never, on any of these. That fewer-than-1% group is not a style choice worth joining.


## Common questions


**Should a resume summary be in first or third person?**
Neither, strictly. Use implied first person: first-person perspective with the pronouns removed. "Managed a team of five" rather than "I managed a team of five" or "She managed a team of five."


**Can I say "my" in a resume summary?**
Same rule as "I": cut it. "My specialty is forecasting" becomes "Specializes in forecasting." About a quarter of the summaries we sampled use "my," and every instance we've seen survives the deletion.


**What tense should a resume summary be in?**
Present tense for who you are and what you do now ("Leads a team of eight"), past tense for completed accomplishments ("Cut onboarding time by 40%"). Same implied-subject style in both.


**How long should a resume summary be?**
Three to four sentences. The median on Simplify is 64 words, and that's about right; past 90 words it stops being a summary.
