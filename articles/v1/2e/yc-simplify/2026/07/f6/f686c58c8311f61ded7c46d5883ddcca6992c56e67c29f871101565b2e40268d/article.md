# Resume Tense: Past or Present? We Checked 27,000 Bullet Points

Past tense, for every job on the page, including the one you have right now. The conventional rule says present tense for your current role, and almost nobody follows it. Below is the data on why, plus the two cases where present tense still earns its place.


## What 27,000 real resume bullets actually do


Career sites agree on this one. Present tense for the job you hold now, past tense for the jobs you held before. It's tidy and grammatically defensible. It's also not what people write.


We pulled 26,981 bullet points from resumes edited on Simplify in the twelve months to July 2026, then looked at the first word of each one. Of those, 6,305 sit under a role the person marked as current, with no end date. Here is how they open:


- 76.9% start with a past-tense verb ("Led," "Built," "Designed")
- 17.8% start with some other base-form verb ("Manage," "Own," "Drive")
- 3.5% start with an "-ing" gerund ("Managing," "Leading")
- 1.8% start with a present-tense third-person verb ("Manages," "Leads")


Past tense beats present tense by roughly 43 to 1 in the exact place the rule says to use present tense. Bullets under past roles open with a past-tense verb 88.5% of the time, so there is a difference between current and past roles, but it's about twelve points. That's a slight tilt, not a switch being flipped.


One caveat on method: we classified tense with a heuristic on the first word of each bullet, suffix matching plus a list of common irregular past forms like "led," "built," "drove," and "wrote." It's approximate, not a grammar parser, so read the percentages as close rather than precise.


We also checked whether people stay consistent inside one job. About a quarter of current roles with two or more bullets mix a past-tense opener with a non-past one. So even among people who never switch tense between jobs, plenty switch between lines of the same job. That's the mistake worth fixing.


The fifteen most common bullet-opening verbs in the whole sample, in order: developed, designed, led, collaborated, managed, built, engineered, conducted, implemented, spearheaded, executed, supported, delivered, created, enhanced. Every one is past tense.


And the honest caveat: no recruiter rejects a resume because a current-role bullet says "Manage" instead of "Managed." Tense is a consistency signal, not a filter. But a resume whose tense wanders looks like a document edited five times and never proofread, which reads worse than either tense on its own.


## What each tense looks like on a current job


The case for past tense is that it reads fine even when the work is ongoing. Here is the same current-role bullet both ways, at four career stages.


**Intern or new grad, current internship.**


- *Present:* "Write test coverage for the payments API and triage bugs filed by support."
- *Past:* "Wrote test coverage for the payments API, raising branch coverage from 54% to 81%."


The rewrite is more than a conjugation change. Past tense pushes you toward finished results, which is why the second version has a number in it and the first one doesn't. (If you have no work history yet, we have a separate guide to[writing a resume with no experience](https://simplify.jobs/blog/writing-a-resume-with-no-experience?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) .)


**Mid-career, current role, three years in.**


- *Present:* "Manage the marketing analytics stack and report on channel performance."
- *Past:* "Rebuilt the marketing attribution model, reallocating $2M in annual ad spend across six channels."


**Career changer, current role, leaving the field.**


- *Present:* "Supervise a retail team of 22 and handle hiring."
- *Past:* "Hired and onboarded 31 seasonal associates while running a store team of 22."


**Senior or management, current role.**


- *Present:* "Lead three engineering teams and own the platform roadmap."
- *Past:* "Grew engineering from 12 to 45 across three product lines and shipped the platform migration two quarters early."


Read the past column straight through. Nothing in it suggests you left those jobs. The dates next to your job title already tell the reader you're still there, so tense doesn't have to carry that information. When it tries, it usually costs you a quantified result.


## Why the convention drifted, and when present tense is worth using


The present-tense rule comes from ordinary grammar. You describe ongoing work in present tense. But resume bullets aren't ordinary prose. They're clipped fragments with the subject deleted, and they get judged on whether they show completed, measurable work. Past tense fits that job better. "Reduced onboarding time by 40%" is a result. "Reduce onboarding time by 40%" reads like a line lifted off the job posting, which is the most common failure in the whole genre.


There's a practical reason too. You will edit your resume dozens of times over a career, and current roles keep turning into past ones. If everything is already past tense, moving a job into history is a date change. If you conjugated by role, every job change means rewriting bullets, and half-finished rewrites are exactly how you end up in that mixed-tense quarter.


Present tense does earn its place in a couple of spots. One is a standing responsibility with no endpoint: "Own the on-call rotation for four services" is a duty rather than an achievement, and past tense would misstate it. The other is the summary at the top of the page, which describes who you are now rather than what you finished. Our companion post on whether a[resume summary should be in first person](https://simplify.jobs/blog/should-a-resume-summary-be-in-first-person?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) covers the pronoun side of that question.


If you do go present tense in a current role, use it for every bullet in that role and don't let one stray "Led" in. Which tense you picked matters less than whether the block holds together.


## Common questions


**Can you mix past and present tense on one resume?**
Between roles, yes. That's the textbook rule: present for the current job, past for everything before it. Inside a single role, no. About a quarter of the current roles in our sample mix openers from bullet to bullet, and that's the version a recruiter actually notices.


**What tense should a resume summary be in?**
Present tense for what you do and who you are ("Leads a team of eight"), past tense for finished accomplishments ("Cut onboarding time by 40%"). Our full guide to[writing a resume summary](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-write-a-resume-summary-in-2024-10-examples?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) has ten examples by role.


**What tense do I use for a promotion at the same company?**
Past tense for the earlier title, and whatever you chose for the current one. List the two titles as separate entries under one company heading. The earlier role is finished work even though you never left the building.


**Are "-ing" bullets like "Managing a team of six" okay?**
They're allowed, and 3.5% of current-role bullets in our sample use them, but they're the weakest of the options. A gerund describes an activity in progress; a past-tense verb describes something you completed. Skip the gerund unless the bullet is genuinely about a process rather than a result.
