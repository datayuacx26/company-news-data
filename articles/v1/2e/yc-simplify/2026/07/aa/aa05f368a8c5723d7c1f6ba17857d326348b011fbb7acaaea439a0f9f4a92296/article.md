To get a UI/UX design internship, choose the design path you are targeting, build case studies that show decisions rather than only final screens, and apply with a portfolio version matched to the role. A strong case study explains the problem, your responsibility, evidence, constraints, alternatives, iteration, and outcome.


UI/UX is not one job. Visual interface design, product design, interaction design, UX research, and web design overlap, but employers evaluate different artifacts. Start with the work you want to do; then decide which projects and skills deserve attention.


## Which UI/UX Internship Path Should You Choose?


Choose one primary path for each application. A product designer can still show visual craft, and a researcher can still understand prototypes, but the portfolio's center of gravity should match the work named in the requisition.


Path Primary question Typical artifacts Strong portfolio evidence


UI and visual design How should the interface communicate and feel? Visual hierarchy, components, responsive screens, motion Polished screens plus the system and constraints behind them


UX and product design How should the product solve a user and business problem? Flows, wireframes, prototypes, research synthesis Problem framing, alternatives, decisions, testing, and outcome


Interaction design How should behavior change across states and inputs? State models, prototypes, motion, device interactions A complex interaction explained through states and feedback


UX research What do we need to learn, and how reliable is the evidence? Research plans, interview guides, usability studies, reports Method choice, recruiting, analysis, limitations, and decision impact


Web design How should content and interaction work across the web? Page systems, responsive layouts, prototypes, production assets Information hierarchy, accessibility, responsive behavior, and handoff


Search beyond one title. Product design intern, UX design intern, interaction design intern, experience design intern, UX research intern, web design intern, and digital design intern can all lead to relevant work. Manually exclude industrial, interior, architecture, software engineering, and mechanical product-design roles when they do not involve digital user experiences.

## What Should a UI/UX Internship Portfolio Include?


A student portfolio usually needs a small number of complete, relevant case studies rather than many shallow projects. Two or three strong cases can be enough to demonstrate range, but this is a practical recommendation, not a universal hiring rule. The right number depends on the role, the depth of each project, and the artifacts requested by the employer.


Nielsen Norman Group's[User Experience Careers report](https://media.nngroup.com/media/reports/free/UserExperienceCareers_2nd_Edition.pdf?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) distinguishes the evidence expected from designers and researchers. Designers can show finished work plus process artifacts; researchers can show research plans, reports, analysis, and the decisions their work informed. AIGA's[student portfolio guidance](https://dc.aiga.org/a-designers-guide-building-a-student-portfolio/?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) also emphasizes clarity about the work and the person behind it.


1. **1. Define the problem and audience.** State what needed to change, for whom, and why the problem mattered.
2. **2. Name your role.** Separate your decisions and artifacts from the team's work.
3. **3. Explain constraints.** Include time, access, technical, business, accessibility, or research limitations.
4. **4. Show evidence and alternatives.** Explain what informed the design and which options you rejected.
5. **5. Include iteration.** Connect critique, testing, or technical feedback to a visible change.
6. **6. Close with an outcome and limitation.** Use measured results when available and state what you would test next.


Avoid turning a case study into a decorative process template. Personas, journey maps, wireframes, and usability tests are useful only when they answer a real question. Label hypothetical projects honestly, do not invent research participants or business outcomes, and protect confidential material.


Make the connection between your portfolio and resume obvious.

## How Can You Get UI/UX Experience Without an Internship?


Create a bounded project with a real stakeholder and permission to share the work. A student organization, campus service, nonprofit, open-source project, local business, or research lab can provide constraints and feedback that a purely fictional redesign lacks. Agree on the problem, scope, timeline, access, deliverable, and portfolio rights before starting.


If a real stakeholder is not available, run a transparent independent study. Choose a narrow workflow, review existing evidence, recruit participants ethically when research is appropriate, document limitations, and label the result as a concept. Do not present speculative conversion numbers or an unsolicited redesign as if the company adopted it. Simplify's guide to[getting an internship without experience](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-get-an-internship-with-no-experience/?ref=simplify.jobs) covers additional ways to build early evidence.


🧭


****Credibility rule:**** a small, honest project with a clear decision trail is stronger than a polished case study built on invented users, results, or stakeholder approval.

## How Should You Apply and Reach Out for UI/UX Internships?


A design application should make one relevant case study easy to reach from both the resume and portfolio home page. Reviewers may have limited time, so lead with the role-matched project and make your contribution visible before asking them to navigate a long process story.


1. **Classify the role.** Decide whether the work is primarily UI, product/UX, interaction, research, or web design.
2. **Check eligibility.** Confirm degree, graduation timing, location, schedule, portfolio format, work authorization, and any GPA rule.
3. **Select the portfolio version.** Reorder projects and introduction copy around the role; do not fabricate a new specialty.
4. **Make artifacts accessible.** Test every link, remove permissions barriers, add readable captions, and provide context without requiring a live presentation.
5. **Write a specific note when outreach is appropriate.** Reference the role and one relevant decision or question. Do not promise a video will raise response rates or send repeated generic follow-ups.
6. **Track what you submitted.** Save the requisition, portfolio version, resume, date, contact, and status.


Cold outreach is optional, not a substitute for the application. A respectful note can clarify a role or create a useful professional connection, but there is no verified response-rate formula. Follow employer instructions and stop after a concise follow-up when no relationship or invitation exists.


Track the role definition, not just “UX intern.”


Save the exact role, term, portfolio version, requested artifact, eligibility rule, application date, and follow-up in one place.

## How Should You Prepare for a UI/UX Interview?


Prepare one case study you can present in ten minutes and discuss in greater depth. Practice a shorter version too. Your goal is to reveal how you make decisions, respond to evidence, collaborate, and work within constraints—not to narrate every portfolio screen.


- Open with the problem, audience, business or organizational context, and your responsibility.
- Explain why you chose each research or design method and what it could not establish.
- Show one meaningful alternative and the tradeoff behind the final direction.
- Connect feedback or testing to a specific change rather than saying you iterated.
- Prepare to critique an interface, sketch a flow, plan a study, or collaborate with engineering depending on the role.
- Use Simplify's[behavioral interview guide](https://simplify.jobs/blog/prepare-behavioral-interview-without-sounding-rehearsed/?ref=simplify.jobs) for teamwork, conflict, ambiguity, and feedback examples.


## Frequently Asked Questions


### Do you need to know how to code for a UI/UX internship?


You do not need to code for every UI/UX internship. HTML, CSS, or JavaScript can help with responsive web constraints, prototyping, and engineering handoff; two of the seven manually reviewed roles mentioned HTML or CSS. UX research and many product-design internships do not make coding a core requirement. Follow the exact posting instead of treating code as a universal gate. When you have technical experience, show how it changed a design decision, reduced handoff ambiguity, or helped you collaborate with engineering rather than listing languages without context.


### How many UI/UX portfolio projects do you need?


There is no universal portfolio-project requirement. Two or three complete, role-relevant case studies are a useful student target because they can show depth and some range without overwhelming the reviewer. One excellent case plus supporting work can still outperform several shallow projects. Choose cases that make your individual contribution, research or inputs, alternatives, decisions, iteration, and outcome visible. Remove redundant process artifacts and place the project most relevant to the internship first. If the application asks for a particular format or number of samples, follow that instruction.


### Can you get a UI/UX internship without a design degree?


You can get some UI/UX internships without a degree titled Design. Current postings can accept HCI, psychology, social science, computer science, communications, or another related background, especially when the work is research, interaction, or implementation-adjacent. Other roles explicitly require enrollment in a design program. Read the degree language before applying. Regardless of major, your portfolio or research artifacts must demonstrate the work expected by the role, and your resume should connect relevant coursework, methods, collaboration, and outcomes to that evidence.


### What is the difference between UX design and UX research internships?


UX design interns typically turn evidence and constraints into flows, interactions, prototypes, interface behavior, and product decisions. UX research interns plan and conduct studies, analyze qualitative or quantitative evidence, communicate limitations, and help teams decide what to investigate or change. Their portfolios therefore emphasize different artifacts: designers often show states, alternatives, prototypes, and iteration; researchers can show research plans, instruments, synthesis, reports, and decision impact. Some internships combine both, so classify the role by its primary responsibilities rather than relying only on the title.


### Where should you look for UI/UX internships?


Search the[Entry-Level UI/UX Jobs page](https://simplify.jobs/l/Entry-Level-UI-UX-Jobs?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) , employer career sites, university channels, student design networks, and accurate title variants. Useful searches include product design intern, UX design intern, interaction design intern, experience design intern, UX research intern, web design intern, and digital design intern. Manually inspect responsibilities because product design and UI/UX taxonomies can include physical-product design, architecture, interior design, software engineering, and other unrelated roles. Set alerts for the titles and locations that match your portfolio rather than relying on one broad search.
