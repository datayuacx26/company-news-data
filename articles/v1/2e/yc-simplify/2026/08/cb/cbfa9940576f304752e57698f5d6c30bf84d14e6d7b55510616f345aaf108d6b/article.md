# Do Recruiters Really Only Look at Your Resume for 6 Seconds?

Mostly, yes. A 2012 eye-tracking study found recruiters spent about six seconds deciding whether a resume deserved a real read. But those six seconds are triage, not reading. And in 2026, the first reader of your resume is often software, which reads every word.


I lead recruiting and employer branding at Simplify, and I spent 17 years in engineering before I moved to this side of the table. The six-second figure is real, but almost everyone misreads it, usually in ways that make resumes worse. Here is what it actually measured, and what has changed since.


## Where the six-second number comes from


The figure traces to a single source: a 2012 eye-tracking study of about 30 recruiters, run by the job site TheLadders. Researchers tracked where recruiters looked and found the initial fit or no-fit call took about six seconds, with attention clustered on a few fields, mostly titles, companies, and dates.


Two things get lost in the retelling. The study was small and is now fourteen years old, so treat the number as a rough gauge of a real behavior, not a law of physics. More important, the six seconds was never the whole review. Recruiters spent far longer on resumes that passed the first look. Those six seconds are a recruiter deciding whether to read, not reading.


That distinction changes the advice. If you think your resume gets six seconds total, you cram everything into a banner up top and treat the rest as filler. If you know the first pass is a gate, you build a resume that opens it quickly, then rewards the longer read.


The gate exists because of volume. Among Simplify users active in the last twelve months, the median user tracked 18 jobs, but the mean is 104, and the top 10% tracked 264 or more (n=1,271 active users from a random sample; "tracked" means saved or applied, so read it as search activity, not confirmed applications). The gap between 18 and 104 is the story: a small group of power users pursues hundreds of roles each, and every recruiter inbox feels it. Simplify as a whole processes over 100 million applications a year. When one opening can draw hundreds of applicants, a first pass measured in seconds is arithmetic, not laziness.


## What a recruiter's six seconds actually scan


When I screen a stack of applications, my first pass looks for four things, roughly in this order:


1. **Current title and company.** Does this person do something adjacent to the job today?
2. **Time in role.** Two years in the title reads differently than two months.
3. **One keyword confirmation.** Not a keyword list. One concrete signal that the core requirement is met, like the exact technology or license the posting names.
4. **Layout sanity.** Can I find the first three without hunting? A resume that fights me loses to the forty waiting behind it.


Notice what is missing: your summary's phrasing, your hobbies, your GPA, your font choice. Those matter at the reading stage, if you reach it. Triage runs almost entirely on the top third of page one, which is why that region matters far beyond its share of the page. Your current title, company, and dates should be legible there at a glance. Your summary sits in that region too; we covered[how to phrase it](https://simplify.jobs/blog/should-a-resume-summary-be-in-first-person?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) separately.


One thing I want to say plainly, because this statistic scares people into overdesigning: I have never rejected a strong candidate over layout alone, and I do not know a recruiter who has. Triage kills ambiguity, not imperfection. If I cannot tell what you do, you are out. If your resume is plain but clear, you are fine.


## The new first reader is often not human


The 2012 study assumed a person picks up the resume first. In 2026, at many companies, the first pass belongs to an AI screener or ranking system, and volume is what put it there.


The machine pass works nothing like the human one. Software does not skim. It parses the whole document in well under a second and weighs a bullet at the bottom of page two the same as a line in your summary. That quietly breaks some old advice:


- **Front-loading keywords matters less.** Stuffing the top of the page with terms to "beat the skim" does nothing for a reader that ingests the entire file.
- **Structure matters more.** A parser that cannot extract your titles and dates cannot rank you on them. Standard headings and a single-column layout keep the machine accurate; our guide to[writing an ATS-optimized resume](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-write-an-ats-optimized-resume?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) covers the mechanics.
- **Stuffing backfires.** Screening tools increasingly flag keyword-stuffed resumes, and anything that slips past the machine gets caught by the human in about two seconds anyway.


What has not changed: the human triage still happens, just later. The software produces a ranked shortlist, then a person like me runs the fast title-company-dates scan on the survivors. Your resume now has two readers in sequence, one that reads everything and one that reads almost nothing, and it has to satisfy both.


## How to survive both passes with one resume


The good news: both readers reward the same document. You do not need a machine version and a human version.


- **Keep the structure boring.** One column, standard section headings, real job titles. "Software Engineer II" beats "Code Ninja" with both readers.
- **Let the top third carry the human pass.** Current title, company, and dates visible at a glance, plus a short summary naming your role and your strongest proof.
- **Put quantified substance everywhere.** The machine reads all of it, and the human who gets past triage will too. The[XYZ resume format](https://simplify.jobs/blog/how-to-use-the-xyz-resume-format?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) is the cleanest template for bullets that carry numbers.
- **Match the posting's language honestly.** Use its actual terms where they genuinely describe your work, once, in context. Repeating "stakeholder management" eleven times is stuffing, and both readers notice.
- **Skip the tricks.** White-text keyword blocks, or hidden instructions telling the AI to rank you first. Vendors test for these, and a flagged resume does worse than a mediocre one.


## Common questions


**Is the 6-second resume rule real?**
Sort of. It comes from one 2012 eye-tracking study of about 30 recruiters, so hold the exact number loosely. The behavior is real: recruiters make a fast fit or no-fit call before reading closely. The six seconds decides whether your resume gets read; it is not the reading itself.


**Do ATS systems automatically reject resumes?**
Mostly no, and this is the most persistent myth in job searching. A typical ATS stores and organizes applications; blanket auto-rejection based on a resume scan is rare. The real mechanisms that knock candidates out are parsing failures (the system cannot extract your titles or dates, so a human never sees them cleanly) and knockout questions ("Are you authorized to work in the US?"). Fix your formatting and answer screening questions carefully instead of fearing a robot with a delete key.


**Does resume length matter for AI screeners?**
Not much for the machine, since it reads everything. It matters for the human pass that follows. One page for most candidates, two if you are senior. Length is a problem less because software chokes on it than because human attention does.


**How do I know if an AI screened my resume?**
Usually you cannot tell for certain. A rejection minutes after you apply, or at 3 a.m., is a strong hint that software made the first call, often through knockout questions rather than resume analysis. The practical move is to assume both readers exist for every application, because at many companies they now do.
