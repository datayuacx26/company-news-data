# Should You Put High School on Your Resume? When to Cut It (and Why 97% Already Have)

Yes, if you are still in high school or just arrived at college. Once you have a semester or two of college behind you, cut it. College replaces high school on a resume, and high school never comes back after your first full-time job.


That is the short version. The rest is where the line falls at each stage, why recruiters read a high school entry the way they do, and the two exceptions worth knowing about.


## The rule by stage


**Still in high school.** Include it. It is your entire academic record, so give it a real entry: school name, city, graduation year, GPA if it is 3.5 or above, and the honors that show initiative rather than attendance.


- *A high school student's education section:* "Westview High School, San Diego. Diploma expected 2027. GPA 3.8. Student body treasurer, first place in the county science fair."


**First semester of college.** Keep it for now if your college line would otherwise stand alone. "Enrolled" is not a record yet, and a strong high school entry does more for you than white space under a one-line degree.


- *An education section that works in October of freshman year:* "University of Michigan, B.S. in Computer Science, expected 2030. Pioneer High School, Ann Arbor. Graduated 2026, National Merit finalist, debate team captain."


**Sophomore year and later.** Cut the high school entry completely, including the school name. By now you have coursework, a club, a campus job, or a project, and any one of those says more about who you are today than anything from senior year.


- *Before:* "Ohio State University, B.A. in Marketing, expected 2029. Westerville North High School, diploma 2025. GPA 3.9, National Honor Society, yearbook editor."
- *After:* "Ohio State University, B.A. in Marketing, expected 2029. Coursework: consumer behavior, statistics, digital marketing. Social media chair, American Marketing Association chapter."


The freed lines are the point of the cut. Relevant coursework, a project with a number attached, or part-time work all pull more weight than a second school. If your experience section is still empty, our guide to[writing a resume with no experience](https://simplify.jobs/blog/writing-a-resume-with-no-experience?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) covers how to fill it without padding.


**Once you have held a full-time job.** Never. A high school line on a working adult's resume reads like the document has not been touched since college applications. Delete it and don't revisit the question.


## Why recruiters want it gone


Recruiters read an education section as a rough answer to "what has this person done lately." When a sophomore's resume still finds room for senior-year honors, the natural next read is that the college years must be thin. That is often unfair, since plenty of students simply never updated the section, but a recruiter can only judge what is on the page.


The second reason is arithmetic. A student resume is one page, and a high school entry with GPA and activities takes two or three lines. Those are lines not spent on the coursework, projects, and jobs that actually generate interview questions. I have never seen a high school entry win a college student an interview, but I have seen it make a resume read two years younger than the candidate.


The data says this cut is already close to universal. Of 77,136 resumes on Simplify that mention a college, only 3.1% still carry a high school line. That figure comes from text matching on resume content, so read it as directional rather than exact. The direction is clear, though: dropping high school is what nearly everyone does, and if your resume is in that 3.1%, consider this the sign to make the cut.


One honest caveat: nobody rejects a resume over this line by itself. It is a positioning signal, not a dealbreaker. But early-career resumes get skimmed in seconds, and small conventions are how recruiters sort candidates who know the genre from candidates who don't. It is the same reason a summary[drops "I" and "my"](https://simplify.jobs/blog/should-a-resume-summary-be-in-first-person?ref=blog.simplify.jobs) : cheap edits that make the page read current.


## The exceptions, honestly narrow


**A national-level high school achievement.** An Olympiad medal, say, or a national championship. Even here it is a judgment call that usually still ends in a cut once you are established in college, because the achievement can live in an awards or honors section without dragging the whole school entry along. If it is genuinely the most impressive thing on your resume, one line for it is defensible.


**Scholarships and hometown employers.** Some scholarship applications ask for high school outright, and if you are applying for a first job in the town where you grew up, the school name can mean something to the person reading. One line, no GPA, no activities.


That is the whole list. "I worked hard in high school" is true of most applicants and an exception for none of them.


## Common questions


**I'm a college freshman. Do I keep my high school on my resume?**
For your first semester, yes, if your college entry would otherwise be a single line. By the end of freshman year you should have replaced it with coursework, a club, or a campus job. Set a reminder for May.


**Should you put your high school GPA on a resume?**
Only while you are in high school, and only if it is 3.5 or above. Once you are in college, your college GPA takes over, and a high school GPA on a college student's resume suggests nothing newer has happened.


**I was homeschooled. What do I list?**
List it plainly while it is your most recent education: "Homeschooled, diploma 2026," plus dual-enrollment coursework or standardized test scores if they are strong. The removal rule is the same: once college gives you a record, the homeschool line comes off.


**What if I didn't go to college?**
Then your high school stays, at any age. It is your most recent education, so list the diploma and year in one line and let your work experience carry the page. The rule is about college replacing high school, not about hiding it.
