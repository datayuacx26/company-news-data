# Two Hundred and Fifty Years of American Invention

# Two Hundred and Fifty Years of American Invention


July 2026


Liren Chen, President and CEO, InterDigital


[AI](https://www.interdigital.com/blog?tags=AI)[Video](https://www.interdigital.com/blog?tags=Video)[Wireless](https://www.interdigital.com/blog?tags=Wireless)[Standards](https://www.interdigital.com/blog?tags=Standards)


This year, the United States marks 250 years since its founding. There are many reasons for America’s success, but I would argue that few have mattered more than invention, and the inventors who refused to accept that a problem could not be solved.


American history is full of them, and some of the most famous set out to change how people connect. In 1876 Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone and made it possible, for the first time, to hear another voice across a distance. A few years later, Thomas Edison was filing the first of more than a thousand patents, treating invention as a discipline in its own right and building a laboratory whose entire purpose was to create what did not yet exist.


The work of these pioneers and the many who followed turned a young country into the place where the modern world learned to communicate, from the telephone to the transistor, and from the first computers to the networks that carry our calls and messages today. The line runs all the way to the smartphone, a device that means you can hold the power of a telephone exchange, a television studio, and a research library in a single hand. What these inventors shared was not luck. It was persistence, technical skill, and a particular kind of genius: the willingness to keep working at a hard problem long after others had given up.


That same quality is what we recognize in our own inventors at InterDigital. The majority of our employees are engineers and scientists, and nearly all of these engineers and scientists are inventors. They spend years solving the hardest problems, working at the cutting edge of what wireless, video, and AI technology can do. Their breakthroughs do not arrive on a schedule. They come from deep expertise, from collaboration with the best engineers in the world, and from the persistence to stay with a question until an answer is found. They continue a centuries-old tradition of building on the successes of inventors who came before them.


In the U.S., what turned this individual genius into national success was the system built around it. The founders wrote a simple idea into the Constitution: that people who invent something new should be able to benefit from it. Article I, Section 8, Clause 8 of the U.S. Constitution gives Congress the power to secure for inventors, for a limited time, the exclusive right to what they create. The first Patent Act followed in 1790. It was a deliberate bargain. An inventor discloses how something works, and in return receives a period of protection. Abraham Lincoln, himself a patent holder, stated that this added the fuel of interest to the fire of genius.


Licensing can carry that fuel furthest. Numerous American inventors have used licensing to generate a return from their discoveries and have then reinvested their royalties in their research. This is one of the beauties of the patent system; it lets inventors share a breakthrough with the companies best placed to use it, and be paid fairly in return. Invention creates the value; licensing spreads it.


InterDigital has worked in that tradition for more than fifty years. From the start, research was at the core of our work. Our engineers helped move the world from analog to digital wireless networks, and we have contributed to every generation of mobile technology since, from the early digital systems through to the green shoots of 6G today. And we’ll continue to contribute to each generation of wireless for decades to come. We do not make phones or operate networks or manufacture TVs and laptops. We invent the foundational technologies these devices and networks depend on, contribute them to the global standards, and license them to the companies that build products and services. It is the same bargain the founders described, carried into the digital age.


The work continues but is changing. Wireless, video, and AI are no longer separate fields. They are converging. The networks of the coming decade will be shaped by AI from the ground up. The way we capture, compress, and watch video is being remade by it. Our researchers are working at the point where all three meet, because that is where the next set of foundational inventions will come from.


Two hundred and fifty years on, the lessons hold. Countries succeed when they reward the people who create new knowledge. The patent system was one of America's earliest and best inventions and is still doing its job today. Connecting good ideas with the benefit that should flow from them, is worth celebrating.
