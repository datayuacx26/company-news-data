# Custom AI signals: Define any buying trigger and act on it automatically

Predefined signals like funding and hiring cover the obvious triggers, but the one that predicts a purchase for your product is often specific to you. Custom AI signals let you describe any trigger in plain English and have AI watch for it across your accounts, score its confidence, cite the evidence, and launch outreach. Your best signal is usually one nobody else tracks.


## What is a custom AI signal?


A custom AI signal is a buying trigger you define yourself, in plain language, that AI monitors on your behalf. Instead of picking from a preset list of signals a vendor decided to build, you write the condition that matters for your product, and the system watches for it across the web and your target accounts. When it finds a match, it tells you why, with a confidence score and links to the evidence.


Predefined signals are a fixed menu; a custom signal is a request you write yourself. Funding rounds, executive hires, and hiring intent are valuable precisely because they are common, which also means every competitor watching the same feeds sees them at the same time. A custom signal is often something only you thought to track, which is exactly why it is underexploited. The trigger that best predicts a purchase for your specific offer rarely fits a category label.


## Why do custom triggers beat predefined signals?


Because relevance beats coverage. A predefined signal is broad by design; it has to be useful to every customer of the tool, so it captures a wide, generic condition. Your best-converting trigger is usually narrow and specific to your value proposition, and no generic signal matches it precisely.


Consider a company that sells a compliance-automation product. "Company just raised a Series B" is a fine generic signal, but it fires on thousands of accounts, most of which do not have an urgent compliance need. "Company in a regulated industry just announced it is expanding into the EU" is a far better trigger for that seller, because it points directly at a new regulatory obligation the product solves. The generic signal casts a wide net; the custom signal points at a live pain.


Custom triggers also let you act on signals that no vendor would ever build as a preset, because they only matter to you. A vendor is not going to ship a native "our top competitor just raised prices" signal, but that event is one of the strongest buying triggers you have, since it puts every one of that competitor's customers into evaluation mode at once. Custom AI signals turn that kind of specific, high-intent event into something you can monitor automatically.


## What are concrete examples of custom buying triggers?


The best triggers are specific and observable. A few map cleanly to real products.


-


**"A company announces it is opening its first office or entity in a new country"** for anyone who sells localization, tax, payroll, or compliance tooling tied to geographic expansion.


-


**"A competitor announces a price increase, sunset, or acquisition"** for anyone whose value proposition is a cleaner alternative. Displacement windows open the moment a rival changes terms.


-


**"A company publishes a job posting that mentions a specific technology or platform"** for anyone whose product integrates with, replaces, or complements that technology.


-


**"A company in a regulated sector announces a data breach or a new compliance mandate in their industry"** for security and compliance vendors.


-


**"A company launches a new product line that requires the capability we provide,"** like a brand that launches a subscription offering when you sell subscription-billing infrastructure.


-


**"A company mentions scaling a specific team or motion in an earnings call, blog post, or executive interview"** for tooling that supports that motion.


Notice the shape. Each is a specific, observable event with a clear line to your product's value. That specificity is what makes the signal fire on qualified accounts instead of everyone.


## How do you build and act on a custom trigger manually?


Without automation, running a custom signal means becoming a part-time analyst.


1. **Write the trigger precisely.** State the observable event and the accounts it should apply to. Vague triggers produce vague results, so define the condition tightly enough that a stranger could tell whether an account matches.


2. **Decide where the evidence lives.** Different triggers appear in different places: news, press releases, job postings, product pages, executive posts, regulatory filings. You have to know where to look.


3. **Monitor continuously.** The signal only helps if you catch it early, which means checking those sources on a schedule, across every target account, indefinitely.


4. **Verify each hit.** Confirm the event happened and genuinely matches your trigger, so you are not acting on a misread headline.


5. **Enrich, personalize, and send.** Find the right person, obtain verified contact details, write a message that references the specific trigger, and follow up.


Every step is doable. Doing all of them, across hundreds of accounts, for multiple triggers, without falling behind, is not realistic for a human team. This is precisely the work that is dropped first when reps become busy, which is why most teams never run their best custom signals at all.


## What is the honest failure mode?


Vague triggers, and unverified ones. A custom signal is only as good as its definition. "Companies that seem like they're growing" is not a trigger; it is a wish, and an AI told to watch for it will return a flood of weak matches. The discipline that makes predefined signals reliable, a clear observable condition, matters even more when you write the condition yourself. Precision in, precision out.


The second failure is acting without checking the evidence. A system that fires a signal but cannot show you why is asking you to trust a black box, and buying triggers misfire: a headline is misread, a rumor is treated as fact, an old event resurfaces and looks new. Without a confidence score and a link to the source, you cannot tell a strong match from a weak one, and a few embarrassing misfires will make your team stop trusting the signal entirely. That is why evidence and scoring are not nice-to-haves; they are what make custom signals safe to automate.
