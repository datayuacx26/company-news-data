# How to find companies by their tech stack (job description signals)

To find companies by tech stack, mine job postings for named tools and combine them with technographic detection like BuiltWith or HG Insights. A posting that asks for Salesforce or Marketo experience tells you what a company runs and often that they are scaling or replacing it. Artisan turns that read into a live tech-stack signal and acts on it.


## Why do job postings reveal a company's tech stack?


Because hiring managers have to. To attract a qualified candidate, a req has to name the tools the person will use every day. "Must have over three years in HubSpot" or "experience administering Zendesk and Jira" is a functional requirement, and it is one of the few places a company publishes its internal stack for free.


That makes job boards a richer source than most technographic databases for anything that lives behind a login. Website-detection tools read what loads in a browser: analytics tags, chat widgets, CDNs, marketing automation pixels. They are strong on front-end and martech and blind to a CRM, a data warehouse, a dialer, or a support desk. Job descriptions cover exactly those gaps because someone has to be hired to run them.


Read postings for three things: the named tool (what they run today), the seniority and scope (a "lead" or "head of" migration signals a decision in motion), and the volume of related openings (five sales roles at once signals a team scaling into new tooling budget).


## How do I find companies using a specific technology or tool?


Start with two lanes and cross-reference them. Lane one is technographic detection: BuiltWith, HG Insights, or Wappalyzer will return a list of domains that run a given technology they can see from the outside. HG Insights leans enterprise and paid; BuiltWith sells flat self-serve access from $295 up to about $995 a month. Use these for anything web-facing. For an enterprise technographic and intent option, see[6sense pricing and reviews](https://www.artisan.co/blog/6sense-pricing-and-reviews) .


Lane two is job-posting search. Query the major job boards and aggregators for the tool name in quotes, filtered to roles that would actually touch it. Searching "Salesforce" inside marketing reqs is noise; searching it inside RevOps and sales-engineering reqs is signal. Save the search and check it weekly, because the value is in new postings, not the standing backlog.


Where the two lanes agree, you have high confidence. Where only the job posting shows the tool, you have found something technographic databases cannot see, which is often the more valuable lead precisely because your competitors that rely on BuiltWith alone will miss it.


## How do I find companies using a competitor's product from their job postings?


Search for your competitor by name inside job descriptions, then read for intent rather than mere presence. A req that says "manage our existing Marketo instance" is a stable user. A req that says "migrate off Marketo and stand up a new platform" is a live deal. Same tool named, opposite meaning.


Competitor-tool users are the strongest cold segment you can build, for one reason: they have already decided the problem is worth solving and the budget is worth spending. The category is already sold; what you are selling is a switch. That halves the work of every message, because you can open on the specific limitation of their incumbent tool that your product fixes, and your reader already knows what you mean.


The honest failure mode is that every vendor in your space runs the same search on the same day. If a company posts a "replacing Salesloft" req on Tuesday, that req has been scraped by six of Salesloft's competitors by Wednesday. The raw list is a commodity. What separates you is how fast you reach out and how specifically you speak to the switch, not that you found the posting at all.


## When should I reach out after spotting a tech-stack signal?


Fast, but with a real reason. The migration or scaling window is measured in weeks, and the value of a tech-stack signal decays as a company moves deeper into an evaluation you were not part of. If you learn a company is standing up a new revenue stack, the useful moment is before they shortlist, not after.


Speed alone is not the goal, though. A same-day generic email is worse than a two-day email that names the exact tool, the exact role they are hiring, and the specific reason a switch tends to happen at their stage. The read has to show up in the message. That is the part manual workflows fumble, because by the time a rep exports a list, enriches it, and writes the sequence, the signal is a week cold and personalization was dropped for volume.

### Can technographic tools see internal tools like a CRM or data warehouse?


Usually not. Website-detection tools read what a browser loads, so they are strong on analytics, chat, and martech and weak on anything behind a login. That is why job postings matter: a company has to name its CRM, dialer, or warehouse in a req to hire someone to run it, which exposes tools no external scan can detect.


### How reliable are job postings as a tech-stack signal?


Reliable for presence, strong for intent when you read the scope. A named tool in a functional requirement is a near-certain confirmation a company runs it. The intent read comes from the role: "administer our existing" means stable, "lead the migration off" means in-market. Cross-referencing with technographic detection raises confidence further.
