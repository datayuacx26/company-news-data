# How to spot companies creating brand-new roles

When a company posts a job title it has never used before, it is telling you an initiative just got funded. A first "head of developer relations" or "go-to-market (GTM) engineer" role is a public flag that a priority is now real enough to hire against. Catch the title on its first appearance and you reach a buyer while the project is still forming.


## What is a first-hire-in-role signal?


A first-hire-in-role signal fires when a company posts a job title it has never used before. What matters is a specific new title entering the org for the first time, not a new person in an existing role and not simply a new function. "Head of AI," "revenue enablement architect," "community manager," "solutions engineer," each one is a small announcement that a particular bet just got a budget line and a name.


The distinction from a first-in-department signal is worth being precise about. A first-in-department signal is about a whole function being created, like a company that is standing up revenue operations (RevOps) or security for the first time. A first-hire-in-role signal is narrower and often more revealing: it is about a specific initiative inside or across functions. A company that already has a marketing team but posts its first "lifecycle marketing manager" is not building a new department. It is starting a lifecycle program, right now, with someone accountable for it.


## Why does a brand-new role predict an emerging initiative?


Because titles are expensive to invent. Companies do not create a new job title casually. A new title usually means leadership has decided a piece of work is important enough to own, staff, and measure separately from everything else. This decision almost always comes with a plan, a timeline, and, critically for sellers, a tooling gap the new hire is expected to fill.


New titles are also leading indicators. They tend to appear before the initiative is visible anywhere else. A company might post for a "GTM engineer" months before it publicly talks about scaling outbound, or a "prompt engineer" before it ships an AI feature. The req is often the first observable evidence that the project exists. This is exactly when you want to reach the account, while the plan is being written and the tools are being chosen, not after. The title also tells you what the initiative needs. Read the title as a spec:


-


**"GTM engineer" or "growth engineer"** points to investment in outbound infrastructure, data, and automation tooling.


-


**"Prompt engineer," "AI engineer," or "head of AI"** points to an AI build that will need models, data, evaluation, and infrastructure.


-


**"Developer relations" or "developer advocate"** points to a platform, software development kit (SDK), or API push that will need docs, community, and event tooling.


-


**"Revenue enablement" or "sales enablement architect"** points to a content, training, and enablement-platform decision.


-


**"Head of community"** points to community-platform and engagement tooling.


## How do you find companies creating a new specialized role?


The manual version is straightforward in theory and painful in practice.


1. **Define the titles that matter.** List the specific role names that map to your product. If you sell developer tooling, "developer relations" and "developer experience" are your triggers. Be specific; this specificity is what separates the signal from generic hiring noise.


2. **Establish a baseline.** For each target account, you need to know which titles the company has used before, so you can tell a brand-new title from a role they have hired for repeatedly. Without a baseline, you cannot detect "first."


3. **Watch for the title's first appearance.** Monitor job postings across your accounts for those titles showing up for the first time.


4. **Interpret before you act.** Confirm the title reflects a real new initiative and is not just a rename of an existing role. A "senior account executive" becoming a "strategic account executive" is not a new initiative.


5. **Reach the initiative owner.** During the req window, this is usually the executive sponsoring the hire. Once the person is in seat, it is them plus their manager. Write to the initiative, not the job.


The message is what makes this signal convert. Because a new title points to a specific project, you can be concrete: "Saw you're bringing on your first GTM engineer, usually that means the outbound stack is about to be rebuilt" lands because it names the exact thing your buyer is thinking about.


## What is the honest failure mode?


False positives, and the interpretation cost of avoiding them. Unlike a funding round, which is unambiguous, a new title requires judgment. Companies rename roles constantly. "Customer success manager" and "customer experience manager" might mean the same job or two different initiatives depending on the company. A title that looks brand-new might be a cosmetic rebrand of a role that already existed. Fire on every novel-looking title and a chunk of your outreach will land on non-initiatives, which reads as noise.


There is also a coverage problem. To know a title is actually new, you need a reliable history of every title a company has posted, which is hard to maintain by hand across a large account list. Most teams either check too few accounts to matter or check them too rarely to be early, and being early is the entire value of this signal. By the time a new role has been in seat long enough for a human to notice organically, the initiative's key tooling decisions are often already made.

### How do I avoid false positives from renamed roles?


Interpret the title before you act. A brand-new role reflects a new initiative; a renamed role does not. Check whether the company already has someone doing the same job under a different title, and whether the responsibilities in the posting describe new work or repackaged work. This judgment is the main reason teams automate the signal, since a system with reliable title history and context handles the distinction more consistently than manual checks.


### How soon after a new role appears should I reach out?


As soon as you can confirm it is real. The value of this signal is that it is early, often the first public evidence an initiative exists, so reaching out during the req window or the new hire's first weeks puts you in front of the tooling decision. Wait until the role has been filled for several months and the key purchases are usually already made, which erases the head start the signal gave you.
