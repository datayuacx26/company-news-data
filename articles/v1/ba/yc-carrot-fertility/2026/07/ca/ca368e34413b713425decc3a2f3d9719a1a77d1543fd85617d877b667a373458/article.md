# Hormone pellets: What to know about HRT pellets for menopause

Hormone pellets are an implanted form of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) that releases estrogen or testosterone gradually over time. It’s one of many[hormone therapy options for menopause](https://www.get-carrot.com/blog/hormone-replacement-therapy-and-menopause) , and has grown in popularity despite significant debate within the medical community.


This guide will help you have a more informed conversation with your care provider about whether or not hormone pellets make sense for you. We'll cover:


- What hormone pellets are and how they work
- The types available and their tradeoffs
- Side effects, safety concerns, and cost
- How pellets compare to other hormone therapy options
- Who this approach is and isn't right for


### Key takeaways


- Hormone pellets are small implants inserted under the skin that release estrogen or testosterone gradually over three to six months.
- Most hormone pellets used for menopausal symptoms are compounded, i.e. custom-mixed by a pharmacist, and not FDA-approved for this use.
- Leading medical organizations, including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), recommend FDA-approved hormone therapy options over pellet therapy due to limited safety data and the inability to remove a pellet once inserted.
- Some people find pellets convenient and report symptom relief, but the risks — including hormone overdose and unverifiable potency — are real and worth weighing carefully.
- A clinician who specializes in[hormonal health](https://www.get-carrot.com/journey/hormonal-health) is your best resource for making an individualized decision.


## What are hormone pellets?


Hormone pellets are a form of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in which tiny, solid cylinders — slightly larger than a grain of rice — are inserted beneath the skin. Once implanted, they release a steady, low dose of hormones directly into the bloodstream over a period of three to six months.


Unlike daily pills or weekly patches, hormone pellets operate on a set-it-and-check-it model: you have the procedure done, and the pellet does its work gradually, without a daily routine to maintain. This convenience factor is a big part of why they've gained traction, particularly among people managing[perimenopause symptoms](https://www.get-carrot.com/blog/what-is-perimenopause) .


Hormone pellets are most commonly used to deliver estrogen (in the form of estradiol) or testosterone, and sometimes both. They're used to address symptoms including hot flashes, night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, low libido, and vaginal dryness.


## How hormone pellet therapy works


The procedure itself is relatively quick and performed in an outpatient setting. Your care provider numbs a small area — typically on the hip or upper buttock — makes a tiny incision, and inserts the HRT pellet into the subcutaneous tissue (the fat layer just beneath the skin). The incision is then taped closed; no stitches are required.


From there, the pellet slowly dissolves and releases hormones into the surrounding tissue and bloodstream. The release rate can vary based on factors like your metabolism, activity level, and overall health, which is part of why absorption is unpredictable from person to person.


The pellets themselves don’t need to be removed. They dissolve completely on their own over the course of the treatment period, which typically lasts three to six months. Most formulations available in the U.S. are compounded, i.e. prepared by a licensed compounding pharmacy rather than manufactured by a drug company and reviewed by the FDA.


## Why choose hormone pellets for menopause treatment


Many people exploring menopause treatment options ask whether pellets are more convenient than pills or patches. In some respects, they are. But the presence of reported benefits does not mean pellets are the right or safest choice for everyone.


Here's what tends to drive interest:


- **Steady hormone levels.** Since pellets release hormones continuously, they don't produce the peaks and troughs associated with daily pills or weekly patches. For some people, this consistency translates to more stable symptom management.
- **Less frequent dosing.** A procedure every few months instead of a daily pill or patch you have to remember to change is appealing, especially for people who struggle with routine medication adherence.
- **Interest in "bioidentical" formulations.** Compounded hormone pellets are often marketed as[bioidentical](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/15660-bioidentical-hormones) , meaning their chemical structure is identical to the hormones your body produces naturally. However, "bioidentical" does not mean risk-free. Compounded preparations still carry uncertainty around potency and purity.
- **Symptom relief.** Some people[report meaningful improvements](https://www.napgo.org/article/147434-comparison-of-two-delivery-methods-of-bioidentical-hormone-replacement-therapy) in hot flashes, sleep, energy, and libido after starting pellet therapy.


## Types of hormone pellets


There are a few different categories of hormone pellets, distinguished by the hormone they contain and how they're formulated.


### Estrogen and progesterone pellets


Estradiol pellets are primarily used to address the classic symptoms of perimenopause and menopause. Estrogen pellets are typically prescribed alongside progesterone for people who still have a uterus. This combination is important because estrogen without progesterone (referred to as "unopposed estrogen")[increases the risk](https://www.cancer.gov/types/uterine/hp/endometrial-prevention-pdq) of endometrial cancer.


Note that there are currently no FDA-approved implantable estradiol pellets available in the U.S. The FDA has found that estradiol pellet formulations can produce unpredictable and fluctuating hormone levels, and has not approved their commercial use for menopausal symptoms.


### Testosterone pellets


Testosterone pellets are used for people experiencing low libido, low energy, and other symptoms associated with declining testosterone levels during midlife. Some providers prescribe them alongside estradiol pellets for a combined approach.


There are[real usage concerns](https://www.gynaecologyjournal.com/articles/1911/10-1-42-858.pdf) with testosterone pellets specifically. Because the dose cannot be adjusted once the pellet is inserted, too much testosterone can cause unwanted effects, including acne, oily skin and increased facial hair. Elevated testosterone levels beyond physiological norms after implantation have also been documented as a concern in the medical literature.


No FDA-approved testosterone formulation currently exists for managing menopausal symptoms. According to[ACOG's guidance](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2023/11/compounded-bioidentical-menopausal-hormone-therapy) , clinicians and patients should use a shared decision-making framework if considering compounded testosterone, and ACOG specifically recommends against pellet delivery due to the lack of safety data and the inability to remove the pellet if problems arise.


### Custom-compounded pellets


Most hormone pellets available in the U.S. are custom-compounded, which means they’re prepared by a licensed compounding pharmacy based on a provider's prescription. Compounding pharmacies play an important role in healthcare by creating formulations for people with specific allergies or needs that standard medications can't meet.


However, compounded preparations — including pellets — are not evaluated by the FDA for safety or efficacy in the same way that manufactured drugs are. This means potency, purity, and quality can vary from batch to batch and pharmacy to pharmacy.


The National Academy of Sciences[has recommended](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25791/chapter/1) that compounded hormone pellet therapies be considered for restrictions due to concerns about the safety and complexity of the delivery mechanism, and inconsistency in production methods.


## Pros and cons of hormone pellets


It helps to see the tradeoffs clearly before making a decision. Here's an overview of what's known.


**Potential benefits:**


- Continuous, stable hormone release without daily dosing
- Convenient since procedure is only required every three to six months
- Some people report improvements in mood, libido, and energy
- May provide relief from menopausal symptoms
- Allows for personalized dosing through compounding


**Known drawbacks:**


- Most compounded pellets are not FDA-approved
- Requires a minor surgical procedure with associated recovery
- Hormone overdose risk, particularly with testosterone, because dosing cannot be adjusted after insertion
- Side effects including acne, hair growth, mood changes, and breast tenderness
- Variable absorption and limited long-term safety data
- Insurance rarely covers the cost

## Hormone pellets for women in menopause: What to expect


If you and your care provider decide that pellet therapy is appropriate for you, here's a general sense of the timeline.


**The**[insertion procedure](https://trocarsupplies.com/pages/insertion-procedure) **itself is less than 15 minutes.** HRT pellets are placed under the skin through a small incision in the hip or buttock and the site is taped closed. Your care provider will give you specific aftercare instructions, but typically you’ll want to avoid strenuous exercise or submerging the insertion site in water for a few days.


**Most people notice some initial changes within a few weeks of insertion** , though the timeline varies widely. Some feel improved energy and sleep quality relatively quickly, while others experience a more gradual shift over the first month or two. Because everyone's metabolism and starting hormone levels differ, symptom response is highly individual.


**The effects of hormone pellets for women typically last three to six months** , at which point you'd return for another insertion. High levels of stress, vigorous physical activity, and certain medications can increase the absorption rate and may mean you need to return sooner.


## Side effects and safety concerns


What are the side effects of hormone pellets? Common side effects include breast tenderness, acne and oily skin, bloating, mood swings, and — particularly with testosterone — increased hair growth on the face or body. Some people experience temporary discomfort, bruising, or swelling at the insertion site.


Less common but more serious risks include infection at the insertion site, pellet extrusion (when the pellet works its way out of the skin), blood clots, and elevated hormone levels beyond what is considered physiologically normal. Estrogen taken without progesterone in someone who still has a uterus carries a risk of endometrial cancer, so progesterone is typically prescribed alongside estradiol-based therapies.


The compounding factor adds another layer of uncertainty. Potency and purity can vary because most hormone pellets are not manufactured under FDA oversight. In 2018,[the FDA investigated](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/statement-improving-adverse-event-reporting-compounded-drugs-protect-patients) one major hormone pellet manufacturer and identified more than 4,000 adverse events that had not been previously reported to the agency. Among the possible events identified were endometrial cancer, stroke, heart attack, and deep vein thrombosis.

Since compounded hormone pellets are not FDA-approved, most insurance plans classify them as elective and will not cover the expense. Annual costs for pellet therapy range from $1,200 to $2,800 for the pellets alone, and that doesn’t include provider visits or lab work. Over time, the total cost is substantially higher than FDA-approved options, which, with insurance, might cost a fraction of that amount annually.


[FDA-approved options](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/hormone-replacement-therapies-can-help-women-bothersome-menopausal-symptoms) include patches, gels, and oral formulations. These forms of hormone therapy have more established dosing and monitoring guidelines, a longer safety track record, and the flexibility to be modified or discontinued as your needs change. These are meaningful benefits that are worth weighing against the convenience of pellets.


## Who should (and shouldn't) consider hormone pellet therapy


People who may find pellets worth exploring:


- Those in perimenopause or menopause with persistent, disruptive symptoms that haven't responded well to other hormone therapy delivery methods
- People who have difficulty maintaining a consistent daily or weekly medication routine
- Those who have had detailed conversations with a hormone specialist and understand the regulatory and safety context


People who should generally avoid hormone pellets:


- Those with a personal or family history of hormone-sensitive cancers, including breast cancer and certain types of uterine cancer
- People with a history of blood clots, stroke, or heart attack
- Those with active liver disease
- Anyone with unexplained vaginal bleeding (until evaluated by a care provider)
- People who are pregnant or trying to get pregnant


These categories are not intended to be prescriptive. Your individual health history matters more than any general list. The right starting point before making any decision is to seek an evaluation with a care provider who specializes in hormonal health.


## FAQ


### What are hormone pellets?


Hormone pellets are small, solid implants about the size of a grain of rice that are inserted under the skin and release hormones (typically estradiol, testosterone, or both) steadily into the bloodstream over three to six months. They are a form of hormone replacement therapy used primarily to manage menopausal symptoms and hormone imbalances. Most formulations available in the U.S. are compounded, meaning they are not FDA-approved.


### Are hormone pellets safe?


Some people use hormone pellets without significant problems, and[some studies](https://www.napgo.org/article/147434-comparison-of-two-delivery-methods-of-bioidentical-hormone-replacement-therapy) suggest potential symptom relief. However, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists specifically[recommends against](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/clinical-consensus/articles/2023/11/compounded-bioidentical-menopausal-hormone-therapy) pellet therapy because of the lack of safety data and the fact that the pellet cannot be removed if side effects occur or if dosing needs to change.


The broader medical consensus from ACOG, the FDA, and the National Academy of Sciences is that FDA-approved hormone therapy options such as pills, patches, or gels/creams have a more established safety profile and should be considered before pursuing compounded pellets.


### Who is a good candidate for hormone pellets?


Hormone pellets may be worth discussing with your care provider if you have persistent menopausal symptoms that haven't responded well to other forms of hormone therapy, and if you have a clear picture of the regulatory and safety landscape. Good candidates are generally those who do not have a history of hormone-sensitive cancers, blood clots, stroke, or liver disease. A thorough evaluation and baseline lab work should always precede this decision.


### Can hormone pellets help with menopause belly fat?


The evidence is limited. Hormonal changes during perimenopause and menopause, particularly declining estrogen, do contribute to shifts in fat distribution such as[increased abdominal fat](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8569454/) . Hormone therapy in general has been studied in relation to body composition changes during this transition, and[some research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7097676/) suggests it may help. However, there is not strong clinical evidence specifically demonstrating that hormone pellets reduce menopause-related abdominal fat.


If body composition changes during menopause are a concern for you, a conversation with your care provider about the full range of evidence-based options — including lifestyle factors, diet, and[physical activity](https://www.get-carrot.com/blog/menopause-and-exercise) — is the most useful place to start.


### How much do hormone pellets cost?


Hormone pellet therapy typically costs between $300–$700 per insertion, though prices vary significantly by clinic, geographic location, and the number of pellets needed. Since most people require two to four insertions per year, annual costs for the pellets alone commonly range from $1,200–$2,800. This does not include initial consultation fees, lab work, or follow-up visits, which can add substantially to the total.


Since compounded hormone pellets are not FDA-approved, most insurance plans do not cover them. FDA-approved hormone therapy alternatives (patches, gels, and oral medications) are typically far less expensive when covered by insurance.
