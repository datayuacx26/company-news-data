# Leading with resilience: How Canadian manufacturers can manage risk and build long-term stability

## What manufacturers are frequently asking


The guide addresses the most pressing resilience questions facing Canadian manufacturing leaders today, including:


##


Manufacturing resilience is the capacity to anticipate disruptions before they arise, adapt with agility when they do, and emerge stronger on the other side. Rather than treating risk management as a reactive safeguard, truly resilient organizations embed it into strategy, operations, and culture — across[property](https://www.marsh.com/en/industries/manufacturing/insights/manufacturing-risk-resilience-guide-property.html) ,[cyber](https://www.marsh.com/en/industries/manufacturing/insights/manufacturing-risk-resilience-guide-cyber.html) ,[climate](https://www.marsh.com/en/industries/manufacturing/insights/manufacturing-risk-resilience-guide-supply-chain.html) ,[supply chain](https://www.marsh.com/en/industries/manufacturing/insights/manufacturing-risk-resilience-guide-people.html) , and people risks. By treating resilience as a capability to be measured, practiced, and scaled, manufacturers can protect their people, assets, and processes while driving long-term, sustainable performance.


##


Canadian manufacturers face five critical and interconnected risk categories: property risks (fire, equipment failure, business interruption); cyber threats to increasingly connected operational technology systems; physical and transition risks from climate change; supply chain vulnerabilities — with Marsh data showing[65% of organizations have at least one critical single point of failure](https://www.marsh.com/en/services/business-interruption-supply-chain/insights/supply-chain-risk-resiliency.html) — and people risks, including a projected[1.7 million unfilled manufacturing jobs in Canada by 2030](https://cme-mec.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/CME-2025-Workforce-Report.pdf) . The[2026 Global Risks Report](https://www.marsh.com/en/risks/global-risk.html) ranks extreme weather as the third most significant risk expected to cause a material crisis globally.


##


The cyberattacks dominating the headlines today are largely insurable. In those cases where companies bought insurance, coverage responded and claims were paid.


While terms and conditions can vary, a cyber insurance policy can include comprehensive coverage in advance of, during, and after a ransomware attack. It may cover, but is not limited to, incident response planning, breach notification services, and restoration and repair.


##


Manufacturing faces four primary threat categories: targeted attacks exploiting OT system vulnerabilities; untargeted wide-area attacks scanning for exposed legacy systems; supply chain and third-party attacks from vendors with network access; and accidental threats from human error or misconfiguration. As IT and OT systems converge — connecting PLCs, SCADA, and HMIs to cloud platforms — cyber incidents can trigger production shutdowns, safety hazards, equipment damage, regulatory action, and significant financial losses. Cyber resilience must be treated as a core operational discipline, not an IT add-on.


##


The talent shortage in Canadian manufacturing is severe and accelerating. A[2025 Canadian Manufacturers and Exporters (CME) report](https://cme-mec.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/CME-2025-Workforce-Report.pdf) projects approximately 22,500 workers will retire per year in Ontario alone through 2033. Over 75% of Canadian manufacturers already have difficulty finding qualified workers, and an estimated 1.7 million manufacturing jobs are projected to remain unfilled across Canada by 2030 if current trends persist. Compounding the challenge: only 32% of Canadians aged 16 to 30 would consider a career in manufacturing ([Manufacturing Institute and Deloitte, 2025](https://themanufacturinginstitute.org/manufacturers-need-as-many-as-3-8-million-new-employees-by-2033/) ), and rapid adoption of automation and AI is widening the digital skills gap.


##


Climate change creates both physical and transition risks for Canadian manufacturers. Physical risks include facility and infrastructure damage from extreme weather (flooding, convective storms, wildfires); supply chain disruption as damaged roads, railways, and ports restrict material and product flows; water scarcity affecting cooling, cleaning, and processing operations; and heat-related safety and productivity challenges for outdoor and non-air-conditioned workforces. Transition risks include stricter emissions regulations and carbon pricing, evolving investor and insurer climate disclosure requirements, and reputational risk from perceived environmental harm. According to[Marsh's 2025 Climate Adaptation Survey](https://www.marsh.com/en/risks/climate-change-sustainability/insights/climate-adaptation-report.html) , 74% of risk managers have already experienced losses from extreme weather events.
