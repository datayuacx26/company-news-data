# 4 rules for improving healthcare market research engagement

# 4 rules for improving healthcare market research engagement


By Jennifer G Hall, Vice President of Research Operations at KJT


●


6 min. read


●


Aug 6, 2026


[Research](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/category/research/)


Improving healthcare market research engagement comes down to four things: understanding your participant population, stress-testing the research experience before launch, running complete screener protocols, and incentivizing both qualified and non-qualified respondents fairly and fast. Healthcare professionals are a finite, overworked pool, and every friction point in your process costs you future participation. This guide covers each lever in plain terms.


The Internet ushered in a boom era for online healthcare market research. Decades later, it's clear that the very same web-based tools and outreach that afforded us easy access to doctors, nurses, and other healthcare professionals might also be our downfall.


But we have an opportunity, as an industry, to come together and reverse the trajectory.


Medical professionals are often treated as if they're an unlimited commodity, but they're not. They're a limited resource, particularly in the upper echelons, and many entities are vying for their attention.


Yet, for years, an ocean of researchers, marketers, salespeople, and the like have been inundating their email inboxes.


In the instances we gain their attention, there's a lack of standardization in how we engage with these healthcare professionals. All of this has led to unopened emails, a general lack of responsiveness, and an overall decline in research participation for this highly sought-after respondent universe.


Back-of-the-napkin sample science easily explains why this is unsustainable. Take neurologists for example. Of the roughly 13,000 in the US, only about 5% participate in research at all. Of those, around 40% respond to a given survey, and just 20% qualify.


If we continue to ignore best practices, there's an immense risk that already-low response rates will inch ever closer to zero, and our industry and the world will be the worse for it.


But this isn't a story of doom and gloom. At least, it doesn't have to be. Like any ailing ecosystem, there's a critical period where intervention can restore balance.


Over my 23-year career in market research, I've honed and crafted strategies for building and fostering trust and respect within my field. I've made it my personal mission to make a positive impact.


The successes of these strategies are evidenced today at KJT, where we have worked methodically to maintain a strong, loyal following and above-average responsiveness when sampling.


Today, I share with you some of the basic guiding principles that led to our success. Consider it a roadmap to help build a more sustainable future in the world of sampling.


## Key takeaways


-


**Respect a finite pool:** Healthcare professionals are overworked and heavily recruited, so every friction point in your process costs you future participation.


-


**Communicate for a yes:** Lead with the study's purpose, your client's credibility, and clear compensation details.


-


**QA the full experience:** Walk the study as a respondent before launch and cut any step that adds friction or confusion.


-


**Screen completely:** Ask every screener question up front, a practice called incidence screening, so you can re-invite respondents later without burning them out.


-


**Incentivize fast and fairly:** Pay promptly, offer a choice of incentive type, and consider extending incentives to non-qualified respondents.


## Why is healthcare market research engagement declining?


In the last 20 years, US healthcare systems aligned themselves around the idea of patient-centered care, meant to provide the best experience and outcomes to patients.


In reality, physicians, nurses, and other practitioners are often consumed by "the drudgery of productivity-driven assembly-line medicine," according to[a 2011 paper in the Annals of Family Medicine](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3056855/) .


That leads to "cognitive overload and exhaustion that makes medical care anything but caring or patient-centered."


Patient centricity requires hospital administrators, pharmaceutical companies, clinicians, and the like to have a deep, rich, and continually evolving understanding of their industry.


They need to keep track of how the healthcare industry actually works, what's broken, and how the newest changes, trends, treatments, and medications impact patient outcomes.


Healthcare market research is central to achieving this goal, and our clients have already started their push towards patient centricity.


Yet, with only a small percentage of healthcare professionals participating in market research, we have an obligation to change some common practices that impede our ability to access this population, which is extremely sparing with their free time.


If we enact commonly practiced standards around engagements, we can begin to rebuild the overall researcher-participant relationship on a foundation of mutual respect and trust.


I foresee a future where we collectively drive the industry responsiveness to double digits and beyond. Ultimately, this will unlock our ability to reach fresh, relevant respondents and expand our insights into the world of healthcare and medicine.


**Key takeaway:** Healthcare professionals are a finite, overworked population, and every negative research experience shrinks the pool available for future studies.


## Rule 1: Know your population and how best to communicate with them


Reaching participants is the hardest part of any research project. It's crucial to understand what will appeal to your target group to make it easy for them to say yes. This is especially true of any highly sought-after or already skeptical population. A few things move response rates more than anything else:


-


**Lead with purpose and benefit:** Open with why the study matters and what the participant gets out of it.


-


**Borrow credibility from the client:** Name the trusted sponsor or institution behind the research where you can.


-


**Be clear about compensation:** Explain how much the incentive is, and how and when it arrives.


-


**Test new sampling methodology:** Don't assume the same outreach works for every specialty or panel.


## Rule 2: Thoroughly QA your participant experience prior to sample launch


As research professionals, we have an obligation to rigorously review the logic and language of a survey. But we often fail to zoom out and consider the entire participant's experience.


Take time to walk through the participant experience from a respondent perspective, ensuring every question and step is necessary. Cut everything that causes friction or confusion.


For example, one platform we use has reCAPTCHA automatically enabled. When QA'ing the respondent engagement, we realized this was an unnecessary step for specific sample sets, so we set rules in place for when to appropriately use this feature. We also adjusted the language respondents see. Informing them that they "are human" for properly passing the security feature seems a little silly and disingenuous, so we removed this prompt completely.


Remember, every second spent on your survey is time spent away from patients, family, or rest. Making the process as smooth as possible is the key to getting respondents to re-engage and want to return to the next research opportunity.


## Rule 3: When qualifying respondents, ask every screener question, even if you intend to terminate them


Imagine this common scenario:


-


Your respondent enters the 10-question screener portion of the survey.


-


On question five, the participant disqualifies, and they're terminated before they can answer questions 6 through 10.


-


You later realize your sample is too small and you need to cast a wider net, so you adjust criteria.


-


You've already accessed most of the available sample, so you require those who did respond to come back and rescreen to see if they qualify this second time.


-


Now you have to repeat this scenario multiple times, asking terminated folks to rescreen continuously, until you achieve an ample sample size.


This poor practice burns out respondents and turns them off from responding to a future invite. Ultimately, it also diminishes your ability to achieve optimal sample sizes, dropping your return rate on rescreening by 30% or more.


If a respondent is terminated three to five times in a short period, chances are high that they'll ignore future invitations for any research.


Instead, ask every participant all of your questions. We often refer to this as incidence screening. If you change your qualification criteria later, you already know their answers, and you can re-invite them without additional friction by notifying them that they now qualify.


## Rule 4: Incentivize fairly and swiftly, and consider extending to your non-qualified sample


Show respect to each respondent by compensating appropriately while communicating clearly and thoroughly.


-


**Set expectations in the invite:** Clearly identify the engagement expectations, incentive amount, and method of payment.


-


**Incentivize immediately:** Send incentives right after an engagement, or no later than five business days after completion. It's a small step with an outsized impact on the overall relationship.


-


**Offer real choice:** Provide a host of options for collecting incentives. Not everyone wants an Amazon.com gift card, and the more options you provide, the better.


-


**Track what's popular:** Use a tool that lets you track the most popular incentives over time, so you can better understand your audience and support future business decisions.


-


**Standardize your rates:** Set incentive rates and stick to them. Refrain from increasing amounts as response rates drop. Nothing turns respondents off quicker than changing rates on them for each invite, or across studies.


-


**Explain any increase:** If you do raise an incentive, send a specialized invite with the reason for the change. For example, "Our goal is to close this survey within the next 48 hours, so we're offering an additional bonus of..."


-


**Always say thanks:** Thank respondents for their participation. It's a small, easy way to show mutual respect and appreciation.


If you terminate a respondent, consider compensating them for their time to respond to your request.


-


**Keep loyalty high:** This encourages respondents to return at the next opportunity.


-


**Automate the send:** You can minimize manual work by programmatically sending incentives to non-qualifiers via an API integration. This instant acknowledgment of their time, despite their qualification, softens the blow and offers a consolation. It's another opportunity to show respect and appreciation for their loyalty to research as a whole.


By using incidence screening practices, you can sift through your sample for your ideal niche while minimizing the risk of over-abusing your sample.


The best advice I can give is to always consider how the respondent will feel about the interaction, and whether it would leave them open to coming back.


At KJT, we go a step further by compensating respondents when it's necessary to reschedule an interview.


Scheduling conflicts are expected and not unusual. When schedules must change, each respondent is fully compensated for their originally scheduled interview slot.


Our belief: they were courteous enough to hold that time for the research, so it's only appropriate that we compensate them accordingly.


## How to deliver research incentives that actually improve engagement


Offering an incentive isn't what drives return participation. How, and how fast, you deliver it is.


Three things matter most:


-


**Speed:** Incentives that land within minutes of completion, not days later, are what make a respondent open your next invite. The gap between finishing a study and getting paid is where goodwill is won or lost.


-


**Choice:** Letting respondents pick their incentive type, whether a prepaid card, a gift card, or a charitable donation, raises perceived value without raising your cost. A fixed $50 feels bigger when the recipient chooses how they receive it.


-


**Automation:** Sending incentives by hand doesn't scale. An API integration lets you trigger payments the moment a study closes, with no spreadsheets and no manual tracking.
