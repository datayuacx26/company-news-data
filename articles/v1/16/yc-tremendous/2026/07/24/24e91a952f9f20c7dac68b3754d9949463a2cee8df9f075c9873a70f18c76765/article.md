# Understanding bonuses for employees: Types, tax rules, and best practices

In the past, a competitive salary was often enough to keep employees around. Today, it takes more to retain top performers. That’s why many organizations are rethinking how to use bonuses as a way to[boost productivity](https://wol.iza.org/articles/performance-related-pay-and-labor-productivity/long) , engagement, and retention.


Here's an overview of the main types of employee bonuses, how they're taxed, and tips on how to build a bonus structure that’s worth the investment.


## Key takeaways


-


A **bonus** is designed to reward a specific outcome without permanently increasing compensation. This gives employers more flexibility than a raise.


-


There are many types of bonuses that serve different purposes, from performance bonuses that motivate employees to hit specific goals to retention bonuses that entice top performers to stick around.


-


Bonus structure and clarity matter as much as the dollar amount. Employees have to understand how to earn the bonus and trust that it’s fair.


-


In the U.S., bonuses are taxed as supplemental wages. Cash-equivalent rewards like gift cards count, too.


-


A strong bonus program keeps criteria simple and achievable.


## What are employee bonuses?


**Employee bonuses** are additional compensation beyond regular salary or wages. They can be tied to individual performance, team milestones, or the company’s overall results.


> What separates a bonus from a raise is flexibility. A raise is a permanent increase in compensation, but a bonus rewards a specific outcome and then resets, so you can shift what you’re rewarding as priorities change.


Done right, bonuses can drive multiple business outcomes at once. They motivate positive behaviors such as hitting sales targets or referring strong job candidates. They also boost morale, productivity, and retention by making employees feel valued and recognized.

## Types of employee bonuses


Most bonus programs fall into two categories: discretionary and non-discretionary.


-


**Discretionary bonuses** are awarded without predefined criteria or guarantees, such as a spot bonus after a strong quarter.


-


**Non-discretionary bonuses** are tied to specific metrics, milestones, or periods. You define the target upfront, and employees earn the bonus by meeting it. A bonus tied to a sales quota is a classic example.


Even when a bonus is guaranteed, the amount given may vary based on individual, department, or company performance.

### Performance-based bonuses


Performance bonuses reward employees for meeting or exceeding specific goals. These incentives[typically range from 2 to 20% of base salary](https://www.oysterhr.com/library/what-is-a-good-bonus-percentage) , depending on the role, industry, and seniority level.


A recent study found that[pay-for-performance is positively linked to job performance](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1039375/full) , but it works best for concrete, measurable output. The same analysis found a weaker effect on softer contributions that are harder to quantify, like collaboration.


The structure of a performance-based bonus matters just as much as the amount. Individual targets make sense when one person’s output is easy to isolate. Team-based bonuses are worth considering when results depend on the whole group’s effort.


### Referral bonuses


Referral programs incentivize employees to recommend qualified candidates for open roles. They’re among the most cost-effective ways to fill open positions, and referred hires tend to stay[70% longer than non-referred hires](https://www.icims.com/blog/how-employee-referrals-impact-retention/) .


Most payouts[land somewhere between $1,000 and $5,000](https://erinapp.com/employee-referral-bonus) , with hard-to-fill technical or clinical roles often climbing into five figures. A common setup pays the bonus in stages: a partial payment when the new hire starts, and the rest after 90 days. This structure intentionally rewards successful hires who stay, not just candidates who sign an offer.


### Signing bonuses


Signing (or sign-on) bonuses help attract top talent in competitive markets. These one-time payments can offset transition costs and incentivize candidates to choose your company. Payout amounts vary by industry and job level, but the general range is[between 5% to 20% of base salary](https://www.roberthalf.com/us/en/insights/research/sign-on-bonuses-the-reality-of-when-and-how-to-use-them) .


Most companies protect their investment with a clawback clause. If the employee leaves within a set window, usually 12 to 24 months, they’re required to repay some or all of the bonus.


How you time the payout matters, too. Some organizations distribute the full amount immediately, while others spread it across multiple installments to encourage employees to stay.


### Retention bonuses


A[retention bonus](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/retention-bonus/) is a one-time payment that rewards employees for staying with the company for a set period. While a signing bonus gets people in the door, a retention bonus is designed to keep them.


Companies often use retention bonuses during periods of change or friction, such as leadership turnover, restructurings, mergers, or acquisitions. They’re also commonly offered to keep employees who’ve received competing job offers.


The structure mirrors a signing bonus: usually a percentage of salary, paid as a lump sum or in installments, with a clawback if the person leaves before the end of the retention period.


These bonuses are an effective tool for retaining talent during a specific event, but they work best as part of a broader retention strategy that addresses the deeper reasons employees leave.


### Holiday bonuses


A holiday bonus is a year-end reward that recognizes employees’ contributions. It’s often paid as a fixed amount or a percentage of salary.


Because it's predictable and tied to the calendar rather than performance, a holiday bonus serves more as a gesture of appreciation than a true incentive. Like other one-time rewards, its impact can fade once the money's spent. That's why holiday bonuses work best when paired with[recognition throughout the year](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/ideas-for-employee-recognition/) .


### Spot bonuses


A spot bonus is a small, one-time reward that recognizes exceptional work. For example, after a product team finishes a successful launch, each team member might receive a surprise bonus in their next paycheck.


The immediacy of spot bonuses makes them particularly effective for reinforcing desired behaviors. They show that great work gets noticed and rewarded promptly.


On average,[spot bonuses range in value from $50 to $500](https://hyring.com/free-hr-toolkit/hr-glossary/spot-bonus) , but can go even higher for senior-level employees. They’re usually paid as cash or[gift cards](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/best-employee-gift-cards/) , and many companies now run peer-to-peer programs that let employees nominate each other to receive them.


Since spot bonuses are one-off payouts, they give managers the flexibility to reward outstanding work in real time without making permanent changes to employee compensation.


### Profit-sharing


Profit-sharing programs give employees a share of the company’s earnings, aligning their financial interests with the company’s success. The goal is to foster a sense of ownership. When the company wins, everyone shares in the rewards.


There are two main types of profit-sharing:


-


**Cash profit-sharing** pays out directly, usually once or twice a year, and is taxed as regular income right away.


-


**Deferred profit-sharing** deposits the money into a retirement account where it grows tax-deferred until it’s withdrawn. There are typically restrictions on when and how employees can withdraw their funds without penalties.


A main upside to profit-sharing is flexibility. Contributions are typically discretionary, so you can give generously in strong years and scale back or skip contributions when business is slower.


The allocation method is also up to your organization. Companies often use the[comp-to-comp](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/choosing-a-retirement-plan-profit-sharing-plan) approach, where profit-sharing contributions are proportional to employee salaries.


## Tax implications of employee bonuses


In the U.S., the IRS treats bonuses as “supplemental wages,” which follow different withholding rules than regular pay. Bonuses under $1 million are generally subject to a[22% federal flat withholding rate](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p15.pdf) .


They’re also subject to Social Security, Medicare, and in most cases, state taxes. State implications vary. Some apply special withholding rates to bonuses, while others treat them as regular income, and a few don’t tax wages at all.


The[same withholding rules apply to cash-equivalent rewards like gift cards](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/are-gift-cards-taxable/) or prepaid cards. Be sure to include the amount in your employees’ W-2s and income tax withholdings, no matter how small.


Clearly communicate the tax implications of your bonus programs to employees upfront. This will prevent surprises and ensure employees understand their actual take-home amount.

## Considerations when building an employee bonus program


A poorly designed bonus program can drain your budget and negatively impact morale. Here are a few important factors to consider that ensure the structure supports both your business goals and your employees.


### Organizational goals


A bonus program should align with your company’s strategic objectives. Start by identifying the outcomes you want to drive, then match bonuses to them.


For example, if your top goal is higher revenue, a[sales performance incentive fund (SPIF)](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/spiff-program/) on top of regular commissions can motivate sellers to push a specific product or meet an important timeline. If your hiring team is struggling to fill open roles, consider a referral bonus. Be sure to define clear metrics that motivate employees yet remain achievable.


### Employee preferences


Employees are motivated by different incentives. If you’re not sure what your team will respond to, survey your workforce to gather information on their[bonus preferences](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/employee-recognition-how-employees-really-want-to-be-rewarded/) . Some people may value immediate, smaller rewards, while others might prefer longer-term, larger incentives.


No matter what bonus structure you choose, be transparent about how bonuses are earned and when they’re paid. Clear communication about bonus criteria and timing builds trust and incentivizes participation.


### Budget and financial variability


Set sustainable bonus targets that work in both strong and leaner years. Review and adjust your bonus structures annually as your business evolves.


As a benchmark, SHRM recommends budgeting[at least 1%](https://www.hrcloud.com/blog/guide-to-employee-rewards-and-recognition-budget) of total payroll to employee rewards and recognition. Some organizations allocate up to 10% of payroll, depending on financial health and how central recognition is to the company culture.


### Fairness and inclusivity


Develop objective criteria for bonus eligibility and amounts. Ensure equal opportunity for employees to earn bonuses across departments and demographics.


Regular audits can help you identify and address any unintended biases in your bonus program requirements. Include clear appeals processes for merit-based bonus decisions.


## How to start an employee bonus program


Here are a few tips for getting a bonus program off the ground:


-


**Document everything.** Write down who’s eligible, how bonuses are earned, and when they’re paid. This prevents misunderstandings and ensures consistent application across teams and roles.


-


**Pick a reliable payout method.** For cash bonuses, running them through payroll is often the simplest route. If you’re offering multiple reward options or paying people who aren’t on your payroll,[an incentive platform](https://www.tremendous.com/blog/send-employee-recognition/) is a strong option.


-


**Pilot before you scale.** Before a full rollout, test your program with one team or department first. A small test can surface confusing rules, payout issues, or other surprises while they’re still easy to fix.


The dollar amount doesn’t dictate the success of your bonus program. The best bonus programs are the ones your employees understand and trust. Start small, measure your results, and use data to refine as you go.
