# Flash Macro: U.S. FOMC

On Wednesday, Kevin Warsh hosted his second Fed meeting as Chairman. The Committee elected to hold rates unchanged and made minimal changes to its meeting statement. That said, there was a more hawkish tilt to Warsh’s comments from the podium, with an emphasis on using markets to tighten on the Fed’s behalf (albeit he conveyed more dependence on market forces than we prefer):


- He noted with apparent gratification that the intra-meeting move in yields across the Treasury curve since his first press conference had been one of the sharpest in recent decades.
- Further, he commented that while the Fed did not hike rates this week, “that’s the beginning of the story, not the end of the story.”
- He placed significant emphasis on how the Fed is pulling back from issuing guidance, expressly so that they can receive a more unfiltered signal from markets (and by implication, allow markets to do more of the work when tighter financial conditions are needed).

In terms of his roadmap, Warsh emphasized four key questions that the Committee will be asking as it assesses next steps:


- Has the “past really passed,” in terms of inflation surges;
- How much of the inflation framework associated with our *Regime Change* thesis is due to ‘one-off’ disruptions, including supply chain disruptions, tariffs, and — more recently — the AI capex boom;
- Do changes in prices for logic and memory chips indicate a broader change in inflation dynamics; and
- How much accommodation are we getting from the balance sheet?


Relating these learnings back to our outlook: As a reminder, our policy indicators (see *Exhibits 1* and *2* ) actually continue to suggest that the appropriate setting for fed funds in late 2026 and early 2027 is in the low-to-mid 4% range (i.e., 75-100 basis points of hikes). Consistent with a more hawkish bias from Chair Warsh relative to Chair Powell, the message that we are taking from the Fed is that they will ultimately become more comfortable ‘moving up to later move down’ when it comes to interest rates. As a result of the Chair’s leanings and what are our models are telling us, we would be inclined to add hikes to our current ‘long-hold’ baseline if incoming data suggest that June’s slowing in payrolls and significant cooling in inflation were one-month aberrations. Meanwhile, we continue to feel good about our 10-year targets of 4.75% this year and 4.25% next year, which we think already reflect much of the dynamic we are describing.

## What do we think the means for our Macro Outlook and for markets?


The bias remains hawkish, which allows the bond market to potentially re-establish credibility for the Fed by tightening. Establishing credibility, which Warsh did in his first meeting, has led to higher real yields but lower forward inflation expectations. In fact, the 10-year break-even fell to 2.23% from 2.50% when he first stood at the podium. At the same time, nominal 10-year yields have increased to 4.67% as of Wednesday from 4.44% just before the first Chair Warsh press conference in mid-June.


At the press conference, Chair Warsh was adamant in two areas: There is no “soft target” for this committee; moreover, the “reduction in market guidance may have been a factor” in improving FOMC credibility. To be sure, Warsh is walking a tight rope, and we do expect the combination of a weakening dollar and higher rates to test his resolve to refrain from providing more guidance in the coming weeks.


As we indicated earlier, our models too suggest a tightening bias, but do not indicate that a major rate cycle is warranted. Specifically, our fed funds model points to a fair value for policy rates in the low-to-mid 4% range in late 2026 and early 2027 (i.e., 75-100 basis points of hikes). This is up from the mid-3% signal that we saw earlier this year. Iran-related energy pressures partially explain the uptick, but a bigger driver has come from the growth backdrop. Namely, productivity growth has accelerated further driven by AI capex, and jobs growth has shown signs of reaccelerating. These hawkish developments notwithstanding, our indicator continues to point to fair values back in the mid-3% range in mid/late 2027, as energy pass-through fades and scorching AI capex growth rates begin to moderate. Our bottom line: our data suggest a tightening bias, but similar to what we heard from central bankers in Europe, now is not the time to engineer a major sustained tightening cycle the way we saw in 2022.


Chair Warsh is clearly comfortable allowing markets to react directly to the data rather than having rates being guided more directly by the Fed. In his view, markets are signaling that economic output is strong, capital investment remains robust, and unemployment is low. We broadly agree. This backdrop points to a modest tightening campaign rather than a major sustained rate cycle, even as the reduction in forward guidance likely contributes to greater market volatility.


On inflation, the U.S. Federal Reserve is still trying to figure out how much weight to put on supply shocks. By comparison, in Europe we heard multiple central bank executives signal to us that supply shocks should be included in monetary policy calculations. The reality for the U.S., however, is that America is less dependent on others for energy resources, and it has above average productivity.


When we pull it all together, our view remains that strong earnings growth, heavy capex investment, and solid productivity will propel risk assets higher in 2026. All told, we are using a 17% EPS growth rate forecast for the S&P 500. So, unless the S&P 500 multiple contracts by that much, Equities are likely to end the year in the green.


However, unlike the past three years, the technical picture is definitely less good, given AI debt raises and related equity capital IPOs. This ‘less good’ technical backdrop, in addition to a concentration of EPS in the semiconductor sector and less guidance from the new Fed, likely means a lot more volatility this year. Meanwhile, in 2027 our outlook is less optimistic, as we see earnings growth potentially slowing more than the consensus thinks, especially in the second half of 2027.


On the interest rate front, we maintain our 10-year 4.75% forecast for 2026 and 4.25% in 2027. However, on the front end of the curve, we do feel more nervous after the FOMC meeting about our current prediction that Chair Warsh will not raise rates in 2026. Key data points we will be watching include near-term employment and inflation prints (with a particular emphasis on energy pass-through), as well as year-over- year comparisons for tariffs.


Stepping back, we think today’s meeting reinforces, not alters, our broader macro framework. In many respects, the Fed is grappling with the same Divergence Conundrum that we have been highlighting all year. Parts of the economy continue to expand at a remarkable pace, supported by AI investment, strong productivity growth, and resilient capital spending. At the same time, more rate-sensitive sectors, including housing and portions of the consumer economy, remain under pressure. The challenge for policymakers is determining how much additional restraint is required to address inflationary pressures without unnecessarily slowing those parts of the economy that are already lagging.
