Through our flagship app Highrise we serve an incredibly diverse global audience. Our commitment to creating an inviting environment involves providing a variety of content that appeals to and represents these global needs.


Pocket Worlds’ art team focuses intently on researching religious holidays, cultural events, and body inclusivity. Artist Johanna here shares some insights about how we approach this research to ensure that we honor everyone equally.


Love of gaming has never known boundaries. As an artistic medium, games hold universal appeal by providing the context for exploring complex emotional experiences; and yet for many, barriers still exist in both the[creation](https://s3-us-east-2.amazonaws.com/igda-website/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/29093706/IGDA-DSS-2019_Summary-Report_Nov-20-2019.pdf) and[consumer](https://venturebeat.com/2021/01/26/diversity-isnt-just-about-inclusion-in-gaming-its-smart-business-too/) aspects of the gaming industry. Though there is a long way to go when it comes to inclusivity, mainstream gaming has been moving in the right direction in recent years. More AAA titles are featuring[women as protagonists](https://www.wired.com/story/women-video-games-representation-e3/) and the issues regarding binary genders in avatar creation have seen attempted remedies take the form of[non-binary body customization](http://ign.com/articles/2019/08/28/cyberpunk-2077-dev-explains-why-theres-no-character-gender-option-anymore) as in Cyberpunk 2077, and in[looser gender definitions](https://gamerescape.com/2020/06/30/animal-crossings-trans-players-identity-expression-and-whats-next/) in Animal Crossing.


While most gamers agree that[inclusivity is essential](https://medium.com/@Electronic_Arts/what-inclusion-means-to-players-db4522bdd8a0) , it can be difficult to gauge audience reactions, let alone tell underrepresented folks’ stories with grace: look only to critiques around[Aloy’s fuller face](https://gamerant.com/horizon-forbidden-west-aloy-appearance-social-media/) in the second chapter of *Horizon Zero Dawn* , or the complicated reception of[Lev’s character](https://www.washingtonpost.com/video-games/2020/07/21/trans-narrative-last-us-part-ii-is-compelling-theres-so-much-more-be-done/) in *Last of Us Part II* .


The Highrise player community has long been highly aware of key issues around representation and actively seeks to make our app a friendly, safe space. It’s not unusual for players to include pronouns in their profile bios, or sport an item or two from our perennial Pride Shop. On occasions when we creators fail to step up and deliver content inspired by diverse cultural sources, players don’t hesitate to let us know. A recent example is the rise of the hashtag #freeourblackhairstyles which comments on the paucity of ethnic hair items, and the lack of accessibility of those hair items currently available in the game’s economy.


That kind of transparency and honesty from our player base is crucial to making the app what it is today: game artist Johanna speaks for all of us when she says, “I like having vocal players that are excited to share what they’re into.” We’re constantly following conversations happening in game, across social media, and on our suggestion site, highrise.canny.io.


With this dialogue between developer and player in mind, it’s only natural that we’re starting to trend more and more in the direction of player-led content. A recent design contest titled #HairRise allowed players to create new items for the game, and came about as a direct response to feedback on our lack of ethic hairstyles.

Johanna’s favorite item collection is the Body Positivity set which released this April: “To figure out what the community really wanted, I went online. I got on the app and made a post saying, ‘If we could put more items in that would represent you and your body, what would you want?’


“I got a lot of really great feedback; I got a lot of people asking for something they had, like if they had stretch marks or acne. One of the players said, ‘Hey, I have a pink cane, it would be awesome if that was in-game.’ And I was like, ‘Of course!’ We have to make that for someone if it would represent them in the game. That’s how I approached it.”


As for the reception? “It’s been one of the really popular ones, since people are like, ‘This is ME. I can make my character ME.’”


Body Positivity!


Inclusivity isn’t an abstract — it’s a[vital element](https://blogs.library.unt.edu/digital-scholarship/2018/03/06/select-your-character-why-representation-in-video-games-matters/) in the recipe for creating transformative and empowering moments. Gaming is often viewed as an escape, and part of our mission in Highrise is to provide a safe space for those who may not always have the opportunity to express themselves in real life. But within these safe spaces is boundless potential: as succinctly stated in a[study on gender expression](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/10.11116/digest.6.2.2.pdf?ab_segments=0%252FSYC-5917%252Ftest&refreqid=excelsior%3A21a3b74f090ee242eb358cb16567db40) , “game play can be a valuable exercise in empowerment, as it makes visible dominant norms, and provides the tools to act out, within a safe environment, alternative identities.”


While noting that we’ll continue to add to the Body Positivity Collection, Johanna explained that in the past, significant technical limitations impacted the diversity of body shapes we’ve been able to create:


“We have a lot of new slots now — we’ve been blessed by the gods to have more slots and be able to make more items and make them more interesting. But the problem is that when we start making the avatars bigger and building in new pieces, it starts messing with what we already have as our base. So a lot of times when you add in bigger arms or bigger legs and you have tattoos you want to add on top of them, they don’t always line up properly because it’s built on the base model.


“In a lot of our recent sets, we’ve started to add in bigger pieces just to get more inclusivity in — a lot of time we only use the base model, and that only represents a small fraction of what people actually look like and want to look like. So we’re trying to trend back into making clothing more unique, making different body types to include everybody.”


Avatars wearing items from our Eid 2021 collection


Johanna also created an item collection for Eid this year. “A couple of times we’ve missed a holiday,” she says, “So then we go, okay, put it on the calendar for next year. That way we *know* it gets made.” The art team’s calendar is peppered with national and religious holidays from Rosh Hashanah to Holi, though a few other universally recognized if lesser-known holidays like Panda Day and National Gamer Day also manage to sneak their way into the rotation.


Representing a global audience can seem daunting — but it shouldn’t be. And, as Johanna says, we’ll always listen to our community to find ways we can make your avatar feel more like you: “I do want the players to know: I do see your comments, I do see when you tag me, I want to add your items in, and eventually they will get added in. I don’t know when, but they will be!”
