Meet co-founder Jimmy! We talk about his career path so far, pandemic life with a newborn, predictions for the future of mobile gaming, and insights about the potential role of NFTs. It’s fascinating stuff. Can he just start a Youtube channel already?

**What have been the most unexpected moments in founding / developing your own company?**


I used to sleep in the meeting room. I had an air mattress, so sometimes I would spend the night. I would have to stay up until the janitor came at around midnight, and just pretend I was working late.


The door wasn’t locked in the office, and it was a pretty sketchy area, right? And once someone came in, woke me up, I was very disoriented — but they were like, going through our stuff, and he ended up stealing my shoes! I woke up and I was like what are you doing? Who are you? And the guy ran off with my shoes.


So… that was probably the most unexpected thing that happened.


**Right? Who would have thought that you’d end up not only sleeping in the office but having THAT happen!**


If it was more conducive to sleeping, or like living in the office, I probably would have just lived in the office. In the beginning especially, Anton and I used to work pretty much seven days a week, and then we were probably in the office for up to 14 hours a day, sometimes longer. We probably did like 20 hours a day at some point. And it would have been way more convenient if I could have lived in the office, but alas — I still had to pay rent.


**Those are insane hours. How did you manage to survive with those kinds of work schedules?**


Well, one: we were two single dudes who didn’t have any attachments or responsibilities — we just had ourselves to answer for, and all we needed to do was eat and sleep. That definitely made it easy — *easier* . And I think, two, just being younger? It was all-consuming.


**Did you have a vision for company culture or product when you started off? Were there any paradigm-shattering moments that made you reassess what you wanted to create?**


Basically, we saw things like Habbo Hotel and Second Life which were really popular in the desktop era as web products, and when we started, iPad and mobile were definitely on the way up. It was very obvious they were going to overtake web *as* the future. And we were like, there’s no one that does this for mobile so why don’t we take what works over there — what works for things like Habbo hotel or Ameba Pigg, which is really big in Japan, and make it for iPad and iPhone. And that ended up being basically Pocketz, the first game that we made. And we’ve stayed true more or less to that original vision which is to build this really social game.


Eventually, I think we refined the vision to be this intersection of game and social app, but certainly, the original vision was to build this game that really focused on social because we saw games as *being* fundamentally social.


**So, what does your current day-to-day look like?**


My team is mostly in Europe, so I start my day at 7:30 to get the biggest amount of overlap with them since I’m based on the West Coast. I have calls from 7:30 to typically around noon: these are 1–1 calls, strategy meetings, product syncs with the different teams, and then anyone who needs me for anything. Then I have a chunk in the afternoon where I’m free to do things like dig into code or write different stuff in Notion, miscellaneous stuff. Sometimes I have calls right before I sign off around 5 to 6, and those are generally calls with Anton Ben and Patrick — people in the States — and they’re generally more product-oriented.


**Busy day! Starting at 7:30 is pretty early. So if you were to tell 18- or 12-year-old you about your day-to-day, and say “this is what you’re going to be doing!” at this age, is it something you’d be surprised by, or?**


When I was younger, I certainly didn’t think that starting or running a company was an option for me. That was never on my radar. In fact, I think my career path from the beginning — I knew I wasn’t good at chemistry so doctor was out of the running. But I was really good at writing back in high school so I wanted to go into journalism, but eventually, for some reason, I went into computer science. And then throughout college, I always thought I’d end up at Google or Apple or Microsoft, and *that* would be my dream job. It wasn’t until I left Apple and I started this company that the current path was set. So yeah, the answer would be no, I don’t think my 12-year-old self would believe or would imagine this is my day-to-day now. It’s pretty exciting to be doing what I’m doing.


**Any insights or predictions about where mobile gaming is going or trends you see happening now that you’re excited about?**


I’m really excited about what we’re doing, especially our current path of turning Highrise into more and more of a platform. Looking forward 3, 4, 5, years, platforms like Roblox or Minecraft are going to be huge, just for the fact that content at the end of the day is ultimately king. Whoever has the most content will win.


The way to really win at content is by becoming a platform and enabling UGC. And, looking at Highrise, we’ve always kind of done that with player submissions in terms of clothing content. With the release of voice chat; with room boost and tipping; now we’re starting to enable UGC *experiences* on top of UGC content — but I think going forward as we evolve and lean more and more in that direction… that’s super exciting. That is a direction that can take us from a path of being worth 100 million dollars to being worth multiple billions of dollars; not to mention the value that we would deliver to our users.


The even longer version of that answer would be that when we first started out, we had this insight that games are fundamentally social experiences, and the best gaming experiences are had when you’re playing with someone else — especially someone that you’re friends with. At the time on mobile, not a lot of folks felt that way. There were a lot of single-player games, maybe like 2 MMOs that were multiplayer, but everything else was basically single-player experiences. But in the ensuing years, the last 8 years or so, that’s definitely become a lot more of an accepted belief: that the future of gaming is multiplayer. And we’re riding in the vanguard of that entire movement. So I would not be surprised if more or less all games in the future had some kind of cooperative or competitive or multiplayer aspect. Humans are fundamentally social creatures and playing a game is a fundamentally social exercise for us.


**I agree- games speak to something very fundamental in the human experience which is play, and that can take many different forms.**


Yeah, exactly. When I answer this question with interview candidates, I usually talk about how when you talk about social, you think about Facebook, you think Instagram, but if you browse Facebook for 30 minutes, after that you feel terrible about yourself. But if you look at something like Fortnite and you playing Fortnite for 20 minutes with two of your buddies and you’re on voice chat with each other, after that you feel very satisfied in terms of your social needs. So even though one has social in the name — social network — the other is a much more social experience.


**Yeah, it’s extremely fulfilling when you have those moments of connection. What are games in your own life that have really made an impact on you?**


The biggest two that come to mind — one is Diablo II. That was my first experience of a social game. Because, well, Diablo II is not a very social game — it’s not designed to be — but there was emergent behavior where people would create clans. They would just change their names to have a tag in front of it and that would be your clan. And because Diablo II had multiplayer and private channels, the private channels would basically be the clan hangouts. So that was my first exposure, it was kind of a glimpse into how powerful as a tool social games can be. I spent two years in Diablo II — a lot of hours — that was definitely very formative.


The other one is Starcraft. That was probably my first exposure to the competitive scene of gaming, and it taught me that games can be a serious business and can be mainstream. Whenever I visited China back in the day, I would once in a while late at night flip through the TV and there would be a channel with Starcraft playing. And that’s not a thing in the States. Even if it was like “competitive e-sports” it was not really competitive: you would have a LAN party in someone’s garage and that that would be the competition. Whereas in South Korea and China it was huge. It felt like something that wasn’t just a hobby; it was real.


**Did it inspire you to compete, or get involved in local clubs or competitions?**


No, I was not very competitive. I was okay, but definitely not on that level.


I did sell stuff in Diablo II. I think I made 4 or 500 bucks selling digital items on eBay, so I guess that was my first dabble in digital goods. That was the first instance of digital goods having real value. I think Diablo II is probably one of the trailblazers in that regard. There were a couple of MMOs that were very early around that time, like Everquest, but for me, Diablo II was the first one that really showed that digital goods can have value.


**Looking back, it’s crazy how much that’s changed; that’s a pretty ubiquitous concept now.**


Yeah, and that’s another thing: even back then, a few years ago, people would be like, “Oh, you’re selling digital farm goods in Farmville? Why would anyone pay money for that?” But in the last year or so, people have really realized that a digital good is no different from a physical good.


**So now, regarding NFTs and other emerging currencies, how do you think that’s going to impact not only our game but the gaming sphere in general?**


So NFTs: they’re certainly an interesting opportunity for us. I wouldn’t say they’re disruptive to our current business model — if anything they’re complementary. Basically, it’s replicating what we already have. NFTs you can think of as one big database that everybody uses to track what you own, whereas right now Highrise has its own database of every user and what they own. The eventual vision of NFTs is that you can now take that digital shirt out of HR and wear it in Fortnite, or you can take your gun in Fortnite and wear it in HR, because there’s now this shared database of digital goods, and it has ownership.


The opportunity for us is that our items already have value within the context of Highrise, and we can give it even more value by attaching it to an NFT token and exporting it to the blockchain, and now people can trade it on open sea, they can send it to each other via the blockchain — eventually, they can import to Second Life or IMVU, who knows? Or import it back into Highrise, so you don’t have to go through the Highrise trading system since now there’s an open trading system. So it just adds a little more functionality to the economy, but fundamentally the scarcity of items is what gives them value, and fundamentally that doesn’t change. We still control how many items we’re issuing and what items we’re creating, but users can have more flexibility and more power in terms of what they do with their items.


**So, continuing to look forward, are there any dream project you’d like to work on that you haven’t yet had time to develop?**


Currently, my dream project *is* realizing Highrise into a platform: making it be the definitive UGC social platform, which I think it has a real chance of being since no one else has filled that role. I think we’re pretty close, we’re on the path to achieving that. And if we can achieve that — that’s huge. That’s like, world-changing, potentially.


**When you talk about it as a platform, do you see it becoming something like Youtube or Twitter, more of an art-based tool for people to express themselves?**


Twitter, Youtube, they’re all platforms, they’re all UGC in the form of content platforms: one’s video content, one is text content. There’s Roblox, which is a platform for UGC experiences, so their players make games that other players play on the platform. If you look at IMVU, they have 3D content creation, so their users create clothing for their 3D avatars. So that’s a little bit of a platform too.


What’s unique about Highrise is because it’s 2D, it’s comparatively easy to create content, whereas for something like Roblox, IMVU, or Second Life, you have to be a 3D modeler to build anything. For us, you just need a pen and paper. It’s pretty cool. It definitely a unique advantage. I think we also have this combination of a platform for content but also a platform for experiences that no one else has really tapped into. So let’s see.


**Wrapping up, how do you usually stay focused working from home, especially with a baby? Do you have any recommendations for how to work from home with a really little one?**


My recommendation is to get a grandparent involved! My mom basically takes care of the baby. Baby wakes up at 4:30 and goes to sleep around 6. So that entire time, it’s my mom. I don’t know how I could do this without my mom. I see friends that have babies now and it’s really hard without the help.


**Rad! Any final thoughts, other things we didn’t cover that you want to throw in?**


Yeah, well to answer one of your earlier questions about what I’ve learned… I didn’t realize this, but we went remote as a company five years ago, and I had been remote for four or five years already. And the year before the pandemic, 2019, we had our first company retreat. It was the first time that everyone in the company flew together and was in the same place, and that was probably the best feeling for me in all of 8 years of doing this. Just seeing everyone together, some people that I had never met before, like Ben, that I would see on calls but would never see face-to-face. Seeing everyone together in the same place and just realizing that all of our destinies are tied together, and we are on the same path — I think there were like 20–30 people there — we’re all focused on one goal and we’re all working toward that goal. That’s incredibly powerful. To think that I lit the spark in the very beginning to set off that path for everybody was super rewarding for me.
