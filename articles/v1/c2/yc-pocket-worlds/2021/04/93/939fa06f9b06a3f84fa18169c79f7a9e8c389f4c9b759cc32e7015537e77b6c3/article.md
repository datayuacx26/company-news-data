# A No-Shoes Kind of Start-Up


## Pocket Worlds has grown 10x in the last year… the vibe check though? It still feels like home.


[Mariana Aguirre](https://medium.com/@pocket_manjee?source=post_page---byline--52de78a58d50---------------------------------------)


9 min read


·


Apr 16, 2021


--


You can never prepare for all of life’s surprises. Take, for example, when your new employer tells you that no one wears shoes in the office, and you know for a fact that at least one toe is currently peeping out of a hole in your sock.


Jimmy was the first person to greet me as I walked up to the San Francisco office of Pocketz World (and no, that’s not a typo: there really was a Z in the name- but more on that later). The office was near AirBnB’s headquarters, with Zynga just down the block. After a 20-minutes sojourn from the Civic Center BART station, I gladly complied with the no-shoe rule, a naked big toe notwithstanding: after all, if I was going to be working for a startup, I was thrilled that it was the cool[no-shoes kind of startup](https://www.businessinsider.com/silicon-valley-shoeless-barefoot-offices-trend-photos-2019-12) .


The office was a wee space, a spare and minimal rectangle through which every word carried a slight echo. Three islands of black desks spanned the pine-floor: ample space for seven local employees. As an intern for a position as community manager, I got a quick rundown of my duties from Anton: liaison with in-game moderators, answer support emails, and add new items to the game shop. I was a fresh college grad with no social media experience, (and possibly fewer social skills), and would proceed to commit more game-breaking blunders than I could count.


Everyone was monstrously forgiving, despite the trouble I would stir up even after hours. And yet, that kindness was just the vibe of the place. Serene, but determined. Gentle, but expecting improvement and quality above all. This was back in 2016, and by now, at the advent of 2021, the company has expanded so much that entire departments are now dedicated to tasks I was able to handle as a lowly intern, and the in-person office has dissolved into a fully remote, global team of 35.


Much has changed, yes, but before we cover the current state of affairs (including our updated shoe policy), let’s start this episode at the beginning.


## The Beginning


Co-founders Anton and Jimmy met as a fortuitous consequence of mutual friends way back in 2013, the same year that “[selfie](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/selfie) ” was added to the dictionary. We’ll chalk it up to fate that these two were both on the hunt for a new challenge. Anton had only just returned to San Francisco after the sale of groundbreaking eCommerce companies Lookingo and Luxury Escapes in France and Australia, respectively, and Jimmy was searching for a smaller, more intimate team following a year-long chapter with Apple. Over burritos the two joined forces with another mutual friend, and the embryonic Pocket Worlds was formed.


Alright, so we’re just going to go ahead and rip this band-aid right off: their first app was a bit of a flop. Photoscore was a dating app in which women took the lead, assigning a numeric value to sort through and rank different fellas. Surprisingly, this was the first female choice-centered dating app of its kind: Bumble wouldn’t appear in the App Store until[late 2014](https://www.businessinsider.com/bumble-dating-app-company-history-2021-ipo-2020-9) . And yet, despite its progressive vision, Photoscore was rejected from the App Store, and the project likewise found no support from the growth accelerator Y Combinator. When their third party member decided to call it quits, Anton and Jimmy changed direction.


Over the next year and a half, they would successfully launch two entirely new apps: this time, both were games with a focus on socializing. Pocketz and Harvest Crossing both featured customizable home bases in virtual worlds with the potential to visit and trade with other members.


The games enjoyed a steady stream of downloads; after all, these were the first virtual worlds to hit the mobile gaming sphere. However, they weren’t without their own share of challenges. Pocketz was born of a partnership with[Battle Camp](https://battlecamp.pennypop.com/) , and featured a cutesy art style largely appealing to the younger segment of the gaming populace. While absolutely charming, an entire demographic of more mature players was left out of the loop.


The Pocketz World Map featured rad unlockable desinations


On the flipside, Harvest Crossing found a ready audience of[casual adult gamers](https://mediakix.com/blog/mobile-gaming-industry-statistics-market-revenue/) . But young Pocket Worlds (or PW, for short) quickly realized fully breaking into this space would require a lot more than cute farm animals; not only was user acquisition difficult due to competition from[thousands of games](https://www.statista.com/statistics/780238/number-of-available-gaming-apps-in-the-apple-app-store-quarter/#:~:text=Apple%20App%20Store%3A%20number%20of%20available%20gaming%20apps%20as%20of%20Q4%202020&text=This%20statistic%20gives%20information%20on,compared%20to%20the%20previous%20quarter) vying for downloads, but players in Harvest Crossing were surprisingly solitary. Most virtual farmers eschewed trade mechanics and the ability to visit other homesteads, preferring to putter about their own farms with only tomato sprouts for company.


Harvest Crossing: home of the most precious cows you’ve ever seen


Though the Pocket Worlds motto “social-first games’’ wasn’t officially coined until 2020, the spirit of driving social- first experiences was always a primary objective of the fledgling startup. Of course, if you’re a fellow introvert currently cringing at the idea of a developer forcing friends on you, rather like that one well-intentioned social butterfly you know who doesn’t understand that you just want to *chill* , think again: social elements are often a[valuable part](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958820300130) of the gaming experience. There had to be a way to marry the successes of Pocketz and Harvest Crossing to create a robust virtual world capable of being called home by any type of gamer.


## Enter Highrise


Pocket Worlds’ flagship game hit App Store shelves in late 2014, leaning into the strengths of much beloved features of its predecessors. The new virtual world, Snap City, is redubbed Highrise a few short months after launch, and boasts a smattering of features that draw in a variety of gamers. Fashion shows, interior design, an intensely active newsfeed, item trading: Highrise is a sandbox MMO that combines the creative appeal of style and design gaming with internal social media, allowing each player an entirely unique experience of their own choosing. It’s been thriving and vibing since the day it was born; and this is totally *not* a plug, but you really should just go[download the game](https://about.joinhighrise.com/) to see what all the hype is about.


This first iteration of Highrise is a Goldilocks dream: just enough social media, just enough game, and in lieu of an official designation in either category, it hits *just* right somewhere in the middle. The PW team quickly expands to include an art team responsible for creating new items for the community every week, and that beloved San Francisco office opens its doors as HQ.


Several key additions soon transform the game into the thriving community it is today. Events of the likes of Prank Wars and Style Challenges make their debut throughout 2017, markedly increasing engagement and progressing the simple gacha mechanic to include a fiercely competitive ranked reward system. Improvements to design and chat functionality are dropped along the way, and a full array of support staff is hired in 2019 to provide for quicker and smoother customer experience. The budding company had finally found its niche.


The current Highrise aesthetic


It’s important to note how quickly PW found a groove, and equally notable is the longevity of the app itself. Despite a few early obstacles, the company[survived](https://venturebeat.com/2019/05/13/50-of-businesses-fail-in-their-first-5-years-whats-the-secret-for-those-that-survive/#:~:text=50%25%20of%20businesses%20fail%20in,VentureBeat) typical new-business growing pains, an achievement all the more impressive in a notoriously failure-riddled game industry. You have only to think back on such[transient wonders](https://gadgets.ndtv.com/games/features/an-app-store-filled-with-one-hit-wonders-585528) as Flappy Bird or Fruit Ninja to recognize that it takes more than an addicting mechanic to evolve from insipid boredom cure to enduring paragon of mobile entertainment. In part, much credit here might be due to the community that calls Highrise home. Highly inclusive and infinitely creative, folks from around the world formed the unbreakable friendships which are the lifeblood of the app, and solidify its place in the mobile pantheon.


With such an emphasis on global connections, you probably won’t be surprised to hear that Pocket Worlds went full remote in 2018, just a few short years after establishing the SF office. It was a wise move: most of the team was already fully distributed, and some “local” staff had been facing commutes of an hour or more to reach the city. As for that update on the shoe policy, you can wear ’em or not, at your own clean-carpeted discretion.


And let’s not forget that for the first several years of its life, PW was entirely self-funded. It’s certainly[not unusua](https://www.fundable.com/learn/resources/infographics/startup-funding-infographic) l for startups to bootstrap their way out of the primordial goo into a lucrative venture, though success[isn’t guaranteed](https://hbr.org/1992/11/bootstrap-finance-the-art-of-start-ups) even with investors. However, it remains to be said that Pocket Worlds has, starting in 2018, raised a cumulative 10 million in funding. With that kind of bread, you gotta wonder how much things have been heating up. So, on to the sweet crunchy toast.


## Acceleration


This is the redemption arc you’ve been waiting for for like, six paragraphs. In 2018, armed with ol’ reliable Highrise and a new beta social media app called #ME, PW was accepted to Y Combinator. This is what dreams are made of.


The addition of fresh capital allowed the team to focus on a few key drivers of growth throughout the following year. Though Highrise continued to perform well, it only enjoyed full support for English-speaking iOS users. To build out the app for Android, the dev crew had to rewrite the entire game from the ground up in C++ from Objective-C, an endeavor that took months of painstaking labor. And, to reach the non-English speaking majority of the global population, PW began to onboard a brand new marketing team.


These developments proved fruitful: 2020 saw a massive 500% increase in revenue, and in the last year alone staff size doubled. With 35 full time teammates and almost a dozen contractors to boot, this ain’t no scrappy little startup any longer; Pocket Worlds is here to kick ass and ran outta bubblegum a long time ago.


It would be remiss not to mention that large and spiky elephant in the room: though PW was already a fully distributed asynchronous team by the time the pandemic rolled around, to say that the team was not unaffected would be an understatement. Many of us have kids, several localities still maintain a state of lockdown to this day. The perks of remote work went poof as the freedom afforded by flexible hours was swallowed by new responsibilities to manage schoolwork and squeeze in precious minutes of personal time for the gym or much-restricted travel.


However, as schools shut down and social venues boarded their windows, virtual spaces naturally filled the gap. There’s much to be said about the[virtues of gaming](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/35734/social-and-emotional-benefits-of-video-games-metacognition-and-relationships) for the little ones, and it’s[taken for granted](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2021/03/20/video-games-pandemic-friends/) that remote gaming continued to bring people together during the loneliest era in memory. More shocking than the fact that people wanted to socialize during lockdowns (gasp) was just how many would find their way to Highrise.


Such a large influx of new faces is exhilarating news in the best of times, but it’s a special sort of warm fuzzy to know you provide something of a pandemic-relief service. Still, the tidal wave of new players brought some unexpected challenges: servers designed to support a modest user base buckled under the additional activity. The support crew, too, bravely fought an onslaught of mushrooming moderation reports.


With such unprecedented growth accelerating faster than our disdain for Disney live-action remakes, it was time for another round of funding. Officially rebranded as Pocket Worlds, the crew raised over $7 million earlier this year, with no end in sight for future development. At the time of writing, Highrise is home to over 115k daily active users, and serves as the hub for over 4 million messages sent every 24 hours. We won’t say much on the topic, but we have other exciting projects in development as well- so keep those peepers peeled!


The Pocket Worlds squad has always been a few steps ahead of the curve, and, at the hazard of sounding alluringly dangerous, isn’t slowing down any time soon. This is the kind of team that has the rare ability to translate key values into a living product- outstanding creativity and resilience echo through the community, and you only have to open the app to see it.
