The Food Safety Modernization Act ([FSMA](https://www.fda.gov/food/guidance-regulation-food-and-dietary-supplements/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma) ) was signed into law in 2011 to shift the focus of food safety in the U.S. from reacting to contamination to actively preventing it. A recent and significant piece of that shift is Rule 204, also known as the[Food Traceability Rule](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-safety-modernization-act-fsma/fsma-final-rule-requirements-additional-traceability-records-certain-foods) .


This rule establishes additional recordkeeping requirements for certain foods on the FDA's Food Traceability List (FTL) as they move through the supply chain. The goal is simple: when foodborne illness strikes, we need to know exactly where the food came from and where it went—fast.


The FSMA 204 compliance timeline has shifted to July 20, 2028, but for many food companies, traceability expectations are already evolving. Major retailers have introduced their own data exchange and labeling requirements, making preparation an immediate priority for suppliers across the food chain.


whether you're preparing for FDA requirements or meeting retailer-specific expectations, one thing is clear: the time to prepare is now. And[Lineage is ready to help](https://www.onelineage.com/about-us/food-traceability-resources) .


## A Rule Rooted in Safety


The goal of FSMA 204 is straightforward: make it easier to trace certain foods through the supply chain so we can act faster when there’s a risk to public health. If there's an outbreak or contamination event, traceability allows for quick identification and removal of affected products—reducing harm, maintaining trust and protecting the people we all serve.


Under the rule, companies that manufacture, process, pack or hold foods on the FDA’s Food Traceability List (FTL) must:


- **Identify applicable Critical Tracking Events (CTEs)** throughout their operations — including activities such as harvesting, cooling, initial packing, shipping, receiving and transformation
- **Maintain required Key Data Elements (KDEs)** associated with those events, such as Traceability Lot Codes (TLCs), product descriptions, quantities, dates, location information and reference document information
- **Maintain traceability records** in accordance with the rule's recordkeeping requirements
- **Provide required traceability information to the FDA** within specified timeframes when requested


### The Food Traceability List includes foods such as:


- Shell eggs (from domesticated chickens)
- Leafy greens like romaine lettuce and spinach
- Finfish such as salmon, tuna and tilapia
- Crustaceans including shrimp, crab and lobster
- Fresh-cut fruits and vegetables
- Soft cheeses like brie and camembert
- Tomatoes (whole and fresh-cut)


It’s more than just a recordkeeping update, it’s a shift toward real-time food safety data flowing across the supply chain.

As products move between supply chain partners, maintaining accurate data and connecting it to the appropriate food and traceability lot becomes increasingly important.

## Don’t Wait for 2028


Even though the FDA announced its intentions to extend the official deadline, many companies—especially those supplying to large retailers—are already being asked to meet FSMA-level traceability standards. That means the countdown has effectively started.


### Here’s what you can do now:


- **Evaluate your data flow.** Understand how product and traceability information moves between your systems and supply chain partners.
- **Define data exchange requirements.** Determine which KDEs need to be shared, who provides them and how that information will be exchanged.
- **Prepare warehouse processes.** Account for traceability requirements across receiving, storage, inventory management and shipping.
- **Align labeling requirements.** Identify applicable GS1, pallet identification and retailer-specific labeling expectations.
- **Test before deadlines.** Test data, labeling and operational processes early enough to identify and address gaps.
