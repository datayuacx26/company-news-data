# How a Microbiologist Approaches Fitness and Nutrition: Q&A with ZBiotics Co-Founder Zack Abbott

What happens when a microbiologist applies their scientific mindset to everyday health?


We sat down with our[co-founder and CEO, Dr. Zack Abbott](https://zbiotics.com/pages/team-zb) , to find out how his background in microbiology shapes his approach to fitness and nutrition. His understanding of the[microbiome and how the body works](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/understanding-the-human-microbiome) —from metabolism to macronutrients—plays a big role in the daily choices he makes. From prioritizing fiber diversity to building movement into a largely sedentary job, Zack’s routine is grounded in science and focused on sustainability. Whether you're building new habits or just curious how a scientist thinks about health, Zack shares some approachable, data-backed strategies for staying consistent—even when life gets busy.


## How science shapes a microbiologist’s approach to health


**As a scientist, how does your understanding of biology influence the way you approach your fitness or nutrition routine?**


Given that I'm a microbiologist, I start by thinking about the microbiome. I try to make sure that my diet has lots of[different kinds of fiber](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/fiber-for-gut-health-balanced-microbiome) . I also think about my macros and my body's biological needs, which really influences my diet. I pay attention to how much protein, carbs, and fat I consume daily and how that affects the function, operation, and health of my body.


I also do research. I’m not an expert in fitness or nutrition specifically, so I look to the experts and credible, well-established sources to learn. I look for data to support my decisions about health. For example: is there actually data on the recommendation to eat five servings of fruit and veggies a day, or is it arbitrary? (By the way, there is good data, and it shows that five a day genuinely does reduce the risk of several health issues!)


## Building a fitness routine that’s sustainable


**What does a typical day of movement or exercise look like for you, and how do you stay consistent when things get busy?**


I try to run 2-3 days per week, lift weights two days per week, and—if I’m really motivated and on it—maybe one higher-intensity workout per week. Most weeks, I work out 4-5 times, and on a really good week, I’ll manage to get 6.


However, my bigger challenge is making sure that 1-2 hours at the gym isn’t my only movement of the day. My job is largely sedentary—sitting in front of a screen all day—so if I’m not really deliberate about it, I’ll sit all day long, then go to the gym, then come home, sit on the couch and relax with my wife and cat, and then go to bed.


The gym is great, but if the other 22 hours of my day are spent sitting or laying down, that’s still a problem. So I try to carve out time to go for walks, use a standing desk for part of the day, and generally move around more. It’s honestly harder for me than going to the gym, but I’m working on it!


**Are there any wellness habits you swear by that might surprise people?**


I think my 20 plants per day habit (more on that below) might surprise some people, just because 20 sounds like a lot! But it’s very doable, and it’s a great way to up my fiber diversity and focus on my diet in a positive way.


## The role of nutrition in supporting a healthy microbiome


**What are your non-negotiables when it comes to your nutrition?**


1. **Fiber:** I shoot for a minimum of 40g per day, with as much fiber diversity as I can manage. To ensure diversity is baked into the goal, if a single food has more than 8g, I only count the first 8g towards my 40g target. For example, if I eat a serving of kidney beans that has 14g of fiber, I only count 8g. I usually end up with 50-60g per day because of this—which is probably even better, since more fiber does continue to improve health outcomes ([albeit sub-linearly at higher daily amounts](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2023.1153165/full) ).
2. **Five a day:** Several[meta-analyses](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996) have now validated that eating at least five servings of fruits and vegetables—specifically two servings of fruits and three servings of vegetables—reduces several health risks and associated mortality. Fruits aren’t my favorite, but I always make sure to get at least two servings, plus at least three servings of vegetables every day.
3. **20 plants per day and 30 plants per week:** In line with my fiber diversity goal, I try to eat at least 20 different plants per day and[30 different plants per week](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/msystems.00031-18) . It sounds like a lot, but it doesn’t have to be a full serving of each plant to count. The goal is variety in fibers and micronutrients, even in small doses. For example, even a handful of peanuts counts as one of your plants. And a Mediterranean salad with spinach, kale, tomatoes, cucumbers, bell peppers, garbanzo beans, onions, sunflower seeds, olives, and artichoke hearts counts as 10 plants—half of the day in one meal!
4. **At least 0.5g of protein per pound of body weight per day:** In general, protein probably gets more attention than it deserves (fiber is so much more important for health for most people), but I do make it a focus since I eat largely a plant-based diet and I exercise regularly. I weigh about 180lbs, so I aim for at least 90g of protein per day, mostly from whole foods. Since I don’t eat much meat, I also supplement at least once a day with a protein powder (I use Gainful’s plant-based protein).


## Data-driven health habits to stay consistent


**What advice would you give to someone who is just getting started on their fitness and nutrition journey?**


There’s so much information out there, and truthfully, most of it probably isn’t that critical. A lot of it has significant tradeoffs, is somewhat dubious, or is focused on optimizing the last 5%. Don’t get overwhelmed by all the recommendations—especially by wacky, restrictive diets.


95% of health comes down to the boring basics you already know: exercise regularly, eat lots of fiber and whole foods, and get good sleep.


And remember—you don’t need to do everything all at once. Every incremental change has an impact. Start small, and build on that by adding one new healthy habit or rule every month. If you just do one new thing for your health every month, by the end of the year you’ll have made 12 meaningful changes. For example, here’s what that could look like for someone starting with no real fitness or nutrition practices:


- January: Exercise 1 day/week
- February: Exercise 2 days/week
- March: Exercise 3 days/week
- April: Eat at least 2 servings of fruits and vegetables a day
- May: Get at least 7 hours of sleep at least 5 days a week
- June: Eat at least 3 servings of fruits and vegetables a day
- July: Exercise 4 days/week (even if the 4th day is light/short)
- August: Eat at least 4 servings of fruits and vegetables a day
- September: Eat at least 15 different plants per day
- October: Eat at least 5 servings of fruits and vegetables a day
- November: Eat at least 20 different plants per day
- December: Don’t let the holidays derail your hard-earned health habits!


## Key takeaways from a scientist’s wellness philosophy


Each of those steps is pretty incremental and doable. And counterintuitively, it actually gets easier to add more habits towards the end when you have momentum! By the end of the year, someone who started with no deliberate habits or routines is exercising 4 days/week, sleeping at least 7 hours most days, and eating lots of fruits, vegetables, and fiber. If they’d tried to do all of that at once, it probably would’ve felt exhausting and unsustainable. But by slowly layering it in, you can accomplish a lot of[sustainable change](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/importance-of-realistic-resolutions) !
