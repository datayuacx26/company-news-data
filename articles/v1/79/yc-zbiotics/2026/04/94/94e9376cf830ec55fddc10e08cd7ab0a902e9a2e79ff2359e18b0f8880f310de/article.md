# Could Engineered Bacteria Remove Greenhouse Gases From the Air?

*This is Part 3 of our four-part series, Microbes on a Mission. Read[Part 1, How Plastic-Eating Microbes Could Clean Up Our Oceans](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/plastic-eating-microbes-ocean-cleanup) , and[Part 2, How Microbes Can Be Genetically Engineered to Clean Contaminated Soil](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/engineered-microbes-contaminated-soil) , before diving in, or start here.*


## Key Takeaways


- The atmosphere is harder to target than the ocean or soil: the gases we most need to remove are present at trace concentrations that most microbes never evolved to handle.
- Researchers have engineered *E. coli* to consume methane in bioreactors or use carbon dioxide as its sole carbon source.
- Both proof-of-concepts demonstrate that the underlying biology can be engineered. Though, the gap between a lab result and atmospheric intervention is large.


---


The ocean had plastic. The soil had heavy metals. The air has everything.


Carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane, and nitrous oxide are greenhouse gases that trap heat in the atmosphere and are accumulating at rates the planet hasn't seen in human history. The scale of the problem is different here, and so is the difficulty of using biology to address it.


In parts 1 and 2 of this series, we looked at engineered microbes doing specific jobs in specific places: bacteria eating plastic in saltwater, cyanobacteria clearing a pesticide from a paddy field. The results were striking because the targets were tractable: a contaminated field, a stretch of polluted water, something a microbe could reach.


Air doesn't work that way. It's enormous, it's everywhere, and the gases we most want to remove are present at trace concentrations—0.04% CO₂, 0.0002% methane. Microbes evolved to work at much higher concentrations than that. So the engineering problem isn't just "can we get bacteria to consume greenhouse gases?"—it's "can we get them to do it at concentrations that matter?".


That question is still open, but researchers are working on it—and the approaches being developed are genuinely interesting.


## What the biology of engineered bacteria looks like


Methane-eating bacteria, called methanotrophs, have existed for billions of years. They consume methane as their sole source of carbon and energy, using an enzyme called methane monooxygenase (MMO) to convert the gas into methanol. Globally, they naturally consume around 30 million metric tons of methane every year[(Curry, 2007)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2006GB002818) . Companies like Mango Materials are already putting methanotrophs to work, capturing concentrated methane from wastewater treatment plants and landfills and converting it into biodegradable plastic. The logic is straightforward: intercept the gas at the source before it disperses into the atmosphere. The problem is that most methane doesn't stay conveniently concentrated, and the methanotrophs that consume it work best at levels far above what's in the open atmosphere and are difficult to engineer directly.


Researchers have been working to make the MMO enzyme function in more engineerable organisms. In 2024, a team at Korea University designed a miniature version of the key methane-processing enzyme and expressed it in *Escherichia coli* , producing methanol from methane at higher yields than natural methanotrophs had achieved[(Yu et al., 2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-48671-w) . That's a meaningful step, though it's still a bioreactor result, not an open-air one.


On the CO₂ side, a team at the Weizmann Institute of Science rewired *E. coli* to grow using CO₂ as its sole carbon source, essentially teaching a bacteria that normally eats sugar to behave like a plant—that is, build its biomass from CO₂[(Gleizer et al., 2019)](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)31230-9?_returnURL=https%3A%2F%2Flinkinghub.elsevier.com%2Fretrieve%2Fpii%2FS0092867419312309%3Fshowall%3Dtrue) . They knocked out key genes in its normal metabolic pathways, inserted the enzymes that plants use for carbon fixation, and then evolved the strain over 350 days until it could sustain itself on CO₂ alone. While the resulting bacteria grows much more slowly than normal *E. coli* , and requires a CO₂ concentration far higher than what's in the open atmosphere, the proof of concept is real: an organism can be fundamentally rewired to fix carbon.


## What this approach isn't yet


None of these results represent a deployable solution for atmospheric greenhouse gas removal. The concentrations involved, the scale required, and the ecological unknowns are all enormous. The gap between a bioreactor demonstration and global atmospheric intervention is not a small one to close.


But they represent something different but still important: evidence that the fundamental biological machinery for tackling these problems can be engineered. The methane-processing enzyme works in a new host. The carbon fixation pathway can be transplanted into an organism never evolved to carry it. The question of whether these capabilities can be improved and eventually deployed at a meaningful scale is genuinely open. That's not a reason for dismissal. It's a reason for continued, careful work.


The air is the hardest problem this series has looked at. It's worth being honest about that—while also being clear that the science is moving, and that what seems impossible at one scale of understanding has a way of looking different at the next.
