# How Plastic-Eating Microbes Could Clean Up Our Oceans

*This is Part 1 of our four-part series, Microbes on a Mission, exploring how[genetic engineering](https://zbiotics.com/pages/how-it-works-genetic-engineering) is being applied across the world's most critical ecosystems—ocean, soil, and air—and what it means for the future of biology. Each post stands on its own, but together they tell a bigger story about what's possible when biology meets intention.*


## Key Takeaways


- Millions of metric tons of plastic enter the world's waterways every year, and the amount is still growing.
- Scientists have genetically engineered a bacteria to break down the plastic used in water bottles and clothing directly in saltwater.
- Nature has also been quietly evolving its own plastic-eating microbes. Genetic engineering is now helping us find and enhance them.


---


At ZBiotics, we spend a lot of time thinking about what happens when you give a microbe a job it wasn't born to do. It's also exactly what's happening in one of the more quietly exciting corners of environmental science right now.


Every year, millions of metric tons of plastic enter the world's waterways: rivers, lakes, and oceans. Estimates range from 1 to over 20 million metric tons depending on what and how you measure, but the trajectory is not in dispute—more goes in each year than comes out. Once it's in the environment, plastic breaks down into smaller and smaller fragments like microplastics and nanoplastics, tiny pieces invisible to the naked eye. They make their way into fish tissue, coral, drinking water, and eventually, us. We've known about this problem for decades, and despite real progress on reducing plastic production, the amount entering the environment is still growing. Plastic-eating microbes are emerging as a potential tool to break down ocean plastic and microplastics in ways traditional cleanup methods cannot.


So what if part of the solution was already living in the water? And what if we could make it better at its job?


## How a plastic-eating bacteria was first discovered


In 2016, researchers in Japan made a genuinely surprising find. A bacteria called *Ideonella sakaiensis* , discovered living outside a plastic bottle recycling facility, had evolved the ability to survive by eating polyethylene terephthalate (PET), the type of plastic used to make water bottles, food containers, and synthetic fabrics like polyester. Over time, the bacteria had developed its own specialized enzyme—called PETase—that could break PET down into its chemical building blocks([Yoshida et al., 2016](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aad6359) ). Nature, it turns out, had already started working on this problem.


But here's the issue. *I. sakaiensis* doesn't survive in saltwater, which means it can't do anything about the plastic that's already in the ocean. And removing plastic from the ocean just to treat it on land, then rinsing off all the salt before you can start isn't economically workable at any meaningful scale. The plastic-eating bacteria was an exciting discovery, but it couldn't go where a lot of plastic waste actually was.


That's a problem that sounded a lot like a job for genetic engineering.


## Engineering a marine microbe to break down ocean plastic


A team at North Carolina State University took the plastic-degrading genes from *I. sakaiensis* and inserted them into a different organism entirely: *Vibrio natriegens* , a marine bacteria that thrives in saltwater and reproduces so fast it can double its population in under ten minutes. The idea was straightforward: combine the digesting ability of one microbe with the saltwater toughness of another, and see if you could get both traits in a single organism.


It worked. The engineered *V. natriegens* successfully broke down PET microplastics in a saltwater environment at room temperature ([Li, Menegatti & Crook, 2023](https://aiche.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/aic.18228) ). Other organisms had been engineered to break down PET before, but none could do it in saltwater, where ocean plastic actually lives. This removed what had long been one of the most critical practical barriers to addressing plastic buildup in the ocean, at least in a lab setting.


But there's still meaningful work to do here. Right now, the plastic-degrading genes sit on a plasmid—think of it as a genetic sticky note inserted into the bacteria, rather than being written into its permanent instruction manual. That makes them less stable over time. The researchers also want the organism to be able to feed on the byproducts it creates when it breaks plastic down to truly close the loop. These aren't unsolvable problems, but they're real ones worth noting.


## Nature is also working on it, too


While engineers were building plastic-eating bacteria in the lab, the ocean was already running its own experiment.


A 2025 study found that marine bacteria in heavily polluted ocean waters had independently evolved their own plastic-degrading enzymes that are functional against PET plastic. When the team analyzed over 400 ocean water samples from across the globe, they found this enzyme in nearly 80% of the waters they tested ([Alam et al., 2025](https://academic.oup.com/ismej/article/19/1/wraf121/8159680) ). The ocean's microbes, it turns out, are already adapting to the plastic we've put there.


That's a meaningful discovery for engineering, because it gives us a blueprint. Rather than inventing something new from scratch, we can study what evolution has already optimized for real ocean conditions and use that as a starting point. That's a principle we believe in deeply: nature is often the best first engineer, and the most powerful bioengineering tends to work with it rather than around it. That philosophy is part of a broader movement where scientists are applying[bioengineering to tackle environmental problems](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/how-bioengineering-is-saving-the-planet) , including plastic waste.


In addition, open-ocean deployment of engineered microbes raises real ecological questions that we don't think should be brushed aside—and the broader scientific community agrees. But the underlying science is working, the pace of progress is real, and we think this is a genuinely promising application of bioengineering. We'll be watching it closely.
