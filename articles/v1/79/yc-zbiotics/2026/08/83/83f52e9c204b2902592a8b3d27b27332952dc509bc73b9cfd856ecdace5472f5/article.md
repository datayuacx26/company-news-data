# How to Drink Responsibly in Summer Heat

This article is for informational and educational purposes only and is not intended as medical advice.


## Key Takeaways


- Heat and alcohol both trigger vasodilation, so drinking on a hot day adds more strain to your body's cooling system.
- Even mild dehydration from the heat can change how alcohol affects you, increasing your level of impairment more than you expect or realize, and making you more likely to take risks.
- Hydrating, eating, and pacing yourself help offset the extra strain heat puts on your liver and fluid levels, so your body has what it needs to process alcohol more efficiently.


---


Summer is in full swing—which means a cold beer at the BBQ, a frozen margarita by the pool, and a tequila soda at sunset. It's part of how a lot of us mark the season, but hot weather changes how alcohol interacts with your body in some real ways. If you choose to drink this summer, being aware of those interactions is one of the simplest things you can do to drink more responsibly when temperatures climb.


## Why heat and alcohol don't quite mix


Heat and alcohol affect some of the same systems your body uses to regulate temperature. When those effects overlap, your body has to work harder to stay cool and maintain its fluid balance.


#### Why alcohol makes you feel warm


Your body handles heat by sending more blood toward your skin to help cool you down. It's why your face gets flushed on a hot day, and why your skin feels warm to the touch after a long walk in the sun. The process is called vasodilation, and it's the body's natural way of releasing heat to the environment. Sweat then evaporates off the surface, carrying that heat away.


Summer can change this ecosystem in ways most people never think about. It independently causes peripheral vasodilation, sending warm blood from your core out toward your skin much like heat exposure does[(Morris et al., 2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11391823/) .


#### What happens when alcohol and heat stack


On a hot day, your body is already working hard to cool itself, and adding a drink means you’re pulling on the same biological levers from two directions at once. And while you feel warmer, your core temperature can actually drop, because that same vasodilation and sweating allow heat to radiate out of your body faster[(Yoda et al., 2005)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0741832905001503) .


You're already feeling warm from the environment, and alcohol layers more warmth on top of it. The vasodilation and sweating do help dissipate some heat, but they also put extra demand on your fluid reserves, which are already getting drained by sweating in the sun.


The result is a body that's working harder to cool itself than it would be on a cooler day or if you weren’t drinking, with less margin for error. Alcohol can also make it harder to tell when something is wrong, which is why slowing down, hydrating, and finding shade matters even more.


#### What the research still doesn’t tell us


One honest caveat worth knowing: the existing science on alcohol and heat is more limited than common heat-health advice tends to suggest. The available studies have mostly looked at young, healthy adults at moderate doses, so we don't have a complete picture of how everyone responds. That's a reason to pay attention to your own body, not a reason to assume you're in the clear.


## Your liver has work to do


The liver does a lot of the heavy lifting when it comes to[processing alcohol](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/how-your-body-breaks-down-alcohol) , and it does that work best when it's getting steady blood flow and enough water to operate.


Both of those conditions change in the heat. When your body is working to cool itself, blood gets redirected from your internal organs toward your skin[(Deja et al., 2010)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.3109/02656730903250574) . Exercise in the heat can have a similar effect, reducing the blood flow your liver normally gets[(Sawka et al., 1993)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK236240/) .


Add dehydration on top of that, and your liver is doing its job with fewer resources than usual. None of this means your liver suddenly stops working. It just means the conditions for processing alcohol aren't ideal, and the smart move is to give it a hand by hydrating, eating, and pacing yourself.


## How dehydration changes alcohol’s effects


Hot weather makes dehydration more likely. You lose water through sweat, and on a sunny patio it can add up faster than most people notice. Alcohol contributes too. It's a[mild diuretic](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/does-alcohol-really-cause-dehydration) (at least initially as your blood alcohol begins to rise), which means it nudges your body toward releasing slightly more fluid than if you were drinking non-alcoholic beverages.


The combination matters. Dehydration can change how a drink lands. Scientists found that mild dehydration of about 2.5% body weight loss can shift how alcohol moves through your body and influence risk-taking behavior, even when the amount of alcohol consumed is the same[(Irwin et al., 2012)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0091305712000585) . The state of the body was enough to change how people made decisions.


Even more striking: cognitive performance can be measurably worse when you're dehydrated compared to fully rehydrated, even when subjective feelings of intoxication aren't dramatically different[(Irwin et al., 2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23352231/) . You might not feel more drunk when you're dehydrated, but the way alcohol affects you under the hood can be more pronounced.


In hot weather, that means the same number of drinks can hit harder, affect your judgment more, and leave you more impaired than you realize.


## How to drink responsibly when it's hot out


-


**Alternate alcohol with water.** A glass of water between drinks is one of the easiest things you can do to support your body in the heat. It keeps you hydrated and naturally slows your drinking pace, which is usually a good thing in the sun.


-


**Choose lower-ABV drinks when you can.** A light beer, a spritz, or a longer cocktail with plenty of mixer will land more gently than straight spirits in the sun. Frozen drinks sometimes mask how much alcohol is in them, so be aware of what you're actually ordering.


-


**Take breaks in the shade.** Cooling off helps your body do its job, and gives you a moment to check in with yourself before the next round. If you're starting to feel overheated, that's a signal to step out of the sun, drink some water, and slow down.


-


**Watch your group.** The social rhythm of summer drinking can make it easy to lose track. Looking out for each other is one of the easiest ways to make sure everyone has a good time.


-


**Know the signs of heat illness.** Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or a lack of sweating despite the heat are all signals to stop drinking, get into shade, and rehydrate. If symptoms get worse, get help. Heat illness is serious and alcohol can mask it longer than you'd expect.


## A little extra care goes a long way


Heat doesn't change what alcohol is, but it does change how alcohol interacts with your body—and on a hot day, those small shifts can add up.


Hydrate, eat, pace yourself, take breaks in the shade, and keep an eye on your friends (let them keep an eye on you, too). The same drink you'd have on a cool evening can land very differently when you're sweating through an afternoon in the sun, and giving your body a little extra support helps you enjoy the season without the day catching up to you.
