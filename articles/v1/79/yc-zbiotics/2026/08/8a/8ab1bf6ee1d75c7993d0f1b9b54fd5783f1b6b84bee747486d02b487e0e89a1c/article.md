# Your Skin Has a Microbiome. Summer Tests It.

## Key Takeaways


- Your skin is home to billions of bacteria, fungi, and other microbes that play an active role in keeping it healthy. That community shifts significantly in summer.
- UV radiation, heat, sweat, and travel each put a different kind of pressure on the skin microbiome.
- Small, consistent habits go a long way toward keeping things in balance through the warmer months.


---


You probably already know that your[gut has a microbiome](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/understanding-the-human-microbiome) . But your skin does too, and it's one of the most fascinating ecosystems on your body.


Billions of bacteria, fungi, and viruses live across the surface of your skin[(Chen & Tsao, 2014)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3686918/) . They're not just along for the ride. They actively maintain your skin barrier, regulate inflammation, and help defend against pathogens you encounter every day. Different parts of your body host entirely different communities. The oily zones around your nose and forehead favor different organisms than the dry skin on your arms or the humid creases behind your knees[(Bay et al., 2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7018652/) .


Summer can change this ecosystem in ways most people never think about. The combination of UV radiation, heat, sweat, and travel to new environments puts real pressure on a system that works quietly in the background to keep your skin healthy. Here's what's actually happening and what you can do about it.


## UV exposure and your skin’s microbial balance


Most people know that UV radiation damages skin cells. Fewer know that it can also reshape the microbial community living on them.


UV radiation has direct antimicrobial effects. It depletes certain bacteria, shifts the balance of the community, and over time can reduce microbial diversity—a trend researchers consistently associate with unhealthy skin. And it doesn't take much. A 2024 pilot study in healthy volunteers found that even a single UV exposure measurably changed the composition and diversity of the skin microbiome on participants' backs[(Schuetz et al., 2024)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ics.12910) .


#### How UV changes the skin’s environment


UV's effects also go deeper than which microbes are present, and in what numbers. A 2023 mouse study showed that UV exposure changed the underlying chemistry of skin itself—the lipids, amino acids, and other compounds that make up its protective layer—which in turn shifted the environment the microbiome lives in[(Patra et al., 2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-34073-3) . Skin and its microbiome aren't two separate things sharing space—they're a connected system, and UV affects all of it at once.


#### Does sunscreen affect skin bacteria?


Given all that, it's worth knowing what your sunscreen is doing to your microbiome too. Mineral sunscreens, which use ingredients like zinc oxide and titanium dioxide to physically block UV, have been the subject of some debate on this front. The good news: a recent 2025 study tested both mineral sunscreen types (zinc oxide and titanium dioxide) on volunteers and found no significant change in skin microbiome composition over 24 hours, suggesting both are safe for the microbiome under normal use[(Smith et al., 2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12838188/) .


Whatever UV protection you choose, your skin and its microbiome face the sun together—and choosing one is what matters most.


#### How heat and sweat affect your skin


Sweat is your body's cooling system. It's also a major force in shaping which microbes thrive on your skin.


When you sweat, you change the pH, moisture, and nutrient content of your skin's surface. Different bacteria respond very differently to those conditions. The warm, humid environment created by sweat favors organisms like Staphylococcus and Corynebacterium, which tend to be more abundant in summer. Some of these are perfectly benign or even helpful. But when the[balance tips too far](https://zbiotics.com/blogs/journal/are-there-good-or-bad-bacteria) , from excessive sweating, sitting around in wet clothes, or infrequent rinsing, opportunistic bacteria and fungi can overgrow. That's what's behind summer skin issues like folliculitis, body acne, and fungal rashes.


Sweat itself isn't the problem. It actually contains its own antimicrobial compounds, including a peptide called dermcidin that helps keep certain pathogens in check[(Schittek et al., 2001)](https://www.nature.com/articles/ni732) . The trouble starts when sweat lingers on your skin long enough for the wrong organisms to take advantage.


## How travel changes your skin’s environment


Every environment—the water, the air, the soil, the surfaces you touch—has its own microbial signature. When you travel, your skin can come into contact with entirely new communities of microbes it hasn't encountered before.


#### New climates, pools, and hygiene routines


This isn't inherently dangerous. The skin microbiome is constantly exchanging microbes with the environment, and that exchange is part of what keeps it diverse and resilient. But rapid changes can temporarily disrupt balance, especially if you're also dealing with other stressors at the same time: long flights, big shifts in climate and humidity, chlorinated pools, or aggressive use of hand sanitizers, which significantly disrupt the skin microbiome.


A 2023 study tracking holidaymakers found measurable changes in skin microbiota composition after just a few weeks of sun and travel[(Willmott et al., 2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10442491/) . Most of those shifts resolve over time, but they're a good reminder that your skin is actively responding to where you are and what you're doing.


#### Tips for supporting your skin microbiome this summer


None of this means you should avoid the sun, skip the beach, or stop sweating. It just means being a little more thoughtful about supporting your skin when it's working harder than usual.


-


**Gently rinse after sweating.** A mild, fragrance-free cleanser removes sweat and opportunistic bacteria without stripping the skin's natural oils and protective microbes. Harsh antibacterial soaps can do more harm than good by wiping out the protective layer of good bacteria along with the bad.


-


**Find your shade.** Hats, clothing, umbrellas, and shade are consistently more effective than sunscreen at limiting UV exposure—partly because they're a physical barrier, and partly because most people don't apply enough sunscreen or reapply it often enough to get the protection on the label[(Linos et al., 2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21637987/) .


-


**Apply and reapply.** Sunscreen is the layer that covers what clothing can't. The single most important thing it does for your skin microbiome is protect it from the UV damage that throws things off balance in the first place. The FDA and American Academy of Dermatology both recommend a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, applied generously and reapplied every two hours.


-


**Be skeptical of sunscreen alternatives.** Oils like coconut, raspberry seed, and carrot seed all get hyped online as “natural sunscreens”. The science doesn't back them up. A rigorous 2021 study tested 14 oils and found the most-hyped options had real-world SPF values of just 1.2 to 2.8—nowhere near the SPF 30 minimum recommended for meaningful protection[(Ácsová et al., 2021)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43630-020-00009-3) . Your skin and your microbiome both need reliable UV protection, and most home remedies don't deliver it.


-


**Wear dry clothes.** Change out of wet swimwear and workout clothes sooner rather than later. Wet, warm conditions are exactly what the less friendly bacteria and fungi prefer. Swapping into dry clothes is one of the simplest things you can do to keep your microbiome happy.


-


**Go easy on antibacterial products.** Sanitizers and antibacterial soaps have their place, but overusing them can reduce microbial diversity on your skin and leave room for less desirable organisms to move in. Regular soap and water is usually plenty.


-


**Moisturize after sun and swimming.** Both UV exposure and chlorinated water can compromise the skin barrier, changing the environment your microbiome depends on. A simple, fragrance-free moisturizer will do the trick, especially if you have sensitive skin.


## Supporting your skin microbiome through summer


Your skin microbiome is working all summer long, adapting to the sun, the heat, the sweat, and wherever you take it. Most of the time it does this quietly and remarkably well. A little support and protection goes a long way.
