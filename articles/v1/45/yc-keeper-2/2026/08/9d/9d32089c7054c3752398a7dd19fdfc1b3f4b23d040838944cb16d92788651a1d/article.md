# VRBO Tax Tips: 2026 Guide for Taxes

Renting a place on VRBO turns your property into a small business, and the tax rules are a bit different from a normal long-term rental. The good news is that most of the confusion comes down to a few specific questions, and once you know the answers, filing is manageable. This guide walks through the 1099-K, the Schedule E versus Schedule C decision, the 14-day rule, and the deductions hosts most often miss.


## Do you owe taxes on VRBO income?


**Yes. Money you earn hosting on VRBO is taxable rental income, whether or not VRBO sends you a form.** The only broad exception is the 14-day rule, covered below.


What you get taxed on is your profit, meaning rental income minus your expenses. VRBO reports the gross, so a big part of doing this right is subtracting everything you are entitled to deduct.

Most VRBO hosts belong on **Schedule E** and do not owe self-employment tax. You only cross into Schedule C if you provide services mainly for your guests' convenience, the kind of thing a hotel does.


One common myth is worth clearing up. A short average stay does **not** by itself push you onto Schedule C. You can host guests for a night or two at a time and still file Schedule E, as long as you are not providing hotel-style services.


## The 14-day rule: when VRBO income is tax-free


There is a genuinely great rule for occasional hosts. If you rent out a home you use yourself for **14 days or fewer** during the year, the rental income is completely tax-free, and you do not even report it. This is sometimes called the Augusta rule.


The trade-off is that you also cannot deduct rental expenses for those days. And if VRBO sends a 1099-K for that income, you will need to report it and then back it out, so the IRS can see why it is not taxed. If your place is a true residence you use often and you rent it more than 14 days, the vacation-home rules kick in and limit your deductions to your rental income.


## Does VRBO collect occupancy tax for you?


Sometimes, and this is worth checking carefully. In areas where VRBO has an agreement, it collects and pays **lodging or occupancy tax** for you automatically, and you should not also collect it. But coverage is uneven. VRBO might handle the state tax but not your city or county tax, which leaves you responsible for the rest.


Check your owner dashboard under Taxes to see exactly what VRBO covers in your location. Occupancy tax is separate from income tax, so VRBO handling it does nothing for the income tax you owe on your profit.


## The short-term rental loophole, used carefully


You may have heard that a short-term rental can create paper losses that offset your regular W-2 income. It can, but only under specific conditions, and this is an area people get wrong.


Rental losses are normally passive, which means they cannot offset your job income. Two things change that for a short-term rental: your **average guest stay is 7 days or fewer** , and you **materially participate** in running it. When both are true, the losses become non-passive and can offset active income. The losses themselves usually come from a cost segregation study plus bonus depreciation, which the 2025 tax law restored to 100% for qualifying property.


Two cautions. First, material participation has to be real and documented with an hours log. This is the number one thing examiners look at. Second, this loophole is about the passive-loss rules, not self-employment tax. Qualifying for it does not put you on Schedule C. Given the dollars involved, this is a strategy to run past a tax pro before you count on it.


## How much tax will you owe?


On Schedule E, you owe income tax on your net rental profit at your regular rate, and no self-employment tax. Depreciation often shelters a good chunk of that profit, so many hosts owe less than they expect in the early years.


VRBO does not withhold anything, so if you expect to owe $1,000 or more, plan on[quarterly estimated payments](https://www.keepertax.com/posts/how-to-file-quarterly-taxes) .

## How to file your VRBO taxes


1.


**Gather your records.** Your 1099-K, your VRBO payout and reservation reports, and receipts for expenses.


2.


**Confirm your form.** Schedule E for most hosts, Schedule C only if you provide hotel-style services.


3.


**Start from the gross,** then subtract VRBO fees, refunds, and any lodging tax VRBO collected.


4.


**Add depreciation,** which for most hosts is a major deduction. A tax pro or good software will calculate it.


5.


**Report the net** on Schedule E (or Schedule C), and pay quarterly going forward if needed.


## Accountant tips most VRBO hosts miss


-


**Add your VRBO fees back as a deduction.** They are baked into the gross 1099-K, so if you do not deduct them, you are paying tax on money that went to VRBO.


-


**Do not forget depreciation.** It is the deduction that most often turns a taxable-looking year into a small or zero tax bill, and skipping it does not save it for later.


-


**Log your hours** if you plan to use the short-term rental loophole. No log, no deduction if you are examined.


-


**Separate the taxes VRBO collected** from your income, so you do not double count occupancy tax as revenue.


## Frequently asked questions


**My VRBO 1099-K is much higher than what I received. Why?**


It reports gross payments, including VRBO's fees, lodging taxes collected, and refunds. Report the gross, then deduct those items so you are taxed on your real profit.


**I earned under $20,000 and got no 1099-K. Do I still report VRBO income?**


Yes. The income is taxable whether or not a form is issued.


**Do I file Schedule E or Schedule C for my VRBO?**


Most hosts use Schedule E and owe no self-employment tax. You only use Schedule C if you provide substantial, hotel-style services during the stay.


**Do I owe self-employment tax on VRBO income?**


Not on Schedule E. You would only owe the 15.3% self-employment tax if your hosting rises to a Schedule C business.


**I only rented my place for a week. Is that income tax-free?**


If you rent a home you use for 14 days or fewer in the year, that income is tax-free and unreported, though you cannot deduct expenses for those days.
