# How to Read Thermal Camera Specifications NETD, Resolution, Range & Spectral Band

**Reading a thermal imaging system datasheet comes down to understanding a handful of numbers and knowing what each one means for your mission.**


- **NETD tells you how sensitive the camera is, resolution tells you how much detail you get, and spectral band tells you what the camera is tuned to see.**
- **Range specs (detection, recognition, identification) describe distance under ideal conditions, so treat them as a ceiling, not a promise.**
- **Frame rate affects both smooth motion and export compliance, which can reshape your procurement timeline.**
- **The best number on paper rarely wins. The spec that matches your operating environment does.**


**Match every spec to how and where the system will actually be used, then hold each vendor to the same short list.**

## How Do You Read Thermal Camera Specifications?


Every thermal imaging system datasheet is built around a few core categories. Get comfortable with these, and you can evaluate almost any


[complete thermal imaging solution](https://www.lightpath.com/thermal-imaging-solutions) on the market. Here's the quick version of what each number is answering.


**Spec**


**Plain-English question it answers**


NETD


How faint a temperature difference can it see?


Resolution


How much detail, and how far?


Spectral band


What is it tuned to detect?


Range (DRI)


How far can it detect, recognize, or identify a target?


Frame rate


How smoothly does it capture motion?


The trap most buyers fall into is chasing the best number in every row. A better approach is to decide what your mission actually needs in each category, then spec to those marks without paying for capabilities you'll never use. That mindset turns a page of thermal camera specifications into a set of decisions you can defend.


## NETD: How Sensitive Is the Camera?


NETD stands for Noise Equivalent Temperature Difference, and it's the most useful sensitivity number on the page. In plain terms, it tells you the smallest temperature difference the camera can pick out from background noise. It's measured in millikelvin (mK), and lower is better. A low NETD thermal camera produces a cleaner image and more easily separates a warm target from a similar-temperature background.


If your targets barely stand out from their surroundings, such as a person in mild weather or a subtle hot spot, sensitivity does the heavy lifting. If your targets run dramatically hotter or colder than the scene, you can often accept a higher number and spend your budget elsewhere. When you compare a NETD thermal camera spec across vendors, confirm they're quoting it under similar test conditions because the value shifts with the scene and the optics.


## Thermal Camera Resolution: How Much Detail Do You Get?


Resolution is the pixel count of the detector, written as something like 320x240 or 640x480. More pixels mean more detail, and more detail generally means you can pick out smaller targets or see the same target from farther away. It's the spec buyers intuitively understand, which is why it tends to get over-weighted.


Higher thermal camera resolution isn't automatically the right call. More pixels can mean a bigger, heavier, more power-hungry system at a higher price, and on some platforms, they push you into stricter export categories. A wide-area scanning role might be better served by a lower-resolution sensor with a wider field of view. The right thermal camera resolution resolves your smallest target at your typical working distance, and no more. This breakdown of


[resolution across different use cases](https://www.lightpath.com/insights/what-thermal-camera-resolution-is-best-per-use-case) shows how the tradeoff plays out.


## Range: Detection, Recognition, and Identification


Range is where datasheets get slippery. Most quote three distances based on a long-standing standard, usually shortened to DRI. It helps to know what each really means before you compare them because the words don't mean what most people assume.


- **Detection** means you can tell


*something* is there, a heat blob against the background, without knowing what it is.


- **Recognition** means you can tell what


*class* it is: person, vehicle, or animal.


- **Identification** means you can make out specific features, like which type of vehicle or what a person is carrying.


Two things are worth remembering. First, these distances assume near-ideal conditions, so treat any published range as a best case that shrinks in fog, smoke, or heavy humidity. Second, range depends on the lens and field of view as much as the sensor. A longer focal length reaches farther but narrows what you see, so a longer-range thermal imaging system often trades away situational awareness. Decide which distance defines mission success, then spec to that one.


## Thermal Imaging Spectral Band: What the Camera Is Tuned to See


The thermal imaging spectral band tells you which slice of the infrared spectrum the camera detects. Three bands show up most often on datasheets.


**Band**


**Wavelength**


**Best suited for**


LWIR (long-wave)


8–14 µm


Ambient-temperature targets, people, vehicles, most surveillance and industrial monitoring


MWIR (mid-wave)


3–5 µm


High-temperature targets and long-range detection, often in cooled systems


Broadband (BBIR)


2–14 µm


Mixed scenes where hot and subtle targets appear together


Most surveillance, security, and industrial monitoring programs land on


[long-wave infrared](https://www.lightpath.com/thermal-imaging-solutions/long-wave-infrared) because it sees ambient-temperature objects well and works with lower-cost


[uncooled detectors](https://www.lightpath.com/insights/uncooled-vs-cooled-lwir-cameras-a-guide) . Programs that need very hot targets or extreme distances often move to


[mid-wave infrared](https://www.lightpath.com/thermal-imaging-solutions/mid-wave-infrared) , which pairs with cooled detectors for higher sensitivity. If your scene mixes hot and near-ambient targets, a broadband thermal imaging spectral band adds flexibility that a single-band system can't. Matching the band to your targets matters more than almost any other line on the datasheet.


## Thermal Camera Frame Rate (and Why It Affects Compliance)


Frame rate, measured in hertz (Hz), is how many images the system captures each second. A higher thermal camera frame rate produces smoother video, which matters when you're tracking fast movement or imaging from a moving platform. Standard systems run around 30 to 60 Hz, which is plenty for most monitoring and surveillance work. When events happen in milliseconds, you move into


[high-speed thermal imaging](https://www.lightpath.com/blog/high-speed-thermal-imaging-camera-in-aerospace-rd) territory, which is a different class of system.


Frame rate is also a compliance specification. U.S.


[export-control rules](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/02/23/2024-03661/revision-of-license-requirements-of-certain-cameras-systems-or-related-components) place license requirements on certain thermal cameras based on frame rate thresholds. If your program might ship internationally, the thermal camera frame rate you spec can affect where you're allowed to sell. Answer that at the specification stage, not at delivery, where a late discovery compresses your schedule.


## Where Datasheet Numbers Meet Reality


A datasheet describes performance under controlled test conditions, and the field is messier. Measured temperature accuracy, for example, depends heavily on a target's surface emissivity.


[Research on infrared emissivity](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11435899/) shows readings can drift as a surface oxidizes over time, so a camera that nails accuracy on a lab reference can read differently on shiny metal.


The same caution applies to detection. Thermal imaging sees through fog, smoke, dust, and darkness far better than a visible camera, but it isn't magic. Heavy rain and high-radiance sources like direct sun degrade performance, and range estimates assume cooperative conditions. Read every number as "up to," and confirm real-world performance against your actual operating environment before you commit.


## 5 Questions to Ask Before You Trust a Thermal Imaging System Datasheet


Once you understand the specs, evaluating vendors gets much easier. Run every datasheet through these five questions:


1. **What conditions were these numbers measured under?** NETD, range, and accuracy all shift with the test setup. Compare thermal imaging system specs that were measured the same way.


2. **Does the resolution resolve my smallest target at my working distance?** Not the demo distance in a vendor video. Yours.


3. **Is the spectral band matched to my targets?** Ambient-temperature targets favor LWIR; very hot or long-range targets often favor MWIR.


4. **Does the frame rate create a compliance issue?** Check export thresholds before you lock the architecture, not after.


5. **Who stands behind the system?** A partner who engineers and supports the full system is worth more than a slightly better number on a catalog part.


Getting clear answers to those five questions turns a page of thermal imaging system specs into a confident buying decision, and it exposes the vendors who can't back their numbers up.


## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is the most important spec on a thermal camera datasheet?


There isn't a single one. The most important spec is whichever maps to your mission. For faint targets, NETD (sensitivity) leads. For identifying targets at a distance, resolution and range matter most. For the right kind of target, the spectral band decides everything. Read thermal camera specifications as a set, not a ranking.


### Is a lower or higher NETD better?


Lower is better. NETD measures the smallest temperature difference a NETD thermal camera can detect, so a lower millikelvin value means a cleaner, more sensitive image. Just confirm that vendors quote it under comparable conditions.


### What thermal camera resolution do I need?


Enough to resolve your smallest target at your real working distance. Common formats are 320x240 and 640x480, and higher resolutions help with identification at range, but they add cost, size, and sometimes export complexity. Match resolution to the task.


### LWIR or MWIR: which spectral band should I choose?


LWIR (8–14 µm) suits most ambient-temperature surveillance and industrial monitoring and works with affordable uncooled detectors. MWIR (3–5 µm) suits high-temperature targets and long-range detection, usually with cooled detectors. Your targets and environment decide the right thermal imaging spectral band.
