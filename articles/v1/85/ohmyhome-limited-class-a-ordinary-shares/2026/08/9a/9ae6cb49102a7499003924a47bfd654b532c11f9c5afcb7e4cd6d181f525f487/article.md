# Why Are Some Singapore Private Homes Selling at a Loss in 2026?

Some private homes in Singapore are selling at a loss because their individual resale prices have not kept pace with what their owners originally paid, even though the overall private residential property price index is still rising.


[Data consolidated by Cushman & Wakefield for The Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/property/loss-making-private-home-resale-deals-rise-q2-price-growth-moderates) found that 4.7% of private home resale transactions in Q2 2026 were loss-making, up from 3.7% in the previous quarter. This was the highest proportion recorded in four years.


Yet[Singapore’s overall private residential property price index still rose by 0.5%](https://www.ura.gov.sg/news/media/pr26-57/) during the same period, according to the Urban Redevelopment Authority (URA). So, how can the wider market move upwards while some owners sell for less than they paid?


The answer often lies beyond the national index. A home’s entry price, its individual characteristics, the competition around it and the owner’s reason for moving can each shape the final outcome.


## What is Considered a Loss-making Private Home Resale?


A private home resale is generally considered loss-making when its recorded selling price is lower than what the previous owner paid.


Suppose someone bought a condo for $1.8 million and later sold it for $1.7 million. Based on those two transaction prices, the property recorded a gross loss of $100,000.


However, that calculation does not tell the owner’s entire financial story. It may exclude stamp duty, mortgage interest, legal fees, renovation and maintenance costs, as well as any rental income earned. The reported loss is therefore a comparison of recorded prices, not necessarily the owner’s complete investment return.


## Are Singapore Private Home Prices Falling?


No. Singapore’s overall private residential property price index still increased by 0.5% in Q2 2026, although growth eased from 0.9% in Q1.


Yet that increase was not shared equally across the market. According to the URA, landed home prices rose by 2.5%, while non-landed private home prices slipped by 0.1%.

Meanwhile, 3,813 private home resale transactions were completed, up from 3,225 in Q1. Resales represented 62% of all private residential sale transactions. Resale activity therefore remained substantial, even though different parts of the market were moving at different speeds.


## Why Can a Seller Lose Money in a Rising Market?


Market indices provide a broad picture of price movements across the market. Your outcome, however, begins with one particular home bought at one particular price.


### 1. You may have entered at a higher price


Two owners in the same development can achieve different results simply because they bought at different times or prices.


Someone who purchased during an especially competitive launch, paid a premium for a preferred unit or entered near a local price peak may have less room to recover the amount later. The relevant starting point is what you actually paid for your unit, not simply the project’s original launch price.


### 2. Your development may not follow the wider market


Even when prices rise across a region, particular developments can underperform. Buyers compare tenure or remaining lease, transport links, amenities, maintenance fees, layouts and competing listings. Even two units within one condo[can attract different responses because of their floor](https://ohmyhome.com/en-sg/blog/high-floor-vs-low-floor-unit-what-its-actually-like-to-live-on-each/?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=backlink&utm_campaign=PrivateHomesSellAtALoss) , facing and condition.


### 3. Sometimes, waiting is not practical


Life does not always follow the ideal property timeline. An owner may need to sell because of divorce, relocation, retirement, changing family needs or difficulty managing the property’s monthly costs.


In those circumstances, accepting a lower price may be more manageable than holding a home that no longer fits. The sale could still help the household reduce its commitments, move nearer to family or find a more suitable home. A loss-making transaction is therefore not automatically a poor decision. The purpose of the move matters too.


## Should You Wait Until Your Condo Returns to Its Purchase Price?


Not necessarily. Waiting may help if holding the property remains affordable and there is a reasonable basis for expecting its position to improve. However, the original purchase price should not become the only number guiding your decision.


Before postponing a sale, consider its monthly holding cost, whether the home still meets your needs, how competing supply may affect it and how long you can realistically wait. Buyers assess a property against today’s alternatives, not what its owner previously paid. Holding out for a particular price only makes sense when it remains compatible with the rest of your plans.


## What Should You Calculate Before Selling Your Condo?


Rather than looking at the purchase and selling prices alone, separate these three figures.


### 1. Your home’s estimated market value


Recent transactions in the same development provide a starting point, adjusted for your unit’s floor, orientation, layout and condition.

### 2. Your potential gross gain or loss


Compare the expected selling price with the amount you originally paid. This indicates whether the transaction itself is likely to be recorded as profitable or loss-making.


### 3. What may remain for your next home


Your estimated cash proceeds are calculated after accounting for the outstanding housing loan, required CPF refund, legal expenses, agent fees and any applicable Seller’s Stamp Duty.


The required CPF refund generally includes the CPF principal used for the property and the accrued interest. This money does not disappear. It returns to your CPF account, although how it is allocated and how much remains available for another property depend on your age and applicable CPF rules. Your gross gain, cash proceeds and next-home budget are therefore related, but they are not the same figure.


## What Does This Mean for Singapore Condo Owners?


The rise in loss-making resales does not mean most private homeowners are losing money. At 4.7%, these transactions still formed a small minority of the deals examined.


What the figure does show is that owning property does not guarantee every household the same outcome. The wider market matters, but so do your entry price, unit, development, holding period and reason for selling.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What happens if my sale proceeds cannot cover the required CPF refund?


You only need to refund the amount remaining after repaying your outstanding housing loan if you sold the property at market value. If this amount cannot cover the required CPF refund, you generally do not need to make up the shortfall in cash.


### Do I still pay Seller’s Stamp Duty if I sell my property at a loss?


Yes, Seller’s Stamp Duty may still apply even when you sell at a loss. It depends on when you purchased the property and how long you held it. SSD is calculated using the higher of the selling price or market value, rather than whether the sale was profitable.


### Is a loss from selling a private home tax-deductible in Singapore?


Generally, no. A loss on a home held as a personal investment is normally capital in nature and cannot usually be deducted against taxable income. The treatment may differ if IRAS considers you to be trading in properties, based on factors such as transaction frequency, purpose and holding period.
