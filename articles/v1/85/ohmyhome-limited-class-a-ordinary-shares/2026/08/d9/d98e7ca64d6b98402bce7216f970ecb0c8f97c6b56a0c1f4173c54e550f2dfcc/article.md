# Singapore’s Proposed En Bloc Consent Thresholds in 2026: What Owners Need to Know

Singapore may lower the consent needed for en bloc sales involving older private developments. Under the proposed Land Titles (Strata) (Amendment) Bill, the threshold would fall to 70% for developments aged 40 to 59 years and 65% for those aged 60 years or older.


These figures are attracting attention, but the rules have **not changed yet** . The Bill was introduced in Parliament on 4 August 2026 and still needs to be debated and passed before any new thresholds can take effect.


There is also more to the proposal than the headline percentages. Consent is measured in two ways, and reaching the required threshold would not guarantee that an en bloc sale succeeds.


Let’s get into it below.


## What En Bloc Consent Thresholds Are Being Proposed?


Only developments aged 40 years or more would receive lower thresholds under the Bill. The requirements for younger developments would stay the same.


**Development age** **Current law** **If the Bill is passed**


Less than 10 years 90% 90% (unchanged)


10 to 39 years 80% 80% (unchanged)


40 to 59 years 80% **70%**


60 years or older 80% **65%**


Each threshold must be met by both share value and strata area. It is therefore not as simple as securing agreement from 70% or 65% of owners by headcount.


Share value is a number allocated to each strata lot and is used for matters such as voting and determining contributions to common expenses. Strata area refers to the total area of the lots recorded in the subsidiary strata certificates of title, excluding accessory-lot areas under the Land Titles (Strata) Act.


Here is how the two-part test could work: If a 45-year-old development had 72% support by share value but only 68% by strata area, it would still fall short of the proposed 70% threshold. Both measures would need to reach 70%.


## How Would a Development’s Age Be Determined?


The relevant age is the age of the development, not the number of years an owner has held a unit or the remaining lease on the land.


For a strata development, age is measured from the latest Temporary Occupation Permit (TOP) issued upon completion of a building in the strata title plan. If no TOP was issued, the latest Certificate of Statutory Completion is used instead.


This detail could matter for developments completed in phases, since the relevant date may be later than some owners expect.


## Why Are Lower Thresholds Being Considered?


As private developments age, they may require more spending on maintenance, repairs and major works. Yet even when most owners are open to redevelopment, an en bloc attempt can stop if it does not reach the current 80% threshold.


The proposed tiers are intended to give ageing developments a more practical route to urban renewal when there is broad support. That does not mean every older development should be sold, or that an en bloc sale would be the best outcome for every owner.


For owners who favour a sale, the lower thresholds could make it easier to move an attempt forward. For those who want to remain in their homes, fewer consenting owners would be needed to reach the statutory majority. This is why the Bill also proposes tighter requirements at other stages of the process and added safeguards for owners who do not consent.


## What Safeguards Are Being Proposed for Owners Who Do Not Want to Sell?


The Bill does more than lower the final consent thresholds. It also proposes changes to how an attempt begins, how long it can gather support and how soon another attempt can follow if the first one fails.


**Proposed safeguard** **What it could mean**


**35% support to call the first meeting** Owners representing at least 35% of the development’s share value **or** 35% of its total units would be needed to call a meeting to form a Collective Sale Committee. More backing would be required before an attempt could begin.


**Six months to collect signatures** Committees would have six months, down from 12 months, to obtain the required signatures for the Collective Sale Agreement. Owners would know sooner whether an attempt has enough support, although committees would have less time to address concerns.


**Three-year restriction after a failed attempt** The restriction period would increase from two years to three years. During this period, greater support would be needed to call another meeting.


**More potential compensation in qualifying cases** The Bill would increase the maximum additional amount a court may award to an objecting owner who would otherwise suffer financial loss. The proposed limit is the higher of $2,000 or 0.5% of the sale proceeds payable to that owner.


One distinction is especially important: the proposed 35% requirement would apply only to calling the meeting. It could be met through either share value or the number of units. Final consent for the collective sale would still need to meet the relevant threshold through **both share value and strata area** .


Additional compensation would not be automatic simply because an owner objects. It would depend on the financial-loss test under the law and the circumstances of the case.


## What Could Happen to En Bloc Attempts Already Under Way?


If the Bill is eventually passed, the timing of the first signature on the Collective Sale Agreement would help determine which rules apply.


Under the Bill as introduced:


- If the first signature has **not** been obtained before the new provisions commence, most of the new rules would apply.
- If the first signature has already been obtained before commencement, the attempt would generally continue under the existing rules.


The Bill would also allow a committee to end an existing Collective Sale Agreement and restart under the proposed framework, subject to the transitional requirements.


**These provisions are not active yet** . If the Bill becomes law, committees and owners involved in an ongoing attempt would need to confirm which rules apply to their specific dates and documents.


## Would a Lower Threshold Make an En Bloc Sale More Likely?


It could help an older development clear the consent stage, but that is only one part of an en bloc sale.


Owners would still need to form a Collective Sale Committee, agree on key terms and secure the required signatures. The development would then need to attract a buyer on acceptable terms. Where not all owners consent, the sale would also need to follow the statutory process for a collective-sale order and satisfy requirements such as the good-faith test.


Put another way, the proposed thresholds could help answer one question: Does the development have enough owner support to proceed?


They would not answer two others:


- Would a developer meet the reserve price?
- Would the eventual proceeds be enough for each owner’s next move?


Developers would still weigh the land price against the site’s planning potential, construction costs, financing and expected demand. Meanwhile, owners may have a reserve price that the market is unwilling to meet. Lower consent thresholds could bring more sites forward, but pricing and project feasibility would continue to influence whether they find buyers.


## Does the Proposal Cover Only Older Condominiums?


No. Condominiums may be the most familiar example, but Singapore’s collective-sale framework also covers other strata developments, including commercial and mixed-use properties.


The Bill also proposes extending the majority-consent framework to certain **non-strata-titled private residential developments** where flat owners hold long leases but do not own the underlying land. These developments generally require unanimous agreement between the flat owners and landowner under the current arrangements, except in limited cases involving registered leases of at least 850 years.


The eligibility conditions are technical, so the proposal should not be read as covering every private estate with a different ownership structure.


## Have the En Bloc Rules Changed Yet?


No. As of the time of publication, the Bill has been introduced but has not been passed or taken effect.


For now, developments less than 10 years old still require at least 90% consent by share value and strata area. Developments aged 10 years or more still require at least 80% through both measures.


The next milestone is the Bill’s second reading and parliamentary debate. If Parliament passes it, the final wording, presidential assent and commencement date would still need to be confirmed. **Until then, owners should treat the 70% and 65% figures as proposals, not the rules governing an en bloc attempt today.**

## Frequently Asked Questions


### Can an owner refuse to participate in an en bloc sale?


An owner may choose not to sign the Collective Sale Agreement. However, if the required consent threshold is reached, the collective sale may still proceed through the statutory process. A dissenting owner may file an objection with the Strata Titles Boards within 21 days of receiving the Notice of Proposed Application. If a collective-sale order is eventually granted, the sale will bind all owners, including those who did not consent.


### How are en bloc sale proceeds divided among owners?


The proceeds are not necessarily divided equally. The proposed method of distribution must be stated in the sale documents and supported by an independent valuer’s report. Factors such as share value, strata area and the valuation of individual units may be considered, depending on the agreed method.


### **Is an en bloc sale the same as SERS?**


No. An en bloc sale generally involves private property owners collectively selling their development to a buyer. The Selective En bloc Redevelopment Scheme, or SERS, is an HDB redevelopment programme for selected public housing precincts and includes rehousing arrangements for affected flat owners.
