# Why Do Some People in Singapore Rent Instead of Buy Property Even If They Can Afford To?

Ask around and you’ll find plenty of Singaporeans who could easily afford to buy a home, yet choose to keep renting. The main reason isn’t one single thing. It’s usually a mix of not wanting to get stuck with a decision they can’t undo, waiting for a better opportunity, or simply having their money tied up elsewhere.


But don’t get me wrong, homeownership in Singapore is still the clear norm. About 91% of resident households own their home, and only about 8.2% rent as of 2025, according to the Department of Statistics Singapore. Within that small renting minority, the reasons are more specific and more personal than most people assume.


## Who Are the Singaporeans Choosing to Rent?


Those renting by choice generally fall into several groups, each with a different reason for holding off on buying:


- Young professionals in their late 20s and early 30s who are single and simply haven’t made buying a property a priority yet.


- People who don’t want to lock themselves into a decision they can’t easily undo, given how much the Minimum Occupation Period (MOP) and Seller’s Stamp Duty (SSD) limit their ability to reverse a wrong call.


- Existing flat owners who want a second home to live in but rent it instead of buying, since Additional Buyer’s Stamp Duty (ABSD) on a second property costs hundreds of thousands upfront.


- Couples waiting out their BTO’s construction period, renting somewhere convenient in the meantime.


- Expats and Permanent Residents (PRs) who aren’t sure how long they’ll actually stay in the country.


- A smaller but growing group who’ve run the numbers and decided renting is the better financial move for their specific situation.


What ties all these groups together is this: none of them are renting because they lack the funds. Many have healthy CPF Ordinary Account balances and could pass a bank’s Total Debt Servicing Ratio (TDSR) test without issue. They’re renting on purpose.


## They Don’t Want to Get Stuck With the Wrong Choice


Buy a flat in Singapore, and you’re not just signing a loan, you’re committing to a lock-in period that isn’t easy to walk away from. HDB flats come with a 5-year MOP before you can sell. Private property comes with SSD if you sell within the first few years. Either way, a wrong call sticks around.


Say you buy in a location that[turns out to have a difficult neighbour](https://ohmyhome.com/en-sg/blog/13-types-of-neighbours-every-hdb-resident-has-had/?utm_source=blog&utm_medium=backlink&utm_campaign=RentInsteadofBuy2026) , or noise issues, or maintenance problems the seller never disclosed. If you own it, you’re stuck living with that until your lock-in period ends. If you’re renting, you break the lease and move.


That risk alone is enough to make some people hold off, even when they can afford to buy. They’re not avoiding commitment out of indecision. They’re avoiding a decision they can’t reverse if it turns out to be the wrong one.


## They’re Waiting for a Better Entry Point


Some renters aren’t waiting on their own finances. They’re waiting on the market. When property prices are high and the job market feels shaky, some buyers would rather sit out and rent than lock in a price they think might drop.

This isn’t about timing the market perfectly. It’s a judgment call: if you believe better opportunities will show up in a year or two, whether that’s a specific unit, a better price, or simply more certainty about your job and income, renting buys you the room to wait without the pressure of a mortgage riding on today’s prices.


## Their Money Is Already Committed Elsewhere


Not every renter in Singapore is saving toward a local property. Some already own a home overseas, or are actively saving toward one. Others are PRs with migration plans in the works, and buying property here doesn’t make sense if they’re not staying long-term.


For this group, renting isn’t a placeholder while they figure out Singapore. It’s a deliberate choice because their long-term plans point elsewhere. Buying a Singapore flat would tie up capital they’d rather keep available for wherever they’re actually headed.


## ABSD Makes a Second Home More Expensive Than It Looks


Not everyone renting is doing so because they don’t own property yet. Some already own a flat, but want to live somewhere else too, maybe closer to work, or in a different neighbourhood for a season of life. Instead of buying that second place, they rent it, while keeping their existing flat as it is.


The reason comes down to ABSD. Buying a second residential property means a Singapore Citizen pays 20% ABSD on top of the purchase price. On a $1.5 million condo, that’s $300,000 in stamp duty alone, before legal fees, agent commission, or renovation.


Faced with that bill, many decide it isn’t worth it. They hold onto the flat they already own and simply rent the second place they want to live in. No ABSD, no fresh mortgage, no long-term commitment to a location they’re not fully sure about yet. If life changes again in a year or two, they’re not stuck holding a second property they no longer need.


It’s a trade-off many make on purpose. They give up the chance to build equity in that second home, in exchange for staying flexible while they figure out what they actually want long-term.


## When Renting Actually Makes Financial Sense


Renting tends to make the most sense in a few specific situations:


- You expect to relocate within 2 to 3 years, whether for work, migration, or simply because you’re not settled on where you want to be.


- You already own a property and don’t want to trigger ABSD on a second purchase.


- You’re not confident about current prices or your job stability, and would rather wait for more clarity before committing.


- Your income is rising quickly, and you’d rather buy in 2 to 3 years with a bigger downpayment and better loan terms than stretch yourself now.


Where renting stops making sense is when it becomes a default rather than a decision. Private residential rents rose 0.3% quarter-on-quarter in Q1 2026, according to URA, after a 0.5% dip the quarter before. HDB rents moved similarly, rising 0.6% month-on-month in June 2026, based on rental transaction data.


If you can already picture yourself settling into a specific location for the long haul and you have the means to buy there, every year spent renting instead is a year of mortgage-equivalent cash going somewhere that doesn’t come back. In that case, buying makes far more sense than renting.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### Is it worth it to rent in Singapore?


Renting is worth it when your situation calls for flexibility, whether that’s an uncertain timeline, avoiding MOP, or simply not being ready to commit to one address. It stops being worth it once you’re confident about where you want to settle and can afford to buy there, since every year of rent after that point is money that doesn’t come back.


### Will rental prices go down in 2026 in Singapore?


Rental prices aren’t expected to drop significantly in 2026, based on current trends. Private residential rents rose 0.3% quarter-on-quarter in Q1 2026 after a brief dip the quarter before, and HDB rents rose 0.6% month-on-month as of June 2026. Expect rents to stay roughly flat to slightly up this year, rather than a sharp decline landlords or tenants can bank on.


### How much should you spend on rent in Singapore?


A reasonable amount to spend on rent in Singapore is around 30% of your monthly income, though this shifts depending on your other financial goals. Someone saving aggressively for a downpayment might aim lower, while someone prioritising location or space might stretch closer to 35% to 40%. The right number depends on what else you’re budgeting for.
