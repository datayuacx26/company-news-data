# Right Blood Volume, Better Smears

Blood smear preparation is a cornerstone of veterinary hematology, but the quality of the smear depends heavily on the amount of blood used. One of the most common issues across species is applying too much blood to the slide, which can lead to non-diagnostic smears. This guide provides an overview of what happens when too much blood is used, how to avoid this pitfall, and a reference table showing typical drop volumes from different needle sizes.


**The Impact of Too Much Blood on Smear Quality**


When too much blood is placed on a slide, it leads to a number of challenges that compromise diagnostic utility:


-


**Thick Smears:** Excess blood results in overly thick smears where cells are stacked or clumped together. This makes it nearly impossible to achieve a monolayer and a proper feathered edge, both of which are critical for microscopic evaluation.


-


**Poor Staining and Visibility:** A thick smear doesn’t allow the stain to penetrate evenly. As a result, cells may appear too dark or too pale, and critical cellular details—like subtle nuclear changes or parasite inclusions—may be obscured.


-


**Loss of Morphological Detail:** In a thick smear, cells overlap so much that it becomes difficult to assess individual cell shapes, sizes, and internal structures. This can prevent accurate identification of abnormalities such as anisocytosis, poikilocytosis, or leukocyte inclusions.


**How to Avoid Using Too Much Blood**


Preventing smears from becoming too thick starts with understanding how much blood is actually needed:


-


**Use a Consistent Blood Volume:** Rather than relying on “a drop,” aim for a small, standardized volume. For most species, around 2–3 microliters of blood is sufficient to create a good smear. This helps ensure a clear feathered edge and a single cell layer.


-


**Adjust for Needle Size:** Drops of blood vary based on the needle or capillary tube used. For example, a drop from a 25-gauge needle will generally be smaller than a drop from an insulin syringe. Understanding these differences is key to consistently using an appropriate volume.


-


**Standardize Your Collection Method:** Use the same gauge needle or microcapillary tube whenever possible. This makes it easier to estimate how much blood will be dispensed and helps maintain consistency between smears.


-


**Monitor the Spread:** If the blood drop feels difficult to control, it may be too large. During the spreading process, an ideal drop should allow for a gradual, smooth taper to a feathered edge without excessive pooling or dragging.
