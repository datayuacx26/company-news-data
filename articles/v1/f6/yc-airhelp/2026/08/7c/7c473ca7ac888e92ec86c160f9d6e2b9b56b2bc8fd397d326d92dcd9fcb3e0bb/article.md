# easyJet cabin crew strike in France: 15–16 August 2026 — your rights

If you're flying with easyJet this weekend, there's a good chance your trip is about to get complicated. Cabin crew across easyJet's French operation are walking out on Saturday 15 and Sunday 16 August — right in the middle of the mid-August holiday rush — and if your flight is canceled or delayed by 3 or more hours as a result, you could be owed €600 in[easyJet strike compensation](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/strike/easyjet-strike-compensation/) . Here's everything you need to know.


## What's happening with the easyJet France strike?


Cabin crew unions representing easyJet's French staff have called a two-day strike for Saturday 15 and Sunday 16 August 2026, running all day on both dates. The action follows months of stalled talks over working conditions — **unions point to last-minute schedule changes, demanding multi-day rotations, and staff exhaustion** , and say easyJet hasn't responded seriously to repeated warnings.


The strike covers easyJet's six French bases: Paris Orly, Paris Charles de Gaulle, Lyon, Nice, Nantes, and Bordeaux. Because it's a nationwide action, any easyJet flight touching one of those airports — arriving or departing — is potentially affected, not just routes within France.


easyJet says it has put forward a proposal to address staff concerns and is pushing for talks to resume in September, while working to limit disruption in the meantime. The unions' underlying strike notice covers 7 August to 2 September, so this weekend's action may not be the last — further specific strike dates are possible if the dispute isn't resolved.


## Does a cabin crew strike mean I can claim compensation?


**Yes** — and this is the detail that matters most. Under EC 261, the EU regulation that protects air passengers, an airline can't treat its own staff walking out as "extraordinary circumstances" — the narrow legal exception that would otherwise let it off the hook. easyJet's cabin crew are easyJet employees, so this dispute sits squarely within the airline's control.


That's a meaningful distinction from disputes involving people outside the airline. An air traffic control strike, for example, is called by workers who don't work for easyJet, so it usually does qualify as extraordinary circumstances. Cabin crew walking out is different — it's easyJet's own staff, so easyJet is responsible for the disruption it causes.


You're likely eligible to claim if:


-


Your flight was canceled with fewer than 14 days' notice


-


Your flight arrived at your final destination 3 or more hours late


-


Your flight departed from an EU airport, or was operated by an EU airline flying into the EU


## How much compensation could I get?


EC 261 sets fixed compensation amounts based on how far you were flying — so the further your journey, the more you could be owed. These figures are set by law; easyJet can't offer you less.

## What should I do at the airport?


Even if your flight is ultimately unaffected, expect French airports to be under pressure across both days. Check in online if you haven't already, and travel with hand luggage only where possible to avoid baggage delays.

Short-haul flights: at least 2 to 3 hours before departure


-


Long-haul connections via an affected base: at least 3 to 4 hours before departure

## My flight's delayed — what am I entitled to?


If you land at your final destination 3 or more hours after your scheduled arrival time, you could be entitled to compensation of €600. It's your arrival time that matters, not how late you left the gate.


Whatever caused the delay, easyJet still owes you a duty of care while you wait. From 2 hours of delay, the airline must provide:


-


Meals and refreshments


-


Access to communication, such as phone calls or emails


-


Hotel accommodation and transport to and from it, if an overnight stay becomes necessary


Keep every receipt. Reasonable costs you've paid out of pocket for food, drinks, or accommodation can often be reclaimed from the airline.

## easyJet isn't responding — what can I do?


When thousands of passengers try to rebook at once, airline phone lines and websites can grind to a halt. A few things help:


-


Don't rely on a single channel — try the app, website, phone, and social media


-


Look for a self-service rebooking option in your original booking confirmation


-


If you booked through a travel agent, they can often act on your behalf faster


-


Document everything — note the times and methods you used to try to reach easyJet


If you end up booking your own alternative flight because you couldn't get through, keep every receipt — you may be able to recover those costs too.
