### Air passenger rights exist for a reason, but many travelers are left in the dark


Disruptions create major stress and a real financial burden. That’s exactly why passenger-rights rules exist.


In Europe,[EC 261](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/ec-regulation-261-2004/) (and[UK 261](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/uk-261/) in the UK) is designed to make sure passengers are properly cared for during serious delays and cancellations — and, in many cases, financially compensated when the airline is responsible. Compensation can be up to €600, depending on the flight distance and how late you arrive.


That law just had its biggest update in more than 20 years. On 7 July 2026, the European Parliament formally approved a reform of EC 261. The core protections held — the 3-hour delay threshold and the €600 compensation bands are unchanged — and passengers gained a few new ones: a guaranteed free personal item, faster rerouting on any carrier when a flight is cancelled, and a clear 9-month window to file a claim. None of it changes what you're owed for a disruption today; airlines have 12 months to adapt, so the practical changes land around late 2027.[Read the full breakdown of what's changing.](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/blog/what-ec261-reform-means-for-you/)


But even when protections exist, passengers don't always get what they're entitled to — often because they're not told. In our survey, only 25% of travelers said their airline informed them of their rights during the disruption, and globally 35% said they weren't aware of passenger rights rules when flying in Europe. That figure rises to 40% in the USA and 50% in Brazil — even though Brazilian air passengers are protected by a dedicated law,[ANAC 400](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/flight-compensation-in-brazil/) .


When people don’t know what they can ask for or that they may be owed compensation, they’re far more likely to pay out of pocket and *“just deal with it,”* even though they shouldn’t have to.

The biggest improvements are also the simplest: communicate early, clearly, and proactively — and take responsibility for care.


When a disruption happens, passengers need the basics fast: what's happening, what their options are, what support the airline will provide (like food, accommodation if necessary, or rebooking), and whether compensation might apply. That responsibility doesn't disappear just because a trip involves a missed connection or a[self-booked connecting flight](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/blog/what-are-your-rights-for-self-connecting-flights/) — though the rules do shift, which is worth knowing before you book.
