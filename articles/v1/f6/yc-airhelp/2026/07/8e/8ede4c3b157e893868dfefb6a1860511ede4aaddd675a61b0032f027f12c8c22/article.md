# Flying while pregnant: safety, airline rules, and tips for every trimester

Flying while pregnant is safe for most travelers, right up until the final weeks. The real questions are when to fly, what your airline requires, and how to stay comfortable in the air. This guide covers the safest weeks to travel, the rules major European airlines apply, the health points worth knowing, and what happens to your rights if the flight itself is delayed or canceled. Always check with your doctor or midwife first, especially in the third trimester.


## Is it safe to fly while pregnant?


Yes. Flying while pregnant is generally safe if you're healthy and your pregnancy has no complications. The[Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists](https://www.rcog.org.uk/for-the-public/browse-our-patient-information/air-travel-and-pregnancy/) confirms there's no evidence that flying increases the risk of miscarriage or harms the baby. The things worth planning around are blood clots, swelling, and limited medical care mid-flight — not the flight itself.


### Risk of blood clots (DVT)


Sitting still for long periods raises the risk of deep vein thrombosis, and pregnancy already increases that risk on its own. Walking the aisle every 30 minutes or so, and staying hydrated, both help.


### Swelling (edema)


Many pregnant travelers notice swollen feet or ankles from fluid retention. Cabin pressure and long stretches of sitting can make it worse, so movement and hydration are the fix here too.


### Cosmic radiation


Radiation exposure at altitude is higher than on the ground, but for the occasional flier it's minimal and not considered a risk to the baby. Frequent fliers, such as flight crew, should ask a doctor for personalized advice.


### Limited medical care onboard


Medical support is limited in the air, and more so on long-haul routes over water or remote areas where diverting isn't always possible. This is one reason airlines set cutoffs later in pregnancy.


## How many weeks pregnant can you fly?


Most European airlines allow travel up to somewhere between weeks 34 and 36 for a single pregnancy, and week 32 for twins or multiples, after which boarding is refused. Many also ask for a signed letter from a doctor or midwife once you pass 28 weeks. ## When is the safest time to fly during pregnancy?


The second trimester, roughly weeks 14 to 27, is generally considered the most comfortable window. Early-pregnancy symptoms like nausea and fatigue have usually settled by then, and the higher risks of the first and third trimesters are both behind and ahead of you rather than immediate.


## Do you need a fit-to-fly certificate?


Once you're past 28 weeks, most airlines want a letter from your doctor or midwife confirming your due date, that you're carrying a single baby or multiples, and that there are no complications. Some airlines only accept a letter dated within a week or two of travel, so get it close to your departure date rather than early in your trip planning.


## When should you avoid flying?


Beyond the airline's own cutoff, it's best to avoid flying if you have a high-risk pregnancy — for example high blood pressure, a history of premature labor, or a multiple pregnancy past its safe window. This isn't an exhaustive list, so if anything about your pregnancy feels out of the ordinary, ask your doctor before you book.


Tips for a safer, more comfortable flight


-


Tell the cabin crew you're pregnant when you board, and keep any medical letter or prenatal notes within reach.


-


Book an aisle seat for easier access to the restroom and more room to stretch.


-


Fasten your seatbelt low, below the bump rather than across it, and ask for an extender if you need one.


-


Wear loose, breathable layers, and consider compression socks to ease swelling.


-


Drink water regularly; cabin air is dry and dehydration makes every other symptom worse.


-


Get up and stretch your legs and ankles every 30 minutes or so, even from your seat if you can't walk the aisle.


-


At security, you can ask for a hand pat-down instead of the body scanner if you'd rather — staff are used to the request.


## What if your flight is delayed or canceled while you're pregnant?


Pregnancy doesn't change your passenger rights. Under[EC 261](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/ec-regulation-261-2004/) , you could still be entitled to up to €600 if your[flight is delayed](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/flight-delay-compensation/) by three hours or more, or if it's[canceled](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/flight-cancellation-compensation/) with less than 14 days' notice. The same applies if you're[denied boarding](https://www.airhelp.com/en-int/overbooking-compensation/) because a flight was overbooked. ## Flying while pregnant: frequently asked questions


### Can you fly in the first trimester?


Yes, flying in the first trimester is generally safe. Nausea and fatigue are more likely to be the limiting factor than any risk from the flight itself.


### How many weeks pregnant can you fly internationally?


Most airlines apply the same cutoff, around week 34–36 for a single pregnancy, regardless of whether the flight is domestic or international, though a few apply a slightly earlier limit on longer routes. Check with your specific airline.


### Do I need a doctor's note to fly pregnant?


Usually not before 28 weeks. After that, most airlines ask for a signed letter confirming your due date and that there are no complications.


### Is it safe to fly at 7 months pregnant?


For most uncomplicated pregnancies, yes — 7 months (around 28–31 weeks) falls well within most airlines' limits, though you'll likely need a fit-to-fly letter from this point on.


### What should I do if my flight is delayed or canceled?


Keep your boarding pass and any receipts for costs the disruption caused you, then check your compensation eligibility — pregnancy has no bearing on what you're owed under EC 261.
