## What Is a Content Audit


A content audit is a systematic review of every page on your site, measured against performance data, search intent, and strategic relevance. Knowing how to run a content audit is the difference between knowing what you have and knowing what's working.


Two terms worth separating: a content inventory is a catalogued list of URLs, publish dates, word counts, and metadata. A content audit layers on the evaluation, showing which pages drive traffic, which ones cannibalize each other, which have decayed past usefulness, and which deserve more investment.


You need both. The inventory is the raw material. The audit turns it into a plan.


## Why Content Audits Improve Rankings


Google's March 2026 Core Update[hit 55% of monitored sites](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/seo-content-audit-after-core-update-template-2026) by penalizing thin content and weak E-E-A-T signals. If you haven't audited recently, there's a decent chance your site took a hit you haven't diagnosed yet.


That's the point of the audit: it converts vague ranking drops into specific, fixable problems. Maybe three posts are cannibalizing the same keyword. Maybe a page that ranked well two years ago now targets an intent Google has reclassified. Without it, you're guessing at causes and applying fixes that might make things worse.


Bloggers who update older posts are[2.5x more likely to report strong results](https://theb2bmix.com/blog/content-audit-best-practices/) . But you can't update strategically if you don't know which posts deserve the investment and which ones are dragging your domain down.


## Step 1: Define Your Content Audit Goals and Scope


Before you pull a single URL into a spreadsheet, decide what you're actually solving for. "Audit everything" sounds thorough, but on a site with 500+ pages, it's a recipe for a stalled project that never reaches the action stage.


Pick a goal first. Recovering traffic after a core update means you're focused on impression trends, ranking drops, and thin content. Improving conversions means you're looking at bounce rate, time on page, and CTA placement across bottom-of-funnel pages. A freshness push means filtering for publish dates and staleness signals. Each goal changes which metrics matter and which pages you even bother reviewing.


Then scope by content type or funnel stage. I'd audit blog content separately from product pages, since the signals you're reading, and the fixes you'd apply, are completely different. Understanding[where keywords sit in the buyer journey](https://maintouch.com/blogs/map-keywords-buyer-journey-seo-funnel) helps clarify which segment deserves priority first. A blog post losing impressions might need a full rewrite. A product page losing conversions might need better schema or a restructured CTA.


Sites under 200 pages can audit everything in one pass. Above that, batch by section and work through the highest-priority segment first.


## Step 2: Build a Complete Content Inventory


Start with a full crawl. Screaming Frog is the standard. It pulls every URL on your site, including orphan pages your sitemap misses. Export that list, then layer in Google Search Console data for clicks, impressions, and average position per URL. Neither source alone gives you the full picture.


Structure your spreadsheet with these columns:


- URL
- Page title
- Content type (blog post, landing page, product page)
- Last updated date
- Clicks (last 90 days)
- Impressions (last 90 days)
- Average position


The last-updated date column matters more than most people think. Google weighs freshness as a ranking signal, and AI engines are even more aggressive about it. Based on Maintouch internal data, content updated within the last 90 days gets cited far more often in AI answers. If you can't sort for staleness, you can't fix it. And staleness is one of the fastest wins in any audit.


Don't skip orphan pages. The Screaming Frog crawl will surface URLs that aren't linked from anywhere on your site, invisible to both users and search engines. They're either wasting crawl budget or missing an internal linking opportunity that costs you nothing to close.


## Step 3: Score Content Performance with the Right Metrics


Two categories of signals matter: quantitative and qualitative. You need both. Numbers without context lead to bad decisions.


### Quantitative Signals


Pull these from Google Search Console and GA4:


- Organic clicks and impressions (trending up, flat, or declining over 90 days)
- Average position per page
- Bounce rate and time on page
- Conversions or goal completions


The combinations tell you more than any single metric. High impressions with low CTR points to a metadata problem, where Google's showing the page, but your title or description doesn't match what the searcher actually wants. High traffic with a high bounce rate is a different animal. That's usually an intent mismatch: the page ranks for something it doesn't actually answer well.


### Qualitative Signals


Numbers won't tell you whether your content is actually good. For that, read each page against a checklist:


- Does the content match current search intent? Pull up the live SERP. If Google's serving videos and listicles for your target keyword and you wrote a 3,000-word essay, you've got a format mismatch.
- Is the information accurate and up to date?
- Are internal links present and pointing somewhere useful?
- Does the page show real E-E-A-T signals, such as an author byline with credentials, first-party data, something that proves a human with expertise touched it?


A page with strong traffic but shallow depth is a ticking clock, because it'll hold position until a competitor publishes something more thorough. A page with real depth but zero impressions usually just needs better internal linking or a metadata rewrite to get Google's attention.


## Step 4: The Keep, Update, Consolidate, or Remove Decision


Every page in your inventory gets one of four labels:


Label When to apply it Action


Keep Ranking well, driving clicks, intent-aligned Light maintenance only


Update Solid topic and backlinks, but impressions sliding because a competitor published something fresher or more thorough Rewrite or expand the existing page


Consolidate Two or more pages targeting the same keyword, splitting authority between them Merge into one stronger piece and 301 redirect the others


Remove Zero traffic, no backlinks, thin content adding crawl waste Noindex or redirect to a relevant page


One word of caution on removals. Nielsen Norman Group flags this in particular: even low-traffic pages can hold residual ranking equity. Before you delete or redirect anything, check whether it has inbound links or quietly ranks for long-tail queries you haven't noticed. A careless 404 can cost you more than the page was worth keeping.


## Step 5: Build and Execute Your Action Plan


Once every page has a label, sequence the work by effort-to-impact ratio.


Start with technical and metadata fixes: broken canonicals, missing title tags, outdated schema. These take minutes per page and can move rankings within a single crawl cycle. If you've got 40 pages with missing meta descriptions, batch those in an afternoon.


Next, hit high-impression, low-click pages. A title tag rewrite or a sharper meta description can lift CTR without touching the content itself, giving your fastest traffic wins, because the rankings already exist.


Then move to consolidations and full rewrites. These take more time, but they carry the biggest upside for pages stuck cannibalizing each other or drifting off-intent.


For each action, log the owner, deadline, and a "before" snapshot of the metric you're trying to move. Track[AI visibility KPIs](https://maintouch.com/blogs/ai-visibility-kpis-track-results) alongside traditional metrics so you don't miss what shifted. Without that baseline, you won't know whether the fix worked or whether something else changed at the same time. Revisit the numbers at 30 and 60 days. If a rewrite hasn't moved impressions by day 60, the problem is usually deeper than content quality.


## E-E-A-T in Practice When Your Content Is AI-Generated


Google doesn't care whether a human or an LLM wrote the first draft. It cares whether the published page carries real authority signals. That distinction changes what you're actually checking for during the audit.


Most advice stops at "add an author bio." That's table stakes. When you're auditing pages drafted with AI assistance, go deeper:


- Does the post have a named author with verifiable credentials, like a real LinkedIn, a body of published work, topical expertise that's checkable?
- Does the content show first-hand experience? Specific outcomes, original data, concrete examples from actual projects.
- Do external citations point to primary sources, or are they linking to secondary summaries and content aggregators?
- Does the schema markup accurately reflect the author and publication details? Mismatches between your Article schema and the visible byline will hurt you.[Structured data for AI search](https://maintouch.com/blogs/structured-data-for-ai-search) has become a signal you can't treat as optional.


[96% of AI Overview citations](https://satelliteai.io/eeat-for-ai) come from sources with strong E-E-A-T signals. And 41% of AI-only sites lost traffic after Google's March 2026 Core Update, while content with genuine authority signals gained rankings.


The question isn't whether you used AI to write the content. The question is whether anyone with real expertise touched it before it went live.


## How to Audit for AI Citation Visibility


A standard content audit checks whether pages rank in Google. It doesn't check whether they[get cited in AI Overviews](https://maintouch.com/blogs/how-to-get-cited-in-ai-overviews) . Increasingly, those are two different things.


Add an "AI citation readiness" column to your inventory spreadsheet. For each page, check:


- Do key answers appear in self-contained passages of 130 to 170 words within the first 30% of the content? AI engines pull from concise, complete answer blocks, never buried paragraphs.
- Are headings formatted as questions, with direct answers immediately following?
- Is FAQPage or HowTo schema implemented, and does it match what's actually on the page?
- Is the dateModified field current? Stale timestamps get filtered out before content quality is even considered.


A page can pass your SEO audit and still be invisible to the citation layer. Treat that as its own action item, separate from any ranking fix.


## How Often to Run a Content Audit


For most sites, every six months is the right cadence. That's frequent enough to catch algorithm changes and content decay before they compound, but not so frequent that you're re-auditing pages that haven't had time to respond to your last round of fixes.


If you're publishing 15+ pieces a month or operating in a fast-moving vertical, run quarterly audits on your highest-priority pages. You don't need to re-crawl everything: focus on bottom-of-funnel and conversion content, where behavior changes hit revenue fastest.


Smaller sites publishing a few times a month can stretch to annual audits without much risk. Top-of-funnel content decays slower and carries less direct revenue weight.


One rule I follow regardless of site size: any time Google rolls a core update, run a targeted audit within 30 days. Don't wait for your scheduled cadence to tell you what broke.


## Content Audit Tools


Four tool categories feed a content audit. Skip any one and you've got blind spots.


Crawl tools handle inventory. Screaming Frog, Sitebulb, or a free Google Sheets script pulling your sitemap XML will get you a full URL list. The crawl catches orphan pages, broken redirects, and metadata gaps that analytics won't surface on their own.


Analytics integrations give you the behavior layer. Google Search Console shows impressions, clicks, and average position. GA4 adds bounce rate, time on page, and conversion data. You need both connected to your spreadsheet, because a page can look healthy in one source and dying in the other.


[keyword competitor analysis](https://maintouch.com/blogs/seo-keyword-competitor-analysis) like Semrush or Ahrefs fill in what Search Console misses: competitor keyword overlap, cannibalization detection, and historical ranking trends over a longer window than 90 days.


Schema validators close the loop on structured data. Google's Rich Results Test and Schema Markup Validator check whether your JSON-LD actually parses and matches what's on the page. If you're auditing for AI citation readiness, this step isn't optional.


None of these tools run the audit for you. They're inputs. The audit is the judgment layer you apply on top, reading the data, labeling each page, deciding what to fix first.

## FAQ


### What does E-E-A-T actually look like in practice when most of your content is AI-generated?


Adding an author bio is the starting point, not the finish line. For AI-assisted content, E-E-A-T means a named author with a verifiable LinkedIn and a body of published work, first-hand experience signals like specific outcomes and original data, external citations pointing to primary sources instead of content aggregators, and Article schema that accurately reflects the byline visible on the page. The question Google is asking is whether someone with real expertise touched the content before it went live, and 96% of AI Overview citations come from sources that can show that clearly.


### How do you optimize content to get cited by Perplexity, ChatGPT, and other AI engines?


AI engines pull from self-contained answer blocks of 130 to 170 words that appear within the first 30% of the page and name the entity inside the block. Structure headings as direct questions with the answer in the first sentence immediately following, implement FAQPage or HowTo schema that matches what's actually on the page, and keep the dateModified field current since stale timestamps get filtered out before content quality is even considered. A page can rank well in Google and still be invisible to the citation layer if those structural signals are missing.

### How do I know which pages to rank first when I have hundreds of URLs to audit?


Start by filtering for two groups: pages with high impressions and declining clicks (quick metadata wins), and pages generating zero impressions for more than six months (removal or consolidation candidates). Bottom-of-funnel pages, conversion pages, and any content tied to your top five target keywords should always be reviewed first, since changes there hit revenue fastest. The goal is sequencing by effort-to-impact ratio, not auditing alphabetically.


### What's the risk of redirecting or deleting a low-traffic page?


Before you remove anything, check whether the page holds inbound links or ranks for long-tail queries that don't show up in your main keyword reports. A page with 10 monthly clicks might be the only URL earning you a backlink from a high-authority domain. If it does have link equity, redirect it to the most topically relevant page instead of returning a 404, which drops that equity entirely.


### What's keyword cannibalization and how do I find it during a content audit?


Keyword cannibalization is when two or more pages on your site target the same query, splitting authority and confusing Google about which one to rank. To surface it, export your Search Console data, group URLs by their primary keyword, and flag any keyword matched to more than one URL with meaningful impressions. The fix is consolidation: merge the weaker page into the stronger one and 301 redirect the old URL.


### How long does it take to see results after updating content from a content audit?


Metadata fixes like title tag rewrites can move CTR within a single crawl cycle, sometimes within days. Rewrites and consolidations typically take 30 to 60 days to show ranking movement, depending on how quickly Google recrawls the page and how competitive the keyword is. If a rewrite hasn't moved impressions within 60 days, the problem is usually deeper than the content itself, for example a structural intent mismatch or a backlink gap.


### Do I need to run a technical SEO audit separately from a content audit?


They overlap more than most people treat them. A content audit that ignores broken canonicals, slow load times, and crawl errors will miss some of the reasons pages aren't ranking despite solid content. I'd run the crawl tool and the content spreadsheet at the same time, then fix technical issues first since they affect every page and often take less time to resolve than a full content rewrite.


### How does content quality affect AI Overview citations compared to regular Google rankings?


For Google rankings, content quality competes on depth, relevance, and authority relative to other pages in the results. For AI Overview citations, the bar is structural: self-contained answer blocks of 130 to 170 words, headings formatted as direct questions, current dateModified timestamps, and accurate schema markup. A page can rank on page one of Google and still be excluded from AI citations if those structural signals are missing, which is why auditing for AI citation readiness is a separate check from standard SEO scoring.
