# The Real Cost of a Change Request

Every rollout platform demo looks the same. The interface is clean, the workflows make sense, and someone on the sales team walks through exactly the configuration your team needs. It all looks straightforward. Then you go live, and six months later your ops lead wants to change how a milestone triggers a handoff, and suddenly you’re looking at a process that has nothing to do with that demo.


That process is the change request. It’s rarely discussed before the contract is signed, and it’s usually where the real cost of a platform shows up.


### **What actually happens between “we need this changed” and “it’s live”**


Picture the sequence. Someone on your team identifies a gap: a new asset type isn’t captured properly, a reporting field is missing, a workflow stage doesn’t match how your crews actually work. They can’t just open a settings menu and fix it. Instead, they write up the request, submit it to an implementation partner, and wait for a scoping call.


The scoping call happens a week or two later, time zone and calendar permitting. The partner estimates the work, quotes a cost, and adds it to a project queue that already has other clients’ requests in it. If you’re lucky, the change ships in a few weeks. If it touches something more foundational, it can take months, and it comes with a change order that has to work its way through your own procurement process before anyone even starts building.


By the time the fix is live, the business problem that prompted it may have already changed shape. Maybe you’ve added a new market. Maybe the reporting need has evolved again. The platform is now solving yesterday’s problem, at the cost of today’s budget.


### **Why this cycle repeats instead of resolving**


The core issue isn’t that any single implementation partner is slow or unresponsive. It’s structural. When configuration lives outside your organization, every change becomes a negotiation between two sets of priorities: yours, and the partner’s other clients. You’re not the only account in that queue, and you have no visibility into where you sit in it.


This also means your team never builds the internal muscle to solve these problems themselves. Every request reinforces the same pattern: identify the gap, describe it to someone else, wait, pay, repeat. Nobody on your team ends up understanding the system well enough to make the next change faster. The dependency doesn’t shrink over time. It compounds.


### **What the actual cost looks like**


The invoice for a single change request is the visible part. The real cost is harder to see on a line item:


The weeks between identifying a problem and having it fixed, during which your team works around a gap using manual processes or a side spreadsheet. The opportunity cost of a reporting fix that arrives after the decision it was meant to inform. The cumulative spend across a year of small changes that, individually, all seemed reasonable, but together add up to a second software budget nobody planned for.


None of this shows up clearly until someone adds it up. And by the time someone does, the platform has often become entrenched enough that switching feels riskier than continuing to pay the toll.


### **What a different model looks like**


The alternative isn’t a platform with fewer configuration options. It’s one where configuration is something your own team can do directly, without submitting a request and waiting for someone else’s queue to clear.


That means a new asset type gets added the same day someone identifies the need. A workflow adjustment happens because your ops lead made it, not because a partner scoped and delivered it three weeks later. Your team’s understanding of the platform deepens with every change, instead of staying static because someone else always does the actual work.


This isn’t about removing complexity from the software. It’s about removing the toll booth between your team and the changes they need to make.


### A simple way to check where you stand


If you’re not sure whether this pattern applies to your current setup, there’s an easy test. Think back to the last three configuration changes your team needed. How long did each one take from request to go-live? Who made the change: someone on your team, or someone at a partner organization? And what did each change actually cost, once you count the time as well as the invoice?


If the answers involve weeks of waiting and a partner’s name, that’s not a flexible platform. That’s a platform where flexibility has a price tag attached, paid every time, indefinitely. Worth asking whether that’s the model you want to keep funding.
