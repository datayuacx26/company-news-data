# The Meeting That Shouldn’t Exist

Every operations team has one. It’s on the calendar every week, sometimes twice a week during a busy quarter. It’s the status meeting where everyone goes around and reports what’s happening with their piece of the rollout. It takes an hour, sometimes more, and by the end of it, half the room is checking their phone.


Nobody loves this meeting. And yet it persists, quarter after quarter, because there’s an unspoken reason it exists: nobody trusts the reporting to be current without it.


### Why the meeting exists in the first place


If everyone genuinely believed the dashboard, the tracker, or the shared file reflected reality, the meeting wouldn’t need to happen. People would just look. But in most infrastructure rollouts, the gap between what’s recorded and what’s actually happening in the field is wide enough that nobody feels confident relying on it alone.


So the meeting becomes a verification ritual. Someone reports a status, and the room implicitly checks it against what they’ve heard elsewhere. “Actually, I talked to the crew yesterday and that site isn’t done yet.” “That number’s from two weeks ago, it’s changed since then.” The meeting exists to catch the gap between the system of record and reality, one verbal update at a time.


That’s a reasonable thing to want. It’s an expensive way to get it.


### What the meeting actually costs


An hour-long status meeting with eight people isn’t an hour. It’s eight hours of collective time, once you count everyone in the room. Multiply that by a weekly cadence and a full year, and the number gets large fast, before you even count the preparation time beforehand, when someone pulls together the slides or notes, so the meeting has something to look at.


And the output of all that time is usually just: everyone now has a slightly more current understanding of status than they did an hour ago. That’s the entire deliverable. No decisions get made that couldn’t have been made by reading an accurate dashboard. No problems get solved that couldn’t have been flagged the moment they occurred, if the system had surfaced them automatically.


The meeting isn’t creating value. It’s compensating for a reporting system nobody fully trusts.


### The tell that gives it away


Here’s a simple test: think about the last status meeting your team ran. How much of it was new information, and how much was people correcting or confirming things that were already supposed to be tracked somewhere? If most of the meeting was spent reconciling what should already be known, that’s the tell. The meeting isn’t delivering new insight. It’s patching a trust gap.


Another version of the same tell: does someone always come to the meeting with a printed spreadsheet or a personal notes doc that doesn’t match what’s on the shared dashboard? That’s usually a sign the official system of record isn’t actually the one people rely on. It’s the one they update for appearances, while the real tracking happens somewhere less visible.


### Why more meetings isn’t the fix


The instinct when reporting feels unreliable is often to add more check-ins: a daily standup instead of a weekly one, an extra sync with the field teams, a mid-week pulse check. Each of these treats the symptom without addressing why the reporting was stale in the first place.


More frequent meetings can even make the underlying problem worse. They add more manual reporting overhead, more time spent preparing updates instead of doing the work those updates describe, and more opportunities for the numbers people present verbally to drift further from whatever’s sitting in the system of record.


### What actually closes the gap


The fix isn’t a better meeting cadence. It’s a system where site, project, and rollout data update as the work happens, not after someone remembers to report it. When field teams log status directly, when milestones update automatically instead of being manually re-entered into a slide deck, and when the same numbers are visible to everyone in real time, the reconciliation ritual has nothing left to do.


That doesn’t mean all meetings disappear. Teams will always need space to talk through judgment calls, unblock decisions, and align on priorities. But there’s a real difference between a meeting where people solve problems and a meeting where people spend an hour confirming what should have already been obvious from the dashboard.


### Worth asking


If your team has a recurring status meeting that mostly exists to catch up the room on what’s actually happening, that’s not a scheduling problem. It’s a signal that the system everyone is supposed to be relying on isn’t the one they actually trust. Fixing that is worth more than any change to the calendar.
