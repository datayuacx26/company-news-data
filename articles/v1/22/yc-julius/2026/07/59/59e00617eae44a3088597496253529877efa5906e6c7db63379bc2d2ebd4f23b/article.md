## What is a product presentation?


A project presentation is a


short, structured walkthrough of a project's goals, progress, and next steps


, usually shared with slides in front of stakeholders, clients, or your own team. You might give one to kick off a new project, request a budget, report on progress, or wrap things up once the work is done.


The format changes depending on the moment, but the goal stays the same. You want your audience to understand what's happening and agree on what happens next.


A good project presentation does a few things behind the scenes, too. It


gets everyone on the same page about goals and deadlines


, so there's less confusion down the line. It gives you a record of what you promised and when, which comes in handy if priorities shift later.


Project presentations also hold your team accountable, since they put progress in front of the people who are counting on it.


## How to structure a project presentation (with examples!)


A project presentation works best when it follows a clear order, from context to next steps, so your audience never has to guess where you're headed.


Here's what to include and in what order:


### 1. Introduction


Open with the project name, who's presenting, and a


one-line summary of what the project covers


. In this example, we're presenting a Q3 website redesign aimed at cutting the homepage bounce rate.


I'd open with "I'm walking you through our Q3 website redesign, aimed at cutting our bounce rate on the homepage." That's it. One sentence, and the room knows exactly what the next ten minutes are about.


### 2. Goals and scope


State what the project aims to achieve


and what falls inside (and outside) its boundaries. For the website redesign, the goal might be "reduce homepage bounce rate by 15%," and the scope might specifically exclude the checkout flow, since that's a separate project.


💡


Tip:


I recommend always calling out what's not included. It saves you from fielding questions about work that was never on the table.


### 3. Timeline


Lay out the key phases and milestones


so your audience can follow the project's pace at a glance


. For a redesign, that might look like: wireframes in week one, design review in week three, development in weeks four through six, and launch in week seven.


💡


Tip:


A simple timeline or Gantt-style breakdown works well here, especially once you've got more than three or four phases to track.


### 4. Budget and resources


Show what the project costs and what it needs


, whether that's more people, tools, or time. On the redesign, this might mean freelance design hours, a stock photo budget, and a few hours of engineering time each week.


I've learned the hard way that stakeholders want this broken down by category. They’d rather not have it handed to them as one lump number they have to take on faith.


### 5. Risks


Name the challenges that could get in the way and


include how you plan to handle them


. In our example, that could be a dependency on the engineering team's bandwidth, or the chance that user testing turns up a bigger issue than expected.


Bringing up risks yourself can build more trust


than waiting for someone else to ask about them, and it makes you look like you've actually thought this through.


### 6. Next steps and Q&A


Close with what happens after the meeting and who owns what. For the redesign, that might be "design starts Monday, and I'll send a status update every Friday."


Leave room for questions


so nothing lingers unanswered once the room clears out.


## Best practices for a project presentation that lands


Structure gets you organized, but delivery gets you remembered. A few of these come down to solid


[design principles](https://julius.ai/glossary/design-principles) , and the rest are habits I've picked up from sitting through more decks than I can count.


Here's what separates a presentation people engage with from one they just sit through:


-


Stick to one idea per slide:


Cramming three points onto one slide usually means your audience remembers none of them. I try to give each idea its own slide, even if that means more slides overall.


-


Let visuals carry the data:


A chart showing the timeline for the redesign says more in three seconds than a paragraph would in thirty. Use charts, timelines, and simple graphics instead of walls of text wherever you can. Check out our article on


[data visualization best practices](https://julius.ai/articles/data-visualization-best-practices) to learn more.


-


Pull your numbers before you touch a slide:


I've started my share of decks by designing first and hunting for data later, and it never goes well. Get your figures sorted before you open your slide software, so you're not scrambling to confirm a number the night before.


-


Double-check your data matches your source:


A wrong number in front of stakeholders can undo a lot of trust, even if the rest of the deck is solid. I always give my figures one more pass against the original source before presenting.


-


Close with clear next steps:


Don't let the presentation end on the last slide with no direction. State what happens next and who owns it, so the room leaves with an answer.

## Tips for presenting with confidence


Presenting with confidence comes down to a handful of habits, from timing your run-through to knowing how to handle a tough question without losing the room.


These tips can help you command the room:


-


Time yourself before the real thing:


I aim for about two minutes per slide when rehearsing, though this varies with how dense the slide is. If you're running long every time you practice, cut a slide instead of talking faster.


-


Build in a pause if you're going over 20 minutes:


Attention drops off after a while, and nobody's exception to that. A quick question to the room or a shift to a visual can bring people back.


-


Adjust your depth for who's in the room:


Executives want the outcome, cost, and risk first, then details if they ask. Your team wants the how and why, since they're doing the work. For the redesign, I open with the bounce rate goal for leadership, but I walk my own team through the wireframe process in more depth.


-


Write down the questions you're hoping nobody asks:


Then prepare an answer for each one. I do this before every presentation, and it's saved me more than once when someone asked exactly what I was dreading.


-


Pause before answering a tough question:


It signals you're thinking it through, and it gives you a second to make sure you understood what was asked.


-


It's fine to say you don't know:


Commit to following up instead of guessing. Audiences notice when someone's bluffing, and it costs more trust than a straight answer would.


-


Treat objections as engagement instead of attacks:


If someone pushes back on your timeline or budget, it usually means they're paying attention. I try to acknowledge the concern first, then answer it directly instead of getting defensive.

## Frequently asked questions


### What should a project presentation include?


A project presentation should include an


introduction, goals and scope, a timeline, budget and resources, risks, and next steps


with room for questions. Each section gives your audience a different piece of the picture. Skipping any of these can leave stakeholders with unanswered questions later.


### How long should a project presentation be?


A project presentation should run about


15 to 20 minutes


, with roughly two minutes per slide as a starting point. Attention tends to drop off after 10 to 15 minutes, so longer presentations need a built-in pause. If you're running long in practice, cut a slide instead of speeding up.


### What is the difference between a project presentation and a project report?


A


project presentation


is a live, structured walkthrough delivered to an audience, while a


project report


is a written document meant to stand on its own. Presentations use slides and spoken explanation to guide an audience in real time. Reports go into more detail and don't need a presenter to add context.
