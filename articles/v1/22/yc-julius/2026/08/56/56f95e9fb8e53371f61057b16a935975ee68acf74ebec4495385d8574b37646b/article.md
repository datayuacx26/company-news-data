A business proposal presentation is the deck you use to pitch a solution, a plan, and a price to someone who can approve it.


I've built and pitched enough of these decks to know that the difference between a yes and a stall usually comes down to a handful of slides doing their jobs well.


Here's how to build a presentation that gets you a yes and leaves the polite stall behind.


## What is a business proposal presentation?


A business proposal presentation is


a slide deck that pitches your solution, plan, and pricing to whoever can approve it


. That could be a prospective client, a partner, or someone deciding internally on the budget.


It's the spoken, visual version of a written proposal, built to be presented live and to leave the room agreeing on a next step.


Whatever you're proposing, a good presentation covers the same core parts:


-


The problem:


The situation or pain your audience is dealing with.


-


The solution:


What you're proposing to do about it.


-


The benefits:


What changes for them, in their terms.


-


The proof:


Evidence the plan works, like results, metrics, or references.


-


The timeline:


What happens, and when.


-


The price:


What it costs and what they get.


-


The ask:


The specific decision or next step you want.


## Structure the narrative and answer objections early


The best proposal decks follow the same story: here's your problem, here's our solution, here's proof it works, here's the ask.


Every slide should serve that story


.


Audiences care whether you understand what's broken before they care about your founding year, so open with their situation and earn the right to talk about yourself after.


Naming your objections first turns weak spots into confidence:


-


If you're the expensive option:


Put the value case next to the price.


-


If switching feels risky:


Show the onboarding plan.


-


If timing is tight:


Address it directly, don’t just hope that no one asks.


In my experience, a vague ask like "let us know your thoughts" invites the silence you don't want. Close on one clear decision, such as "Do we have approval to start the pilot on the 15th?"


## Tailor the deck to match who's in the room

### The 10 slides your business proposal presentation needs


Most winning proposal decks turn that same story into slides in a set order. I've watched decks lose momentum the moment pricing shows up before the problem feels real,


so this sequence keeps the room following along:


1.


Title slide:


Your company, the prospect's name, and the date. Set the frame in one line, like "A proposal to cut your onboarding time in half," so the reader knows the stakes before you get to slide two.


2. Executive summary:


The whole pitch on one slide. If someone only sees this, they should still get it, since it's the one slide that has to land on its own. I've seen a strong executive summary alone buy a proposal a second meeting when nobody in the room had time to read past it.


3. The problem:


Their situation, in their words. Show you understand the pain before you sell the cure, and pull language straight from what they actually told you in discovery calls.


4. Your solution:


What you're proposing, described plainly. Keep the jargon out and the specifics in, since the reader wants to picture how this works for them.


5. Why you:


Your differentiation and track record. A clean


[comparison chart](https://julius.ai/articles/comparison-chart) against the alternatives works better here than a wall of logos, and I'd rather show three sharp differences than list ten features nobody asked about.


6.


Proof and metrics:


This is the numbers slide. Results, ROI, and case data, since this is often where deals get decided. I've sat in enough of these meetings to know a specific number tends to hold a room's attention longer than an adjective would, so if you only sharpen one slide, sharpen this one.


7. Timeline:


What happens, and when, after they say yes. Specific dates beat vague phases like "phase one," since a calendar makes the plan feel real.


8. Pricing:


The investment and what's included. Show the breakdown, so cost doesn't feel like a mystery box opened at the end.


9. Next steps:


The specific ask. Name the date, the signature, or the kickoff call you need, and skip the open-ended request that invites silence.


10. Team and contact:


Who they'll actually work with. Faces and names build trust that a generic "our team" slide can't.


Beyond the core ten,


two more slides are worth building depending on your deal:


-


A short case study slide:


Earns its spot when you have a strong result. Pick one from a client in a situation close to the prospect's if you can, since proof from a peer often lands harder than a general claim.


-


An appendix:


Worth building whenever you expect detailed questions on the numbers, since it keeps the main deck lean while the underlying math stays ready for whoever asks.


💡 Tip:


Aim for 8 to 12 slides. Fewer can make you look underprepared, and more can start to read like a document projected onto a screen.


## How to build an effective metrics slide


If I had to point to the one slide that decides most proposals, I’d say it's the metrics slide, and it's also the one I see people phone in hardest.


The usual version stacks a few round, confident figures with nothing behind them, like "save 40%" or "cut reporting time in half." A sharp buyer hears that and thinks, 40% of what, measured how? Once they distrust one number, they might start doubting the rest.


A convincing metrics slide shows three things:


-


Their current cost, quantified:


What the problem costs them today, in dollars, hours, or lost deals.


-


Your projected outcome:


Where your solution gets them.


-


The gap, with its source:


The difference between the two numbers is your ROI, and every figure needs a basis, whether that's their own data, a public benchmark, or your past results.


Once those three numbers are solid,


one clear before-and-after chart carries them further


than a table of raw figures. If you're building the visual from your own data, here's


[how to create charts](https://julius.ai/articles/how-to-create-charts-in-excel) that read cleanly in a deck.


## Design best practices for clean business proposal presentations


A proposal deck gets read in a conference room, on a laptop screen, and sometimes projected badly on a wall that washes out half your colors. Design for those conditions, since how it looks on your monitor is the least reliable preview you have.


Keep these principles in mind as you build:


-


One idea per slide:


If a slide has two points, it's two slides.


-


Headline the takeaway:


"Our rollout takes 3 weeks" beats "Timeline."


-


Keep it consistent:


One font, one color system, everything aligned. A


[presentation maker](https://julius.ai/articles/best-ai-presentation-makers) can enforce this for you if manually checking every slide isn't practical.


-


Make it legible from the back of the room:


Big text, high contrast, and few words per slide.


-


Use visuals with a job:


A chart that proves a point earns its place. I've cut plenty of stock handshake photos from decks I've built, since they take up space without backing up a single claim.


-


Don't lean on color alone:


Some of your audience won't see red-versus-green the way you do, so label things too.


💡 Tip:


Write the headline of every slide first, then read just the headlines top to bottom. If that sequence tells the whole story on its own, your structure is sound.

## Frequently asked questions


### How many slides should a business proposal presentation have?


A business proposal presentation should have


8 to 12 slides


. That's enough to walk through the problem, solution, proof, price, and ask without turning your pitch into a document. If you need more detail, move it to an appendix or the written proposal.


### What software is best for a business proposal presentation?


PowerPoint


and


Google Slides


give you the most control, while tools like


Gamma


and


Beautiful.ai


get you a polished deck faster. If you're stuck between two, this


[Gamma vs Beautiful.ai](https://julius.ai/articles/gamma-vs-beautiful-ai) breakdown covers the tradeoffs.


### Should I include pricing in a business proposal presentation?


Yes, most business proposal presentations should include pricing


. A proposal that hides the price can make buyers nervous and stall the decision, so put pricing after you've built the value and frame it as an investment against the return you've shown.


### How do I turn a written proposal into a presentation?


Turn a written proposal into a presentation


by keeping the argument and cutting the prose


. Pull out the core points, put one idea per slide, and move supporting detail into an appendix or leave the full document as a handout.


### How long should a business proposal presentation be?


A business proposal presentation should run


10 to 20 minutes, with room left for questions


. Time it in rehearsal, since decks tend to run longer out loud than they do in your head.
