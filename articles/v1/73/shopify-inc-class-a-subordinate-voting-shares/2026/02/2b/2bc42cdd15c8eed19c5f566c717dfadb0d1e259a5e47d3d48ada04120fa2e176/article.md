### 1. Research food laws and regulations for selling food from home


Knowing how to sell food online starts with understanding the law and food safety best practices. Burst


Normally, the first step to[starting a business](https://www.shopify.com/blog/how-to-start-a-business) is landing on a great idea. With food businesses, however, it's important to understand the complexity of food laws before diving in.


When making and selling food online, safety is always a concern. Government organizations closely regulate and monitor the food industry to ensure public safety, but the onus is on the business owner to stick to the rules and be obsessive about quality.


> Food inspectors offer you some pretty sage and, frankly, free advice on how to make sure you're making a good and reasonable product.


Provided you do your homework, acquire the proper licenses, and meticulously track everything, the likelihood of getting into hot water with the health department is low. But if you do, the consequences can be severe, says food lawyer[Glenford Jameson](http://food.gsjameson.com/) .


"The government can throw you in jail, take all your products and destroy them, shut you down, or give you a big fine," says Glenford. "Food inspectors offer you some pretty sage and, frankly, free advice on how to make sure you're making a good and reasonable product."


If your product is complicated or requires additional licensing (meat, fish, and some agricultural products, for example), you may want to consult a lawyer with experience in the food industry. Though the upfront legal cost may be high for a new business owner, it could avoid even costlier consequences down the road.


If you choose to navigate the legalities of running a food business without a lawyer, be sure to follow these general best practices:


- **Properly handle and store food.** Learn how to properly handle and store food. Keep tabs on food preparation practices and food safety regulations. Ensure food safety so that people don't get sick eating your food.
- **Trace the supply chain.** Ask questions of your suppliers and get referrals.
- **Work with a lab to test your products.** Labs can help identify and trace elements that may be known to cause allergic reactions.
- **Keep thorough records.** Track everything coming in and going out of your facility.
- **Make friends with the food inspector.** Your local health department is there to identify issues and set you up for success. "Food inspectors offer you some pretty sage and, frankly, free advice on how to make sure you're making a good and reasonable product," says Glenford.
- **Get liability insurance.** Be sure you're covered in case anyone does get sick.
- **Follow licensing and permitting requirements.** You may need to acquire things like a food handler's permit, food establishment permit, and food establishment license before selling your product.


**Note:** Every country and region differs in terms of food laws and licensing requirements, and some industries, like dairy and alcohol, may be subject to additional rules. Be sure to consult with a lawyer and your local government for information specific to your business and region.


For the purposes of this article, information and tips will be general, and should not be taken as legal advice.


#### Understanding cottage food laws by state


Whether or not you can test your food business from home depends on cottage food laws in your state.


Cottage food laws regulate how entrepreneurs can prepare and sell homemade food from their own kitchens. These laws are set by each state's health department, and thus vary by state. Cottage food laws only allow for the sale of certain, approved low-risk foods, like baked goods, candy, or tea.


In order to sell homemade food online, businesses must acquire a cottage food license. In some states, for certain classes, you must also acquire a cottage food permit. For example,[California](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CEH/DFDCS/Pages/FDBPrograms/FoodSafetyProgram/CottageFoodOperations.aspx) breaks down its cottage food operations (CFO) into two classes, Class A and Class B.


Class A allows direct sales to customers only, while Class B allows sales to customers and to restaurants. Class B requires a permit as well as a license.

The business model you choose determines everything from startup costs to legal requirements. If you want to sell food online, there are two main business models.


#### Producing food products to sell


This business model refers to producing food in either a[home-based business](https://www.shopify.com/blog/home-business) or a commercial facility and selling it direct to consumers (DTC). In this model, you may also expand your selling channels and consider wholesaling to other shops.

This model involves reselling by curating other brands' products under your own banner. ### 4. Source ingredients


Trace the supply chain, says food lawyer Glenford Jameson. Burst


Glenford stresses the importance of doing your homework when sourcing your ingredients. "Trace the supply chain," he says, so that your packaging claims match what's inside and you're working with trustworthy companies.


If you plan to produce a product that will be labeled organic, for example, be sure your raw ingredient supplier has the[proper certification](https://www.ams.usda.gov/services/organic-certification/becoming-certified) before making claims on your packaging.


Developing a relationship with your supplier improves trust and makes them feel more invested in your business. "Sometimes our suppliers make suggestions based on something new that comes to market," says Jodi from Grain Zero. "It's a collaborative partnership."


- When you're just starting out and producing small batches, it may be cost effective to shop for ingredients at consumer warehouse club stores like Costco or Sam's Club.
- Make connections. In certain industries, finding suppliers may rely on word of mouth and personal introductions. The founders of[Soul Chocolate](https://www.shopify.com/blog/201398985-bean-to-bar-chocolate-business) networked in the industry to make connections with regional cacao farmers.
- Alternatively, for commodities like cacao and coffee beans, look for a distributor or broker who works directly with farmers.
- Team up with other small-batch producers to purchase bulk wholesale ingredients together.


The founders of Omsom leaned on family and friends to support their sourcing process.


"In the early days," cofounder Vanessa Pham[told Shopify Masters](https://www.shopify.com/blog/omsom-storytelling) , "we literally had my parents translating for us on certain calls, having people around the country running errands for us because a lot of the partners that we initially had to work with were mom-and-pop Asian distributors that were bringing in the real good stuff from Asia."

### 5. Determine your food production model


A professional kitchen may be out of reach at first, but many co-ops offer the option of sharing space.[Kitchen Shopify Collective](http://www.thekitchencollective.ca/)


Though McClure's Pickles started in a family kitchen, the family continuously upgraded their production.


"When we were first starting out, we rented a kitchen that had a larger stove," says Bob, "and we would call up our friends and say, 'Hey, I'll buy you pizza and beer if you come make pickles with me on the weekend.'"


The company eventually landed a 20,000-square-foot factory space. McClure's serves as a great example of how to scale production as you grow. Starting from a home-based facility is a low-risk way to test your business model.


#### Selling food from home


Some food items can legally be produced and sold right from your domestic kitchen, but look into the regulations surrounding your chosen product. In the US, the FDA requires you[register your home-based business as a facility](https://www.fda.gov/Food/ResourcesForYou/Industry/ucm322302.htm#home) . You may need to make upgrades to your home setup, such as improved ventilation. Be sure you secure the required license to sell food from home in your region.


#### Renting from shared commercial kitchens


Many facilities offer shared or co-op kitchen space that you can rent hourly or monthly, depending on your production needs.


The benefits are:


- **Reduced costs.** You don't need to buy equipment from scratch and the shared model means you're not covering rent on your own.
- **Less paperwork.** These facilities are already registered as commercial space and have appropriate insurance.
- **Shared knowledge.** Exposure to other small business owners allows you to learn from others and build community.


That's what[Pop & Bottle](https://www.shopify.com/blog/pop-and-bottle-finding-a-gap-in-the-market) cofounders Jash Mehta and Blair Fletcher Hardy did when they were first starting out.


"We found a commercial kitchen set up for food entrepreneurs like us where you could rent the space on a daily or an hourly basis and produce products within that space under licensing. We started there,"[Jash told Shopify Masters](https://www.shopify.com/blog/pop-and-bottle-finding-a-gap-in-the-market) .


#### Setting up your own commercial facility


Have full autonomy and start from scratch with this model, building a facility that meets your needs from the start. This is the most expensive option and one that requires due diligence—you are responsible for ensuring your facility meets code and is properly licensed.


This may not be the best option for new entrepreneurs, but rather a future goal. "We started small in our home kitchen and then grew from there," says Jodi. "We did not move out until we were bursting at the seams and knew we had a big enough business to support the move."


> It is really impressive how much diligence goes into running a USDA-inspected facility.


#### Working with an existing manufacturer


This option is great for hands-off entrepreneurs who are more interested in the business than the production. It's a safe option for industry newbies, too, as the manufacturers should already be versed in food safety and regulations. While the idea, recipe, and brand are all yours, the execution is left to the pros. This gives you more time to invest in other aspects of the business.


"We partnered up with someone that had a USDA-inspected facility so that we could ship across the US sooner, without any food safety issues," says[Bull & Cleaver](https://bullandcleaver.com/) founder Daniel Patricio. "It is really impressive how much diligence goes into running a USDA-inspected facility."

### 7. Run the numbers and choose your business structure


How much does it really[cost to start a food business](https://www.shopify.com/blog/cost-to-start-business) ? If you were to open a restaurant or physical food store, your upfront costs could be[upward of $175,000](https://www.restaurantowner.com/public/Survey-How-Much-Does-it-Cost-to-Open-a-Restaurant.cfm) or more. But there are many ways to get started with much smaller startup costs.


Home-based cottage food businesses have the lowest-cost entry point, while business structures requiring commercial facilities need substantial investment. Small-batch producers who start home-based businesses have far less overhead and can expand into commercial spaces later as the business grows.


Without expensive leases or staffing costs, you could realistically start selling food online with just a few hundred dollars to cover your business license, ingredients, website and marketing costs, and packaging.


**Tip:** You may be able to claim some of your housing and utility costs at tax time if you have a separate space in which you work on your business inside of your home.


If it's not possible to run your business from home, consider the monthly cost of a commercial facility when calculating your startup costs. Production facilities can be expensive, but many co-ops and incubators offer shared kitchens to entrepreneurs for a fraction of what you'd spend in a dedicated space.


#### Choosing your business structure


The three most common business structures include sole proprietorships, LLCs, and[corporations](https://www.shopify.com/blog/what-is-a-corporation) . Food entrepreneurs often start as sole proprietors before transitioning to an LLC. Below, find brief explanations of each.


- **Sole proprietorship.** A[sole proprietorship](https://www.shopify.com/blog/sole-proprietor) is the simplest type of business structure and the most affordable to establish. In a sole proprietorship, there is no legal distinction between the business and individual. You may need to obtain a[business license](https://www.shopify.com/blog/business-license) if your local jurisdiction requires it.
- **LLC.**[Unlike sole proprietorships](https://www.shopify.com/blog/sole-proprietorship-vs-llc) , a limited liability company ([LLC](https://www.shopify.com/blog/what-is-an-llc) ) separates the business from the individual, which provides liability protection. LLCs can have one or more owners and may benefit from tax advantages over other business structures.
- **Corporations (S corp and C corp).** The main types of corporations include[S corps](https://www.shopify.com/blog/what-is-an-s-corp) ,[C corps](https://www.shopify.com/blog/what-is-a-c-corporation) , and nonprofit corporations. C corps tax individuals and businesses separately and can have unlimited shareholders. S corps are similar, but their[special tax treatment with the IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporations) , known as pass through taxation, offers some advantages. S corps have a max of 100 shareholders who must be US Citizens.

## Selling food online FAQ


### Can you make food at home and sell it?


You can make food at home and[sell it online](https://www.shopify.com/blog/opening-an-online-store-tips) or in-person. Certain countries and states have specific cottage food laws that govern the production of food like baked goods in domestic kitchens. You can run an online food business while also selling at local farmers markets.


### Is selling food online profitable?


Yes, selling food online can be a profitable business. However, how profitable it is depends on a variety of factors, like how much you sell, market perception, reach, marketing, your profit margins, cost of goods, and more.


### What is the cheapest food business to start?


The most[cost-effective way to start a food business](https://www.shopify.com/blog/117806725-how-to-launch-an-online-store-with-no-cash-investment) is to make your products at home and sell them online. This model allows you to bootstrap your food business and grow it slowly.


### How do you price food for sale?


When pricing any product, a simple formula is to add up your variable costs plus your fixed costs plus your profit margin. It's also important to keep in mind who your consumer is and what they're willing to pay for the food you're offering. Compare your products to other successful online food businesses and use the average pricing in the market as a benchmark.


### How can I sell food on the internet?


To sell food on the internet, first determine what kind of food you want to sell and your production model. Next comes branding, setting up shipping, building an ecommerce site and considering other sales channels like online marketplaces.


### What are cottage food laws, and do they apply?


Cottage food laws apply to people who make food to sell from a residential kitchen. A home-based food business can operate legally if it sells only foods on the approved list and obtains the necessary permits and/or licenses their state and local area requires.


Cottage food laws each follow local regulations by state. Some states refer to them as cottage food laws, others home processing, and some refer to it as the[Food Freedom Act](https://alec.org/model-policy/food-freedom-act/) .
