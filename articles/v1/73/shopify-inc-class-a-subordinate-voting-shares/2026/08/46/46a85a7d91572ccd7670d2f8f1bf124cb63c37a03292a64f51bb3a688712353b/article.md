# Commerce now moves at the speed of culture. And anyone can move with it.

by Shopify Data Science


Entrepreneurs turn culture's biggest moments into something you can hold. New data shows how fast they do it, and how it shapes the way we live and connect.


The distance between a cultural moment and a business built on it used to be measured in weeks or months. Now it's hours. Sometimes even minutes.


Starting a business and reaching that first sale has never been faster or more open to more people. It means a seller can respond to a cultural moment almost as it happens. And that becomes the enabling condition for everything else.


In 2025, nearly 10% of new shops made their first sale within a single day of signing up, an increase of 43% in just three years. Half of all new sellers now reach their first sale within about three weeks.


On May 8, 2025, white smoke rose over the Sistine Chapel and the world learned the name of the new pope. Within an hour, you could buy a T-shirt emblazoned with "da pope."


One of the shops selling Pope Leo gear was[Obvious Shirts](https://obviousshirts.com/?srsltid=AfmBOoqT9iO9891PNhPEnEMcOOpcggOPxHAfmOEo6Bw1AEtMxSYDNDXt) , run by Joe Johnson, a Chicago entrepreneur who'd started years earlier making one-liner shirts for Cubs fans. He didn't have a manufacturing deal or a warehouse full of inventory waiting on a papal conclave. He had an idea, a storefront, and the instinct to move fast.


For most of retail history, turning a moment into a product meant already having capital, inventory, manufacturing relationships, and shelf space. The moment would pass long before an ordinary person could act on it. Today, commerce can move at the speed of culture.


Entrepreneurs are now the fastest translators between culture and the economy, taking the moments we witness and turning them into something we can hold. And you no longer have to already be in business to do it.


When the time from idea to income narrows, high-agency people can act on an idea fast, whether they're starting something new or already running a shop. As those barriers keep falling, less and less stands between spotting a moment and selling into it.


## Moving at the speed of the moment


A century ago, sociologist Émile Durkheim studied why people gather for rituals and named the energy that builds when a crowd experiences something together: *collective effervescence* . He noticed that the feeling needs somewhere to live once everyone goes home, so it attaches itself to an object, a totem, that lets people carry the belonging with them.


A championship tee or a World Cup scarf is a totem in that sense. Commerce is how an experience gets a physical form. It takes a moment you'd otherwise just watch on a screen and makes it something you can touch, wear, and stand inside of. That's the difference between witnessing a moment and belonging to it. And why merch is so enduring.


Sports are the purest test of speed, because there's a shared, public time stamp. Everyone knows the exact minute the final whistle, the buzzer-beater, or the trophy lift happened, so it’s easy to see how quickly commerce catches up.


During the New York Knicks' 2026 playoff run, more than 3,000 shops listed orange-and-blue products, and the typical one made its first sale within three days.


Then there's the World Cup, which wrapped in July and created breakout moments faster than anyone could plan for. When an underdog became the tournament's improbable hero, people around the world reached for a way to celebrate him. Sellers kept pace, half of them making a first sale within three days, this time for fans spread across continents.


**** And then a certain soccer player wandered into[Wild Bill's Western Store](https://wildbillswestern.com/) , a family-run shop in downtown Dallas, and walked out with a taxidermied raccoon clutching a whiskey bottle. The image went viral, and since that June visit Wild Bill's online sales rose 10x compared to a month earlier. The owners switched on global shipping for the first time.


## When a trend becomes a category


The same low barriers that let someone catch a one-week wave also build lasting categories.


Take matcha. It didn't explode overnight. It was a wellness habit, then an aesthetic, then a genuine staple. A wave of independent brands launched or expanded alongside it, from[Morning Made](https://www.morningmade.com/) in Australia to[Matcha Mystic](https://matcha-mystic.com/) in the Netherlands.


When the craze peaked in 2025, nearly 1,500 new shops launched to meet it. The easy assumption is that they would disappear when the hype cooled. Instead the majority of them (70%) are still active today, with most reaching their first sale within about two weeks of listing a product. What started as a trend is becoming a category, with sales continuing to climb quarter over quarter.


As[protein](https://www.shopify.com/news/protein-trend) changed from a bodybuilder's obsession to a number that all shoppers scan for on the back of a package, entrepreneurs met the moment. Over the past year, protein coffee sales climbed 507% and prepared protein foods 194%, while traditional protein shakes fell 40%. Sellers moved in to meet this shift in demand, with 76% more shops selling protein coffee and 37% more selling prepared protein foods year over year, each with its own idea of what a protein product can be, from ramen to cereal.


Both of these were dismissed as passing trends until, at some point no one can quite name, they became the default.


## The barriers are collapsing


The time it takes to reach that first sale keeps shrinking, and that helps entrepreneurs whether they're building for a moment or building for a decade. The sooner someone makes a sale, the sooner they know the idea works.


Now the distance between what culture wants and what an entrepreneur can deliver has disappeared.
