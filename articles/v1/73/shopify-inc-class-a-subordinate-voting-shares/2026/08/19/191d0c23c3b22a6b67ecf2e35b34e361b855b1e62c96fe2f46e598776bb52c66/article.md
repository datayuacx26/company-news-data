Trade credit is a business-to-business (B2B) arrangement that lets a buyer purchase goods or services from a supplier and pay at a later date. This arrangement functions as an interest-free loan for the buyer, while for sellers it offers an incentive to customers that helps build strong relationships.


Access to flexible, short-term financing is critical for small businesses struggling with[cash flow](https://www.shopify.com/blog/cash-flow) . ## What is trade credit?


Trade credit is a commercial lending relationship between two businesses that bypasses a traditional bank. In[business-to-business commerce](https://www.shopify.com/enterprise/blog/b2b-ecommerce) , a seller lets a buyer take delivery of goods or services with an agreed upon date for payment in the future. This period is known as the credit period, which typically spans 30, 60, or 90 days.


Because there’s no financial institution acting as an intermediary, trade credit is often an informal agreement, though larger transactions frequently use a formal agreement or a signed promissory note to legally protect the seller.


When a buyer is using trade credit, they effectively obtain an interest-free loan, helping to improve cash flow. Instead of paying for inventory upon ordering, the business retains that capital. It can then sell the finished products to customers, collect the revenue, and use the funds to pay off the supplier invoice on or before the due date. This cycle minimizes the need for outside commercial financing, keeping the business’s debt in check.


A business typically resorts to using trade credit during periods of rapid expansion or seasonal demand shifts. When demand spikes, ordering larger quantities requires significant upfront capital. Startups and mid-sized brands use trade credit when their[accounts payable](https://www.shopify.com/blog/accounts-payable) volume grows to a point where upfront cash payments strain daily operations. It is also an option when a company has established a consistent payment history and wants to leverage that credibility into extended payment terms to conserve cash.

## Trade credit vs. other types of financing


Trade credit differs from commercial loans, business credit cards, and lines of credit in the following ways:


-


**Traditional bank loans.** A[bank loan](https://www.shopify.com/blog/commercial-loans) provides a lump sum of cash that incurs interest charges from day one, typically requiring monthly payments. Trade credit does not give you cash but rather inventory or materials with a deferred payment window, incurring zero interest if the debt is paid by the due date.


-


**Business credit** **cards.**[Business credit cards](https://www.shopify.com/blog/business-credit-cards) offer short-term funding but have high interest rates if you carry a balance, along with transaction fees for the vendor. Trade credit provides customized terms for the buyer and can reduce or avoid credit card processing fees for the seller if invoices are settled via bank transfer or check.


-


**Lines of credit.** A[business line of credit](https://www.shopify.com/blog/business-line-of-credit) is a revolving loan from a bank. It requires extensive bank underwriting, regular maintenance fees, and audits of your financial statements. Trade credit is easier to secure because the supplier evaluates your payment history and credit history without involving a bank.

## How trade credit works for buyers and sellers


To make trade credit work smoothly, both parties must understand their specific responsibilities, operational workflows, and potential financial risks.


### How trade credit works for the buyer


From the buyer’s side, the process begins when you establish a relationship with a vendor. You submit a commercial credit application detailing your business history, trade references, and financial statements. The supplier evaluates the buyer’s credit profile, checking credit reports through bureaus such as Dun & Bradstreet to review the buyer’s credit rating.


Once approved, the supplier grants you a specific credit limit. You can then issue a[purchase order](https://www.shopify.com/blog/purchase-orders) without sending immediate payment. Items arrive with an[invoice](https://www.shopify.com/blog/retail-invoice-guide) stating the trade credit terms. Your accounting team logs this under accounts payable, tracking the deadline to ensure timely payments and avoid late payments that could damage your credit history.


### How trade credit works for the seller


For the seller, extending trade credit is a tool to increase order sizes and cultivate customer loyalty. When you offer terms, you act as the lender. However, trade credit introduces the risk of delayed revenue and bad debt. A strict credit management process involves reviewing the credit ratings of every business customer before fulfilling orders.


Once you ship the goods, the transaction moves into accounts receivable. If a buyer makes late payments or defaults, your cash flow suffers. This poses one of the disadvantages of trade credit if you end up spending time and money chasing payment.


## Common trade credit payment terms


The[payment terms](https://www.shopify.com/blog/what-are-payment-terms) dictate when payment is due and whether there are any incentives for early payment. Typical terms include:


-


**Net 7, 15, 30, 60, 90.** The number represents how many days the buyer has to pay the full invoice amount from the invoice date. For example,[net 30](https://www.shopify.com/blog/net-30-payment-terms) means that payment is due within 30 days.


-


**Cash on delivery (COD).**[Cash on delivery](https://www.shopify.com/blog/order-to-cash-process) means that the buyer pays the full amount when the goods are delivered rather than ordered. This is common when a buyer has a poor credit history or is working with a supplier for the first time.


-


**Payment in advance/upfront payment** **.** The buyer must make upfront payment before the supplier begins production or ships the items. This carries no risk for the seller but can strain the buyer’s cash on hand.


-


**Early payment discounts.** An[early payment discount](https://www.shopify.com/blog/early-payment-discount) is a notation that indicates the buyer will receive a discount if they pay within a specified number of days. For example, 2/10 net 30 means the seller offers a 2% discount if the buyer pays within 10 days, which incentivizes buyers to pay earlier, helping the seller manage cash flow.

## How sellers collect trade credit payments


Sellers use several methods to process trade credit payments:


-


**Vaulted cards.** The buyer puts a credit card on file before or during checkout. The seller automatically charges the card once the net terms deadline is reached.


-


**ACH and bank transfers.**[Automated clearing house (ACH)](https://www.shopify.com/blog/what-is-ach) direct deposits move money electronically from the buyer’s bank account to the seller’s account, processing transactions in batches rather than individually. This cuts overhead, letting banks charge flat, nominal fees instead of the percentage-based fees required by credit card networks.


-


**Manual payment.** The buyer initiates a wire transfer, sends a physical check, or makes a payment from an online bank account when the invoice comes due.

## Best practices for managing trade credit


- For buyers: Negotiate payment terms that fit your cash cycle
- For buyers and sellers: Use automated tracking for due dates
- For sellers: Establish a strict credit policy and stick to it


Implementing these best practices for managing trade credit protects your business from cash crunches, whether you are buying supplies or selling finished products.


### For buyers: Negotiate payment terms that fit your cash cycle


Aim to align revenue with expenses. If it takes your business an average of 45 days to convert raw inventory into customer cash, you might negotiate net 60 terms with your suppliers. This buffer allows you to collect revenue from your own sales before you have to pay for the materials, maintaining a positive cash balance.


### For buyers and sellers: Use automated tracking for due dates


Use automated accounting tools or Shopify’s integrated B2B dashboard to track due dates.


If you are a buyer, set up automated alerts before an invoice becomes due so you can schedule payments efficiently, protect your credit history, and take advantage of any early payment discounts.


If you are a seller, configure automatic notifications for the day an invoice becomes past due. This flags delinquent accounts, letting your team pause new shipments and send payment reminders.


### For sellers: Establish a strict credit policy and stick to it


When offering trade credit, protect your business from bad debt by providing clear policies. Establish explicit credit limits based on verified credit reports, charge transparent late fees or other such penalties for overdue accounts, and consider trade credit insurance.


## Trade credit FAQ


### What is an example of a trade credit?


Imagine Company B orders $10,000 worth of blank T-shirts from a textile manufacturer to print graphics on them. Instead of demanding an upfront payment, the manufacturer ships the shirts immediately along with an invoice marked with net 30 terms. Company B receives the shirts, prints its designs, sells them to retail customers during the next two weeks, and uses that revenue to pay the $10,000 invoice to the manufacturer 28 days later.


### What are the advantages and disadvantages of trade credit?


Using trade credit gives sellers a competitive advantage by helping to encourage customers to buy more, boosting sales volume and customer loyalty. For buyers, it functions as an interest-free loan that helps manage cash flow by avoiding upfront payment. However, buyers and sellers should consider the advantages and disadvantages. Buyers face late fees or a damaged credit rating if they miss payments, while sellers risk delayed revenue and bad debt if a buyer defaults on their open accounts.


### What are trade credits used for?


Businesses use trade credit primarily to purchase physical inventory, buy raw manufacturing materials, or secure recurring corporate services without draining their cash reserves. It serves as short-term financing that bridges the time gap between buying goods from suppliers and collecting revenue from customers.
