# From Goldman Sachs to HGTV: How Galey Alix Rebuilt Her Life Through Design

Galey Alix spent 13 years at Goldman Sachs closing billions in deals before a personal collapse—losing her job, her engagement, and her mental health—pushed her toward renovating homes on weekends as a way to heal. That creative outlet became[Galey Alix Design](https://galeyalixdesign.com/) , a brand with more than five million followers across TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook and one renovation video that pulled 30 million views. What’s easy to miss is how much of that growth runs on skills she learned on the trading floor. Here, Galey breaks down the finance disciplines she ported into a creative business: story selling, competitive-niche analysis, and a service model built entirely around what her customer actually fears.


### On the Goldman skill she leans on most—story selling:


I closed $5 billion for my firm, and a lot of what I learned there came down to pattern recognition. The better you get at it, the more efficient you get at your job. The pattern I lean on most now is story selling. If you can tie a product or a service to a story, it becomes memorable, it becomes repeatable, and your close ratio goes way up.


I noticed it in my pitches. I was selling complex financial products, and if I could wrap each one in a story, I’d walk out more likely to get a trade—and a month later another one—because they remembered what I told them. My clients were financial advisers, so a good story was something they could repeat to their own clients.


Here’s one I’d use if I wanted people to look at emerging markets instead of the usual US large cap. Back in the ’90s, a wealthy businessman pulls up to a bank in Manhattan in a $500,000 Rolls-Royce, walks in, and asks for a $5,000 loan for two weeks while he’s traveling to Europe. He has no ID or proof of funds on him, so he offers the Rolls as collateral and hands over the keys. Two weeks later he comes back, pays the $5,000 plus $15 in interest, and asks for his keys. The loan officer can’t help himself: "You’ve got a Rolls-Royce—why did you need a $5,000 loan?" And he says, "Where else in New York City can I get secured covered parking for $15?"


Then I’d land it: If you want $20,000 worth of parking for $15, the place to invest right now is emerging markets. You’ve tied a concept to something they’ll feel and remember. I’ve translated that straight into my business. Every video you see, I’m telling a story—how a room comes together, how a family gets surprised—and I’m wrapping my products into it. People remember it, they feel something, and that feeling ties right back to the product.


Galey has always had an eye for design. *Galey Alix, Livabliss*


### On why she uses “show, don’t tell” in her short-form videos:


It’s a lot of show, don’t tell. I want to show you how something’s created—I don’t need to narrate it, because you download it as you watch. And I do it fast and punchy on purpose. If it’s that digestible, you only need to watch it once. But you want multiple views to help your engagement and get caught in the algorithm.


So I show just enough to make it interesting without letting you fully absorb it. Then you watch again and again to really catch it. Once you’re making short-form, you have to think about the algorithm too.


### On tailoring the same content to TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook:


Between Facebook, TikTok, and Instagram, I’m a little over five million followers, and I change what I show based on the audience. I definitely use different music on TikTok. Facebook actually does better with slower videos—it tends to be an older crowd, they want to sit with it, they have more patience.


TikTok is the opposite. Gen Z has really short attention spans, so you have to be quick and punchy or they’ll keep scrolling. Instagram sits somewhere in the middle.


Galey is dedicated to designing each client’s dream home. *Galey Alix*


### On finding a competitive niche by studying the end user:


You’d be a fool to think you can go out and do the same thing as everyone else and expect different results. You have to do things different, otherwise you’re just getting into the exact same bowl of soup everybody else is swimming in. ****


As an investor, I’d look at two companies and ask what the edge was—why is Netflix going to outperform Hulu next month? Netflix has a much broader global reach; Hulu is still very much domestic, so Netflix has a billion more viewers a month. That’s the competitive niche, and it’s why I’d invest. So when I invest in myself as a business, I ask the same thing: What’s my niche, how am I going to be different? ****


The best place to start is the end user. In my case, the homeowner. If I’m a homeowner interviewing 20 designers, they all give me the same pitch—we care about your home, we’re personalized, we’ll stay on budget. Everybody promises the same thing, and honestly most of them would do a great job. So how do I set myself apart?


I thought about what would actually give me anxiety if my house were under construction. Making a million decisions—do I want the baseboards to be 9 or 10 inches, aged brass hardware or pewter—until I get decision fatigue. Living through the VOC fumes and compound dust. Strangers walking through my house all day. Weeks without a kitchen, washing dishes in a guest bathroom sink. There are so many stressors. So I decided to just design every one of them out.


### On the 72-hour renovation model where the client knows nothing:


I do the entire renovation in 72 hours. You go to your parents’ house for the weekend or to Disney World, and I don’t tell you a single thing I’m doing—you can’t have anxiety about what you don’t know. The only two decisions you make are: Are you going to trust me with your home and your life savings, and what’s the budget? That’s it. ****


I walk through the house with you, I open your drawers, I look behind doors, I check the electrical panels. I’m like a CIA agent—I ask all the questions, I’m downloading everything, but I’m saying nothing. Then you give me the budget and I tell you how many rooms I can do at a level I know will create ROI \[return on investment\]. Sometimes you don’t even know which rooms you’re getting. You could come home to a new bedroom and kitchen, or a whole new backyard and patio. You just have to trust me. ****


The really big jobs I split into phases—some of my multimillion-dollar projects have four. It’s the only way it’s physically possible for my team, and it’s the only way I can spend real time on each room instead of rushing through it. Every inch of that room gets touched by us.


### On why she still struggles to charge for her design work:


This is where I’ve struggled going from Goldman to design. At Goldman I ate whatever I hunted—they paid me X basis points on it and it was done. I never had to pitch myself or the value of my work. And as somebody who’s struggled with a sense of self-worth for years, that was really hard. So all the way through the end of the TV show, I never charged a single client. My team tells me what they’re going to charge and the client pays them, but I personally never charged for anything.


I believed that if I was doing this for the right reasons—because I genuinely wanted to make people’s lives better—it would eventually come back to me. And it did. The videos turned out well, the reactions were amazing, I got more followers, then more product deals. This past year my team is the one pushing me to start charging, and I’m trying really hard now. But even so, whatever I’m charging, I’m still giving them more in product value than the fee. In my mind it comes out as a wash.


Galey’s new book, *You Can Do Beautiful Things* , describes all of the ways one can build a business from scratch—in the design sector and beyond. *Galey Alix, PJ Fetscher*


### On turning licensing deals into a product line—and a storefront that sells the whole room:


Once a product of mine is on ecommerce, it’ll be in a few stores—Wayfair, Home Depot—except my wallpaper, which is only on homedepot.com. Sometimes a brand reaches out through my social media; sometimes, if I really want to work with someone, we go to them. Huon does flooring, and I told them I wanted to turn that flooring into ceiling strips and create a whole new product out of it, plus do a flooring deal with them. So we built the partnership. And because I get so much of that product for free now, using it in a client’s home is its own advantage—there’s real ROI in working with me. ****


All of it runs through my Shopify site, which I’ve had from day one, back when our first product was baseball hats. Now you can shop the individual products, but you can also shop my projects. Any room you’ve seen me post—sliding a chair around, rolling out a rug, installing wallpaper—you can click that specific project and I’ve got every single thing linked, down to the paint color. Anybody can replicate the whole room.


### On why she shares all her sources instead of hiding them:


A lot of people in design don’t reveal their sources because they don’t want the work and effort they put in replicated for free, and I totally understand that. But all my projects are so customized to each client that even if you bought every single thing I linked, it still wouldn’t look exactly like what I did in their space. So why not share it? If somebody really wants to know where I got that rug, chances are it’s a Galey Alix rug anyway—so I benefit all over again when they buy it. It’s a win-win.
