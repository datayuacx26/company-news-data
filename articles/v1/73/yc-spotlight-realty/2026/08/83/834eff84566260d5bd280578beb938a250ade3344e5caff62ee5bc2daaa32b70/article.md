# Franklin Street Looks Reborn. Look Closer and It's Two Different Streets.

Sometime this week, the Orange County Sheriff's Office padlocked Breadman's, the longtime Chapel Hill bakery that had been fighting a rent dispute with its landlord. A few blocks away, a sign went up announcing that a 7 Brew Coffee Shop is moving into the storefront Starbucks moved out of last year, right next to Raising Cane's. Both things are true at the same time, on the same street, in the same month. If you've been reading the "look at all these new restaurants" roundups that keep circulating about Franklin Street, you've gotten half the picture. The other half explains why the half you've heard about keeps happening.


## The wave everyone's already noticed


Walk Franklin Street this August and the new signage tells a genuinely interesting story about what Chapel Hill wants to eat. Géos Tavern & Oyster Bar opened June 8 at East 54, bringing North Carolina seafood and a 220-seat dining room from the team behind Giorgios Hospitality Group. Good Fortune, a cocktail bar, is now open on West Franklin. Kolapasi Indian Canteen, a South Indian fast-casual chain, opened its second North Carolina location on West Franklin. Próximo, a Spanish tapas and wine bar built around chef Brandon Sharp's cooking from Hawthorne & Wood and Bluebird, has been serving crispy sea bass and seared meatballs on East Franklin since September 2024. The Latin Effect, which started as a food truck in 2020, now has a permanent home on West Franklin serving Honduran and Central American dishes like catrachitas and pupusas. Road to Myanmar has brought Burmese, Thai, and Malaysian cooking to East Franklin as well.


None of this reads like a street in decline. It reads like a street getting more interesting, one small independent lease at a time.


## The one who's actually buying the buildings


Here's the part that doesn't show up in the "new restaurants" lists. When Raising Cane's arrived on Franklin Street, it didn't just sign a lease. It bought the whole building, including the retail space Starbucks used to occupy. Starbucks didn't close. It relocated across the street into something bigger. Jeri Lynn Schulke, executive director of the Chapel Hill Downtown Partnership, described the relief plainly, saying "everybody in our town was hoping that somebody would come along" to fill those long-empty corners.


That relief is real. Empty storefronts at Franklin and Columbia had been a visible wound for years. But the fix came from a national chain with the capital to purchase real estate outright, not lease it. Now the pattern is repeating in smaller ways. Construction began in July on a shared Jimmy John's and Baskin-Robbins location, taking over a long-vacant lot between the Target and the Panera on West Franklin. Zaxbys opened on East Franklin in March 2025. 7 Brew is filling the space Starbucks left behind. None of these are villains. They're simply a different kind of tenant than the ones filling the storefronts one block over, and they're arriving with a different kind of financial backing.


## What closed, and why it's not the same story as what opened


Linda's Bar & Grill, a Chapel Hill fixture for years, closed in January 2025 citing lost revenue and staffing problems it couldn't solve. Ram's Corner, a sports bar that opened on West Franklin in October 2025 with ping pong tables and a front porch built for game nights, had already closed by the time trade listings were checked again this year, less than a full year after its debut. Breadman's, a longtime Chapel Hill bakery, is now padlocked over a rent dispute with its landlord, its future genuinely uncertain as of this writing.


Every one of these closures happened for its own reason. But line them up against the property purchase that brought in Raising Cane's, and a shape starts to emerge. The businesses closing are the ones absorbing rising costs directly, on leases they don't control. The businesses expanding into bigger footprints, or buying buildings outright, are the ones with the balance sheets to do it. Franklin Street isn't choosing between decline and revival. It's running both processes at once, and they're not affecting the same kinds of businesses.


## Two different reasons a storefront turns over


This is worth sitting with if you live here, because it changes what a "new restaurant opened" headline actually tells you. A new Burmese restaurant or a Honduran food truck's first permanent home is a story about somebody taking a chance on a small lease, often after years of saving toward exactly that storefront. A Jimmy John's and Baskin-Robbins opening in a lot that's sat empty for years is a story about a large operator with the capital to develop ground that smaller tenants couldn't touch. Both make the street feel busier. Only one of them tells you anything about who can still afford to open a business here on their own terms.


The independent wave and the corporate wave are both genuinely happening on Franklin Street right now, in the same eight or nine blocks, sometimes in storefronts next door to each other. Mediterranean Deli reopened last year with a larger, more expansive layout after a two-year closure following a fire, proof that the street's older institutions can still come back stronger. The Yogurt Pump, owned by UNC alumni Jim and Susan Kitchen, is still there, unmoved, uninterested in getting bigger. That kind of staying power sits a few doors down from a building a national chain just purchased outright. Both are real. Neither cancels the other out.


## What this actually means if you're walking the street this month


If you're trying to make sense of what's new, the useful move isn't to treat every opening as equally telling. Notice which storefronts are filled by someone who scraped together a first lease, and which ones are filled because a company with real estate money moved in and rebuilt from the ground up. Yeti Bowl and Shakes is set to take over the old Hibachi & Company space on East Franklin, a smaller-scale transition worth watching for what comes next. Naan by Gulshan is still waiting on final inspections before it can open its doors, another small operator working through the same permitting grind everyone else does.


None of this requires you to pick a side. It just means the next time a friend forwards you a list of Franklin Street's newest restaurants, you'll know to ask the second question: who owns the building they're in, and what happened to whoever was there before.
