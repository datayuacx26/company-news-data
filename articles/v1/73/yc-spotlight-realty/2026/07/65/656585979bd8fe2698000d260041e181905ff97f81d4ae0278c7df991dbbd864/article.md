# Is Durham, NC Actually Safe? What the Crime Statistics Don't Tell You


If you're thinking about moving to Durham, North Carolina, chances are you've already searched one question:


["Is Durham safe?"](https://youtu.be/k_nxw_dCeOE?si=vsOntGXU-7Z3N18K)


And if you did, you probably found websites handing Durham a failing grade, warning that your chances of becoming a crime victim are alarmingly high, and leaving you wondering whether you should cross the city off your list altogether.


The truth is much more complicated.


Yes, Durham has a higher reported crime rate than many surrounding communities. But citywide statistics rarely tell the full story—and if you're relocating, they're often answering the wrong question.


Let's break down what the numbers actually mean, where crime is concentrated, and what buyers should know before choosing a neighborhood.


## The Numbers Are Real...


There's no benefit in pretending[Durham](https://youtu.be/k_nxw_dCeOE?si=vsOntGXU-7Z3N18K) doesn't have challenges.


According to recent FBI reporting, Durham's violent crime rate is higher than the national average, and the city recorded dozens of homicides in 2024. Those are real numbers that represent real people and deserve to be taken seriously.


But those statistics don't automatically answer the question most homebuyers are asking.


You're not choosing an entire city.


You're choosing one neighborhood.


Often, you're choosing one street.


## Why Crime Statistics Can Be Misleading


One of the biggest mistakes people make is assuming that an average tells the whole story.


Many crime-rating websites compare Durham to neighboring cities using percentages.


For example, saying Durham's homicide rate is "more than double" Raleigh's sounds dramatic—and technically, it's true. But looking only at percentages ignores how small the actual individual risk remains for the average resident.


Statistics can be presented in ways that create fear without adding useful context.


For someone deciding where to buy a home, neighborhood-level information is far more valuable than citywide averages.


## Most Crime Isn't What People Imagine


When many people hear "crime," they picture violent incidents.


In reality, the overwhelming majority of reported crime in Durham is property crime.


The most common offense?


Larceny—theft, often involving items left inside unlocked vehicles.


That's not to minimize property crime, but it's a very different reality than many online crime maps imply.


Simple habits—locking your vehicle and avoiding leaving valuables in plain sight—go a long way.


## Crime Isn't Evenly Distributed


Perhaps the single most important thing buyers should understand is that crime is **not spread evenly across Durham.**


Like most cities, some neighborhoods experience significantly more crime than others.


Historically, much of Durham's violent crime has been concentrated in portions of the east-central part of the city, while many western and southern neighborhoods experience dramatically lower crime rates.


Areas such as:


- South Durham
- Hope Valley
- Woodcroft
- Garrett Farms
- Duke Forest


are often compared favorably with neighboring Chapel Hill because of their residential character, school options, and overall quality of life.


This is why local knowledge matters so much when buying a home.


## Neighborhood Matters More Than the City Name


Two homes can share the same city address while offering very different lifestyles.


One neighborhood may be highly walkable, filled with families, and served by excellent schools.


Another may face completely different challenges.


That's why broad city rankings rarely provide enough information to help relocation buyers make confident decisions.


When choosing where to live, neighborhood-level research is far more meaningful than a single letter grade assigned to an entire city.


## [Durham](https://youtu.be/k_nxw_dCeOE?si=vsOntGXU-7Z3N18K) Has Changed Dramatically


Another important factor is time.


Many people still picture Durham based on its reputation from decades ago.


Today's Durham is very different.


Downtown has been revitalized, neighborhoods have grown, new employers continue to relocate to the area, and many crime trends have improved over the past twenty years.


While no city is without challenges, Durham continues to attract families, professionals, healthcare workers, university faculty, and technology employees from around the country.


## [So...Is Durham Safe?](https://youtu.be/k_nxw_dCeOE?si=vsOntGXU-7Z3N18K)


The honest answer is:


**It depends where you live.**


No city can guarantee anyone's safety.


But for the vast majority of families moving to Durham to work at Duke, UNC, Research Triangle Park, or one of the region's many healthcare and technology employers, daily life looks remarkably ordinary.


Parents take children to school.


Neighbors walk their dogs.


People enjoy restaurants, parks, trails, and community events.


The experience most residents have is very different from what broad crime rankings often suggest.


## How Buyers Should Evaluate Safety


Instead of relying solely on citywide crime grades, consider asking questions like:


- What is crime like in this specific neighborhood?
- What schools serve this area?
- How active is the community?
- How does this neighborhood compare to nearby alternatives?
- What do local residents say about living here?


Those answers usually provide a much clearer picture than a single statistic ever could.


## Final Thoughts


Durham is a city with real strengths and real challenges.


Its nationally recognized healthcare systems, thriving downtown, proximity to Duke University and Research Triangle Park, diverse neighborhoods, and strong housing market continue to attract thousands of new residents each year.


If you're considering relocating, don't let a citywide crime grade make the decision for you.


Instead, learn about the neighborhoods that match your lifestyle, your priorities, and your budget.


Because when it comes to choosing a place to call home, context matters.
