# Fast Food and Phone Use: How Avoiding Drive-Thrus Can Help Us Improve Our Relationship With Our Phones

I am a huge sucker for a fast-food hamburger (or burrito, or doughnut, or chicken product formed into a blob and deep-fried). I love the taste of a French fry, freshly salted seconds after getting pulled from the oil. I crave the carbonation of a nice fountain Coke, sipped through a big McDonald’s straw and set in the cup holder of my front seat.

A glorious pile of fast food’s greatest French fry.


But — fast food isn’t healthy (at least, the good stuff isn’t), it’s getting kind of expensive, and it makes me feel sluggish for at least the next twenty-four hours (which can lead to even more fast food consumption).


I’ve tried to cut back in various ways— I’ve given up fast food for Lent, I decided I’d only use cash so I could feel what the meal really cost, and, just before the pandemic, I decided that I wouldn’t use drive-thrus. When I wanted fast food, I’d have to get out of my car, walk into the store, and order it while looking a real human being in the eyes as we stood around a cash register.


Then, the pandemic hit, and with it came a fundamental change in how food is ordered and consumed. Some fast-food restaurants have[completely eliminated in-store dining](https://www.thestreet.com/lifestyle/food-drink/chipotle-follows-starbucks-and-opens-store-without-a-dining-room) , others have clearly reduced their focus on it by turning towards[takeout and delivery apps](https://www.apptentive.com/blog/2017/12/07/mobile-apps-changed-restaurant-industry/) , and the industry as a whole[seems to have admitted](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/03/12/drive-thru-ordering-surged-during-the-pandemic-heres-what-comes-next.html) that the days of in-store dining are long gone. There’s probably a lot of reasons for this change; namely, staffing issues and a public still hesitant at times to sit down and dine for both social and medical reasons, especially at a restaurant type that does not intrinsically require immediate, fully focused consumption.

Luckily, with the world slowly trending back to some pre-pandemic norms, I can reinstitute my “no drive-thrus” rule going forward. Introducing the little bit of friction — the small hurdle of getting out of my car, walking inside a fast food place, and having to order from a person at a cash register— has made me realize that I didn’t really want or need that fast food in the first place.


I think there’s a lot of similarities between my “no drive-thrus” rule and the way that I use my cell phone. Rushes of dopamine have never been easier to access — my phone unlocks with a scan of my thumbprint, and within two clicks and even fewer seconds, I’m able to access piles of content and information.


Unsurprisingly, the immediate access hasn’t been all positive. It’s hard for me to remember a world before iPhones and instant internet access, but I can’t remember a time where I didn’t feel at least a little unhappy with how much I was using my phone. The immediate phone access has an interesting number of similarities to eating fast-food: grogginess, short bursts of energy followed by lower valleys, and that nagging feeling that my consumption (of both food and content) could be healthier.


What’s been helpful to me in parts of life is inserting micro-barriers — hoops to jump through that are achievable (that way, I don’t just ignore them) but are powerful enough that they help me achieve a meaningful change in my life and habits.


I helped develop an[iOS app](http://clrsp.com/fastfood) that does precisely this job — anytime I open a “fast-food” app like Snapchat, YouTube, Twitter, or Instagram,[ClearSpace](http://getclearspace.com/) forces me to wait for 15 seconds before deciding if I want to actually the app I originally opened. Often, the answer is actually no — I’ve opened that app out of a misplaced hunger for cheap dopamine, or simply out of boredom or a lack of ideas of what else to do with my time.


Using my phone becomes most problematic when I treat it like a drive through: too easily accessed, too quick, avoiding meaningful interactions. ClearSpace isn’t the only solution that I’ve tried, and isn’t the only one that works, either — I work with my phone in another room as much as possible, I monitor my Screen Time using Apple’s on-device tracking, and my friends and I have both official and unofficial competitions that challenge us to use our phones in better ways. I like ClearSpace because I can’t hide from it — it’s the friend on my phone that asks me if I’m sure I want to scroll through that “fast food”.

My challenge to you is to take up the one I’ve given myself —find the bad habits in your life you want to change, and make them a little harder to do. I’d love to hear from anyone else who wants to take up this idea — let me know how it’s going for you, and if there are any other proverbial “drive-thrus” in your life you’ve decided to try to change.
