It’s no secret that our warped relationship with social media has cascading negative effects in the lives of millions. Jonathan Haidt, a social psychologist, is widely renowned for his academic research on how social media influences daily life. At the theoretical level, he documents how social media has eroded societal trust, increased political polarization, and left us feeling isolated. However, more practically, he has shown how abusive social media patterns can directly lead to individual harm. In a 2021[Atlantic article](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2021/11/facebooks-dangerous-experiment-teen-girls/620767/) , Haidt writes:


*Social media — particularly Instagram, which displaces other forms of interaction among teens, puts the size of their friend group on public display, and subjects their physical appearance to the hard metrics of likes and comment counts — takes the worst parts of middle school and glossy women’s magazines and intensifies them.*

Throughout the article, he details the specific harm experienced by teens — especially teen girls. Before wrestling with potential policy solutions, he includes an interesting response to a Facebook expert who likens the platform to “sugar”:


*But social-media platforms are not like sugar. They don’t just affect the individuals who overindulge. Rather, when teens went from texting their close friends on flip phones in 2010 to posting carefully curated photographs and awaiting comments and likes by 2014, the change rewired everyone’s social life…Improvements in technology generally help friends connect, but the move onto social-media platforms also made it easier — indeed, almost obligatory — for users to perform for one another.*


*Public performance is risky. Private conversation is far more playful. A bad joke or poorly chosen word among friends elicits groans, or perhaps a rebuke and a chance to apologize. Getting repeated feedback in a low-stakes environment is one of the main ways that play builds social skills, physical skills, and the ability to properly judge risk. Play also strengthens friendships.*


In sum, compulsive social-media use is worrisome because of the scarcity it generates around time. It diminishes the ability to moderate virtual interaction that floods an individual with negative (and frequently addictive) feedback loops. When individuals consistently “displace other forms of interaction,” as Haidt writes, it becomes harder to build a flourishing life in reality.


Haidt’s solutions point to legislative measures to curb the negative effects of compulsive use. We’re focused on a different and more sustainable approach.[Clearspace](http://getclearspace.com/) is a mechanism for achieving moderation in social media engagement. We want to help people say “yes” to meaningful relationships, activities, and rhythms. Even further, we’re hoping to reclaim a positive relationship with social media that doesn’t leave us feeling our time could have been better spent.
