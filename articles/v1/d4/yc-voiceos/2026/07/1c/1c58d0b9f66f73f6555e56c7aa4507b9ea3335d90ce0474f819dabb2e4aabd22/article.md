## What is different now


Lots of people have tried talking to a computer. Most gave up. The words showed up on the screen, and that was it. You still had to click around and finish the job yourself.


Hearing you correctly is not the hard part anymore. Computers are good at that now. They understand accents. They work in noisy rooms. What changed is what happens after you speak.


Say “reply to Dana that Thursday works.” Your computer finds the right email. It writes the reply. Then it waits for you to say yes before it sends. That is the difference. Before, you got words. Now you get the job done.

## Start with things you were going to type anyway


The easiest place to start is a message you already owe someone. A Slack reply. A few lines in a doc. A note on a ticket. The job is the same. You just talk instead of type.


Start here because it builds the habit. Talking to a computer feels weird for a day or two. If you start with something big and it goes wrong, you will give up. If you start small and it works, you keep going.


Talk the way you would talk to a friend. You do not need to say “comma” or “period” out loud. The computer adds those for you. Old voice tools made you say them. New ones do not.


Say


“Tell the team the release slipped to Tuesday and I'll post a proper update Monday morning.”


Your words show up as clean writing, wherever your cursor is.


Say


“Draft a short reply saying I can't make Thursday, but Friday afternoon is wide open.”


You get a finished message, not a messy one.


## Let it do the jobs that jump between apps


Typing is not what slows you down. Jumping around is. You find the email. You copy a date. You open the calendar. You type the date again. You go back. Ten seconds each time, a few hundred times a day.


These are the best jobs to hand over. One sentence can do all the steps. You are not just saving typing. You are skipping the whole back and forth.


Before it sends anything, it should show you first. Every time. If a tool sends an email without asking you, stop using it.


Say


“Find the invoice Marcus sent last week and put the due date on my calendar.”


Two apps. One sentence. You just say yes.


Say


“Summarize this thread and send the summary to the design channel.”


It reads it, shortens it, and posts it. You see it first.


Say


“Remind me to follow up on this Monday morning.”


The kind of thing you forget the moment you look away.

## Ask about what is on your screen


A lot of your questions are about something you are already looking at. A long error message. A rule buried deep in a contract. A chart you do not get. A form in a language you cannot read.


Typing those questions is slow. You have to explain the thing before you can ask about it. Talking is fast. Your computer sees the same screen you do. So you can just say “this.”


This is the part that wins people over. You are not doing an old job faster. You are doing something your keyboard never could.


Say


“What is this error actually telling me?”


No copying and pasting into a chat box.


Say


“What is the notice period in this contract?”


It reads the file that is open in front of you.

## How to say it so it works


When voice goes wrong, it is usually how you said it. The fix is simple. Say who or what first. Then say what you want.


Be clear about who. “Reply to Dana about the budget” works. “Reply to that message” makes it guess. Sometimes it guesses wrong.


Say what you want, not how to do it. You do not need to say “open the calendar.” Just say “put a thirty minute call with Priya on Thursday afternoon.”


Do not ask for three things at once. Two things that go together is fine. Three that do not will come back messy.


If it gets something wrong, just say so. “Make that shorter” is faster than starting over. People who learn this keep using voice. People who do not, quit.


## What your first week looks like


Day one and two. Only use it for stuff you were going to type. Replies. Rough drafts. Notes to yourself. Nothing fancy.


Once it feels normal, pick one job that makes you switch apps. Adding a meeting from an email is a good one. You do it all the time, it is annoying, and you can see right away if it worked.


By Friday, ask yourself one thing. What three jobs do I never want to do by hand again? For most people it is replies, calendar stuff, and turning messy thoughts into clean notes.


## When typing is still better


Voice will not replace your keyboard. If you expect it to, you will be let down.


If you need to look at something while you change it, type. Spreadsheets. Code you are fixing. Small edits to a sentence right in front of you. Talking pulls your eyes away.


Sitting near other people is a real thing. But it is less of a problem than you would think. Most of the time you only talk for a few seconds. Some of it can wait until you are walking back from a meeting.


Voice takes over part of your day, not all of it. But it takes over the boring part.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)


### Can I really control my computer by talking?


Yes. And it does more than you think. It can type for you, open apps, write and send messages, add meetings, search the web, and answer questions about what is on your screen. Usually the only thing holding it back is how you ask.


### Is talking really faster than typing?


Yes. Most people talk about 150 words a minute. Most people type about 40. But the bigger win is the jobs that used to take five clicks across three apps. Now they take one sentence.


### Do I have to say “comma” and “period” out loud?


No. Old voice tools made you do that, which is why people remember it being annoying. New ones work it out from the way you talk. Just talk normally.


### Does it work in every app?


Talking to type works anywhere you can click and type, even in apps with no voice feature. Bigger jobs, like sending a message or adding a meeting, only work with apps the tool connects to. So check that your main apps are on the list.


### What if other people sit near me?


Most people only talk for a few seconds at a time, so it is quieter than you think. You can also split it up. Long stuff when you are alone. Short commands at your desk.


### Could it send something without asking me?


It should never do that. If a tool does, stop using it. Anything that goes out to another person should be shown to you first, and wait for you to say yes.


### Does it work on Mac and Windows?


Yes, both. VoiceOS sits on top of all your apps instead of living inside one. So the same words work in your browser, in your apps, and anywhere else.
