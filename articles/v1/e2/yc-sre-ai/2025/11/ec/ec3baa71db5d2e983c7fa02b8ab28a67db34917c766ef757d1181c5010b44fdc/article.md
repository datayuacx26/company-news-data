# The Anxiety Is Real (And So Is the Opportunity)

There's this feeling hanging in the air right now.


You're scrolling through LinkedIn and every third post is about AI. Someone's demo-ing an agent that writes code. Another person is sharing a thread about how their entire workflow got automated. A thought leader is posting about "10x engineers" being replaced by "1x engineers with AI."


And somewhere in your chest, there's this tightness. This quiet voice asking: *What does this mean for me?*


If you work in tech, especially if you work in DevOps, SRE, or infrastructure, you're watching AI tools multiply and you're wondering which parts of your job are about to get absorbed into a model.


The anxiety is real. And honestly? It makes sense.


# **The Precedent**


In 1811, a group of English textile workers started smashing machines.


They weren't irrational. They weren't technophobic. They were skilled artisans watching mechanical looms do in minutes what took them hours. They saw their expertise, the element that fed their families, being rendered obsolete by a device that didn't need to eat or sleep or negotiate wages.


History remembers them as Luddites. The word became shorthand for anyone who resists technological change. But here's what history often leaves out: they were right to be anxious.


The Industrial Revolution *did* displace them. Factories *did* replace artisans. Entire professions vanished within a generation.


But here's what else happened.


New jobs emerged. Jobs that didn't exist before the machines. Factory supervisors. Mechanical engineers. Logistics coordinators. Roles that required different skills but created new forms of expertise.


The transition wasn't smooth. It wasn't fair. A lot of people got left behind. But the long arc bent toward something we recognize now as progress: more abundance, more access, more possibility for more people.


## **The Calculator Didn't Kill Mathematics**


Here's a closer-to-home example.


When electronic calculators became affordable in the 1970s, there was genuine concern about what it meant for math education. If students could just press buttons to get answers, would they ever learn to *think* mathematically?


Forty years later, we have more mathematicians, more engineers, and more people doing quantitative work than ever before. Not fewer.


Because here's what actually happened: calculators didn't replace mathematical thinking. They freed people from the tedious arithmetic so they could focus on the interesting problems. The problems that required insight, not just computation.


The same thing happened with spreadsheets. With IDEs. With version control. Each tool that automated some piece of the work didn't eliminate the role, it changed what the role could focus on.


## **AI Is Following the Same Pattern**


Right now, AI feels different. It feels bigger. It feels like it's moving faster than anything we've seen before.


And maybe it is.


But the pattern is the same. We're watching tools get better at doing the repetitive, the rote, the things we do because they need to get done, not because they're interesting.


Writing boilerplate code. Searching through logs for the third time this week. Manually correlating timestamps across five different systems. Drafting the same incident summary you've drafted a dozen times before.


These tasks take time. They require skill. But they're not where the value lives.


The value lives in the decisions. In the judgment calls. In understanding the context that separates a real issue from a false alarm. In knowing when to escalate and when to wait. In designing systems that don't break in the first place.


AI can help with the first part. It's not close to being able to handle the second.


# **What Changes, What Stays**


We're not naive about this. AI is going to change how DevOps work gets done. Some tasks will get automated. Some workflows will get streamlined. Some roles will evolve.


But here's what we believe: the engineers who understand systems deeply, who can diagnose the weird edge cases, who know how to balance speed and stability, those people aren't going anywhere.


What's changing is the *nature* of the work. Less time fighting with tools. Less time doing detective work through logs. Less time on the repetitive grind that burns people out.


More time on the architecture. The strategy. The resilience. The part of the job that actually requires a human who understands not just *what* the system is doing, but *why* it matters.


AI isn't replacing DevOps engineers. It's giving them leverage. The same way calculators gave mathematicians leverage. The same way spreadsheets gave analysts leverage.


It's automating the chore work so the skilled work can shine.


## **The Optimistic Case**


Here's what we think happens over the next five years:


On-call becomes less brutal because the AI handles the initial triage. Not the fix, the triage. The "here's what's broken, here's the last time it broke, here's what fixed it then" part. You still make the call. You just make it faster and with better context.


Code reviews become less about catching syntax errors and more about architectural decisions. The AI flags the obvious stuff. You focus on whether the design is sound.


Incident response becomes less about scrambling and more about coordination. The AI surfaces the relevant context. You direct the response.


Deployments become less nerve-wracking because the AI is watching for the patterns that precede failures. You still own the decision to ship. You just ship with more confidence.


The job doesn't disappear. It simply matures as it becomes less reactive and more strategic. Less firefighting and more fire prevention.


And maybe, just maybe, more people stay in DevOps longer because the job becomes more sustainable. Because the cognitive load drops. Because the tools finally help instead of just adding more noise.


## **What We're Building Toward**


At SRE.ai, we're not trying to replace engineers.


We're building AI that handles the tedious pattern-matching so you can focus on the judgment calls.


Because the truth is, the world needs more people who understand complex systems. More people who can design for resilience. More people who know how to keep things running when everything wants to break.


AI doesn't change that. If anything, it makes it more important.


# **Sitting with the Discomfort**


The anxiety you're feeling? It's valid.


We're in a moment of uncertainty. Things are changing fast. It's not always clear what the path forward looks like.


But if history teaches us anything, it's that tools don't reshape what expertise means. They create new problems that require new kinds of thinking. And the people who lean into that, who figure out how to work *with* the tools rather than against them, are the ones who thrive.


The Luddites were right to worry. But the world that came after the looms wasn't worse. It was different. And eventually, it was better.


We think the same will be true for AI in DevOps. Not without friction. Not without trade-offs. But better.


If you're feeling the anxiety, you're not alone. We're feeling it too. And we're building with that in mind.


Let's figure out what comes next together.
