## 1. Accreditation


[Accreditation](https://catalogs.uti.edu/accreditation-and-licensing) can help students understand whether a school or program has been reviewed against established standards. When comparing diesel mechanic schools, it’s worth looking at both the institution’s accreditation and any program-specific recognition connected to the field. Together, they can give students a clearer picture of educational quality, oversight and industry alignment.


### Trade School Accreditation


Trade school accreditation applies to the institution itself. It shows that a school has been reviewed by an accrediting agency based on factors like education quality, administrative standards and student support


UTI is accredited by the[Accrediting Commission of Career Schools and Colleges](https://www.accsc.org/) (ACCSC), which is recognized by the[U.S. Department of Education](https://www.ed.gov/) as an institutionally recognized accrediting agency.


Institutional accreditation can also connect to practical considerations, not just credibility. We are eligible to participate in federal student financial assistance programs administered by the[Department of Education under Title IV of the Higher Education Act of 1965](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/fsa-handbook/2020-2021/appendices/appx-g-higher-education-act-1965-table-contents-august-26-2020) , as amended.10


### Diesel Mechanic School Accreditation


Program-level accreditation looks more closely at a specific training area. For diesel and automotive training, the[National Institute for Automotive Service Excellence](https://www.ase.com/) (ASE) Education Foundation accreditation is an important industry-recognized standard. ASE stands for the National Institute for Automotive Service Excellence, an organization known for technician certification.


UTI’s diesel program is accredited by the ASE Education Foundation at most UTI campuses. Graduates of the program are prepared to take ASE examinations and can substitute their training for one of the two years of experience required to test for ASE[diesel mechanic certification](https://www.uti.edu/blog/diesel/diesel-mechanic-certification) . For students who want to become ASE certified diesel mechanics, this can be an important step toward earning[industry-recognized credentials](https://www.uti.edu/blog/diesel/how-to-stand-out-with-manufacturer-specific-diesel) .

## 2. Hands-on Training Focus


Diesel training should not feel like learning a hands-on trade from the sidelines. To help aspiring mechanics get career-ready,[diesel mechanic courses](https://www.uti.edu/programs/diesel/courses) should give students time around tools, parts, systems and shop-style environments. That practice can help students understand how classroom concepts show up in real repair scenarios.


UTI’s Diesel Technology program follows a blended learning model that combines instructor-led online coursework with hands-on application in the lab. Students can train on diesel systems like brakes, chassis, engines, electrical systems and more, depending on the campus and course sequence. This gives students a mix of concept learning and physical practice, instead of relying on one format alone.


A hands-on focus also helps students figure out how they learn best. Some concepts click faster when you can see the system in front of you. Others make more sense after you’ve taken a diagnostic process step by step.

Courses and credentials vary by MSAT program. These programs are separate from the core diesel training program and may have their own application or eligibility requirements.
