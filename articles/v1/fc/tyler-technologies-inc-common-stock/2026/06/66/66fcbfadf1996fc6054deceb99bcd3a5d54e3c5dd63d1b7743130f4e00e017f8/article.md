# Where State Efficiency Breaks Down Across Agency Systems

# Where State Efficiency Breaks Down Across Agency Systems


June 25, 2026


by Elliot Flautt


##


State governments operate at enterprise scale, with systems that support millions of interactions across agencies and public touchpoints. As those systems evolve, more of the work required to keep them aligned falls to staff.


Data moves across applications, and gaps in integration are often bridged manually. Teams route information, answer repeat questions, and navigate disconnected environments to keep operations moving.


Over time, that coordination effort becomes a significant source of operational cost.


Agencies that achieve measurable efficiency gains reduce that coordination effort. They simplify how systems connect and how information is accessed, allowing more work to be handled within the system itself.


## When Systems Connect, Demand Shifts Away From Staff


[North Carolina’s Administrative Office of the Courts](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/case-studies/north-carolina-embraces-a-shifting-technological-era) illustrates how coordination costs accumulate and what changes when they are addressed.


Over several decades, the court’s information system expanded to include more than 40 applications, many operating independently. Each served a purpose, but connections between them were limited. Staff and users absorbed the gaps through manual searches and routine inquiries.


A unified, cloud-based case management system centralized access to case data across users and functions. Court staff, attorneys, and the public now interact through a single platform with real-time data.


The system has processed more than 10 million electronic filings while reducing paper use by over 11 million pages and replacing 30 million physical documents. More than 1.5 million online searches occur each month for case events and court dates.


Direct access to information reduces the need for staff involvement, shifting capacity toward higher-value work.

## Where Small Inefficiencies Become System-Level Costs


In high-volume environments, small inefficiencies repeat at scale.


The[New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/resource-downloads/new-jersey-saves-over-3800-hours-with-process-innovation) faced this in its inspection process. Inspectors conducted fieldwork on paper, then entered data into a separate system, creating delays and inconsistent reporting.


A mobile, integrated system has restructured the workflow. Inspectors access historical data in the field, capture information during inspections, and submit reports in real time. Data flows directly into the department’s environmental management system through a single interface.


Inspectors save approximately 20 minutes per inspection, and the department has recovered more than 3,800 hours within a single program. These gains come from removing the handoffs between fieldwork and reporting, where time is typically lost and data becomes inconsistent.


## How Systems Absorb Routine Demand


A significant share of workload in state environments comes from fielding routine questions about existing information.


When those requests depend on staff involvement, they limit capacity for more complex work. The constraint is how information is accessed.


The[Nebraska Department of Motor Vehicles](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/case-studies/ai-that-transforms-the-dmv-experience) introduced an AI assistant to guide residents through common questions. Within three months, the system handled nearly 1,000 inquiries per day and engaged 59% of website visitors.


Time spent navigating the website has decreased by 10% and call volumes have dropped by roughly 20%. More routine demand is now resolved within the digital experience, reducing reliance on staff as intermediaries.


## What Changes When Systems Work as One


Across these examples, a consistent pattern emerges: efficiency gains are tied to how work moves between systems. When systems share information directly, the need for manual coordination drops, and demand begins to shift away from staff and into the system itself.


As coordination effort decreases, improvements extend beyond individual workflows. Access to information reduces call volumes. Integrated workflows reduce reporting time. Consistent data improves oversight. For large, multiagency environments like state governments, this compounding effect yields sustained efficiency improvements.


Achieving that outcome depends on how systems function together over time. It requires interoperability, shared data, and consistent user access across functions. Establishing that level of alignment requires coordination across systems that are often procured, implemented, and managed independently.


Agencies that take a long-term view of how their systems fit together create conditions where efficiency gains can build on each other.
