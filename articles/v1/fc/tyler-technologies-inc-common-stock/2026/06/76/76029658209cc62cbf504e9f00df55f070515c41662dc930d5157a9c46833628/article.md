# Where City Efficiency Improves in Everyday Interactions

# Where City Efficiency Improves in Everyday Interactions


June 18, 2026


by Craig Patton


##


For residents, getting a permit, paying a bill, or accessing a record often involves more effort than it should.


A single request can trigger follow-up calls, status checks, or repeated data entry across multiple systems. What appears to be a routine task becomes a series of interactions between residents, staff, and disconnected processes.


Those interactions create operational strain over time. As service demand grows, staff workload grows with it — not always because the work itself is more complex, but because too many routine tasks require unnecessary follow-up and coordination to complete.


Cities that improve efficiency often do so by reducing those unnecessary interactions. When workflows are simplified and information moves more directly through the organization, the same work can be completed with less administrative effort.


## When Manual Follow-Up Becomes Part of the Process


In[Raytown, Missouri](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/videos-and-webinars/missouri-city-streamlines-permitting-with-digital-solution) , routine permitting tasks meant residents had to visit city hall, call for updates, and track progress manually. Completing a request often required multiple touchpoints between residents and staff.


After moving permitting services online, applications, payments, and status updates were consolidated into a single portal. Residents no longer needed separate calls or office visits to complete routine requests.


The operational impact was measurable. Office visits declined by 85%, and postage expense dropped by 60% as communication shifted to digital channels.


The improvement was not simply faster processing. It was the removal of unnecessary follow-up work that previously consumed staff time and extended the completion cycle for residents.


# When Operational Complexity Slows Routine Work


School districts often face similar challenges to cities. The payroll processing at[Dunlap Community Unit School District](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/case-studies/tea-winner-streamlining-payroll-and-workforce-management) in Illinois depended on paper-based tracking and balancing using spreadsheets. Completing a payroll cycle often took up to two weeks and required late nights and weekend work from staff.


Payroll Specialist Jennifer Carroll described the situation directly: “The system needs to be helping us, not making it harder.”


After moving payroll into an integrated, cloud-based ERP system and enabling employee self-service access, the district reduced payroll completion time from roughly two weeks to three days.


Equally important, less work had to be handled manually. Timekeeping data began flowing directly into payroll, reports were used proactively to catch errors earlier, and employees gained direct access to payroll documents without requiring administrative handling for every request.


## When Disconnected Systems Create Administrative Overhead


In[Madison, Wisconsin](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/case-studies/how-a-modernized-payments-system-met-city-residents-needs) , residents could already pay bills online, but each payment type operated separately. Utility bills, taxes, and other transactions required different systems and separate interactions, while information moved slowly between payment platforms and the city’s ERP environment.


The city implemented an integrated payments platform connected directly to their ERP system, allowing residents to manage multiple payment types through a unified interface.


For city staff, the operational impact extended beyond convenience. Payment reconciliation became more streamlined, fewer third-party connections required IT maintenance, and payment activity updated across systems in real time.


As Financial Systems Analyst Jeff Dempsey explained, “When a payment is made, we can instantly see it flow throughout the system.”


That visibility matters operationally. When systems remain disconnected, staff spend additional time reconciling transactions and responding to issues created by delayed or incomplete information flow. Integration reduced those administrative handoffs.

## When Efficiency Expands Staff Capacity


In[Roosevelt City, Utah](https://www.tylertech.com/resources/case-studies/tea-winner-efficient-management-supports-community-growth) , administrative staff spent significant time managing purchasing approvals, tracking invoices, and duplicating data entry across outdated systems. The workload had grown large enough that the city considered adding staff at an estimated annual cost of $180,000.


Instead, the city restructured procurement and approval workflows within a cloud-based ERP system. Processes that once took two days were reduced to roughly two hours, while employees gained the ability to manage more purchasing activity independently.


The change reduced administrative overhead and freed staff to focus on broader operational priorities across the city.


As HR and IT Manager Brock Arnold explained, “It’s not always the right answer to hire more people.”


## What Changes When Work Requires Fewer Handoffs


Efficiency in city operations is shaped by how much manual effort is required to complete routine work.


When requests depend on repeated follow-up, duplicate entry, or movement between disconnected systems, administrative workload grows alongside service demand. Over time, cities can end up adding staff simply to sustain routine processes.


Cities that simplify how work moves through the organization change that equation. When applications, payments, approvals, and records move through more connected workflows, the same staff can handle more requests with less administrative effort.


For cities managing constant day-to-day service demand, reducing unnecessary handoffs becomes one of the most practical ways to improve operational efficiency at scale.
