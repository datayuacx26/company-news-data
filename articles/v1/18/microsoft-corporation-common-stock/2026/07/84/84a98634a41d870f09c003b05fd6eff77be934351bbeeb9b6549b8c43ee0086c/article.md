[Kaan Can G.](https://ae.linkedin.com/in/kaancanguven?trk=public_post_comment_actor-name) 4d


The strongest part of this is the security argument, and it's the one that'll get missed. "Closed equals safer" is the same bet the industry already lost with crypto and with software. Security through obscurity failed everywhere it was tried, because defenders can't inspect what they can't see. Which reframes the whole risk conversation: openness is often the safer path, since a thousand researchers finding and patching holes beats a handful of insiders hoping nobody notices theirs. One practitioner note though. Open weights are necessary, but they aren't where most companies are actually locked in. The real lock-in lives in the harness and the evals wired around the model. You can have fully open weights and still be trapped if your whole system assumes one provider's API. Open weights give you the option to leave. Building the layer around them is what lets you actually take it.

[Sumeet Bansal](https://ae.linkedin.com/in/sumeetbansal?trk=public_post_comment_actor-name) 4d


The part worth noticing isn’t the open-weight argument, it’s the signatory list. NVIDIA, Dell, Microsoft, Palantir, ServiceNow, Box, CrowdStrike, plus the open labs and the VCs. Almost none of them earn their money from the weights. They earn it from the compute, the distribution, the security and the application layers around the weights. A coalition like this arguing to keep the model layer open and plural is a rational business position, not just a principled one. You commoditize the layer you don’t own and protect the layers you do. What’s telling is who isn’t on it. The closed frontier labs are absent. So underneath the national-security language, a lot of this is a fight over where the value in AI consolidates, and everyone here has an interest in it not consolidating at the model. The enterprise version of the operational point, that owning weights and using them are different things, is true but fairly well understood by now. The sharper question for a leadership team is which layer above the model you intend to own, because that’s where the margin and leverage sit, and openness at the model layer is quietly being handed to you while nobody in that coalition plans to give away what they built on top.

[Ram Varadarajan](https://www.linkedin.com/in/ramvaradarajan?trk=public_post_comment_actor-name) 2d


I've been thinking a lot about what happens after an organization chooses an open-weight model. The conversation usually revolves around performance, cost, or benchmark scores, but once that model starts making decisions inside real business processes, the more important question is whether you can trust how it reaches those decisions. Fine-tuning helps, but it doesn't erase everything a model learned during pre-training. If the underlying corpus reflects jurisdiction-specific information controls or persistent biases, those characteristics can still surface in unexpected ways -- especially when autonomous agents encounter situations no one anticipated. That's why I don't think model selection is the finish line. If enterprises are serious about deploying autonomous agents at scale, they'll need runtime controls that understand how a model behaves in the wild. For me, that points toward model-aware, agent-centric deception: continuously observing behavior, detecting manipulation or goal drift as it develops, and intervening before a bad reasoning path becomes an operational problem.
