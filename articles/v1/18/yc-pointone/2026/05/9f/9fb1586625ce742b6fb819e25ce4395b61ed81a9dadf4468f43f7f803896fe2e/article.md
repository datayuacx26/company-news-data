## What is a law firm time tracking audit?


A time tracking audit is a systematic review of how your firm captures, records, and manages billable and non-billable time entries. The purpose is straightforward: ensure compliance, maximize revenue, and reduce billing disputes before they happen.


This differs from a billing audit, which looks at invoices after they go out. A time tracking audit focuses upstream, on how time data gets created in the first place.


The audit typically examines five core areas:


-


**Timekeeping policies and procedures:** Whether documented standards exist and are actually enforced.


-


**Time entry data quality:** Accuracy, completeness, and timeliness of entries


-


**Technology and tool adoption:** Whether attorneys are using the firm's timekeeping software effectively.


-


**Compliance with client billing guidelines:** Alignment with Outside Counsel Guidelines (OCGs) and firm-level rules.


-


**Timekeeper behavior and habits:** How and when attorneys actually record their time.


## Why law firms should audit their time tracking process


### Uncover revenue leakage from uncaptured billable time


Attorneys routinely miss short tasks. A quick email reply, a five-minute phone call, a hallway conversation about a matter. Individually, none of them seem worth logging. But over weeks and months, they add up to meaningful lost revenue. This[uncaptured billable time](https://pointone.com/capture-more-billables) adds up to meaningful lost revenue.


An audit quantifies the gap between work performed and time recorded. Often, firm leadership is surprised by what they find.


### Identify billing compliance gaps before clients reject invoices


Clients increasingly enforce OCGs with automated eBilling systems that flag or reject noncompliant entries. Block billing, vague narratives, prohibited task codes — all of them can trigger rejections.


An audit catches compliance issues before they become write-offs or payment delays. Finding problems internally costs far less than discovering them through rejected invoices.


### Expose inconsistent timekeeping habits across attorneys


Without an audit, firms often don't realize how widely timekeeping practices vary. Some attorneys enter time daily. Others reconstruct weekly from memory and calendar scraps.


This inconsistency makes utilization data unreliable and performance comparisons across practice groups nearly impossible.


### Gain operational visibility into utilization and profitability


Firm leadership often lacks confidence in reports on attorney productivity, matter-level profitability, and staffing efficiency. The root cause is almost always incomplete or inconsistent time data upstream. An audit establishes whether the data feeding management reports is actually trustworthy.


## Signs your law firm needs a time tracking audit


### Rising invoice rejections or client write-downs


When clients push back on invoices, request reductions, or reject line items outright, that's a leading indicator that time entries don't meet billing standards.


### Attorneys consistently entering time days or weeks late


Delayed time entry is the single most common symptom of a broken timekeeping process. If most attorneys aren't entering time the same day work is performed, the data is already degraded.


### Vague or noncompliant time entry narratives


Entries like "research" or "review documents" without matter context or task specificity signal both a training gap and a compliance risk.


### No documented firm-wide timekeeping policies


If your firm has no written policy governing time entry expectations — deadlines, narrative standards, minimum increments — there's no baseline to audit against. The absence of policy is itself a finding.


### Firm leadership lacks confidence in profitability data


When partners or the CFO question whether utilization or realization reports are reliable, the problem usually traces back to poor upstream time data.


## How to audit your law firm's time tracking process step by step


### Step 1. Define the audit scope and objectives


Start by defining what you're evaluating. Will you audit all timekeepers or focus on a specific practice group? What time period will you review? What outcomes are you hoping to achieve?


Sample objectives might include reducing late entries, improving narrative quality to reduce eBilling rejections, or evaluating whether current technology supports efficient time capture.


### Step 2. Review existing timekeeping policies and procedures


Examine whether your firm has written policies for time entry deadlines, narrative requirements, billing increment standards, and consequences for noncompliance.


If policies exist, assess whether they're current and communicated. If they don't exist, flag that gap immediately.


Key policy elements to check:


-


Time entry submission deadlines (same-day, next-day, weekly)


-


Narrative format and minimum detail requirements


-


Billing increment standards (sixth-of-an-hour, tenth-of-an-hour)


-


Block billing prohibitions


-


Guidelines for non-billable time recording


### Step 3. Analyze time entry data for patterns and gaps


Pull time entry data from your practice management or billing system and look for patterns. Compare entry timestamps to work dates. Check average narrative length. Look for clusters of after-hours bulk entries and entries with minimal descriptions.


Specific data points worth examining:


-


Time between work performed and entry submitted


-


Average narrative word count by timekeeper


-


Percentage of entries submitted after 5 pm on Fridays


-


Entries with descriptions under five words


### Step 4. Evaluate compliance with client billing guidelines


Cross-reference time entries against your firm's most common OCGs. Look for entries that would trigger automated rejections—block billing, prohibited task codes, vague descriptions, and entries exceeding maximum hourly increment, task codes, vague descriptions, and entries exceeding maximum hourly increments.


This step requires pulling a sample of pre-bill or submitted invoices and checking them against relevant OCG requirements.


### Step 5. Assess timekeeping technology and tool adoption


Evaluate what timekeeping software your firm uses (Aderant, Elite 3E, Clio, Intapp Time, and others), how widely it's adopted, and whether attorneys are actually using the tool's features or working around it.


Questions to answer:


-


Are all timekeepers actively using the designated tool?


-


Does the tool integrate with the firm's billing system?


-


Does the tool offer automated capture or AI-assisted entry?


-


Are timekeepers using workarounds that bypass the system?


### Step 6. Interview timekeepers and billing staff


Data alone won't tell the full story. Conduct brief interviews or surveys with a cross-section of attorneys and billing coordinators. Ask why attorneys delay time entry, what frustrates them about the current tool, and where billing staff spend the most time cleaning up entries.


### Step 7. Benchmark findings against industry standards


Compare your findings to accepted law firm timekeeping benchmarks. Same-day time entry expectations, minimum narrative lengths, and target realization rates vary by firm size and practice area, but they help you understand where your firm falls relative to peers.


### Step 8. Document findings and build a remediation plan


Compile all findings into a structured report organized by category: policy, data quality, compliance, technology, and behavior. For each finding, recommend a specific corrective action, assign an owner, and set a timeline.

## Common problems a time tracking audit reveals


### Delayed and reconstructed time entries


Attorneys often batch-enter time at the end of the week by reconstructing activities from memoryAttorneys often batch-enter time at the end of the week by reconstructing activities from memory, calendars, and email. Reconstructed entries are less accurate and tend to undercount billable time.


### Missed billable time from short tasks and communications


Brief phone calls, quick email replies, and short document reviews frequently go unbilled because attorneys don't think they're worth logging. In aggregate, they represent a significant share of lost revenue.


### Block billing and ethical risk exposure


Block billing means lumping multiple tasks into a single time entry. Many courts, clients, and OCGs prohibit it. An audit often reveals widespread block billing that the firm didn't realize was occurring.


### Poor narrative quality that triggers eBilling rejections


Entries with vague descriptions like "work on matter" or "legal research" get flagged or rejected by client eBilling platforms. Narrative quality is both a compliance issue and a revenue issue. Narrative quality is both a compliance issue and a revenue issue.


### Underutilized or outdated legal timekeeping software


Many firms pay for timekeeping software but only use basic features, or they're running legacy tools that lack modern capabilities. The audit often reveals that technology isn't the constraint — adoption and configuration are.


### Inconsistent practices across practice groups


Different practice areas within the same firm often develop their own informal timekeeping norms. Litigation timekeepers may enter time daily while transactional attorneys batch-enter weekly. This inconsistency makes firm-wide data unreliable.

## What to do after completing a law firm time tracking audit


### Establish or update firm-wide timekeeping policies


If the audit revealed missing or outdated policies, the priority is creating clear, enforceable standards covering entry deadlines, narrative requirements, and compliance expectations.


### Train attorneys on compliant time entry practices


Policy alone isn't enough. Targeted training helps, especially for associates and laterals. Cover narrative standards, OCG requirements, and how to use the firm's timekeeping tools effectively.


### Evaluate whether current timekeeping tools are the bottleneck

## FAQ about law firm time tracking audits


How often should a law firm audit its time tracking process?


Most firms benefit from quarterly or biannual audits, with an initial comprehensive audit followed by lighter check-ins. The cadence depends on firm size and the severity of issues found in the first audit.


Who should be responsible for conducting a time tracking audit at a law firm?


The audit is typically owned by the COO, director of operations, or billing manager, with input from practice group leaders and IT for the technology assessment.


Can a time tracking audit help reduce outside counsel guideline rejections?


Yes. One of the primary outcomes is identifying entries that would fail OCG checks, allowing the firm to fix compliance issues at the source before invoices are submitted.


What is the difference between a time tracking audit and a billing audit?


A time tracking audit focuses on how time data is captured and recorded upstream. A billing audit examines invoices, rates, and payments downstream. The time tracking audit addresses root causes; the billing audit addresses symptoms.


How long does a typical law firm time tracking audit take to complete?


A focused audit can take a few weeks for a small firm and several weeks to a couple of months for a larger firm, depending on scope, data availability, and the number of timekeepers involved.


Do small law firms need to audit their time tracking process?


Small firms are often more vulnerable to revenue leakage because they lack dedicated billing or operations staff to catch issues. An audit is simpler to execute at a small firm and can have an outsized impact on revenue.
