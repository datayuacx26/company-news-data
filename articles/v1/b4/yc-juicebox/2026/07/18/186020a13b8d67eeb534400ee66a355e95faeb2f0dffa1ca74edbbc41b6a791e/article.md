# What is an interview rubric? Build and use guide

Most interviewers can intuitively grasp the purpose of an interview rubric. The problem is building one your interview panels *want* to use. To encourage adoption, you need a resource that’s easy for interviewers to implement and makes their jobs easier.


In this guide, we’ll walk you through how to build functional interview rubrics that help hiring managers evaluate candidates objectively. Along the way, we’ll define how these rubrics work and what to include to keep scoring consistent.


## **What is an interview rubric?**


Interview rubrics are structured guides used by interviewers to evaluate different candidates for a role against the same set of job-relevant criteria.


A complete rubric has three parts:


- **Competencies:** Rubrics detail the skills and ideal behaviors a role requires. They should also provide short definitions for each competency to leave no room for ambiguity.
- **Rating scale:** Rubrics include a fixed scale, such as 1–4 or 1–5, where each number on the scale represents a pre-defined meaning.
- **Example benchmarks:** Rubrics also include behavioral indicators, short descriptors of what a 2, for example, sounds like when compared to other numbers on the scale. This makes it easier for interviewers to tie what candidates say or demonstrate to a specific score, instead of going off of gut feeling.


### **Interview rubrics vs interview scorecards: What is the difference?**


While the two terms are often used interchangeably, interview rubrics and scorecards refer to different artifacts in practice.


Rubrics are the standard-bearing frameworks for a role. Your team builds them once per role to define competencies, the ratings scale, and example benchmarks.


Interview scorecards, on the other hand, are the live documents filled out during the interview to record candidate answers and provide a score.


Take the example of an open role with a four-person hiring panel interviewing six candidates. The hiring team creates the rubric first, one time. Then the individual interviewers use the rubric to create 24 different interview scorecards, one for each interview.


## **Why interview rubrics improve hiring**


Rubrics ensure that candidate-specific evidence is used to drive interviewer recommendations and that the defined job criteria are applied consistently.


Some tangible ways rubrics improve hiring include:


- **Quality of hire:** Scoring based on pre-defined competencies helps ensure candidates are advanced based on the demonstrative behaviors that predict success in a role, rather than factors like general likeability, pedigree, or a vague sense of cultural fit.
- **Speed and alignment:** When panels assess candidates on the same scale, disagreements that arise in the debrief are easier to reconcile, allowing teams to forge alignment and move forward faster.
- **Fairness and compliance:** Hiring decisions can be challenged in the future. But using a rubric with defined competencies and scales creates a paper trail that helps panels defend decisions if needed.
- **Candidate experience:** Every candidate gets asked the same questions and is held to the same standard, making it easier for interviewers to provide concrete feedback when asked.


## **Common reasons rubrics fail**


Rubrics don’t usually fail because of some inherent flaw in the competencies selected by hiring panels or the framework they use to score candidates. They fail because rubrics fossilize into untouched documents that never make it into the interview or influence proceedings.


Some other common failure points:


- **Vague scales:** A scale labeled 1–5 without concrete definitions and examples of behavior or competencies at each step sets up your interviewers to rely on instinct instead of evidence. And relying on instinct can lead to unconscious biases that unfairly skew the hiring process.
- **Misweighted priorities:** If the nice-to-have skills carry the same weight as core job requirements, the rubric doesn’t accurately reflect the bar for candidates to clear.
- **Delayed scoring:** When interviewers wait until too long after the interview to record scores, solid evidence tends to be replaced by vague impressions.
- **Opinion-based notes:** Comments with no evidence behind them ("strong communicator") leave nothing to compare against, so the bar shifts from candidate to candidate depending on who's assessing.


## **What to include in a rubric**


Rubrics get adopted when they’re specific to roles and encourage interviewers to rely on evidence. Two core sets of elements come together to make that happen.


### Competencies and question alignment


Start by defining core competencies drawn from the job’s requirements, not a template. Finding the right balance is key; include too few competencies, and interviewers will feel it’s missing important context. Too many, and you risk interviewers glossing over them.


Each competency should include a short definition and be mapped to specific questions (and vice versa). Build competencies without questions, and you risk interviewers inventing their own inconsistent questions. Build questions without attached competencies, and interviewers end up with potentially helpful information, but no way to map it to the core job requirements in the hiring process.


### **Scoring and evidence fields**


On the scoring side, rubrics should include the following:


- **Calibrated rating scales:** Where each point maps to a written definition.
- **Behavioral indicators:** Each point in a score should describe what an answer at that level conveys.
- **Weights:** Core competencies that drive success in the role should be properly weighted.
- **Comment fields:** For interviewers to easily and consistently record their remarks in one place.
- **Overall scoring ranges:** Tie these to a decision, so scores map cleanly to “advance,” “hold,” or “reject” recommendations.


## **How to build and use interview rubrics**


Building and using your interview rubric is a continuous workflow that extends from role calibration all the way to post-interview review. Here’s how to run it.


### **Build the rubric around competencies and questions**


Hold a calibration conversation with the hiring manager before you put the first candidate interview on your calendar. Then, ask what success looks like for this new hire in their first six months on the job. That answer informs the core competencies you’ll be hiring for.


From there:


- **Define role-specific competencies:** In one sentence, if possible, in terms everyone on the interview panel understands.
- **Define score meanings:** Articulate what makes a 2 a 2 and a 4 a 4, then fill in the rest of the steps between them on the ratings scale.
- **Assign weights:** Work backward from the most essential competencies, making sure core skills and behaviors are highlighted.
- **Write standardized questions:** Ensure these are tied to job outcomes, with examples of follow-up prompts in case interviewers need to drill down for evidence.
- **Add a notes field:** These should live under each competency and be required for each interviewer to fill out.


### **Use the rubric during and after interviews**


During the interview, lean on the[assessment questions](https://juicebox.ai/blog/job-interview-questions) defined in the rubric and use the notes field to actively jot down evidence. Try to capture what candidates say, including verbatim quotes where possible. Then, review the evidence to score the candidate for each competency as soon as the interview ends. The longer you wait to finish scoring, the fuzzier your memory gets, so it’s best to finalize ratings as soon as possible.


The evidence that matters most varies depending on the type of role you’re hiring.


In an SDR interview, for example, it’s not enough to hear that the candidate is resilient; try to get them to explain in detail how they handle rejection and what they’ve changed specifically in the past when working through a rough sales stretch. For software engineer interviews, on the other hand, you want to understand how candidates approach technical problems—e.g., whether they can articulate any trade-offs between different approaches—not just hear an answer devoid of context.


These sorts of deep candidate insights are exactly what rubrics are for and where they prove their value in the evaluation process.


## **Next steps for consistent scoring**


Teams unlock consistent scoring when they use rubrics to recalibrate their hiring, not just evaluate candidates.


The scores a panel produces—and how effectively they achieve alignment—are themselves records of how well a particular rubric performs. As roles, candidate expectations, and markets evolve, reviewing rubrics becomes necessary to ensure they don’t drift from their intended purpose.


### **Calibration and drift review**


Teams should aim to review rubric data on a quarterly basis, or after a set number of interviews (10–15) for high-volume roles.


Three patterns worth keeping an eye out for:


- **Consistently low-scoring competencies:** If most or all candidates are routinely earning low marks on a specific competency, odds are good that something is misaligned. Questions might be framed incorrectly, or the bar might be set too high for the role's expected seniority level.
- **Interviewer leniency:** If one interviewer on the panel’s candidates are averaging a full point higher than the others’ for their assigned competency, they might be waving candidates along without the proper scrutiny.
- **Score drift across panels:** On some panels, scores tend to drift upwards over time when interviewers start comparing candidates against each other, and not the rubric itself.


In any of the above scenarios, taking the time to recalibrate with the panel members or retool problem areas in the rubric itself is a valuable intervention that drives improved consistency over time.


### **Operationalizing the rubric**


Remember: Rubrics earn staying power when they live in the systems your team already uses.


Get a few key elements right, and adopting the rubric becomes the path of least resistance. One of these is using embedded score capture in the tools your team already has open. This ensures scorecards get filled out inside the interview window, and not hours (or days) later once recall has already been significantly degraded.


Keep one standard-bearing rubric per role. Copying the rubric into a new document for each search allows small tweaks to accumulate over time; eventually, your interviewers are unwittingly working with completely different documents. Filtering reports by competency and user makes those changes visible. Finally, connecting the entire workflow to your ATS keeps scoring history attached to the role, and not stowed away in various team members’ folders.

## **Frequently asked questions**


### **What are interview rubrics?**


Interview rubrics are structured scoring guides used to evaluate candidates against the same set of job-relevant criteria. Rubrics define competencies, assign a rating scale and weights, and provide behavioral indicators describing the type of evidence from candidates that justify a given score.


### **What is a rubric for interviews?**


A rubric for interviews is the framework a hiring team uses to score candidates. It’s built once per role and used for every candidate in that search.


### **What is an example of an interview rubric?**


An example rubric for an SDR might measure four competencies: e.g., prospecting approach, objection handling, resilience, and coachability. Each competency will carry a percentage weight that aligns with its importance relative to the others; prospecting might account for 30% of the total, while a lesser factor like coachability would count for 20%.


Each competency is anchored by behavioral indicators that help interviewers discern a candidate's score on a set scale, such as 1–4. Lower scores might indicate the candidate answers the question without providing specifics, while higher scores involve a thorough description of the candidate’s experience and aptitude with concrete examples.
