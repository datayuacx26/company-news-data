# From Storage to Service: What It Really Takes to Return an Aircraft to the Skies After a Lease Ends

When Spirit Airlines ceased operations in May 2026, dozens of Airbus A320-family aircraft went from active revenue service to idle almost overnight. Most of the fleet was leased rather than owned outright, which meant the jets didn't simply disappear into a bankruptcy estate, they went back to their lessors. Aircraft that had been flying passengers between Fort Lauderdale and the Caribbean one week were being ferried to storage facilities in Arizona and California the next, awaiting their next chapter.


For the leasing companies now holding those aircraft, the immediate question isn't just "who's the next operator?" It's "what does it actually take to get this aircraft ready for them?"


That question comes up more often than most people outside the industry realize. Leases end. Airlines restructure, downsize, or shut down entirely. Fleets get renewed. Whatever the reason, a lessor eventually ends up holding an aircraft that needs to go from parked to airworthy to earning revenue again, and the path between those two states is longer and more technical than it looks from the outside.


### Why "return to service" is its own discipline.


An aircraft coming off lease isn't simply refueled and handed to the next airline. Every transition involves reconciling years of maintenance records, verifying the aircraft's configuration against what the next operator actually needs, and addressing whatever crept in during the handoff period itself.
