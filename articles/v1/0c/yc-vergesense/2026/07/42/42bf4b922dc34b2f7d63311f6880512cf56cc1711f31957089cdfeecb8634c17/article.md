## What Is Workplace Experience Design?


Workplace experience design is the practice of shaping physical space around how people actually behave at work: where they go, when, and why, rather than around a fixed seat count or a floor plan inherited from a pre-pandemic lease. It treats the office as something employees opt into.


For you as a Workplace Experience Leader, that reframes the core decision away from filling seats or hitting an occupancy target on a report. The job becomes designing an experience good enough that employees choose it over working from home, without over-building space that then sits empty or under-building it in a way that pushes people out during the hours they do come in.


Below, we cover what data-driven workplace design looks like in practice and the office design trends shaping the employee experience today.

### Office Design Trends Shaping the Employee Experience


The specific trends shift year to year, but the ones holding up in a hybrid steady-state share a common thread: they respond to measured demand rather than what looks good in a rendering.


- **Neighborhood-based layouts** that group teams by how often and with whom they collaborate, rather than by department org chart.


- **Activity-based zones** that separate focus work, informal collaboration, and video calls into distinct settings instead of one generic open floor.


- **Collaboration-ready meeting rooms** sized and equipped for how rooms are actually used, matching a floor plan to observed demand instead of the headcount it was drawn against.


- **Right-sized shared space** that matches the ratio of desks, huddle rooms, and lounge areas to observed attendance instead of a fixed headcount formula.


Energy and operational efficiency, once treated as its own design trend built around preset heating and cooling schedules, is better understood today as an outcome of good design. A building that runs its systems around real occupancy patterns tends to be more sustainable by default, without needing a separate initiative to get there.


Agile seating and hot-desking remain useful tools, but only when they follow observed utilization rather than trend-following for its own sake. The case below shows what that looks like when the underlying data tells a different story than the floor plan assumed.

### Neighborhood Design and Space Mix


Team neighborhoods work best when the mix of individual, collaborative, and quiet space reflects how each team actually uses the floor. A standard ratio applied portfolio-wide misses the differences that matter: an engineering team deep in focus work and a sales team that's constantly on calls need different space mixes even if they sit on the same floor.


Occupancy Intelligence is how leaders see that neighborhood-level demand before committing to a layout, rather than after the walls have moved and the complaints start.


The starting move is an honest audit of current usage: which zones are full every day, which sit empty, and which only look busy because belongings are parked there. From there, underused space can be reallocated into the settings employees actually reach for. That audit shouldn't be a one-time event; attendance patterns drift, so the assessment needs to keep repeating.


A newer capability, Capacity Pooling, extends that assessment beyond a single floor. Instead of modeling risk floor-by-floor, teams can group segments of space into shared capacity pools, evaluating two zones that share demand together, or isolating a single self-contained neighborhood to see exactly where it breaks under added density.


That gives a clearer, threshold-based basis for whether to consolidate two half-empty floors, where to rebalance the space mix, and which specific neighborhoods need attention first. Modeling risk thresholds against a proposed layout means a redesign starts with a defensible read of where employee experience is likely to hold and where it's likely to break.

### Why Hybrid Is the Design Baseline


Hybrid work has become the baseline workplace teams design against. The


[9th-edition Occupancy Intelligence Index](https://www.vergesense.com/resources/occupancy-intelligence-index/9th-edition-index) , drawn from 200+ enterprises across 50 countries, found that stricter attendance mandates haven't meaningfully moved the underlying pattern: offices still peak midweek, hitting 52-60% attendance on the busiest days, while the average across the week sits closer to 9-11%.


That gap between peak and average is the design problem. Space built to comfortably fit a Tuesday crowd sits mostly empty on a Friday, and space trimmed to the weekly average turns Tuesday into a scramble for a desk. Stable hybrid patterns mean workplace leaders can design for the actual shape of the week, rather than rebuilding every time a new policy memo goes out.


### Measuring Whether the Experience Is Working


A redesign isn't finished when the furniture ships. The only way to know whether it worked is to close the loop: pair employee sentiment, from surveys, feedback, and informal signals, with what the occupancy data shows actually happened afterward.


A Workplace Experience Leader is ultimately measured on two things: whether the space gets used, and whether teams choose to come in for it. If sentiment improves but attendance and utilization don't, the redesign solved a perception problem while leaving the design problem intact.


If both move together, that's the signal worth trusting, and the same data should keep getting checked as attendance patterns continue to shift.

## How to Get Started With Workplace Experience Design


Workplace experience design works best as an ongoing practice, not a one-time project with a launch date. It runs on three moves, repeated on a cycle: audit how space actually gets used today, design the next change around that measured demand rather than assumptions, and keep measuring after the change ships to confirm it worked.


Start with whichever floor or neighborhood has the loudest complaints or the emptiest look on a Tuesday. Use what you find there to build the case for expanding the practice portfolio-wide, and use

## FAQs About Workplace Experience Design


### What Is the Difference Between Workplace Experience and Workplace Design?


The difference between workplace experience and workplace design is that workplace design refers to the physical layout (desks, rooms, floor plans), while workplace experience design shapes that layout around how employees actually behave. The space itself becomes part of why someone chooses to come in, and part of how well the day goes once they arrive.


### How Do You Measure the Success of a Workplace Experience Design?


You measure the success of a workplace experience design by pairing occupancy data with employee sentiment. If both attendance and satisfaction improve after a redesign, it worked. If sentiment improves but usage doesn't, the change addressed perception while the underlying design problem is still there.


### What Data Do You Need Before Redesigning an Office?


Before redesigning an office, you need measured occupancy and utilization data by space type and neighborhood. That means knowing which rooms and zones get used, when, and by whom, so the redesign responds to real demand. Survey input can supplement, but on its own it's a report of preferences rather than behavior.


### How Does Workplace Experience Design Support Employee Retention?


Workplace experience design supports employee retention by shaping the office around how people actually want to work, so it becomes part of why someone stays. That investment in the day-to-day experience signals employees are valued, which supports the broader case for attracting and retaining talent.
