# Building a culture of privacy: Lessons in data security from Marco Walraven

# **Building a culture of privacy: Lessons in data security from Marco Walraven**

*A reflection on insights shared by Marco Walraven, Director of Operations at Tucows Domains*


When it comes to data privacy and security, many organizations focus on tools and technology. But as we heard from Marco Walraven, Director of Operations at Tucows Domains and former Interim Security Officer at a well-known health insurance service provider, the key to a secure organization isn’t just firewalls and encryption — it’s culture. In a recent discussion, Marco shared his insights into **how businesses can foster an environment where employees become advocates for privacy** .

## The power of education over restriction


One of the standout lessons from Marco’s experience is the **effectiveness of education over rigid security restrictions.** “People are adults,” Marco emphasizes. “You want to give them a little bit of ownership.” Instead of creating a culture of fear where employees worry about making mistakes, organizations should focus on training employees to recognize and respond to security threats.


### This approach can take several forms:


- **Regular, ongoing training sessions** to educate employees on handling sensitive data
- **Collaborative discussions** , rather than just dry online modules
- **Proactive security drills** , including social engineering tests
- **Empowering employees** with responsibility for their own devices, while ensuring security guidelines are clear


By choosing to **engage employees as partners in security** rather than just enforcing rules from above, it’s critical to create a workforce that is both aware and invested in protecting data.


## Making security a part of daily work


One of the best ways to instill data protection in an organization’s culture is by integrating security into everyday activities. Marco’s team encouraged employees to report suspicious activity and even conducted hands-on security exercises. For example, they tested their team’s awareness by leaving USB sticks in the parking lot to see if anyone would pick them up and plug them into their computers. Employees passed the test by turning the USB drives into the front desk, proving that security awareness had become second nature.


## Lowering the barriers to reporting


A critical takeaway from Marco’s experience is the importance of making employees **feel safe reporting security incidents** . “If you lower the bar so that people don’t think they’re getting penalized for making a mistake, they’ll come forward much earlier,” Marco explained.

Creating an open and non-punitive environment around security mistakes means issues get addressed faster, reducing the potential for larger security breaches.


## Balancing convenience and security


Security policies should support productivity, not hinder it. Marco highlighted that overly restrictive security tools — like aggressive antivirus software — can sometimes make it difficult for employees (especially developers) to do their work efficiently. Instead, businesses should **aim for a balanced approach** that ensures protection without unnecessary roadblocks.


## Small changes, big impact


Beyond major initiatives, small changes can also make a big difference in company culture. One simple adjustment Marco’s team made was removing company-branded stickers from laptops. While having a branded laptop may seem harmless, it can make employees a target for attackers when traveling. Avoiding such obvious identifiers helps minimize risk in public spaces.


Additionally, Marco’s team emphasized awareness around phishing emails and other social engineering tactics. They ran exercises where employees received realistic-looking phishing emails from a domain name with slight misspellings. Only a handful of employees clicked on the email, and those who did immediately reported it — demonstrating that education efforts were paying off.


## Key takeaways for any organization


So, what can other organizations learn from Marco’s approach to security and privacy?


**Prioritize education over restriction** — Employees should understand why security measures matter, not just be told to follow rules.


**Foster a culture of responsibility** — Give employees ownership of their devices and train them on best practices.


**Make security a daily habit** — Regular training, hands-on exercises, and real-world scenarios help keep security top-of-mind.


**Encourage reporting without fear** — Employees should feel safe admitting mistakes so issues can be addressed quickly.


**Balance security with usability** — Avoid overly restrictive tools that hinder productivity while still maintaining strong security practices.


By taking a thoughtful and education-driven approach to security, organizations can build a culture where **privacy and data protection aren’t just IT concerns — they’re everyone’s responsibility.** And as Marco’s experience shows, when employees feel empowered and informed, they become the strongest line of defense against security threats.
