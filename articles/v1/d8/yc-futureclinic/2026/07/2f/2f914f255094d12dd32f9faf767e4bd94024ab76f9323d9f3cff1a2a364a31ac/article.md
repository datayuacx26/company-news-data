# What to Check When OTC Minoxidil Isn't Working: A Dermatologist's Guide


Months of minoxidil with no visible change doesn't automatically mean the treatment failed. A dermatologist walks through the short list of things that actually matter: application habits, hair loss pattern, labs, and whether something else is working against it.


[Dr Dennis Porto](https://futureclinic.com/chat?clinic=drporto&origin=blog_byline) Jul 20, 2026 · 12 min read


If you've been applying over-the-counter minoxidil for months and your hair doesn't look any different, the answer probably isn't "minoxidil doesn't work." It's more likely that something about the application, the expectations, or the underlying diagnosis needs a closer look. I walk through this exact situation regularly, and there's a short list of things that actually matter when you're trying to figure out why you're not seeing results.

## What are we actually checking for here?


This isn't about picking a new treatment yet. Before you switch anything, you need to know whether the minoxidil genuinely failed or whether the setup was off from the start. That means looking at how you've been using it, what kind of hair loss you have, and whether there's something else going on that minoxidil alone was never going to fix. Get the diagnosis and the application right, and the treatment decision becomes obvious.


## What should a thorough investigation include?


### History


This is the piece most people skip, and it's the piece that changes everything. Your dermatologist needs to know:


- When did you first notice thinning, and where? Vertex only? Temples? Diffuse all over? The pattern tells us which type of hair loss is dominant.
- How long have you been on OTC minoxidil, and how consistently? Once a day vs. twice a day matters. Using it "most days" vs. every single day matters. Applying it to wet hair right out of the shower vs. a dry scalp matters. These details separate a true non-responder from someone whose technique was diluting the effect.
- Family history of hair loss. Both sides. This helps confirm whether androgenetic alopecia is the primary driver.
- Recent stressors, illness, surgery, or major life changes. Any of these can trigger telogen effluvium, a temporary shedding event that layers on top of androgenetic alopecia and makes everything look worse than it is.
- Current medications. Some medications contribute to hair thinning, and if you're on one of those, minoxidil is fighting an uphill battle.
- For women: menstrual history, postpartum status, signs of hormonal imbalance. These shift the differential and the lab panel.


A thorough history through a chat consultation often takes longer than what you'd get in a 15-minute office visit, because there's no clock running. Your doctor reads everything, asks follow-ups, and actually thinks about it.


### Photos


Good photos are genuinely diagnostic for hair loss. Here's what your dermatologist wants to see:


- Top of the scalp in bright, overhead light. Part your hair down the center and photograph the part line.
- Hairline, temples, and crown separately. Different patterns of loss point to different diagnoses.
- Close-ups of any areas where you see thinning, scarring, redness, or flaking. Scalp inflammation and seborrhea can be contributing factors that change the plan.


If you have older photos from before you started minoxidil, those are gold. Sometimes stabilization happened and you didn't recognize it because you were expecting regrowth.


### Prior records


If you've had labs drawn before, or if another doctor has notes on your hair loss, your FutureClinic doctor can pull those through Health Information Exchanges. This saves you from repeating tests you've already done and gives your doctor a fuller picture without starting from zero.


### Labs


Not every case of hair loss needs labs. If the pattern is classic androgenetic alopecia in a young man with a strong family history, the diagnosis is clinical and labs won't change much. But when OTC minoxidil isn't doing what you expected, labs help rule out contributing factors that need their own treatment. A solid panel includes:


- Ferritin. This is the one that gets skipped the most. Your CBC can look completely normal while your ferritin is low enough to contribute to hair shedding. Ferritin below 50 ng/mL is associated with increased hair loss in multiple studies, even when you're not technically anemic \[per Trost et al., *J Am Acad Dermatol* , 2006\]. I check ferritin on nearly every hair-loss patient regardless of what the CBC says.
- TSH with reflex free T4. Thyroid dysfunction, both hypo and hyper, causes diffuse hair loss. It's common enough to be worth screening.
- Vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D). Low vitamin D is associated with telogen effluvium and may worsen androgenetic alopecia. It's frequently low, easy to correct, and worth checking.
- Zinc. Zinc deficiency causes telogen effluvium. It's less common than the others but shows up often enough that I include it.
- Total testosterone and free testosterone (especially in women with signs of excess androgen activity, or in men where the pattern doesn't quite fit classic male-pattern loss). In women, DHEA-S may also be relevant if there are signs of hormonal imbalance.
- ANA (only if there's visible scarring, smooth patches without follicular openings, or other features that suggest an autoimmune or scarring alopecia). This isn't routine for androgenetic alopecia, but it matters when the pattern doesn't fit.


Each of these has a reason. Ordering all of them blindly is unnecessary for every patient, but skipping ferritin and vitamin D when someone's not responding to minoxidil is a common miss.


## What red flags change the investigation?


None of the following are emergencies, but each one shifts what your dermatologist should be looking at:


- Smooth, well-defined bald patches with no visible follicle openings. This pattern suggests alopecia areata, not androgenetic alopecia. Minoxidil can help with alopecia areata, but the treatment approach and expectations are different.
- Scalp scarring, redness, or tenderness in areas of hair loss. Scarring alopecias (like lichen planopilaris or frontal fibrosing alopecia) destroy follicles permanently. These need a different investigation and sometimes a biopsy to confirm.
- Sudden, diffuse shedding that started after a specific event (illness, surgery, crash diet, stopping birth control, postpartum). This is likely telogen effluvium layered on top of whatever else is happening. The good news: telogen effluvium is usually temporary. The treatment plan changes when you know it's in the mix.
- Hair loss with systemic symptoms like fatigue, joint pain, weight changes, or new skin rashes. This pulls in thyroid, autoimmune, and connective-tissue considerations. Your lab panel expands.


Each of these changes the next step your dermatologist takes on the chat consultation, from adjusting the lab panel to recommending in-person evaluation for the small subset of cases that need it.


## What should you do first, and what should you keep monitoring?


If you're currently on OTC minoxidil and not seeing results, the first move is getting the history and application technique reviewed. That alone resolves a surprising number of "non-responder" cases. A lot of patients are applying once a day instead of twice, using foam on a wet scalp, or only treating the crown when the frontal scalp also needs coverage.


If the history and technique check out, labs are the next step. Start with ferritin, TSH, vitamin D, and zinc. If those come back normal and the pattern is consistent with androgenetic alopecia, the conversation shifts to treatment escalation rather than further testing.


Keep monitoring: take the same set of scalp photos every three months under the same lighting. Progress in hair loss treatment is slow and hard to see day to day. Standardized photos are the only reliable way to track change.


## What are the most common mistakes I see?


### Quitting because of shedding


This one drives me crazy. Shedding in the first few weeks to months of minoxidil is the most promising sign there is. It means the medicine is pushing resting hairs out of the way so growth-phase hairs can replace them. The bigger the shed, the better the response tends to be. When patients quit during the shed, they lose the window where the medicine was actually working. I never let a patient stop over shedding if I can help it.


### Only applying to the vertex


The OTC box says to apply to the crown. A lot of guys take that literally and skip the frontal hairline and temples entirely. Minoxidil works on the frontal scalp too. If your thinning includes the hairline, you should be treating it.


### Applying to wet hair


Putting minoxidil on a soaking wet scalp right after a shower dilutes absorption. Your scalp should be dry or at most towel-dried before you apply. Small detail, real difference in how much active ingredient reaches the follicle.


### Skipping ferritin because the CBC was normal


I check ferritin on every hair-loss patient, even when the CBC is completely normal. A normal hemoglobin does not mean your iron stores are adequate for hair growth. Other dermatologists may not include ferritin routinely, and that's a legitimate difference in practice. But in my experience, catching a low ferritin and correcting it makes a noticeable difference in patients who weren't responding to minoxidil alone.


### Expecting regrowth when stabilization already happened


A lot of patients come in convinced minoxidil "did nothing" when their comparison photos show significant stabilization. They expected thick new growth, and instead they got a halt in the loss. Stabilization is a real result. If your hair loss stopped progressing, the medicine is working. Regrowth is the bonus, not the baseline expectation.

## Frequently asked questions


### Can my FutureClinic doctor order the labs I need for a hair-loss investigation?


Yes. Your doctor can order the full lab panel through the chat consultation. You'll get a requisition for a lab near you, complete the draw, and your doctor reviews the results with you over chat.


### How long should I try OTC minoxidil before deciding it's not working?


Give it at least six months of consistent, twice-daily use before making a judgment. You may see early signs of fine vellus hairs at three months if you look closely, but noticeable density improvement usually takes six months. If you've been inconsistent, the clock resets.


### Is the shedding from minoxidil permanent?


No. The shedding is temporary. It typically lasts a few weeks and reflects resting hairs being replaced by growth-phase hairs. The hairs that shed were going to fall out regardless. The new hairs coming in behind them are the ones you want.


### What if my labs all come back normal and minoxidil still isn't working?


Then the conversation shifts to treatment escalation. Options include switching to oral minoxidil (which is more effective and much easier to use consistently), adding an antiandrogen like finasteride or dutasteride, compounding a higher-strength topical, or combining multiple approaches. Your doctor walks through which combination makes sense for your specific situation.


### Do I need to see someone in person for a hair-loss investigation?


In most cases, no. A detailed history, good scalp photos, and the right labs cover the diagnostic territory for the majority of hair-loss patterns. In-person evaluation becomes relevant only when there's suspicion of a scarring alopecia that needs a biopsy, and your FutureClinic doctor can tell you if that applies to your case.


### Should I be using any supplements alongside minoxidil?


Biotin can make hair grow slightly faster, but it doesn't reverse hair loss on its own. Correcting actual deficiencies (iron, vitamin D, zinc) matters more than adding supplements on top of adequate levels. The prescription options are both cheaper and more effective than most over-the-counter hair-loss supplements.


### Can I use minoxidil on my frontal hairline, not just the crown?


Yes. Despite what the OTC packaging says, minoxidil can be applied to the frontal scalp and temples. If you're thinning in those areas, you should be treating them.
