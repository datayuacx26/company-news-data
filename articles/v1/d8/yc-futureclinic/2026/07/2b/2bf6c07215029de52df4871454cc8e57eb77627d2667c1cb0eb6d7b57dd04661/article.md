## The decision in one paragraph


For most men dealing with androgenetic alopecia, oral finasteride is the stronger choice. It lowers DHT across your entire scalp, compliance is simpler with a daily pill, and the evidence base is deeper. Topical finasteride is a reasonable starting point if side-effect anxiety is genuinely holding you back from starting treatment at all. It does reduce systemic DHT exposure compared to oral, though it doesn't eliminate it. The factor that moves the needle most isn't potency on paper. It's whether you'll actually stay on the medication long enough to see results, and what's standing between you and consistency.


---


## What does each one actually do?


### Oral finasteride


Oral finasteride blocks the enzyme (5-alpha reductase) that converts testosterone into DHT, dihydrotestosterone. DHT is the hormone that shrinks hair follicles in androgenetic alopecia, miniaturizing them over years until they stop producing visible hair. When you take finasteride by mouth, it lowers DHT levels systemically, in your bloodstream, throughout your body. That means every follicle on your scalp benefits, including areas you might not have noticed thinning yet. It also means surrounding hair improves in quality and thickness, which makes thinning areas less obvious even before the thin spots themselves fully recover.


### Topical finasteride


Topical finasteride uses the same molecule, applied directly to the scalp. The goal is to block DHT conversion locally, at the level of the skin and follicle where you apply it, while limiting how much of the drug enters your bloodstream. In theory, that means you get the hair benefit with less systemic DHT suppression and a reduced chance of sexual side effects. In practice, there is still systemic absorption. It's not a perfectly local treatment. Some of the drug gets into your bloodstream regardless of how carefully you apply it.

## Where does the evidence land?


Oral finasteride has decades of data behind it. It's FDA-approved for androgenetic alopecia at 1 mg daily, and the clinical trial evidence is extensive and consistent. It reduces hair loss progression and produces measurable regrowth in the majority of men who take it \[per FDA prescribing information for Propecia\].


Topical finasteride has a smaller and more recent evidence base. Some head-to-head studies suggest similar improvement in hair counts between topical and oral formulations, and somewhat lower serum DHT suppression with topical, which is the basis for the "fewer side effects" claim. But most topical finasteride in the US comes from compounding pharmacies, not from a standardized FDA-approved product. That means the vehicle, concentration, and quality control vary enormously depending on where it's compounded. The studies that show comparable efficacy were done with specific formulations under controlled conditions, not necessarily the same bottle you'd get from an online pharmacy.


What I see in practice lines up with this gap. Oral finasteride just produces better, more consistent results across the scalp. The topical version can work, but the variability in compounding makes it harder to predict what any individual patient is actually getting.

## What are the side effects, and how are they managed?


This is the section most comparisons online either skip or sensationalize. Neither is helpful.


### Oral finasteride


- Decreased libido is the most commonly reported side effect. In clinical trials, it occurred in roughly 1–2% of men taking 1 mg daily, which is only slightly higher than placebo \[per the original Propecia phase III trials\].
- Erectile changes are reported at a similar low rate.
- Gynecomastia, breast tissue tenderness or development, is rare but documented.
- The anxiety factor is real. A lot of younger men start finasteride already hyper-aware of every fluctuation in libido or sexual function. The problem is that libido and erectile quality genuinely vary day to day. That's normal physiology. Once you're watching for side effects, every normal dip feels like confirmation. That's not the medication. That's monitoring bias. Having a doctor who can walk you through what's normal variation versus what's actually a side effect makes a significant difference.


### Topical finasteride


- The same side effects are possible, because there is systemic absorption. The rates appear to be somewhat lower based on available data, but "somewhat lower" is the honest characterization, not "eliminated."
- Scalp irritation is an additional consideration depending on the vehicle used in compounding.
- The side-effect avoidance story is overstated. Brands that market topical finasteride as a "side-effect-free" version of finasteride are giving patients incomplete information. You're reducing systemic DHT suppression, not removing it.


### How side effects are managed over time


Side effects with finasteride, oral or topical, are not a one-and-done conversation. If something comes up, the response isn't to panic and stop. It's to talk to your doctor, figure out whether what you're experiencing is actually the medication or normal variation, and adjust. That might mean lowering the dose, switching routes (oral to topical or vice versa), or in some cases just giving it more time. Most side effects that do occur are reversible when the medication is adjusted or stopped.

## What's it like to use each one day-to-day?


### Oral finasteride


- One pill, once a day. That's it.
- Widely available as a generic. Easy to fill at any pharmacy.
- Very forgiving if you miss a dose. DHT suppression doesn't collapse overnight. Missing a day here and there doesn't reset your progress.
- Compliance is high because there's nothing to apply, no mess, no waiting for it to dry.


### Topical finasteride


- Applied to the scalp once daily, usually with a dropper or spray.
- Requires compounding in most cases, since there's no widely available FDA-approved topical finasteride product in the US. That means quality, vehicle, and concentration depend on which pharmacy compounds it.
- Can be combined with minoxidil in the same solution, which is a genuine practical advantage: two active ingredients, one application step. If you're already using topical minoxidil, adding finasteride to the same bottle simplifies the routine instead of adding to it.
- Messier and more time-consuming than swallowing a pill. You need to apply it to the right areas, let it absorb, and deal with the texture of whatever vehicle the pharmacy used.
- Compliance tends to be lower in my experience, because the application step adds friction that a pill doesn't.

## Who fits each one?


-


**You want maximum efficacy and you're not paralyzed by side-effect anxiety.** Oral finasteride, 1 mg daily. It's simpler, better studied, and it treats the entire scalp, not just the spots you'd think to apply a topical to. This is my default for most men with androgenetic alopecia.


-


**You're already on topical minoxidil and want to add finasteride without adding another step.** Topical finasteride compounded into your minoxidil solution is a practical choice. You get two active ingredients in one application. The trade-off is somewhat lower efficacy compared to oral, and you're dependent on the quality of the compounding pharmacy.


-


**Your hair loss is early and you're not asking the medication to do heavy lifting.** Topical finasteride is an easier entry point when the bar for what you need from it is lower. If thinning is just starting at the temples or crown, the somewhat reduced efficacy of topical matters less.


-


**Side-effect anxiety is genuinely keeping you from starting treatment at all.** If the choice is between topical finasteride and no finasteride, topical wins every time. It does reduce systemic DHT exposure compared to oral, even if it doesn't eliminate it entirely. Starting something is better than researching forever and starting nothing.


-


**You've been on oral finasteride and you're convinced you're experiencing side effects.** Switching to topical can resolve the anxiety piece, which is sometimes the actual problem, while keeping you on a DHT blocker. I've seen this arc many times. The topical version gives patients a way to stay consistent without the mental overhead.

## The honest take, and why it can change


I used to recommend topical finasteride pretty confidently as a way to get the benefits of finasteride while avoiding the side effects. I don't think that anymore.


My default for most men is oral finasteride, 1 mg daily. It's more effective in practice, compliance is better because a pill is simpler than a scalp application, and it improves hair quality across the entire scalp, not just where you'd think to apply a topical. Thin areas become less apparent not just because the hair there gets better, but because the surrounding hair gets better too. That's something most patients haven't considered. They think of topical vs oral as "same drug, different application." It's not. The route of delivery changes the scope of what the medicine can do.


The side-effect avoidance story for topical finasteride is overstated. There is systemic absorption. You're not eliminating the risk. You're reducing it somewhat while also reducing efficacy and adding the hassle of a messy application. That trade-off doesn't always make sense.


But I also want to be clear: this is my default, not a universal law. Other dermatologists default to topical first, and that's a legitimate approach. ## Frequently asked questions


### Can I switch from oral finasteride to topical (or vice versa)?


Yes, and it's common. Switching routes doesn't require a washout period. If oral finasteride is working but side-effect anxiety is weighing on you, moving to topical can maintain results while reducing the mental overhead. If topical isn't producing enough improvement, switching to oral is a straightforward escalation. Your doctor adjusts the plan based on how you're responding.


### Does topical finasteride really have fewer side effects than oral?


It appears to reduce systemic DHT suppression, which may lower the rate of sexual side effects, but it doesn't eliminate them. There is still systemic absorption with topical finasteride. The framing that topical is "side-effect-free" is marketing, not medicine. The risk is reduced, not removed.


### How long does finasteride take to work?


Expect to wait. Around month three, a dermatologist with a dermatoscope can usually see early improvement at the follicle level, fine vellus hairs forming. You probably won't see anything yet. By month six, you might start noticing less shedding or subtle improvement in photos. Month twelve is when results become hard to miss. If you quit at month four because nothing looks different, you're stopping right before the payoff.


### Is topical finasteride FDA-approved?


Not as a standalone product in the US. Most topical finasteride is obtained through compounding pharmacies, which means the vehicle, concentration, and quality vary depending on the pharmacy. There would be much better standardization and data if an FDA-approved commercial version existed the way it does for topical minoxidil. Until that happens, compounding quality is a real variable.


### Can I use topical finasteride and oral minoxidil together?


Yes. Combining a topical DHT blocker with oral minoxidil is a reasonable approach. The two medications work through different mechanisms: finasteride blocks DHT-driven miniaturization, minoxidil stimulates blood flow and extends the growth phase. Layering them can produce better results than either alone.


### What if finasteride doesn't work for me?


First, make sure you've given it enough time. The most common reason patients think finasteride "isn't working" is impatience. The second most common reason is that it did work, it stabilized their hair loss, but they expected dramatic regrowth. Stabilization is a win. If results are genuinely insufficient after 12 months of consistent use, the next step is usually layering in additional treatments like minoxidil, adjusting the dose, or exploring newer options as they become available.


### Will I need to take finasteride forever?


If you stop, DHT goes back to doing what it was doing before. Finasteride doesn't cure androgenetic alopecia. It manages it. Hair loss will resume if you discontinue. That's why the ongoing relationship with your doctor matters. It's not a one-time prescription. It's a treatment you stay on, and your doctor stays with you to adjust it over time.

## Bottom line


Oral finasteride is the stronger, simpler, better-studied option for most men with androgenetic alopecia. Topical finasteride has a role, especially when side-effect anxiety is the barrier to starting treatment, when hair loss is early, or when you want to combine it with topical minoxidil in one step. But the idea that topical is the "safe version" and oral is the "risky version" is an oversimplification that leads to worse decisions.


The part that matters more than which route you pick is whether you stick with it long enough to see results, and whether you have a doctor who adjusts the plan as your response unfolds.
