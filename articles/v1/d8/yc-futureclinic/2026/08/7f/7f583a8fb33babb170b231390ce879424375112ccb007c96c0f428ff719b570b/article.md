# New Mom? These Symptoms Are NOT Just "Normal" — An Internist Walks Through What Gets Missed

If you've been up at 3 a.m. Googling "is it normal to feel this exhausted postpartum" and every result tells you some version of "yes, welcome to motherhood," I want you to know that answer is incomplete. Postpartum exhaustion is real. But postpartum exhaustion that's getting *worse* at 8 or 10 weeks, hair that's coming out in clumps, a heart that races when you're sitting still, a mood that feels less like "tired" and more like "gone"? That's not just the price of admission. That's your body asking you to check. And the fact that you're here, searching, means part of you already knows something might be off. ## What's the short answer for the most common case?


Most new moms experiencing symptoms that feel "worse than expected" postpartum are dealing with one of four things: iron-deficiency anemia (especially after any significant blood loss during delivery), postpartum thyroiditis, postpartum depression or anxiety, or some combination of all three. These conditions overlap almost perfectly with "normal new mom tired," which is exactly why they get missed. The difference between normal postpartum recovery and something that needs a doctor isn't how the symptoms feel. It's whether they're improving over time or getting worse. Normal recovery trends better. Pathology trends worse or stays stuck. You can't tell which one you're in without checking.


## What does "not feeling right" postpartum actually mean?


New moms describe it differently. Some say "I'm tired, but it's not sleep-deprivation tired. It's a different kind of tired." Some say "my heart races for no reason" or "I'm losing more hair than seems right" or "I don't feel like myself and I can't explain why." Some say "I feel anxious all the time, not just worried, like something terrible is about to happen." And some say nothing at all, because everyone around them has been saying "that's just how it is now."


What people call "not feeling right" postpartum usually maps to one or more of these clinical realities: fatigue that's disproportionate to sleep loss, mood changes that go beyond adjustment, physical symptoms (hair loss, palpitations, temperature intolerance, swelling) that don't fit the expected recovery pattern, or anxiety that's interfering with daily functioning or bonding. The problem is that every single one of these can also be part of normal postpartum recovery. The biology that makes recovery hard is the same biology that creates the window for real pathology. You can't separate them by how they feel. You separate them by checking.


## What are the most common causes, and how do they typically present?


-


**Iron-deficiency anemia.** After delivery, especially if there was any significant bleeding, even a "normal" amount, iron stores can be depleted well beyond what a prenatal vitamin repletes. The fatigue from anemia is indistinguishable from the fatigue of sleep deprivation unless you check a lab. The distinguishing clue: it doesn't improve with rest. You could sleep eight straight hours (unlikely, but hypothetically) and still wake up feeling drained. Dizziness, shortness of breath going up stairs, and feeling cold when others don't are other signals.


-


**Postpartum thyroiditis.** This is inflammation of the thyroid gland triggered by the immune system recalibrating after pregnancy. It can swing between a hyperthyroid phase (anxiety, racing heart, weight loss, feeling hot) and a hypothyroid phase (fatigue, hair loss, weight gain, feeling cold, brain fog). It typically shows up between 2 and 6 months postpartum. Every one of those symptoms gets attributed to sleep deprivation and "adjusting." It affects roughly 5 to 10% of postpartum women \[per the American Thyroid Association\], and most of them never get diagnosed because nobody checks.


-


**Postpartum depression and anxiety.** Depression can look like sadness, but it can also look like numbness, irritability, loss of interest in things you used to care about, or a persistent feeling of being underwater. Postpartum anxiety is its own entity. The kind where you can't put the baby down because you're convinced something terrible will happen, where you're checking breathing every 10 minutes, where intrusive thoughts consume you. That is not "normal new mom worry." That is a condition, and it responds to treatment. Roughly 1 in 7 new mothers experiences postpartum depression \[per ACOG\], and postpartum anxiety may be even more common.


-


**Combined presentations.** In practice, these rarely show up in isolation. A woman with depleted iron and a destabilized thyroid is going to have worse mood symptoms. A woman with untreated postpartum depression is going to feel more fatigued. The overlap is part of why a single lab draw can change the entire picture.


## What are the less common causes that still get missed?


-


**Postpartum cardiomyopathy.** This is the rare but serious one. The heart muscle weakens, usually in the last month of pregnancy or the first few months after delivery. It can present as shortness of breath, swelling in the legs or feet, and fatigue, all of which get written off as "deconditioning" or "you just had a baby." The distinguishing feature: shortness of breath that's worsening, especially at rest or with minimal activity, and swelling that's new or getting worse rather than resolving. It's uncommon, but when it's missed, the consequences are significant \[per the American Heart Association\].


-


**Postpartum preeclampsia.** Most people think preeclampsia only happens during pregnancy. It can develop up to 6 weeks after delivery, sometimes later. Severe headache that won't go away, visual changes (blurriness, seeing spots), upper abdominal pain, and elevated blood pressure are the signals. This gets missed because the postpartum visit is at 6 weeks and the assumption is that delivery resolved the risk.


-


**Vitamin D deficiency and B12 deficiency.** Less dramatic than the above, but they genuinely contribute to fatigue, mood changes, and brain fog postpartum. They're easy to check, easy to treat, and routinely overlooked because the standard postpartum lab panel doesn't always include them.


## Red flags that mean: don't wait


*If you have any of the following, this is an emergency. Go to the nearest emergency room or call 911. Don't start a chat consultation for these:*


- **Chest pain or severe shortness of breath at rest or with minimal activity.** Postpartum cardiomyopathy until proven otherwise.
- **Severe headache that won't resolve, especially with visual changes or elevated blood pressure.** Postpartum preeclampsia can happen weeks after delivery.
- **Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby.** This is a medical emergency, full stop, and there is no shame in it. Call 911 or the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
- **Heavy vaginal bleeding that soaks through a pad in an hour or less.**
- **High fever with chills.** Possible postpartum infection.
- **Sudden severe swelling or pain in one leg.** Postpartum blood clot risk is real.


None of these are "just adjusting."


*If you have any of the following, it's worth getting a doctor's eyes on it, and that's exactly what the chat consultation is for:*


- **Fatigue that's getting worse, not better, at 6 to 8 weeks postpartum.**
- **Hair loss that feels excessive or is accompanied by other symptoms** (fatigue, palpitations, cold intolerance).
- **Heart racing at rest without an obvious trigger.**
- **Persistent sadness, numbness, or anxiety that's interfering with daily life or bonding.**
- **Feeling cold when others don't, unexplained weight changes, or brain fog that feels disproportionate.**


## How do you figure out which one is yours?


These questions can help you narrow it:


-


**Is the fatigue improving, staying flat, or getting worse?** Normal postpartum fatigue gradually improves as sleep patterns stabilize. Fatigue that's worsening or stuck at 8 to 10 weeks is a signal.


-


**Did you have significant bleeding during or after delivery?** Even a "normal" amount of blood loss can deplete iron stores enough to cause symptoms. If you bled more than average, iron-deficiency anemia moves to the top of the list.


-


**Are you experiencing physical symptoms beyond fatigue?** Hair loss, heart palpitations, temperature sensitivity (feeling unusually hot or cold), unexplained weight changes. These point toward thyroid involvement.


-


**Is your mood different from "tired"?** There's a difference between "I'm exhausted and I wish I could sleep" and "I feel nothing" or "I can't stop the anxious thoughts." The second category is worth naming out loud.


-


**When did the symptoms start, and have they changed?** Thyroiditis often has a timeline: hyperthyroid symptoms (anxiety, racing heart) in the first 1 to 4 months, then hypothyroid symptoms (fatigue, hair loss, cold) at 4 to 8 months. If your symptoms shifted character, that's a clue.


-


**Are you having intrusive thoughts or checking behaviors?** Checking the baby's breathing every 10 minutes, being unable to let anyone else hold the baby, persistent images of something terrible happening. That's postpartum anxiety, not "being a good mom." It responds to treatment.


## What can you try at home?


Three specific things:


-


**If you had any significant bleeding during or after delivery, you probably need a dedicated iron supplement, not just the prenatal vitamin.** Take it with vitamin C (a glass of orange juice works) and away from coffee, tea, and calcium, which block absorption. If it makes you nauseous, try taking it every other day instead of daily. The absorption is nearly as good and the side effects are much more manageable.


-


**Track your mood honestly.** Even if it's just one word in a notes app on your phone each day. "Fine." "Terrible." "Numb." "Anxious." If you look back at two weeks and every day says something that worries you, that's data. Bring it to your doctor. It takes the pressure off trying to articulate everything in a single appointment or message.


-


**Say it out loud to someone.** Your partner, your mom, your friend, your doctor. "I don't feel right." The act of saying it out loud is the first step to getting it taken seriously. You are not being dramatic. You are not failing at motherhood. You might just need a blood test.


What you can't do at home: diagnose the cause. Home adjustments can support recovery, but they can't tell you whether your fatigue is iron, thyroid, depression, or something else. That takes labs and a conversation with a doctor.

## What will a doctor actually do with this?


The first step is history, and it's the most important one. A good postpartum evaluation starts with questions most new moms don't get asked: How are you sleeping beyond the baby's schedule? How is your mood, honestly? Are you having thoughts that scare you? Are you feeling like yourself? Is the fatigue getting better or worse? Did you have significant bleeding during delivery? Those questions change outcomes. They open the door for a woman who has been white-knuckling it in silence.


The labs that earn their keep in this picture: a CBC (to check hemoglobin and look for anemia), ferritin (iron stores, this is the one that catches iron deficiency before it shows up as full-blown anemia), and a thyroid panel, TSH with at least Free T4. Depending on the picture, vitamin D and B12 may be added. For mood, a validated screening tool like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale gives structure to a conversation that's hard to have.

The thing most postpartum content gets wrong is the framing. "Is this normal?" is the wrong question. The right question is: "Is this something I should check?" And the answer, almost always, is yes.


Just because something is common doesn't mean it's something you should push through. Postpartum thyroiditis is common. Postpartum anemia is common. Postpartum depression is common. "Common" doesn't mean "acceptable to ignore." It means there are millions of women walking around with treatable conditions because someone told them this is just what motherhood feels like.

## Frequently asked questions


**Is it normal to still feel this exhausted at 3 months postpartum?**


It can be, but "can be normal" and "should be investigated" aren't mutually exclusive. If your fatigue is the same or worse at 3 months as it was at 6 weeks, that's worth a lab check. A CBC, ferritin, and thyroid panel can rule in or rule out the most common treatable causes in one draw. It's better to check and have it come back normal than to assume everything is fine and miss something.


**Can postpartum hair loss be a sign of something besides hormones?**


Yes. Telogen effluvium, the hormonal hair shedding after pregnancy, is real and typically resolves by 6 to 12 months. But hair loss postpartum can also signal iron deficiency or thyroid dysfunction, and if you write it off as "just hormonal," you might miss something treatable that's making it worse or last longer. If hair loss is accompanied by fatigue, palpitations, or cold intolerance, a thyroid panel and ferritin level are worth checking \[per the American Thyroid Association\].


**How do I know if what I'm feeling is postpartum depression or just being tired?**


The overlap is real, and there's no clean line. But there are patterns that point toward depression: persistent sadness or numbness that doesn't lift with rest, loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, difficulty bonding with the baby, irritability that feels disproportionate, or intrusive thoughts. If you're asking the question, that itself is a signal worth bringing to a doctor. A screening tool like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale can help structure the conversation.


**Could my symptoms be misdiagnosed as "just postpartum adjustment"?**


This is one of the most common misdiagnosis patterns in postpartum care. Thyroid dysfunction, anemia, and depression all present with symptoms that overlap with normal recovery: fatigue, mood changes, hair changes, difficulty concentrating. The only way to separate them is to check. The default assumption that everything is "just adjusting" is what keeps treatable conditions undiagnosed for months.


**Is it normal that I don't feel bonded to my baby?**


It can be. Bonding isn't always instant. It doesn't always look like the movies. And it doesn't make you a bad mom. But if it's accompanied by persistent sadness, numbness, intrusive thoughts, or a feeling of disconnection that's getting worse rather than better, it may be postpartum depression, and that is treatable. Naming it is the bravest thing you can do, not a sign of failure \[per ACOG Committee Opinion No. 757\].


**Should I worry about my heart racing postpartum?**


Heart palpitations postpartum can be caused by dehydration, sleep deprivation, caffeine, or anxiety, all common. But they can also be a sign of postpartum thyroiditis (especially the early hyperthyroid phase) or, less commonly, a cardiac issue. If your heart races at rest, if it's getting more frequent, or if it comes with shortness of breath or chest tightness, that's worth checking. A thyroid panel is the first reasonable step.


**Can I get a real evaluation for this through chat, or do I need to go in person?**


For the vast majority of postpartum symptom investigations, a chat consultation covers what you need. History, lab orders, prescriptions, mood screening, and ongoing follow-up are all doable through chat, and the doctor stays with you over time, so if the first approach needs adjusting, you message and say so. In-person evaluation becomes the recommendation only if something in the picture raises concern for a cardiac issue or a presentation that needs hands-on assessment, and your doctor will tell you plainly if that's the case.
