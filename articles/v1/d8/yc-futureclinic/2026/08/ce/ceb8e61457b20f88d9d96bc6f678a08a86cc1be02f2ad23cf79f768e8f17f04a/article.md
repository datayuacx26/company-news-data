# What Tests Do You Actually Need Postpartum? An Internist's Guide to What Should Be Checked and Why

If your six-week postpartum visit ended with "everything looks fine, get some rest" and zero lab work, you didn't get a thorough evaluation. You got a quick check on the baby's delivery system. A real postpartum investigation covers specific labs, a careful symptom history, and a set of red flags that should never be brushed off as "just new-mom tired." I want to walk you through what I'd actually want to check, and why most of it can be done without dragging yourself and a newborn to an office.

## What are we actually checking for here?


This is about the diagnostic side: figuring out whether what you're feeling is normal postpartum recovery or something medical that's hiding behind it. We're not talking about treatment plans yet. We're talking about the tests and the questions that separate "exhausted because you have a newborn" from "exhausted because your ferritin is in the single digits or your thyroid just destabilized." Get this part right and the next steps become obvious.


## What should a thorough postpartum investigation include?


### History: the part that matters more than people think


- **Delivery details.** How much blood did you lose? Was there a postpartum hemorrhage, even a "minor" one? Did you need any transfusions? The answer changes whether iron-deficiency anemia is likely or almost guaranteed.
- **Symptom trajectory.** This is the most important question: is what you're feeling getting better, staying the same, or getting worse? Normal postpartum fatigue gradually improves week over week. If you're more exhausted at 10 weeks than you were at 4, that's not adaptation. That's a signal.
- **Mood and mental health, asked directly.** "Are you having thoughts that scare you?" and "Do you feel like yourself?" are two questions that change outcomes. Most new moms won't volunteer this information because they've been told what they're feeling is just part of the adjustment. A good history asks anyway.
- **Family history.** Thyroid disease in a first-degree relative raises the probability of postpartum thyroiditis. A personal or family history of depression or anxiety raises the probability of postpartum mood disorders.
- **Medications and supplements.** Are you still on a prenatal? Are you taking a standalone iron supplement? Are you breastfeeding, and has anyone adjusted your supplement regimen accordingly?


Your FutureClinic doctor takes this history on the chat consultation, and the chat format actually helps here because you can think through your answers instead of trying to remember everything in a rushed 15-minute appointment while your baby is crying in the carrier.


### Labs: the actual panel


- **CBC (complete blood count).** Looks at hemoglobin and hematocrit, the basic screen for anemia. But a normal CBC does not rule out iron deficiency on its own, which is why the next test matters.
- **Ferritin.** This is your iron storage marker. You can have a normal hemoglobin and still be profoundly iron-depleted. Ferritin catches what the CBC misses. I want to see ferritin on every postpartum panel, especially if there was any significant bleeding. A ferritin in the single digits or teens explains fatigue that no amount of sleep will fix \[per WHO iron deficiency guidelines\].
- **TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) with Free T4.** Not just TSH alone. Postpartum thyroiditis affects roughly 5–10% of women in the first year after delivery \[per American Thyroid Association guidelines\]. It can swing between a hyperthyroid phase (anxiety, racing heart, weight loss) and a hypothyroid phase (crushing fatigue, hair loss, brain fog, depressed mood). Every one of those symptoms gets written off as sleep deprivation unless someone checks. Free T4 tells you what the thyroid is actually doing right now, not just what the pituitary is asking it to do.
- **Vitamin D.** Deficiency is common in the general population and more common postpartum, especially in women who were deficient during pregnancy. It contributes to fatigue, mood changes, and bone health, all things that matter when you're recovering and potentially breastfeeding.


### Validated screening tools, done through the chat


- **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS).** This is a 10-question validated screening tool specifically designed for postpartum depression and anxiety. It takes a few minutes to complete and it catches what casual conversation misses. A score above 10 warrants a deeper conversation; a score above 13 is a strong signal for clinical depression \[per ACOG Committee Opinion 757\]. This can be administered through the chat consultation. You answer the questions honestly, and the doctor interprets the pattern.


### Prior records


If you've had labs drawn at any point during pregnancy or at the six-week visit, your FutureClinic doctor can pull those through HIE (Health Information Exchange) so you're not starting from zero. Sometimes the answer is already sitting in a result from month 7 of pregnancy that nobody followed up on.


## What red flags change the investigation?


Some symptoms are never "just adjusting." These need to be separated clearly.


**These are emergencies. Go to the ER or call 911, not the chat:**


- **Chest pain or severe shortness of breath at rest or with minimal activity.** That's postpartum cardiomyopathy until proven otherwise. It can present weeks to months after delivery.
- **Severe headache that won't resolve, especially with visual changes or elevated blood pressure.** Postpartum preeclampsia can happen weeks after delivery. Most people don't realize that.
- **Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby.** This is an emergency, full stop, and there is no shame in it. Call 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), go to your nearest ER, or call 911.
- **Heavy bleeding that soaks through a pad in an hour or less.**
- **High fever with chills,** which could signal postpartum endometritis or another infection.
- **Sudden severe leg swelling or pain on one side.** Postpartum blood clot risk is real and elevated for weeks after delivery.


None of these are "just adjusting." Do not start a chat for these. Get emergency care.


**These are red flags that warrant prompt evaluation, and the chat consultation is the right place to start:**


- **Fatigue that's getting worse, not better, past 6–8 weeks.** Normal trajectory is gradual improvement. Worsening fatigue is a lab signal.
- **Heart racing at rest without an obvious trigger.** Could be thyroid. Could be anemia. Could be anxiety. All checkable.
- **Hair loss that feels disproportionate or isn't slowing down by 6 months postpartum.** Telogen effluvium is real and usually self-resolving, but hair loss can also be a sign of iron deficiency or thyroid dysfunction. If you just write it off as "hormonal," you might miss something treatable that's making it worse or making it last longer.
- **Persistent anxiety that's interfering with bonding or daily function.** Checking the baby's breathing every 10 minutes, unable to put the baby down, intrusive thoughts that won't stop. That's not "normal new-mom worry." That's a condition, and it responds to treatment.
- **Persistent swelling, especially in the legs or face, beyond the first few weeks.**


Each of these pairs with a concrete next step your doctor would take on the chat: ordering the right labs, screening for mood disorders, or having the conversation about whether something more needs to happen.


## What should you do first, and what can you keep monitoring?


**First priority:** If you had any significant bleeding during or after delivery, get a CBC and ferritin checked. Iron-deficiency anemia is the most common and most fixable thing hiding behind postpartum exhaustion, and it won't get better on its own without repletion.


**Same priority:** If your mood is consistently low, numb, or anxious in a way that feels bigger than situational stress, or if you're having intrusive thoughts, bring it up now. Not next month. Now. The EPDS screening takes minutes and opens the door to treatment that works.


**Within the first few months:** Get a thyroid panel (TSH + Free T4). Postpartum thyroiditis can present anywhere from 1 to 12 months after delivery. If you're at 8 weeks and your energy is tanking while your heart races for no reason, this is worth checking sooner rather than later.


**Ongoing monitoring:** Track your mood. Even if it's just one word a day in a notes app on your phone. Two weeks of "terrible" or "numb" is data you bring to your doctor. Track your energy trajectory: is it improving, flat, or declining? That trajectory is the most useful piece of information for distinguishing normal recovery from something medical.


## What are the most common postpartum investigation mistakes I see?


**1. Checking TSH alone and calling it a complete thyroid screen.** TSH is a screening test. It tells you what the pituitary is asking the thyroid to do. It doesn't tell you what the thyroid is actually producing right now. In postpartum thyroiditis, the TSH can lag behind what's actually happening. Free T4 fills that gap. Skipping it is the most common shortcut I see, and it misses the women who are actively symptomatic from thyroid dysfunction even when the TSH hasn't caught up yet.


**2. Skipping ferritin because the CBC came back normal.** This one genuinely frustrates me. A woman can have a hemoglobin of 12, technically "normal," and a ferritin of 8. She's profoundly iron-depleted. Her body is running on fumes. And because the CBC looked fine on paper, nobody dug deeper. Ferritin should be on every postpartum panel. I personally include it even when the hemoglobin is normal. Other doctors may not, and that's a legitimate difference in practice style. But I've seen too many women suffer for months with something a simple lab would have caught.


**3. Treating the six-week visit as the finish line.** The system gives new moms one postpartum visit and then essentially says "good luck." But postpartum thyroiditis can present at 3 months. Postpartum depression can present at 6 months. The body is still in upheaval well beyond week six. One visit is a starting point, not a conclusion.


**4. Attributing every symptom to sleep deprivation without checking.** Yes, you're sleep-deprived. Of course you are. But sleep deprivation doesn't cause your ferritin to drop to single digits. Sleep deprivation doesn't destabilize your thyroid. The overlap between "tired new mom" and "anemic or hypothyroid new mom" is almost perfect on symptoms alone. You can only separate them by running the labs. It's better to check and have everything come back normal than to assume and miss something that was treatable the whole time.


**5. Not asking about mood directly.** If you wait for a new mom to volunteer that she's having intrusive thoughts or that she doesn't feel bonded to her baby, you'll be waiting a long time. She's been conditioned to believe that struggling means failing. The questions have to be asked directly, without judgment, every time. "Are you having thoughts that scare you?" is a question that changes outcomes.

## Frequently asked questions


### What blood tests should I ask for after having a baby?


At minimum: a CBC, ferritin, TSH with Free T4, and vitamin D. If you had significant bleeding during or after delivery, iron studies become even more important. If you have symptoms of a mood disorder, the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale is a validated screening tool your doctor can walk you through. These aren't exotic tests. They're basic labs that catch the most common conditions hiding behind "normal new-mom tired."


### How do I know if my fatigue is normal postpartum exhaustion or something medical?


Trajectory. Normal postpartum fatigue gradually improves as you and the baby settle into a rhythm (even if slowly). If your fatigue is flat or getting worse at 6, 8, 10 weeks, or if it feels disproportionate to your sleep situation, that's a signal to check labs. You can't tell the difference by how it feels. You can only tell by checking.


### Can postpartum thyroid problems really cause all of these symptoms?


Yes. Postpartum thyroiditis can cause fatigue, hair loss, heart palpitations, mood changes, weight changes, brain fog, and anxiety. It affects roughly 5–10% of women in the first year after delivery. Every one of those symptoms gets attributed to sleep deprivation and stress unless someone checks a thyroid panel.


### Is it normal that I don't feel bonded to my baby?


It can be. Bonding isn't always instant, and it doesn't always look like the movies. But if the lack of bonding is accompanied by persistent sadness, numbness, or intrusive thoughts, it might be postpartum depression, and that is treatable. You are not a bad mom for feeling that way. You might just need help, and that's okay.


### Do I really need to go in for an appointment, or can this be done through chat?


The history, the lab orders, the mood screening, the follow-up: all of that can be done through a chat consultation. You don't need to find childcare, drive to an office, and sit in a waiting room with a newborn to get these answers. Your doctor reviews your history, orders the labs, interprets the results, and starts treatment if needed, all through the chat, on your schedule. If something comes back that genuinely needs in-person evaluation, your doctor will tell you and help you figure out the next step.


### When should I worry about postpartum hair loss?


Some hair shedding after delivery is expected. Telogen effluvium is a real physiological process triggered by the hormonal shift, and it usually resolves on its own by 6–12 months. But if the shedding feels severe, isn't slowing down, or is accompanied by fatigue or other symptoms, it's worth checking ferritin and thyroid function. Iron deficiency and thyroid dysfunction can both worsen or prolong hair loss that would otherwise have resolved.


### How long after delivery can postpartum depression or anxiety show up?


It can present anytime in the first year, though it most commonly shows up in the first 3–6 months. Postpartum anxiety specifically, the kind with intrusive thoughts, hypervigilance, and an inability to relax even when the baby is safe, often gets missed because it doesn't look like the "classic" image of postpartum depression. Both respond to treatment. Both are worth screening for.
