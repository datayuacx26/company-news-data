# Postpartum Symptoms, Explained: A Beginner's Guide from an Internal Medicine Doctor

If you're a few weeks (or a few months) postpartum and something feels off, but everyone around you keeps saying "that's just how it is now," this article is for you. Postpartum recovery is one of those things doctors study for years and then explain to new moms in about ninety seconds between the discharge paperwork and the car seat instructions. The fatigue, the hair changes, the racing heart, the mood shifts. Some of it genuinely is part of recovery. But some of it isn't, and the only way to tell the difference is to actually check. This is the plain-language version of what I wish every new mom heard before she left the hospital.

## What you actually need to know


-


**Your body just went through one of the most dramatic hormonal shifts it will ever experience.** Estrogen and progesterone crash after delivery. Thyroid function can destabilize. Iron stores may be depleted. All of that is happening while you're sleep-deprived and physically healing.


-


**Many postpartum symptoms overlap almost perfectly with real medical conditions.** Fatigue, hair loss, heart palpitations, mood changes. These can be normal recovery, but they can also be signs of postpartum thyroiditis, iron-deficiency anemia, or postpartum depression. You can't separate them by how they feel. You can only separate them by checking.


-


**The system gives you one postpartum visit at six weeks and acts like that's enough.** It isn't. Postpartum thyroiditis can show up at three months. Postpartum depression can show up at six months. Your body doesn't stop changing at the six-week mark just because your follow-up appointment happened.


-


**Struggling postpartum is not a character flaw.** It's often a medical condition. Naming it isn't weakness. It's the most useful thing you can do for yourself and your baby.


-


**A few basic labs can answer the question "is this normal or not" in days, not months.** A complete blood count, a thyroid panel, and a validated depression screening tool cover most of what hides behind "just being a new mom."

## What's actually going on in your body after delivery


During pregnancy, your estrogen and progesterone levels climb higher than they've ever been. After you deliver, they crash. That crash is one of the most abrupt hormonal shifts in human biology. Your thyroid, which was working overtime to support the pregnancy, can swing in either direction: overactive for a while, then underactive, or just underactive from the start. Your blood volume, which expanded by nearly 50% during pregnancy, is shifting back. If you had any significant bleeding during or after delivery, your iron stores may be genuinely depleted.


All of this is happening while you're sleeping in fragments, physically recovering from labor or surgery, and learning how to keep a newborn alive. So yes, fatigue, mood swings, hair shedding, feeling like your heart is racing: those can absolutely be part of normal recovery.


But here's the part that matters. That same hormonal upheaval is also the exact environment where postpartum thyroiditis, iron-deficiency anemia, postpartum depression, and even postpartum cardiomyopathy develop. The symptoms of "normal tired new mom" and "something is actually wrong" look almost identical from the outside. The biology that makes recovery hard is the same biology that opens the window for real pathology.


The way you tell the difference is not by pushing through and seeing if it gets better. The way you tell the difference is by checking.


## The terms you're going to hear


-


**Postpartum thyroiditis:** Inflammation of the thyroid gland after delivery that can cause it to release too much thyroid hormone at first, then too little. That leads to fatigue, hair loss, heart palpitations, and mood changes that get blamed on sleep deprivation.


-


**Ferritin:** The protein that stores iron in your body. This is the number that tells you how depleted your iron reserves actually are, not just whether you're technically anemic right now.


-


**CBC (complete blood count):** A basic blood test that checks your red blood cells, hemoglobin, and other markers. It's the first thing ordered when a doctor suspects anemia.


-


**TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone):** The screening blood test for thyroid function. But it's not always enough on its own postpartum, which is why a full thyroid panel (including Free T4) matters.


-


**Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS):** A short, validated questionnaire used to screen for postpartum depression and anxiety. It's the kind of tool that turns "I feel off" into something measurable and actionable.


-


**Telogen effluvium:** The medical term for the hair shedding that happens a few months after delivery when a large number of hair follicles shift into their resting phase at once. Usually temporary, but sometimes worsened by low iron or thyroid problems.


-


**Postpartum cardiomyopathy:** A rare but serious condition where the heart muscle weakens in the last month of pregnancy or the first few months after delivery. It can cause shortness of breath, swelling, and fatigue that gets written off as being out of shape.


-


**SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor):** The most commonly prescribed class of medication for postpartum depression. Typically takes two to four weeks to start working, with full effect around six to eight weeks.


## What the typical path looks like


When a new mom brings up symptoms that could be more than normal recovery, the first step is usually straightforward: a CBC to check hemoglobin and iron stores, a thyroid panel (TSH and Free T4 at minimum), and a depression screening with a validated tool like the EPDS.


What matters most in the conversation isn't the lab order. It's what your doctor listens for in your story. The question is trajectory: are things gradually getting better, or are they staying the same or getting worse at six, eight, ten weeks? Normal postpartum fatigue trends toward improvement. Fatigue that's getting worse, or a symptom that feels out of proportion to the sleep deprivation, that's the signal that labs are needed.


If labs come back showing low iron, treatment is usually aggressive repletion with oral iron (or IV iron if it's severe), and most women start feeling a difference in energy within two to four weeks. If the thyroid is off, levothyroxine for the hypothyroid phase typically brings improvement in fatigue and mood within four to six weeks. If the screening points toward postpartum depression, an SSRI is the most common starting point. Honest timeline: two to four weeks before you notice a shift, full effect at six to eight weeks.


Most of this can happen through a consultation without needing to physically get to a clinic. History, lab orders, prescriptions, follow-up. That matters when you're the one taking care of a newborn.


## What to actually do next


-


**If you had significant bleeding during or after delivery, ask about a dedicated iron supplement.** The prenatal vitamin you're still taking is probably not enough. Take iron with vitamin C for absorption, not with coffee or calcium, which block it.


-


**Track your mood, even if it's just one word a day.** A notes app on your phone works. Write down how you felt. If you look back after two weeks and every day says "terrible" or "numb" or "scared," that's data. Bring it to your doctor.


-


**Pay attention to trajectory, not just how you feel today.** The question isn't "am I tired." Of course you're tired. The question is whether the fatigue, the mood, the physical symptoms are getting better over time or getting worse. Worse at eight weeks is not "adjusting." Worse at eight weeks is worth investigating.


-


**If something feels wrong, not just tired, but *wrong* , say it out loud.** To your partner, your mom, your doctor. The act of naming it is the first step to getting it taken seriously. You are not being dramatic. You are not failing at motherhood. You might just need a blood test.

## These are emergencies, not "adjusting"


If any of the following are happening, this is an emergency. Go to the nearest emergency room or call 911. Don't start a chat for these:


- **Chest pain or severe shortness of breath,** especially at rest or with minimal activity. That's postpartum cardiomyopathy until proven otherwise.
- **Severe headache that won't resolve, especially with visual changes or high blood pressure.** Postpartum preeclampsia can happen weeks after delivery.
- **Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby.** This is an emergency, full stop, and there is no shame in it.
- **Heavy bleeding that soaks through a pad in an hour or less.**
- **High fever with chills.**
- **Sudden severe swelling or pain in one leg.** Postpartum blood clot risk is real.


None of these are "just adjusting." If you're experiencing something that feels urgent but doesn't match this list, like shortness of breath that's mild but persistent, anxiety that's interfering with your ability to care for your baby, fatigue that's genuinely getting worse, that's worth getting in front of a doctor. And that's exactly what the chat consultation is for.


## Common mistakes new moms make


-


**Assuming the prenatal vitamin covers iron repletion.** If you lost a meaningful amount of blood during delivery, the iron in your prenatal is maintenance-level, not repletion-level. It's like trying to fill a pool with a garden hose when someone left the drain open.


-


**Stopping an SSRI in the first two weeks because it made things feel worse.** Many SSRIs cause a temporary increase in anxiety or a "flat" feeling before they start working. If nobody warned you that was expected, it feels like the medication is wrong, when really it just hasn't kicked in yet.


-


**Writing off hair loss as "just hormonal" without checking labs.** Telogen effluvium is real and it does resolve on its own. But postpartum hair loss can also be worsened by low iron or thyroid dysfunction, and if you just wait it out without checking, you might be waiting out something that has a treatable cause.


-


**Waiting for the six-week postpartum visit to bring up symptoms.** If something feels wrong at three weeks, you don't need to wait for a scheduled appointment to say so. And if the six-week visit already happened and you didn't bring it up, it's not too late.


-


**Confusing postpartum anxiety with "normal new-mom worry."** Checking the baby's breathing every ten minutes, being unable to put the baby down because you're convinced something terrible will happen, intrusive thoughts that won't stop. That's not standard-issue worry. That's a condition, and it responds to treatment.


## Frequently asked questions


### Is it normal that I don't feel bonded to my baby?


It can be. Bonding isn't always instant, and it doesn't always look like the movies. Many women describe feeling more like a caretaker than a parent for the first few weeks or months. If that feeling is accompanied by persistent sadness, numbness, or intrusive thoughts, it may be postpartum depression, and that's treatable. You are not a bad mom for feeling this way.


### How do I know if my fatigue is "normal tired" or something medical?


Trajectory is the giveaway. Normal postpartum fatigue gradually improves, even if slowly. If your fatigue is staying the same or getting worse at six to ten weeks postpartum, or if it feels disproportionate to how much sleep you're actually losing, that's worth a lab check. A CBC and thyroid panel can answer the question in a few days.


### Can postpartum depression start months after delivery?


Yes. Postpartum depression can develop any time in the first year, and sometimes beyond. The six-week visit is not the only window. If you're at four months or seven months and something doesn't feel right, it still counts and it still deserves attention.


### Is postpartum hair loss always temporary?


Telogen effluvium, the shedding that happens when a large number of hair follicles shift into their resting phase, usually resolves on its own within six to twelve months. But if your hair loss is severe, prolonged, or accompanied by fatigue and other symptoms, it's worth checking ferritin and thyroid levels. Low iron and thyroid dysfunction can make postpartum hair loss worse and last longer than it should.


### Should I stop breastfeeding if I need medication?


Not necessarily. Many common postpartum medications, including most SSRIs, levothyroxine, and iron supplements, are compatible with breastfeeding. This is a conversation to have with your doctor about your specific situation, because the answer depends on the medication, the dose, and your baby's age. A doctor who knows your full picture can walk through the options with you through a chat consultation.


### Isn't some anxiety just part of being a new parent?


Some vigilance is normal and even protective. But postpartum anxiety disorder, the kind that interferes with your ability to function, sleep when the baby sleeps, or put the baby down, is not "just part of it." It's a condition with effective treatments. Normalizing it as standard motherhood keeps women from getting help they deserve.


### What if my six-week visit already happened and I didn't mention any of this?


It's not too late. The six-week visit is a starting point, not a deadline. If symptoms have developed or worsened since then, you can bring them up at any time. You don't need to wait for your next annual appointment to say "something doesn't feel right."


### Why didn't my OB catch this?


The postpartum visit is often brief, focused on physical recovery from delivery, and scheduled at a point when many of these conditions haven't fully declared themselves yet. It's not that your OB didn't care. The system is designed around one visit at six weeks, and a lot of postpartum pathology shows up after that window closes. That's a gap in how we do postpartum care, and it's one of the things that needs to change.
