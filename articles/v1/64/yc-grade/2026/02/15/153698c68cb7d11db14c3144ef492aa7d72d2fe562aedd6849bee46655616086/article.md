# How to Onboard Contractors in 24 Hours (Not 2 Weeks)

We hired a frontend contractor in Colombia last year. She was available to start immediately, which was the whole point — we needed someone fast. Then we spent 11 days getting her access to our repos, setting up payments, collecting tax forms, and writing onboarding docs we should have had ready.


By day 12, she'd already taken another gig. Can't blame her.


Contractor onboarding shouldn't take two weeks. If your process takes longer than a day, you're either over-engineering it or you don't have a process at all. Both are fixable.


## The problem with copying your employee playbook


Most founders default to whatever HR onboarding flow they've seen before. A welcome email, a company handbook, a week of "getting settled in," maybe a buddy system.


Contractors don't need any of that. They're not joining your company — they're doing a job. The faster they can start the actual work, the better it is for everyone. A contractor sitting through orientation slides about company values is a contractor billing you to be bored.


The goal is simple: get them from "signed agreement" to "shipping work" as fast as possible.


## What actually needs to happen in the first 24 hours


Here's what a contractor needs to start working. Not what would be nice — what's actually required.


### Hour 1-2: Agreement and payment setup


Before anything else, handle the money. This is where most startups stall.


You need a signed contractor agreement (keep it short — 2-3 pages max), a W-9 or W-8BEN depending on their location, and payment details. If you're collecting bank info manually and setting up wire transfers for each person, that's your bottleneck right there.


We built Grade partly because this step used to take us days. Now we add a contractor with their email, they pick their own payment method, and we move on. The whole thing takes about 10 minutes instead of a week of back-and-forth about SWIFT codes.


### Hour 2-4: Access and tools


Give them exactly the access they need. Not more, not less.


For a developer, that's usually: repository access (scoped to relevant repos only), a Slack or Discord channel for communication, project management tool (Linear, Jira, whatever you use), and any staging or dev environment credentials.


For a UGC creator: brand guidelines doc, asset library access (logos, product images), communication channel, and brief template.


Don't make them request access to things one by one. Have a checklist for each contractor type and run through it in one sitting.


### Hour 4-8: First task, not first meeting


Skip the introductory call where everyone says their name and role. Instead, give them a real task on day one.


Pick something small but meaningful — a bug fix, a component build, a single piece of content. Something they can finish in a few hours that gives you signal on their work quality and gives them a win.


The best onboarding is doing the work. Everything else is overhead.


### Hour 8-24: Feedback loop


Review their first deliverable quickly. Within hours, not days. This does two things: it shows them you're engaged and responsive, and it lets you course-correct early if something's off.


If the first task goes well, hand them the next one. If it doesn't, you've lost a day, not a month.


## The three things most companies forget


### 1. Payment terms should be clear before work starts


"We'll figure out payments later" is how you lose good people. Contractors have heard this before, and it usually means they'll chase invoices for weeks. Spell out the rate, payment schedule, and method on day one.


If you're paying per deliverable, define what "done" means. If you're paying hourly, agree on how hours are tracked. Don't leave room for awkward conversations later.


### 2. Communication expectations need to be explicit


Contractors don't know your team's norms. Do you expect them on Slack during business hours? Are async updates fine? How fast should they respond to messages? Should they join standup?


Write it down. Two sentences is enough: "We communicate async on Slack. Post daily updates in #project-name by end of your workday."


### 3. Documentation should exist before you hire


If a contractor needs to ask five questions before they can start, your documentation is the problem, not the contractor. Before you bring someone on:


- Is the project brief written?
- Are the technical specs or brand guidelines accessible?
- Can they find what they need without scheduling a call?


If not, write the docs first. It takes an afternoon and saves you days of back-and-forth with every new person you bring on.


## A simple onboarding checklist


Here's what we use internally. It fits on an index card, which is the point.


**Before they start:**


- Contractor agreement signed
- Payment method set up (we use Grade — add email, done)
- Tax form collected (W-9 or W-8BEN)
- Access provisioned (repos, tools, channels)
- Project brief or first task ready


**Day one:**


- Quick intro message (Slack, not a 30-minute call)
- Share communication expectations
- Assign first task
- Confirm they can access everything


**End of day one:**


- First deliverable submitted or in progress
- Any blockers identified and resolved


That's it. No welcome packet, no culture deck, no onboarding buddy. Just the things that let someone start working.


## Why speed matters more than thoroughness


Every day between "contractor says yes" and "contractor ships first thing" is a day they might take a different offer. Good contractors have options. The ones with the most options are usually the best.


Being fast also signals that you have your act together. A contractor who gets access, payment setup, and a first task within hours is going to feel good about working with you. One who's still waiting for a Slack invite on day four is going to assume you're disorganized about everything.


## What this looks like at scale


When you're managing 5 contractors, you can wing the onboarding. When you're managing 30, you can't. The checklist approach scales because it's the same steps every time, just with different access levels depending on the role.


The payment piece especially needs to be automated. Manually collecting bank details and setting up individual transfers for 30 people across 8 countries will consume someone's entire week, every month. Grade handles this — add their email, they set up their own payment, you pay everyone at once. But even if you use a different tool, automate the payment onboarding or it'll become your biggest time sink.


## The actual takeaway


Contractor onboarding is a logistics problem, not a cultural one. Strip it down to what someone needs to start working, and get them there in 24 hours. Everything else can happen after they've shipped their first piece of work.


The companies that hire the best contractors aren't the ones with the best onboarding decks. They're the ones who respect people's time enough to not waste it.
