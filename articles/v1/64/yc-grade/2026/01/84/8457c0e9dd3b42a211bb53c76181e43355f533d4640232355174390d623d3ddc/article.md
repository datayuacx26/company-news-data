# The UGC Creator Brief That Actually Gets You Good Content

I sent my first creator brief in a Slack DM. Three sentences. "Make videos about our app. Post daily. Be authentic." Two weeks later I had 14 videos I couldn't use.


The creator was talented. Her content looked good. But none of it explained what our app actually did. She'd filmed herself using a generic "productivity app" — never showed the features that mattered, never mentioned the problem we solved. She thought she was being creative. I thought I'd been clear. We were both wrong.


That $800 was a lesson in why vague briefs produce vague content.


## Most briefs fail because they're either too vague or too rigid


There's a spectrum. On one end: "Just make content about our app, you know what works." On the other: a 12-page document with shot-by-shot scripts, exact phrases to say, and mood boards.


Both fail.


Too vague and you get content that's technically "about" your product but misses everything that matters. Creators fill the gaps with guesses. Their guesses are usually wrong.


Too rigid and you get robotic content that performs terribly. TikTok audiences can smell scripted. If a creator is reading your words instead of speaking naturally, the algorithm buries it and viewers scroll past.


The sweet spot: constrain the message, free the delivery.


## What belongs in a brief (and what doesn't)


After running UGC campaigns for Pray Screen and now working with teams who manage dozens of creators, here's what actually needs to be in a brief:


**The problem you solve, in one sentence.** Not your features. The problem. "People waste hours on their phone and feel guilty about it." That's what the creator needs to communicate. How they communicate it is their job.


**One thing viewers should do.** Download the app. Visit the site. Use a promo code. One thing. If you list three CTAs, creators will pick the weakest one or mention all three so fast nobody catches any.


**What NOT to say.** This matters more than what to say. "Don't mention we have a free trial — we don't anymore." "Don't compare us to Headspace." "Don't say it's free if you're going to show premium features." The things creators get wrong are usually things you forgot to rule out.


**2-3 example videos that work.** Not from your brand — from other brands in adjacent spaces. "This video does a good job of showing the problem without being preachy." "Notice how she mentions the app in the first 3 seconds." Creators learn from examples faster than from instructions.


**What you're NOT looking for.** "We don't want talking-head videos." "No green screen." "Nothing that looks like an ad." Explicit anti-patterns save everyone time.


That's it. One page. Maybe two if you include screenshots.


## What to leave out


**Scripts.** Giving a creator a word-for-word script is paying for the worst of both worlds. You get none of their creativity and all of their awkwardness reading lines. If you want scripted content, hire actors.


**Detailed shot lists.** "Open on phone screen, zoom to app icon, cut to face, show reaction..." This isn't a brief, it's a production document. If you need this level of control, you need a video production company, not UGC creators.


**Brand voice guidelines.** Your brand voice document is for your marketing team. Creators have their own voice. That's why you hired them. Don't make them pretend to be your brand.


**Everything about your company.** Nobody needs your founding story, mission statement, or competitive landscape. A creator making a 30-second TikTok doesn't need to know you raised a Series A.


## The hook matters more than anything else


Here's the part most briefs miss entirely: the first 2 seconds.


Creators know TikTok. They know hooks matter. But they don't know YOUR audience's hooks. A creator who makes fitness content has great fitness hooks. That doesn't mean they know what makes someone stop scrolling for a productivity app.


Give them 3-5 hook options to test. Not scripts — concepts.


"Start with the guilt of checking screen time." "Open with the moment right before you pick up your phone out of habit." "Begin with someone trying and failing to be productive."


Let them execute these their way, but point them at what resonates with your audience. You have data they don't.


## Feedback rounds: do one, maybe two, then stop


First-time clients send 15 rounds of notes. The creator makes tiny changes each time. By round 8, everyone hates each other and the content is worse than round 2.


The agency we worked with, Plutus, had a rule: one round of feedback before posting, and it had to be specific. Not "make it more engaging" but "the CTA at 0:18 is getting cut off, move it earlier."


If a video needs three rounds of major changes, the brief failed. It's not the creator's fault you didn't tell them what you wanted.


Also: sometimes good enough is good enough. UGC is a volume game. 10 pretty-good videos beat 3 perfect ones. You don't know which one will pop until you post it.


## The brief template we actually use


**Product:** \[Name, one-line description\]


**The problem:** \[What pain point does this solve? One sentence.\]


**The action:** \[What should viewers do? One thing.\]


**Don't mention:** \[Thing 1\], \[Thing 2\]


**Don't do:** \[Anti-pattern 1\], \[Anti-pattern 2\]


**Hook ideas:** \[Hook concept 1\], \[Hook concept 2\], \[Hook concept 3\]


**Reference videos:** \[Link 1\] — why this works: \[one sentence\]


**Tracking link / promo code:** \[Link or code\]


That's the whole document. Everything else is a conversation.


## Testing hooks across creators


If you have multiple creators, don't give them all the same hook. Split test.


Creator A gets hooks about guilt. Creator B gets hooks about productivity hacks. Creator C gets hooks about screen addiction. After two weeks, you'll know which angle resonates.


Most brands give the same brief to everyone and wonder why results are inconsistent. The inconsistency IS the data. Use it.


## Iteration beats perfection


Your first brief will be wrong. You'll forget to mention something obvious. A creator will interpret a phrase differently than you meant. A reference video you loved will inspire content you hate.


That's fine. Update the brief after every campaign. Add the edge cases you discovered. Remove the sections nobody reads.


The teams that win at UGC aren't the ones with perfect briefs. They're the ones who iterate fast. Bad video? Figure out why, update the brief, try again next week.


## Tying it back to payment


One thing we learned at Pray Screen: if your brief is unclear, you'll fight about payment later.


Creator delivers content you can't use. You don't want to pay full rate. They think they delivered what you asked. Now you're arguing about what the brief meant.


We built Grade partly because of this. You can attach briefs and define what "delivered" means before any money moves. If a creator uploads a video that doesn't match the brief, you're not scrambling to figure out whether to pay. The terms were clear from the start.


It also helps when payment is tied to performance. A creator who gets a CPM bonus for views actually cares about making content that works, not content that technically fulfills the brief but performs badly.


## The real test


Before you send a brief, ask yourself: if a creator followed this exactly, would the content work?


Not "would it be perfect" — would it be usable? Would it communicate the right thing to the right people?


If you can't answer yes, the brief isn't done. Add what's missing. Remove what's distracting. Then send it.


The 30 minutes you spend on a good brief saves 30 hours of unusable content and awkward conversations. I learned that the expensive way. You don't have to.
