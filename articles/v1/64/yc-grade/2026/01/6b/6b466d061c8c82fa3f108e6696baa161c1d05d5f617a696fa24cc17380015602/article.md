# What to Do When a Contractor Ghosts You Mid-Project

Three weeks into a rebrand, my designer stopped replying. No warning. No "hey, something came up." Just silence. Her last message was a thumbs-up emoji on a Monday. By Thursday I was refreshing my inbox like a jilted ex.


She had all the source files. Half the deliverables were done. And I had no backup plan because I never thought I needed one.


If you work with contractors long enough, this will happen to you. Not might. Will. Here is what I have learned about handling it, preventing it, and building systems so it does not wreck your timeline when it does.


## First 48 hours: do not panic, but do act fast


When a contractor goes quiet, your first instinct is to wait. Give them a day. People get sick. Phones break. Life happens.


Give them 24 hours, then send a clear message: "Hey, haven't heard from you since Monday. Are you okay? Need to know if you're still able to continue this project." Direct. Not passive-aggressive. Not apologetic. Just clear.


If another 24 hours passes with nothing, you are being ghosted. Accept it and move to damage control.


The mistake most people make is waiting a week "to be respectful." A week is eternity when you have a launch date. Respect is a two-way street, and someone who disappears without a word has already broken that contract.


## Secure what you have


Before you do anything else, lock down the work that exists. This means:


- Download every file, design, code commit, or document they have shared so far
- Check shared drives, repos, and project management tools for anything in progress
- Revoke access to sensitive systems if they had any
- Document exactly what was delivered and what is still missing


With my designer, I got lucky. She had been uploading to a shared Figma. I had most of the in-progress work. But I have heard horror stories of people whose contractor had everything on a local machine and just vanished with it.


If your work lives on someone else's hard drive and they disappear, you have nothing. Keep that in mind for next time.


## Figure out what you owe (and what you do not)


This is where things get awkward. You hired someone. They did some work. They disappeared. Do you pay for the partial work?


My take: yes, if the work is usable. No, if it is not.


If they completed 60% of the deliverables and you can actually use that 60%, pay for it. Pro-rate the agreed amount. Send the payment with a note explaining the calculation. Be fair even when you are frustrated.


If they delivered nothing usable, you do not owe anything. This is why milestone-based payments exist. If you paid everything upfront and got ghosted mid-project, that is an expensive lesson in payment structure.


We built Grade partly because of situations like this. When payments are tied to verified deliverables, ghosting does not mean you lose money on unfinished work. You pay for what was actually done.


## Finding a replacement without starting over


The panic move is posting on Upwork with "URGENT" in the title. Do not do this. Desperate hiring leads to desperate choices.


Instead, look at who you already know. Previous contractors who did good work. People in your network. Someone who already understands your product or industry. Cold-starting with a stranger on an urgent timeline is the worst-case scenario.


When you bring someone new in, give them the honest picture: "Our previous contractor left mid-project. Here is what is done. Here is what is left. Here is the timeline. Can you do it?" Good contractors appreciate honesty. Bad ones will say yes to anything.


One thing that saved me: having documentation. Not just the deliverables, but notes on decisions made, brand guidelines, technical specs. The new designer did not have to guess what the old one was thinking because I had written it down.


## Why contractors ghost (it is usually not malicious)


I eventually heard from my designer. Three weeks later. She had a family emergency and was embarrassed about going silent. By the time she felt ready to explain, she was too ashamed to reach out.


That is the most common reason, honestly. It is rarely "I took your money and ran." It is usually some combination of:


- They got overwhelmed and did not know how to say so
- A personal crisis hit and work fell off their radar
- They took on too many projects and yours got deprioritized
- They realized they were in over their head and froze instead of admitting it


Understanding this does not fix the problem, but it changes how you prevent it. The solution is not finding "more reliable people." It is building systems that catch silence early.


## How to ghost-proof your contractor relationships


After getting burned, I changed how I work with contractors. Not dramatically. Just a few things that make ghosting less likely and less damaging when it happens.


Set up regular check-ins. Not daily standups. That is micromanagement. But a weekly "here is where I am, here is what is next" message keeps both sides honest. If someone misses two check-ins, you know something is wrong before the project derails.


Use milestone payments, not lump sums. Break the project into chunks. Pay after each chunk. This protects you from paying for unfinished work and gives the contractor regular wins. People are less likely to disappear when they are three days away from a payment.


Keep all work in shared systems. Code goes in your repo. Designs go in your Figma. Documents go in your drive. Nothing should live exclusively on someone else's machine.


Have a backup. Not a full second contractor on standby. Just know who you would call. Keep a short list of people who could step in with a week's notice. When you need this list, you will be glad it exists.


Make it safe to say "I'm struggling." The reason most contractors ghost instead of communicating is that they are afraid of the reaction. Tell people upfront: "If you are falling behind or something comes up, just tell me. I would rather adjust the timeline than find out by silence."


## It happened to me, it will happen to you


Working with contractors means accepting some level of unpredictability. You are not their employer. You do not control their schedule, their life, or their other commitments. That is the tradeoff for the flexibility and cost savings that contractors offer.


The goal is not to eliminate ghosting. It is to make it survivable. Milestone payments so you do not lose money. Shared systems so you do not lose work. Documentation so you do not lose context. And a short list of backups so you do not lose time.


My rebrand shipped two weeks late. The new designer did solid work. And I never heard from the original designer again after her apology message. No hard feelings. Just a lesson I only needed to learn once.
