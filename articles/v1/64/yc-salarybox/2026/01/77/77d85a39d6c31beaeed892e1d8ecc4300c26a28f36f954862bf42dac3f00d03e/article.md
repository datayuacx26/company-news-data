## What Is Buddy Punching and Why Is It a Problem?


Buddy punching, also known as "time clock fraud" or "proxy punching," happens when one employee uses a traditional time clock, such as a punch card, PIN entry, or swipe badge—to record time for another. Common scenarios include:


-


An employee running late asks a coworker to clock them in at the scheduled start time.


-


Someone leaving early has a colleague clock them out later.


-


In extreme cases, an absent employee has a friend cover an entire shift.


This practice is surprisingly common. Studies from the American Payroll Association indicate that 75% of U.S. businesses are affected by time theft, with buddy punching being one of the most prevalent forms. Another survey revealed that up to 16-30% of employees admit to engaging in or witnessing buddy punching.


While employees might view it as harmless, buddy punching is a serious form of payroll fraud. It results in companies paying for hours not worked, inflating labor costs and distorting productivity metrics. Over time, it fosters a culture of dishonesty, lowers morale among honest workers, and can even lead to legal issues under wage and hour laws like the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA).

## How Biometric Time Clocks Work to Prevent Buddy Punching


Biometric time clocks use unique physiological traits to authenticate employees. The process is simple:


1.


Enrollment


: Employees provide initial scans (e.g., fingerprint or face) to create a digital template stored securely.


2.


Clocking In/Out


: At shift start/end, the employee scans their trait. The system compares it to the template.


3.


Verification


: If it matches, the time is recorded. No match? No punch.


Since traits like fingerprints or facial geometry can't be shared or faked easily, buddy punching becomes impossible without the actual person present.


## Types of Biometric Time Clocks


Several modalities are available, each suited to different environments:


-


Fingerprint Scanners


: Most common and affordable. Scans finger ridges for quick verification. Ideal for offices but may struggle with dirty/wet hands.


-


Facial Recognition


: Contactless and hygienic. Analyzes facial features (e.g., eye distance, jaw shape). Great for high-traffic areas or post-pandemic workplaces.


-


Iris/Retinal Scans


: Highly accurate, scanning eye patterns. More expensive, used in high-security settings.


-


Palm/Vein Pattern


: Scans vein structures under the skin. Resistant to surface damage; contactless options available.


-


Hybrid Systems


: Combine multiple methods (e.g., fingerprint + facial) for extra security.


Many modern clocks integrate with cloud software for real-time reporting, payroll export, and remote access.

## Addressing Privacy Concerns and Legal Considerations


Biometric data is sensitive, raising valid privacy questions. Employees may worry about data misuse or health revelations (e.g., iris changes indicating conditions).


Key concerns:


-


Data breaches (biometrics can't be "changed" like passwords).


-


Potential bias in facial recognition (e.g., accuracy variations by skin tone).


-


Religious objections to facial scans.


Legal landscape: No federal U.S. law mandates specifics, but states like Illinois (BIPA), California (CCPA), Texas, and Washington have strict rules requiring:


-


Written consent.


-


Public data retention/destruction policies.


-


Secure storage.


Violations can lead to hefty fines ($1,000-$5,000 per infraction). Always consult legal experts and prioritize transparency.


Best practices:


-


Obtain informed consent.


-


Use encrypted, minimal-data storage.


-


Offer alternatives for objections.


-


Delete data upon termination.
