**What is HRA (House Rent Allowance)?**


House Rent Allowance (HRA) is a salary component paid by employers to employees to cover the cost of rented accommodation. It is one of the most common components in the salary structure of Indian companies, appearing in the CTC breakdown of nearly every salaried professional who receives a structured pay package.


HRA exemption under **Section 10(13A) of the Income Tax Act, 1961** , allows salaried individuals to reduce their taxable income by claiming a deduction on the rent they pay. This exemption is not automatic — it must be calculated using a specific formula and supported by proper documentation submitted to the employer.


According to payroll data aggregated from major Indian HR platforms, over **60% of salaried individuals in metro cities** claim HRA exemption every financial year, making it one of the most widely used tax-saving provisions in the country. For employees in cities like Mumbai, Bengaluru, and Delhi — where rental costs can consume 30% to 50% of take-home pay — the HRA exemption provides significant financial relief.


For HR teams and payroll administrators, understanding HRA is not optional. Incorrect HRA calculations can lead to **TDS short-deduction notices** from the Income Tax Department, employee grievances during tax filing season, and compliance issues during payroll audits. Getting HRA right requires understanding the formula, the documentation rules, the metro city classifications, and the interplay between the old and new tax regimes.


HRA is typically structured as **40% to 50% of Basic Pay** in most Indian companies, though the exact percentage varies by employer policy and location. While HRA is fully taxable by default, the exemption provisions allow employees to claim partial or full relief — provided they actually pay rent and submit the required proofs.


**Key Point for HR Teams** HRA exemption must be calculated **month-wise** (not annually) for TDS purposes. If an employee’s rent, salary, or city changes mid-year, the calculation must be updated for each relevant month. **HRA Exemption Formula Under Section 10(13A)**


The HRA exemption under Section 10(13A) of the Income Tax Act is calculated as the **lowest of three prescribed amounts** . This three-way comparison ensures that the tax benefit is proportional to the employee’s actual rent burden and is not inflated beyond what they actually spend on housing.


**HRA Exemption = LOWEST of the following three:** **1. Actual HRA received from the employer** **2. 50% of salary (metro cities) OR 40% of salary (non-metro cities)** **3. Actual rent paid MINUS 10% of salary**


This formula means the exemption can never exceed the HRA you receive, can never exceed a threshold linked to your salary and city, and is always reduced by 10% of your salary (representing the portion you are expected to bear regardless of your rent).


**Definition of ‘Salary’ for HRA Calculation**


For the purpose of HRA exemption, **‘Salary’** is defined narrowly and includes only the following components:


• Basic Pay


• Dearness Allowance (DA) — only if it forms part of retirement benefits (i.e., if DA is considered for PF/gratuity calculation)


• Commission — only if it is a fixed percentage of turnover (not discretionary bonuses)


**Important:** Other allowances such as Special Allowance, Conveyance Allowance, Medical Allowance, Performance Bonuses, and Overtime Pay are **NOT included** in the salary definition for HRA calculation. This is one of the most common errors HR teams make when computing HRA exemption. Including these components inflates the salary figure, which distorts both Option 2 and the 10% deduction in Option 3, leading to incorrect exemption amounts.


**New Metro Cities for HRA from April 2026**


**Major Update — Effective April 1, 2026** From FY 2026-27, **Rule 279 of the Income Tax Rules 2026** expands the list of metro cities for HRA purposes from 4 to 8. Employees in the newly classified metro cities — Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad, and Ahmedabad — now qualify for the higher **50% of salary** benchmark instead of the previous 40%.


**Metro vs Non-Metro Classification (FY 2026-27 Onwards)**


**City** **Classification** **HRA % of Salary** **Status**

**Why This Change Matters:** India’s IT and business landscape has evolved significantly since the original four-city metro list was established. Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad, and Ahmedabad have become major employment hubs with rental costs approaching those of the traditional metros. The expansion recognizes this reality and provides fairer tax treatment to employees in these cities.


**Impact for HR Teams:** If you have employees based in any of the four newly added metro cities, their HRA exemption calculation changes from April 2026. Payroll systems must be updated to apply the 50% rate instead of 40% for these locations. **SalaryBox has already updated its HRA engine** to incorporate the expanded metro city list automatically.


For example, an employee in Bengaluru with a basic salary of Rs 50,000 previously qualified for 40% (Rs 20,000) under Option 2, but now qualifies for 50% (Rs 25,000). Depending on which option ends up being the lowest, this can result in additional annual tax savings of **Rs 15,000 to Rs 30,000** based on the employee’s tax bracket.


**Step-by-Step HRA Calculation with Worked Examples**


Below are three detailed examples covering the most common scenarios HR teams encounter. Each example follows the standard three-step comparison method under Section 10(13A).


**Example 1: Metro City Employee (Bengaluru — Newly Classified Metro)**


**Employee Profile:** Rahul is a software developer based in Bengaluru. He lives in a rented apartment in Whitefield.


**Component** **Monthly Amount (Rs)**


Basic Pay 40,000


Dearness Allowance (DA) 5,000


HRA Received from Employer 20,000


Actual Rent Paid to Landlord 18,000


**Step-by-Step Calculation:**


**Step 1:** Determine Salary for HRA purposes


Salary = Basic Pay + DA = Rs 40,000 + Rs 5,000 = **Rs 45,000**


**Step 2:** Calculate all three options


**Criterion** **Formula** **Amount (Rs)**


Option 1 Actual HRA received 20,000


Option 2 50% of Salary (Bengaluru is metro from Apr 2026) 22,500


Option 3 Rent paid – 10% of Salary = 18,000 – 4,500 13,500


**Step 3:** Select the lowest


**Result** **Exempt HRA = Rs 13,500/month** (lowest of the three) **Taxable HRA = Rs 20,000 – Rs 13,500 = Rs 6,500/month** Annual tax-exempt HRA = Rs 13,500 x 12 = **Rs 1,62,000** Annual taxable HRA = Rs 6,500 x 12 = **Rs 78,000**


**Note:** Before April 2026, Bengaluru was non-metro. Option 2 would have been 40% of Rs 45,000 = Rs 18,000. In this specific case the exemption remains Rs 13,500 (Option 3 is still lowest), but for employees where Option 2 was the binding constraint, the reclassification delivers tangible savings.


**Example 2: Non-Metro City Employee (Jaipur)**


**Employee Profile:** Priya works as an HR manager at a manufacturing firm in Jaipur. She does not receive DA as part of her salary.


**Component** **Monthly Amount (Rs)**


Basic Pay 30,000


HRA Received from Employer 12,000


Actual Rent Paid 10,000


**Calculation:**


**Salary = Basic = Rs 30,000** (no DA applicable)


**Criterion** **Formula** **Amount (Rs)**


Option 1 Actual HRA received 12,000


Option 2 40% of Salary (Jaipur is non-metro) 12,000


Option 3 Rent paid – 10% of Salary = 10,000 – 3,000 7,000


**Result** **Exempt HRA = Rs 7,000/month** (lowest) **Taxable HRA = Rs 12,000 – Rs 7,000 = Rs 5,000/month** Annual tax-exempt HRA = Rs 7,000 x 12 = **Rs 84,000**


In this case, Option 3 is significantly lower than the other two options. This is common when the rent-to-salary ratio is moderate. To maximize exemption, the employee would need to either pay higher rent or have a higher salary component dedicated to HRA.


**Example 3: Employee Who Does Not Pay Rent**


If an employee owns their home, lives with family without paying rent, or occupies employer-provided accommodation, the **entire HRA received is fully taxable** . No exemption is available under Section 10(13A) because the fundamental condition — actually paying rent for residential accommodation — is not met.


When rent paid is zero, Option 3 (Rent paid minus 10% of salary) becomes **negative** , and the lowest of the three options is therefore zero or negative, meaning the exempt amount is nil.


For instance, if an employee receives Rs 15,000 as HRA but pays no rent, the entire Rs 15,000 per month (Rs 1,80,000 annually) is added to taxable income. There is no way to claim partial exemption when no rent is paid.


**HR Tip** Even when an employee does not claim HRA exemption, the HRA component must still appear in the salary structure and on the payslip. It is simply added to taxable income in full. Do not remove HRA from the payslip — only the exemption portion changes. The gross HRA figure remains the same regardless of whether exemption is claimed.


**Documentation Requirements for HRA Claim (Updated 2026)**


The Income Tax Department requires specific documentation to validate HRA exemption claims. Under the revised rules effective from FY 2026-27, documentation standards have been tightened, and new disclosure requirements have been introduced. Below is a comprehensive breakdown of what employees must submit and what HR teams must verify.


**1. Rent Receipts**


Monthly rent receipts signed by the landlord are the primary proof of rent payment. Each receipt should include: landlord’s full name, landlord’s address, tenant’s (employee’s) name, rental property address, rent amount, payment period, date of receipt, and landlord’s signature. For receipts exceeding **Rs 5,000** , a revenue stamp must be affixed. Many organizations collect rent receipts quarterly — in April, July, October, and January — to maintain an ongoing compliance trail.


**2. Rent Agreement**


A registered or notarized rental agreement is strongly recommended, though not always legally mandated. A written agreement serves as the primary evidence during tax scrutiny or assessment proceedings. The agreement should clearly specify the monthly rent amount, security deposit, lease duration, renewal terms, and complete details of both the landlord and the tenant. For rent agreements with a duration exceeding 11 months, registration with the local sub-registrar is advisable.


**3. Landlord PAN**


Providing the landlord’s PAN (Permanent Account Number) is **mandatory** when annual rent exceeds **Rs 1,00,000** (i.e., monthly rent above approximately Rs 8,333). The employee must obtain the landlord’s PAN and submit it to the employer as part of the HRA declaration process. If the landlord does not possess a PAN, the employee must obtain and submit a **written declaration** from the landlord containing their full name, complete address, and an explicit statement confirming that they do not have a PAN. This declaration must be submitted along with Form 124.


**4. Form 124 (Replaces Form 12BB)**


Form 124 is the updated investment and expenditure declaration form that employees submit to their employer at the beginning of each financial year. Effective from FY 2026-27, Form 124 replaces the erstwhile Form 12BB. For HRA claims, Form 124 includes a **new mandatory field: relationship with landlord** . The complete HRA section of Form 124 must contain:


• Estimated annual HRA claim amount


• Landlord’s full legal name and complete postal address


• Landlord’s PAN (or a signed declaration of PAN non-availability)


• Relationship with landlord (new field — e.g., unrelated third party, parent, sibling, relative)


• Period of tenancy covered by the claim


• Monthly rent amount and total rent for the financial year


**5. Payment Mode and Proof**


Rent payments through **bank transfer, UPI, or cheque** are strongly preferred as they create an automatic, verifiable payment trail. Bank statements showing regular monthly transfers to the landlord serve as robust supporting evidence. Cash payments, while not explicitly prohibited by the Income Tax Act, attract **significantly higher scrutiny** from the Income Tax Department during assessment proceedings. For rent amounts above Rs 50,000 per month, digital payment is practically essential for the claim to withstand examination.

The landlord PAN requirement is one of the most frequently misunderstood and mishandled aspects of HRA compliance. Let us break down the rules with complete clarity.


**Annual Rent Paid** **Landlord PAN Required?** **Action if Landlord Has No PAN**

**Form 124: What HR Teams Must Know**


Form 124 replaced Form 12BB effective from FY 2026-27 as the standard employee investment and expenditure declaration form. Key differences and compliance points:


• New mandatory field requiring disclosure of the landlord-tenant relationship


• Digital submission is accepted — e-signed copies are valid


• Provisional Form 124 must be submitted by April 30 each year for initial TDS calculation


• Final proofs and updated Form 124 must be submitted by January 15 for actual TDS adjustment in February-March


• Employer must retain Form 124 and all supporting documents for 7 years from the end of the relevant assessment year


• Discrepancies between provisional and final declarations must be reconciled in the last quarter TDS


**HRA Under Old Tax Regime vs New Tax Regime**


The choice between the Old and New Tax Regimes is one of the most impactful financial decisions for salaried employees, and HRA exemption availability is a major differentiating factor. HR teams are frequently asked for guidance on this choice, making it essential to understand the comparison clearly.


**Parameter** **Old Tax Regime** **New Tax Regime (Sec 115BAC)**


HRA Exemption Available under Sec 10(13A) NOT available – HRA fully taxable


Standard Deduction Rs 50,000 Rs 75,000


Section 80C Deduction Up to Rs 1,50,000 NOT available


Section 80D (Medical Insurance) Available NOT available


Home Loan Interest (Sec 24) Up to Rs 2,00,000 NOT available


Tax Slab Rates Higher rates, more deductions Lower rates, minimal deductions


Best Suited For Employees with rent + investments Employees with few deductions


**Decision Framework for Employees** **Choose Old Regime** if your total deductions (HRA + 80C + 80D + home loan + NPS + others) exceed approximately Rs 3,75,000 per year. The higher tax rates are more than offset by the deductions. **Choose New Regime** if your deductions are minimal — for example, if you own your home (no HRA claim), have few investments under 80C, and prefer simplicity with lower tax rates. **Special HRA Scenarios Every HR Team Must Know**


**1. Paying Rent to Parents**


An employee **can pay rent to their parents and claim HRA exemption** . This is a legally valid arrangement confirmed by multiple Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) decisions. However, certain conditions must be strictly met for the claim to hold up during scrutiny:


• The parent must be the legal owner of the property (ownership documents should be available)


• A formal rent agreement must exist between the employee (tenant) and parent (landlord)


• Rent must be paid through traceable means — bank transfer or UPI, not cash


• The parent must declare the rental income received in their own Income Tax Return (ITR)


• Form 124 must disclose the relationship as ‘Parent’ in the new mandatory relationship field


**Tax planning note:** If the parent is a senior citizen in a lower tax bracket — or has income below the basic exemption limit — this arrangement creates a legitimate net tax saving for the family. The employee saves tax through HRA exemption, and the parent’s rental income may fall within their exemption limit or attract a lower tax rate.


**2. Joint Rent with Spouse**


When both spouses are salaried and live in the same rented accommodation, **only one spouse can claim HRA for the same property** . The claim must be made by the spouse in whose name the rent agreement and rent receipts are issued. Dual claims on the same property will be flagged during automated ITR processing and can trigger scrutiny notices for both individuals.


However, if both spouses work in different cities and maintain separate rented accommodations, each can independently claim HRA for their respective rental property.


**3. HRA and Home Loan Together**


Claiming both HRA exemption (Section 10(13A)) and Home Loan interest deduction (Section 24) simultaneously is **legally permissible** — but only under a specific condition:


**Dual Benefit: HRA + Home Loan** The rented property (for which HRA is claimed) must be in a **different city** from the owned property (for which home loan deduction is claimed). For example, an employee working in Mumbai who rents an apartment there but owns a home in Lucknow can claim HRA exemption for the Mumbai rental and home loan interest deduction for the Lucknow property — both under the old tax regime.


**4. Part-Year Metro, Part-Year Non-Metro**


If an employee transfers from a metro city to a non-metro city (or vice versa) during the financial year, HRA exemption must be calculated **month-wise** based on the actual city of residence for each month. The 50% and 40% rates are applied to the respective months, and the annual exemption is the sum of all monthly calculations.


For example, if an employee works in Bengaluru (metro, 50%) from April to September and transfers to Jaipur (non-metro, 40%) from October to March, each six-month period uses its respective rate. The employer must track the transfer date and adjust TDS calculations accordingly.


**5. Multiple Employers in the Same Year**


When an employee changes jobs mid-year, each employer calculates HRA exemption independently for their respective employment period. The employee must provide rent details and Form 124 to each employer separately. At the time of filing their Income Tax Return, the employee aggregates the exemptions from both employers and reports the total taxable and exempt HRA. Any discrepancy or over-claim is resolved during ITR assessment.


**10 Common HRA Calculation Mistakes to Avoid**


Based on payroll audits conducted across thousands of Indian SMBs and analysis of common errors flagged by the Income Tax Department, here are the ten most frequent HRA mistakes that lead to incorrect tax deductions, employee grievances, and compliance notices:


**1. Using annual salary instead of monthly salary**


HRA exemption must be computed on a monthly basis. Using annual figures inflates the 10% deduction in Option 3 and produces incorrect exemption amounts. The month-wise calculation is especially critical when salary or rent changes mid-year.


**2. Including allowances beyond Basic + DA in salary**


Only Basic Pay, DA (if part of retirement benefits), and fixed commission are included in the salary definition for HRA. Including Special Allowance, HRA itself, Transport Allowance, or Performance Bonuses inflates the salary figure and produces wrong results.


**3. Not recalculating when rent or salary changes mid-year**


If an employee receives a mid-year increment, changes rental accommodation, or moves to a different city, the HRA exemption must be recalculated from the month of change onwards. Many payroll teams make the mistake of using start-of-year figures for the entire year.


**4. Claiming HRA under the new tax regime**


HRA exemption is NOT available under Section 115BAC (new regime). Employees who opt for the new regime cannot claim any HRA deduction — the entire HRA received is taxable. HR teams must validate regime selection before processing HRA exemptions.


**5. Missing landlord PAN for rent above Rs 1 lakh annually**


Failure to collect and report the landlord’s PAN when annual rent exceeds Rs 1,00,000 can result in the entire HRA exemption being disallowed during assessment. The employer is also liable for not performing due diligence.


**6. Not updating metro classification for the four new cities**


From April 2026, Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad, and Ahmedabad are classified as metro cities for HRA. Using the old 40% rate instead of 50% understates the employee’s exemption and results in excess TDS deduction.


**7. Calculating HRA for the full year when rent was paid for fewer months**


If an employee paid rent for only 8 months (e.g., moved to own house mid-year), HRA exemption is available only for those 8 months. The remaining months’ HRA is fully taxable.


**8. Not collecting Form 124 on time**


Provisional declarations should be submitted by April 30 and final proofs by January 15. Late submissions create TDS adjustment complications and may result in excess tax deduction that the employee must claim as refund during ITR filing.


**9. Ignoring the landlord relationship disclosure requirement**


The new Form 124 requires disclosure of the landlord-tenant relationship. Omitting this field can trigger scrutiny, especially when rent is paid to family members like parents or siblings.


**10. Allowing HRA claims without rent receipts**


Even if a rent agreement exists, monthly rent receipts are the primary supporting evidence required. Receipts above Rs 5,000 must have revenue stamps. Claims without receipts are routinely disallowed during assessment.year
