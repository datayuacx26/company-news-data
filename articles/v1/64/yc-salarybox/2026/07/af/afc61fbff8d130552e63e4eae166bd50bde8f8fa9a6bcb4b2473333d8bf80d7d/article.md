**What Is Employee Termination?**


Employee termination is the formal process of ending an employment relationship between an employer and an employee. It can be initiated by either party — the employer (dismissal, retrenchment, layoff) or the employee (resignation, retirement). In every case, specific legal procedures must be followed to ensure the termination is valid and enforceable under Indian law.


In India, the termination process is primarily governed by:


**• Industrial Relations Code 2020** (effective 21 November 2025) — replaced the Industrial Disputes Act 1947 and covers workers in industrial establishments.


**• Shops & Establishments Act** — state-specific legislation governing employees in commercial establishments, shops, and offices.


**• Individual employment contracts** — terms agreed upon at the time of hiring, including notice period, probation clauses, and separation procedures.


**Why Does the Process Matter?** Wrongful termination in India can result in reinstatement orders by the Labour Court, full back wages from the date of termination, monetary penalties for the employer, and lasting reputational damage. Following the legally prescribed process protects both the employer and the employee.


Whether you are an HR manager handling an employee exit for the first time, a small business owner navigating compliance, or an employee wanting to understand your rights, this guide walks you through every aspect of the employee termination process in India — from legal framework and step-by-step procedures to settlement calculations and ready-to-use letter templates.


**Types of Termination in India**


Not all terminations are the same. The type of termination determines the notice requirements, compensation obligations, and legal procedures that apply. Here is a comprehensive overview of every category recognised under Indian law:


**Type** **Reason** **Notice Required** **Compensation**


**Voluntary Resignation** Employee chooses to leave As per contract (30–90 days) Nil (F&F settlement applies)


**Termination for Cause** Proven misconduct after inquiry No notice needed if dismissed No retrenchment compensation


**Poor Performance** Consistent underperformance despite PIP Yes, after PIP process As per contract


**Retrenchment** Economic or business reasons 1 month notice or pay in lieu 15 days avg. pay per year of service


**Layoff** Temporary suspension of work Layoff compensation applies 50% of basic + DA for layoff period


**Probation Termination** During probation period 7–30 days or as per offer letter Nil


**End of Contract** Fixed-term contract expires No notice (end date applies) Pro-rata gratuity and bonus


**Retirement** Reaching retirement age (58–60) Advance intimation by employer Gratuity + PF + leave encashment


Each of these categories has distinct legal implications. This guide focuses primarily on employer-initiated termination — particularly termination for misconduct, retrenchment, and the full and final settlement process, as these are the areas where compliance errors are most common and consequences most severe.


**Legal Framework: Key Laws Governing Termination**


India’s employee termination laws have been consolidated under the new Labour Codes, which came into effect in late 2025. Understanding which law applies to your establishment and your employees is the first step toward compliant termination.


**1. Industrial Relations Code 2020 (Effective 21 November 2025)**


This is the primary legislation governing termination of workers in factories, mines, plantations, and other industrial establishments. It replaced the Industrial Disputes Act 1947 and brought significant changes:


**• Standing Orders:** Establishments with 300 or more workers must have certified standing orders that define misconduct, disciplinary procedures, and termination rules.


**• Retrenchment threshold raised:** Only establishments with 300+ workers (up from 100) need prior government permission for retrenchment, layoff, or closure.


**• Fixed-term employees:** Entitled to the same benefits as permanent employees, including pro-rata gratuity regardless of service length.


**• Dispute resolution:** Grievance Redressal Committee mandatory in establishments with 20+ workers.


**2. Shops & Establishments Act (State-Specific)**


Each Indian state has its own Shops & Establishments Act covering employees in commercial establishments, shops, offices, restaurants, theatres, and similar workplaces. These laws prescribe:


**•** Notice period requirements for termination


**•** Working hours, leave entitlements, and overtime


**•** Conditions under which termination is permissible


**•** Registration and record-keeping obligations for employers


Since these laws vary by state, employers must check the specific provisions applicable in their state of operation.


**3. Payment of Gratuity Act 1972 / Code on Social Security 2020**


Gratuity is payable to every employee who has completed five or more years of continuous service (or pro-rata for fixed-term contract employees under the new codes). The gratuity formula, tax exemption limits, and payment timelines are discussed in detail in the Gratuity on Termination section below.


**4. Code on Wages 2019**


This code mandates that when an employee is terminated or resigns, all wages due must be settled within two working days of the last working day. This includes salary for days worked, leave encashment, bonus, and any other dues. Failure to comply can result in penalties and legal action against the employer.


**Step-by-Step Termination Process for Misconduct**


Termination for misconduct is the most legally sensitive type of termination. If the procedure is not followed correctly, the termination can be challenged in court and overturned. Below is the legally compliant seven-step process that every employer must follow:


**Step 1:** **Document the Misconduct** Before any action is taken, the employer must collect and preserve evidence of the alleged misconduct. This includes: **•** CCTV footage, access logs, or digital records **•** Emails, chat messages, or written correspondence **•** Witness statements with dates and details **•** Attendance records, performance data, or audit findings Ensure the act constitutes “misconduct” as defined in the company’s standing orders, employee handbook, or employment contract. Common examples include theft, fraud, insubordination, habitual absenteeism, and violation of safety norms.


**Step 2:** **Issue a Show Cause Notice** A show cause notice is a formal written document that: **•** Describes the alleged misconduct with specific dates, incidents, and evidence references **•** Asks the employee to explain why disciplinary action should not be taken **•** Provides 7 to 15 days to respond in writing The notice must be delivered personally with an acknowledgement signature, or sent via registered post with acknowledgement due (RPAD). Verbal warnings do not constitute a valid show cause notice.


**Step 3:** **Review the Employee’s Response** Once the employee submits their written response: **• If the explanation is satisfactory:** Close the matter. Issue a written warning if appropriate and place it on record. **• If the explanation is not satisfactory:** Proceed to a formal domestic inquiry. Do not skip this step — direct termination without an inquiry is the most common ground for wrongful termination claims.


**Step 4:** **Conduct a Domestic Inquiry** A domestic inquiry (also called a departmental inquiry) is a quasi-judicial proceeding conducted within the organisation. The procedure includes: **• Appoint an Inquiry Officer:** Must be impartial, typically from a different department or an external person. **• Frame charges:** Clearly articulate each charge of misconduct. **• Allow the employee to defend:** The employee has the right to present evidence, cross-examine management witnesses, and bring a co-worker as a defence representative (an external lawyer is generally not permitted). **• Maintain records:** Detailed minutes of every hearing session must be recorded and signed by all parties. **• Inquiry report:** The Inquiry Officer submits findings and recommendations to the management.


**Step 5:** **Management Decision** Based on the inquiry report, the management (typically the disciplinary authority) must: **•** Review the findings objectively **• If misconduct is proved:** Issue a termination order. The punishment must be proportionate to the misconduct — courts frequently overturn dismissals where a lesser penalty would have been appropriate. **• If misconduct is not proved:** Reinstate the employee with a clean record. Any suspension period must be treated as duty, with full wages paid.


**Step 6:** **Issue the Termination Letter** The termination letter is the formal document that ends the employment. It must include: **•** Effective date of termination **•** Reference to the domestic inquiry, its findings, and the management’s decision **•** Full and final settlement details and the timeline for payment **•** Instructions for return of company assets (laptop, phone, ID card, access cards)


**Step 7:** **Complete Full & Final Settlement** Under the Code on Wages 2019, all dues must be settled within two working days of the employee’s last working day. The settlement includes payment of all salary, leave encashment, gratuity (if applicable), bonus, reimbursements, and any other contractual dues — minus recoverable amounts such as notice period shortfall, outstanding loans, and unreturned assets. The detailed calculation of F&F settlement is covered in the next section.


**Notice Period Rules for Termination**


The notice period is the advance intimation that either party must give before ending the employment. The length of the notice period depends on the employee’s category, the applicable law, and the terms of the employment contract.


**Employee Category** **Typical Notice Period** **Legal Basis**


**Probationary Employee** 7–30 days Appointment / Offer letter


**Confirmed Employee (Private Sector)** 30–90 days Employment contract


**Workman (Industrial Establishment)** 1 month (30 days) Industrial Relations Code 2020


**Manager / Senior Executive** 60–90 days Employment contract


**Fixed-Term Contract Employee** As per contract or till end date Contract terms


**Notice Period Pay in Lieu**


Either party can choose to pay salary for the notice period instead of requiring the employee to serve it. This is known as “pay in lieu of notice.”


**• Employer terminates without notice:** Must pay the employee’s full salary for the applicable notice period.


**• Employee resigns without serving notice:** The employer can recover the notice period salary from the employee’s F&F settlement.


Important: Some employment contracts allow the employer to waive the notice period entirely. Always check the specific contract terms before making deductions.


**Full & Final Settlement: Components & Calculation**


The full and final settlement (commonly called F&F) is the complete financial reckoning between the employer and the employee at the time of separation. Under the Code on Wages 2019, this must be completed within two working days of the last working day.


**F&F Formula** Net F&F Payable = Total Payable Components − Total Recoverable Components


**Payable Components**


**1. Salary for days worked** in the last month (pro-rata calculation)


**2. Leave encashment:** Earned leave balance multiplied by the daily wage rate


**3. Gratuity:** If 5+ years of service (formula: 15 × last drawn salary × years of service ÷ 26)


**4. Bonus:** Pro-rata entitlement under the Payment of Bonus Act, if applicable


**5. Retrenchment compensation:** If termination qualifies as retrenchment (15 days average pay per completed year)


**6. Notice period pay:** If the employer terminates without serving notice


**7. Pending reimbursements:** Any approved expense claims, incentives, or variable pay due


**Recoverable Components**


**1. Notice period recovery:** If the employee does not serve the required notice period


**2. Outstanding loan balance:** Any salary advance or company loan


**3. Advance salary recovery:** Over-credited salary amounts


**4. Training bond amount:** If applicable and the bond is legally enforceable


**5. Unreturned company assets:** Value of laptop, phone, ID card, or other assets not returned


**6. Excess leave adjustment:** Salary deduction for leave taken beyond entitlement


**F&F Settlement: Worked Example**


Let us calculate the full and final settlement for a typical employee scenario:


**Employee Profile** Name: Rahul Sharma | Designation: Marketing Executive Monthly Salary: Rs 50,000 | Service: 3 years | Earned Leave Balance: 15 days Exit Type: Voluntary resignation | Notice Period: 30 days (served in full) Days Worked in Last Month: 20 out of 30


**Payable Component** **Calculation** **Amount (Rs)**


Salary for days worked (20 days) 50,000 / 30 × 20 33,333


Leave encashment (15 EL days) 50,000 / 30 × 15 25,000


Gratuity Not eligible (< 5 years) 0


Bonus (pro-rata) Annual bonus pro-rated 8,333


**Total Payable** **66,666**


**Recoverable Component** **Amount (Rs)**


Notice period recovery Nil (full notice served)


Outstanding loans / advances Nil


Unreturned company assets Nil


**Total Recoverable** **0**


**Net Full & Final Settlement Amount** Rs 66,666 (Payable) − Rs 0 (Recoverable) = Rs 66,666 Net Payable to Employee


Note: If Rahul had not served his notice period, the employer could recover Rs 50,000 (one month’s salary) from the payable amount, reducing the net F&F to Rs 16,666.


**Retrenchment: Special Rules Under Industrial Relations Code 2020**


Retrenchment refers to the termination of a worker’s service by the employer for economic or business reasons — not due to any fault or misconduct of the worker. It is one of the most regulated forms of termination in India.


**Key Rules for Retrenchment**


**• Government permission:** Establishments with 300 or more workers must obtain prior written permission from the appropriate government authority before retrenching any worker.


**• LIFO principle:** The Last In, First Out rule applies — the last person hired in a particular category must be the first to be retrenched.


**• Notice requirement:** The employer must give one month’s written notice or pay one month’s salary in lieu of notice.


**• Retrenchment compensation:** 15 days’ average pay for every completed year of continuous service. This is in addition to the notice period pay.


**• Government reporting:** The retrenchment must be reported to the appropriate government authority in the prescribed form.


**• Re-employment priority:** If the employer recruits for the same or similar role within one year, retrenched workers must be given preference.


**Retrenchment Compensation Calculation**


**Formula** Retrenchment Compensation = 15 days average pay × completed years of service Average pay = Average of last 3 months’ wages (basic + DA)


Example: A worker with 10 years of service and an average monthly wage of Rs 30,000 would receive:


Compensation = (30,000 ÷ 26) × 15 × 10 = Rs 1,73,077 (approximately)


**Gratuity on Termination**


Gratuity is a statutory benefit paid by the employer to the employee as a reward for long and faithful service. Under the Payment of Gratuity Act 1972 (now subsumed under the Code on Social Security 2020), gratuity is payable on termination, resignation, retirement, or death of the employee.


**Eligibility**


**• Permanent employees:** Must have completed a minimum of five years of continuous service. Service of 4 years and 240 days (or 190 days for seasonal establishments) is treated as five years.


**• Fixed-term contract employees:** Under the new Labour Codes, fixed-term employees are entitled to pro-rata gratuity regardless of the length of service. The five-year minimum does not apply.


**• Exception:** If the employee dies or becomes disabled, gratuity is payable regardless of service length.


**Gratuity Formula**


**Calculation** Gratuity = 15 × Last Drawn Salary (Basic + DA) × Years of Service ÷ 26 Each completed year of service counts. Months beyond 6 are rounded up to the next year. Tax exemption limit: Rs 20,00,000 (Rs 20 lakh)


Example: An employee with 12 years and 8 months of service (rounded to 13 years) and a last drawn salary of Rs 40,000 (basic + DA):


Gratuity = 15 × 40,000 × 13 ÷ 26 = Rs 3,00,000


**Wrongful Termination: Consequences for Employers**


Wrongful termination (also called illegal or unfair termination) occurs when an employer terminates an employee without following the legally prescribed process, without valid cause, or in violation of statutory protections. The consequences can be severe:


**Consequence** **Details**


**Reinstatement with Back Wages** The Labour Court or Industrial Tribunal can order the employer to reinstate the employee with full back wages from the date of wrongful termination to the date of reinstatement.


**Compensation in Lieu of Reinstatement** If reinstatement is not practical, the court can award compensation of up to 12 months’ wages to the employee.


**Penalty for Unfair Labour Practice** Under the Industrial Relations Code 2020, the employer may face fines and penalties for engaging in unfair labour practices, including wrongful termination.


**Reputational Damage** Wrongful termination cases damage the employer’s brand, make it harder to attract talent, and can lead to negative coverage on employer review platforms.


**Legal Costs and Management Time** Labour disputes can take years to resolve. The legal costs, management time, and operational disruption far exceed the cost of following the correct process.


The best protection against wrongful termination claims is a well-documented, transparent, and legally compliant process. Every step — from the show cause notice to the inquiry proceedings to the termination letter — must be recorded and preserved.
