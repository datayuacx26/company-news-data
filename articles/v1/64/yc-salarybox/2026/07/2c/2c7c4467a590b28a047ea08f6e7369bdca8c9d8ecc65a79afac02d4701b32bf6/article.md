## **Introduction**


When a new employee joins an organisation in India, the first few months are rarely just about settling into a new desk. This initial phase, commonly known as the **probation period** , serves as a critical evaluation window for both the employer and the employee. It is the period during which the employer assesses the employee’s competence, cultural fit, and ability to meet job expectations, while the employee evaluates whether the organisation, role, and work environment align with their career goals.


Despite being one of the most common employment practices in India, probation periods are frequently misunderstood. Employers sometimes assume they have unlimited power to terminate a probationer, while employees often believe they have no rights until they are confirmed. Both assumptions are incorrect. Indian labour law, a complex tapestry of central statutes, state-specific legislation, and judicial precedent, provides a nuanced framework that balances flexibility with fairness.


Whether you are an HR professional drafting appointment letters, a business owner managing a growing team, or an employee navigating your first few months at a new job, understanding probation period rules in India is essential. This comprehensive guide covers everything from legal provisions and typical durations to extension protocols, confirmation processes, and termination rights under Indian law in 2026.


## **What Is a Probation Period?**


A **probation period** (also referred to as a trial period or provisional period) is a defined span of time at the beginning of employment during which the employer evaluates a new hire’s suitability for the role. It is essentially an assessment phase built into the employment relationship, allowing both parties to determine whether the arrangement is a good fit before the employment becomes permanent.


During probation, the employee is expected to demonstrate competency in their assigned responsibilities, adhere to company policies, and integrate effectively with the team. The employer, in turn, is expected to provide adequate training, resources, and feedback to support the employee’s transition.


It is important to note that probation is not mandatory under any central Indian law for the private sector. There is no statute that requires private employers to place new hires on probation. However, the practice is so widespread that it is considered a de facto standard across industries. The terms and conditions of probation are governed primarily by the contract of employment (offer letter or appointment letter), supplemented by applicable labour legislation and the company’s internal policies.


From the employee’s perspective, the probation period is equally significant. It is an opportunity to assess the company’s work culture, management style, growth opportunities, and whether the reality matches the promises made during the recruitment process.


## **Legal Framework Governing Probation in India**


India does not have a single, unified statute that exclusively governs probation periods. Instead, the legal framework is drawn from multiple sources, and the applicable law depends on the nature of the establishment, the industry, and the state in which the business operates.


### **Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946**


This Act applies to industrial establishments employing 100 or more workers (50 in some states). The **Model Standing Orders** issued under this Act define a probationer as a person who is provisionally employed to fill a permanent vacancy and who has not completed the period of probation. The Model Standing Orders suggest a probation period of **three months** , extendable by a further three months, making the maximum probation six months in total. However, individual establishments can adopt certified standing orders with different probation durations.


### **State Shops & Establishments Acts**


Each Indian state has its own Shops and Establishments Act that governs working conditions in commercial establishments, shops, restaurants, theatres, and similar workplaces. These Acts often contain provisions relating to probation, notice periods, and termination. The probation period and rules vary significantly from state to state. For example, the Maharashtra Shops and Establishments Act and the Karnataka Shops and Establishments Act have different provisions regarding probation duration and deemed confirmation.


### **Industrial Disputes Act, 1947**


While this Act does not directly regulate probation, it provides critical protections. Under Section 25F, a workman who has been in continuous service for at least 240 days (now 180 days under the new Labour Codes) is entitled to retrenchment compensation and notice before termination. This means that even if an employee’s probation extends beyond 240 days, these protections may apply, regardless of their probationary status.


### **New Labour Codes (2025 Implementation)**


The Industrial Relations Code, 2020, which is part of India’s consolidated Labour Codes, replaces several older statutes. Key changes relevant to probation include the reduction of the continuous service threshold from 240 days to 180 days for retrenchment protections, and the requirement for establishments with 300 or more workers to obtain government approval before retrenchment. The Code on Social Security, 2020 also affirms that benefits like PF and ESI apply from the date of joining, irrespective of probation status.


### **Contract of Employment**


For the vast majority of private-sector employees, especially those in managerial, supervisory, or white-collar roles, the appointment letter or employment contract is the primary document governing probation terms. Courts have consistently held that the terms agreed upon in the contract are binding on both parties, provided they do not violate any statutory provisions or public policy. This makes it essential for employers to draft clear, comprehensive, and legally sound probation clauses.

## **Employee Rights During Probation**


A common misconception is that probationary employees have few or no rights. This is far from the truth. Indian law guarantees several important protections to employees from the very first day of employment, regardless of their probation status.


### **Statutory Rights Guaranteed from Day One**


- **Minimum Wages:** Every employee, including probationers, is entitled to receive at least the minimum wage prescribed under the Minimum Wages Act, 1948 (now subsumed under the Code on Wages, 2019). The employer cannot pay a lower wage simply because the employee is on probation.
- **Provident Fund (PF):** Contributions to the Employees’ Provident Fund are mandatory from the date of joining for establishments covered under the EPF Act. The employer must deduct the employee’s share and contribute the employer’s share from the first month of employment. Probation status does not exempt the employer from PF compliance.
- **ESI Benefits:** If the establishment is covered under the Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948, and the employee’s wages fall within the prescribed threshold (currently Rs. 21,000 per month), ESI contributions and benefits apply from the date of joining. Probationers are entitled to medical benefits, sickness benefits, and maternity benefits under ESI.
- **Gratuity Accrual:** While gratuity is payable only after five years of continuous service under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, the accrual of service for gratuity calculation begins from the date of joining, not from the date of confirmation. This means the probation period counts towards the five-year qualifying period.


### **Additional Rights and Protections**


- **Leave Entitlement:** Probationary employees are entitled to leave as per the company’s leave policy and the applicable state Shops and Establishments Act. While some employers restrict certain leave categories during probation (such as earned leave accumulation), basic leave entitlements like casual leave and sick leave generally apply.
- **Protection Against Discrimination:** Probationary employees are protected against discrimination on the basis of gender, religion, caste, disability, or any other protected characteristic. Termination during probation that is motivated by discriminatory reasons can be challenged in court.
- **Right to Notice Before Termination:** Unless the employment contract specifies otherwise, probationary employees are generally entitled to reasonable notice before termination. Many appointment letters specify a notice period of 7 to 30 days during probation.
- **Sexual Harassment Protection (POSH Act):** The Protection of Women from Sexual Harassment at Workplace Act, 2013, applies to all women employees from day one, including probationers, contractual workers, and interns. Employers must provide a safe working environment to every employee, regardless of their employment status.


## **Employer Rights During Probation**


While probationary employees enjoy significant protections, the probation period also provides employers with greater flexibility compared to dealing with confirmed employees. Understanding these rights helps employers manage the probation process effectively and within legal boundaries.


- **Greater Flexibility in Termination:** Employers have more latitude to terminate a probationary employee compared to a confirmed employee. The threshold for termination is generally lower, and a domestic inquiry (which is mandatory for terminating a confirmed employee for misconduct) is not required for probationers in most cases.
- **Setting Performance Benchmarks:** Employers can establish specific performance targets, KPIs, and milestones that the probationary employee must achieve to be confirmed. These benchmarks should be reasonable, measurable, and communicated clearly at the start of probation.
- **Extending the Probation Period:** If the employer is not satisfied with the employee’s performance but believes the employee has potential, the probation period can be extended. This must be done in accordance with the terms of the appointment letter and communicated in writing before the original probation period expires.
- **Shorter Notice Period:** Most appointment letters specify a shorter notice period during probation (commonly 7 to 30 days) compared to the notice period applicable after confirmation (typically 30 to 90 days).
- **Withholding Certain Benefits:** Employers may withhold certain discretionary benefits during probation, such as performance bonuses, variable pay, stock options, or eligibility for internal job postings. However, statutory benefits (PF, ESI, minimum wages) cannot be withheld.


## **Extension of Probation Period**


Extending a probation period is a common practice when an employer needs more time to evaluate an employee’s performance. However, it must be handled carefully to avoid legal complications.


### **When Extension Is Justified**


- The employee’s performance is below expectations but shows signs of improvement.
- Insufficient data is available to make a fair assessment (e.g., the employee was on medical leave for a significant portion of the probation period).
- The employee was assigned to a project that did not fully test their capabilities.
- Organisational restructuring or role changes affected the evaluation process.


### **Legal Requirements for Extension**


- **Written Communication:** The extension must be communicated to the employee in writing before the original probation period expires. Oral communications or informal notifications are not sufficient and may be challenged.
- **Clear Reasons:** The extension letter should clearly state the reasons for the extension and the specific areas where the employee needs to improve. Vague reasons like “unsatisfactory performance” without details can weaken the employer’s legal position.
- **Maximum Extension Limits:** Most employment contracts specify a maximum extension period, typically equal to the original probation period. For example, if the initial probation is six months, the extension is usually limited to an additional six months. The Model Standing Orders under the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act cap the total probation (including extension) at six months.
- **Defined Timeline:** The extension must have a defined end date. Indefinite extensions are legally risky and may be treated by courts as deemed confirmation of the employee.
- **Documentation:** Maintain detailed records of performance reviews, feedback sessions, warning letters, and improvement plans issued during the probation period. This documentation is crucial if the termination is later challenged.


**Legal Risk: Indefinite Extension**
Courts have held that keeping an employee on probation indefinitely without a bona fide reason amounts to unfair labour practice. In several cases, the Supreme Court of India and various High Courts have ruled that indefinite extension of probation is tantamount to deemed confirmation. Employers should avoid open-ended extensions and ensure every extension has a specific end date and documented justification.


## **Confirmation After Probation**


Confirmation is the formal transition from probationary employment to permanent (or confirmed) employment. It typically involves the issuance of a **confirmation letter** by the employer, signifying that the employee has successfully completed the probation period and is now a permanent member of the organisation.


### **What Confirmation Means Legally**


Once confirmed, the employee is entitled to the full range of benefits specified in the employment contract and company policy, including longer notice periods, eligibility for bonuses and increments, and enhanced protection against arbitrary termination. A confirmed employee in an industrial establishment generally cannot be terminated without following due process, which may include a domestic inquiry in cases of misconduct.


### **The Concept of Deemed Confirmation**


One of the most significant legal concepts in Indian probation law is deemed confirmation. If the employer fails to take any action at the end of the probation period, meaning the employer neither confirms the employee, extends the probation, nor terminates the employment, the employee may be deemed to have been automatically confirmed. This principle has been upheld by the Supreme Court in several landmark judgments.


The rationale is straightforward: if the employer allowed the employee to continue working beyond the probation period without any communication, it implies acceptance of the employee’s suitability. However, this principle is subject to the specific terms of the employment contract. Some contracts contain clauses stating that probation continues until explicit confirmation, which can modify the deemed confirmation rule.


### **Key Elements of a Confirmation Letter**


- Employee’s name, designation, and department
- Date of joining and probation period served
- Effective date of confirmation
- Revised terms (if any) including notice period, salary revisions, and benefits
- Reference to the company’s policies and code of conduct applicable post-confirmation


### **Changes After Confirmation**


- **Notice Period:** The notice period typically increases from 7-30 days to 30-90 days after confirmation.
- **Benefits:** Confirmed employees usually become eligible for additional benefits such as performance bonuses, variable pay, higher insurance coverage, and stock option grants.
- **Termination Protection:** Confirmed employees enjoy greater protection against arbitrary termination, including the right to a domestic inquiry in cases of alleged misconduct.


## **Termination During Probation**


Termination during the probation period is one of the most sensitive areas of employment law in India. While employers have greater flexibility to end the employment relationship during probation, this flexibility is not unlimited.


### **Common Grounds for Termination**


- **Poor Performance:** The employee consistently fails to meet the performance benchmarks set at the beginning of the probation period, despite receiving adequate training and feedback.
- **Misconduct:** The employee engages in behaviour that violates the company’s code of conduct, such as dishonesty, insubordination, harassment, or substance abuse at the workplace.
- **Role Unsuitability:** The employee lacks the skills, qualifications, or temperament required for the role, and this becomes apparent during the assessment period.
- **Attendance and Punctuality Issues:** Persistent absenteeism, habitual tardiness, or unexplained absences without prior approval.
- **Falsification of Information:** Discovery that the employee provided false information in their resume, application form, or during the interview process regarding qualifications, experience, or background.


### **Notice Period Requirements**


The notice period during probation is governed by the terms of the appointment letter. In the absence of a specific clause, reasonable notice (typically 7 to 30 days) is expected. Some contracts allow the employer to terminate the probationer with pay in lieu of notice, meaning the employer pays the employee’s salary for the notice period instead of requiring them to work through it. It is advisable for employers to always provide notice or pay in lieu of notice to avoid potential legal disputes.


### **Key Legal Principles**


- **No Domestic Inquiry Required:** Unlike confirmed employees, probationary employees can generally be terminated without a formal domestic inquiry, even in cases of misconduct. However, the principles of natural justice should still be followed, and the employee should be given an opportunity to explain their position.
- **Termination Must Not Be Discriminatory:** Termination based on gender, caste, religion, disability, pregnancy, or any other protected characteristic is illegal, even during probation. Such termination can be challenged under the Constitution and applicable anti-discrimination laws.
- **Final Settlement Obligations:** Upon termination, the employer must settle all dues, including salary for days worked, any accrued leave encashment, proportionate bonus (if applicable), and PF contributions. The final settlement should be completed within a reasonable time, typically within 30-45 days of the last working day.


### **Key Court Rulings**


Indian courts have consistently held that while the employer has the right to terminate a probationer, this right must be exercised **bona fide** and not as a punitive measure without justification. The Supreme Court in *Parshotam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India* established that an order terminating a probationer in accordance with the terms of appointment does not amount to punishment. However, if the termination is stigmatic (i.e., it casts a stigma on the employee’s character), the employee is entitled to an opportunity to be heard.


## **Best Practices for Employers**


Managing probation effectively requires a structured approach that balances legal compliance with practical people management. Here are the key best practices every employer should follow:


- **Draft Clear Appointment Letters:** The appointment letter should explicitly state the probation period duration, conditions for extension, notice period during probation, performance expectations, and the confirmation process. Ambiguity in the appointment letter is the single biggest source of legal disputes related to probation.
- **Conduct Regular Performance Reviews:** Do not wait until the end of the probation period to evaluate the employee. Conduct structured reviews at 30-day, 60-day, and 90-day intervals (or equivalent milestones for longer probation periods). Document the feedback and share it with the employee.
- **Communicate in Writing:** Every significant decision related to probation, including extension, confirmation, or termination, must be communicated in writing. Verbal communications are difficult to prove in case of a dispute.
- **Document Everything:** Maintain comprehensive records of all interactions, performance reviews, warning letters, improvement plans, and the employee’s responses. Good documentation is your strongest defence in the event of a legal challenge.

## **Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)**


### **1. Can the probation period be extended multiple times?**


Technically, yes, the probation period can be extended more than once if the employment contract permits it. However, there are practical and legal limits. Most contracts cap the total probation (including extensions) at a maximum duration, typically 12 months. Courts have frowned upon repeated or indefinite extensions, viewing them as an attempt to deny the employee the benefits and protections of confirmed employment. Each extension must be supported by documented reasons and communicated in writing before the current period expires.


### **2. Is PF (Provident Fund) mandatory during probation?**


Yes, absolutely. PF contributions are mandatory from the date of joining for all establishments covered under the Employees’ Provident Fund and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952. The probation status of the employee has no bearing on PF applicability. Both the employer’s contribution (currently 12% of basic salary) and the employee’s contribution (12% of basic salary) must be deposited from the first month of employment. Non-compliance can result in penalties and prosecution under the EPF Act.


### **3. What happens if the employer does not issue a confirmation letter?**


If the employer does not issue a confirmation letter, extend the probation, or terminate the employee at the end of the probation period, the employee may be deemed confirmed by operation of law. This principle of deemed confirmation has been upheld by the Supreme Court of India and various High Courts. Once deemed confirmed, the employee is entitled to all the rights and protections of a permanent employee. The employer cannot later claim that the employee was still on probation if no written communication was issued at the end of the probation period.


### **4. Can an employee resign during the probation period?**


Yes, an employee can resign during the probation period. The resignation should be in accordance with the notice period specified in the appointment letter for the probation phase, which is typically 7 to 30 days. If the appointment letter is silent on the notice period during probation, a reasonable notice should be provided. Some companies allow probationary employees to resign immediately or with a shorter notice period compared to confirmed employees. The employee should submit the resignation in writing and serve the notice period unless the employer waives it.


### **5. Is gratuity applicable during the probation period?**


Gratuity is payable under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, only after the employee has completed five years of continuous service. However, the probation period counts towards this five-year calculation. So, while gratuity is not payable during the probation period itself (since the five-year threshold has not been met), the time served during probation is included when calculating the total length of service for gratuity eligibility. If an employee joins on probation and works continuously for five years, the entire five-year period, including the probation phase, qualifies for gratuity.


### **6. Can an employer terminate a probationary employee without reason?**


An employer can terminate a probationary employee with relatively greater ease compared to a confirmed employee, but “without reason” is a grey area. While the employer is not legally required to conduct a formal inquiry or provide elaborate justification (as would be needed for a confirmed employee), the termination must not be arbitrary, discriminatory, or mala fide. Courts have held that the employer must exercise this right in good faith. Termination motivated by personal bias, retaliation, or discrimination can be challenged and overturned. It is always advisable to document the reasons for termination, even if the contract does not require it.
