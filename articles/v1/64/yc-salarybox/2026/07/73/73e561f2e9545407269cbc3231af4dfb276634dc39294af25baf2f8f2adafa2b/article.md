### **1. What is a Notice Period?**


A **notice period** is the duration of time between an employee’s resignation (or termination by the employer) and their last working day in the organisation. It serves as a critical transition window that allows both parties to prepare for the separation in an orderly manner.


During this period, the outgoing employee is expected to:


• Complete pending tasks and ongoing projects


• Hand over responsibilities, documents, and knowledge to a successor or team


• Return company assets such as laptops, ID cards, access badges, and keys


• Facilitate knowledge transfer sessions for continuity


• Allow the employer adequate time to recruit or reassign a replacement


For the employer, the notice period provides a buffer against sudden departures that could disrupt operations, damage client relationships, or cause loss of institutional knowledge. For the employee, it offers time to wrap up professional relationships and ensure a clean exit that protects their professional reputation and future career prospects.


**Key Point**
In India, notice periods are primarily governed by employment contracts and offer letters, not a single central law. The actual duration depends on your contract, company policy, seniority level, and the applicable state-level Shops & Establishments Act.


### **2. Legal Framework: Laws Governing Notice Period in India**


Unlike many Western countries where notice periods are standardised by national labour law, India does not have a single central statute that mandates a specific notice period for all private-sector employees. Instead, the legal framework is a patchwork of central laws, state regulations, and contractual provisions.


**2.1 Industrial Relations Code, 2020**


The Industrial Relations Code (IRC) 2020, which subsumes the Industrial Disputes Act 1947, applies primarily to “workers” and “workmen” in industrial establishments. Key provisions include:


• Retrenchment Notice: Employers must give 1 month’s written notice (or pay in lieu) when retrenching workers in establishments with fewer than 300 employees.


• For establishments with 100-299 workers: The notice period for retrenchment has been effectively increased to 60 days under the new provisions.


• Establishments with 300+ workers: Prior government approval is mandatory before retrenchment, layoff, or closure. A simple notice is not sufficient.


• Worker resignation: A worker must give 14 days’ notice before going on strike; for resignation, the contract or standing orders govern.


**2.2 State Shops & Establishments Acts**


Each Indian state has its own Shops & Establishments Act that governs working conditions for commercial establishments, shops, restaurants, theatres, and other non-factory entities. Notice period provisions vary by state:


• Maharashtra: 30 days’ notice or salary in lieu for termination after 3 months of service.


• Karnataka: 30 days’ notice or pay in lieu for employees with more than 6 months of service.


• Delhi: 30 days’ notice or salary in lieu for employees with more than 3 months of service.


• Tamil Nadu: 30 days’ notice for termination of employees who have completed 6 months.


• Gujarat: 30 days’ notice or salary in lieu for permanent employees.


**2.3 Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code, 2020**


The OSH Code applies to establishments with 10 or more workers and contract labour. While it primarily addresses workplace safety, it also contains provisions related to employment terms and conditions, including notice requirements for certain categories of workers.


**2.4 Employment Contracts & Offer Letters**


For the vast majority of private-sector employees, especially in IT, BFSI, consulting, and other white-collar industries, the notice period is determined entirely by the employment contract or offer letter. These contractual notice periods are legally binding, provided they are reasonable and not unconscionable.


**Important**
Courts have generally upheld contractual notice periods of up to 90 days as reasonable. However, excessively long notice periods (such as 6 months) or unreasonable penalty clauses may be struck down as restraint of trade under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

India’s IT sector has among the **highest notice periods globally** . Major IT services companies such as TCS, Infosys, Wipro, and HCL Technologies typically require **90 days (3 months)** of notice for confirmed employees. This is significantly longer than the 2-4 week standard in the United States or the 1-month standard in most European countries.


The rationale for extended notice periods in IT includes the time needed for client relationship transition, project handover, knowledge documentation, and the critical nature of ongoing technology projects.


**Industry Insight** Startups typically have the shortest notice periods (15-30 days) as they prioritise agility and speed. In contrast, Government and PSU roles often involve the longest notice periods (up to 3 months), reflecting bureaucratic processes and the time needed for formal transfers and charge handover.


### **4. Notice Period During Probation**


[Probation periods](https://salarybox.in/probation-period-tracker/) are trial phases during which both the employer and the employee assess mutual fit. Notice period rules during probation are notably different from those applicable to confirmed employees.


**Standard Probation Notice Periods**


• Typical range: 7 to 30 days (as specified in the appointment letter)


• Some companies allow termination with just 24 hours’ notice during probation


• Probation notice is almost always shorter than the confirmed employee notice period


• If not explicitly specified in the appointment letter, a reasonable notice of 15-30 days is generally expected by labour courts


**Key Considerations**


During probation, the employment relationship is more flexible. However, this does not mean either party can leave without any notice. Courts have held that even during probation, the principles of natural justice and fairness apply.


• Always check your appointment letter for the specific probation notice clause


• If the probation period is extended, the probation notice period typically continues to apply


• Some companies require a different notice period once probation is confirmed mid-way


• Exit during probation generally does not attract recovery or buyout penalties


**Pro Tip** Before joining any organisation, carefully read the probation clause in your offer letter. Understand not just the duration of probation but also the notice period during probation, performance review criteria, and what happens if probation is extended.


### **5. Notice Period Buy-Out: Calculation & Process**


A notice period buy-out occurs when an employee (or their new employer) pays the current employer an amount equivalent to salary for the remaining unserved notice days, in order to leave before completing the full notice period.


**5.1 When is Buy-Out Used?**


• Employee has received a better offer and the new employer needs them to join urgently


• Personal or family reasons require an early departure


• The employee is relocating to a different city or country


• Critical project deadlines at the new employer require immediate joining


• Mutual agreement between employee and current employer to end the relationship sooner


**5.2 Buy-Out Calculation Formula**


**Notice Period Buy-Out Formula** **Buy-Out Amount = (Monthly Salary / 30) x Unserved Notice Days**


**Important:** Some companies calculate the buy-out based on Basic Salary only, while others use Gross Salary (Basic + DA + HRA + other fixed components). Always refer to your employment contract for the exact clause.


**5.3 Buy-Out Example**


**Worked Example** Employee: Rahul, Software Engineer at a Bengaluru IT firm Notice period: 90 days | Days served: 30 days | Unserved days: 60 days Monthly Gross Salary: Rs 80,000 Buy-Out = (Rs 80,000 / 30) x 60 = Rs 1,60,000 Rahul (or his new employer) must pay Rs 1,60,000 to the current employer for early release.


**5.4 Who Pays the Buy-Out?**


**Scenario** **Who Pays** **Notes**


Employee resigns & wants early release Employee Deducted from F&F or paid separately


New employer reimburses New Employer Common in IT for senior hires


Employer releases early (employer-initiated) Employer Must pay salary for full notice period


Mutual agreement Negotiable Can agree on reduced amount or waiver


**5.5 Tax Implications of Buy-Out**


• When employer pays notice salary in lieu of notice: Taxable as salary income for the employee under the Income Tax Act.


• When employee pays buy-out to employer: This amount is NOT deductible as an expense under Income Tax for the employee. It is treated as a personal expense.


• When new employer reimburses buy-out: The reimbursement is part of the new employment terms and is typically taxable as part of salary or a joining bonus.


### **6. Notice Period Recovery: What Employers Can Deduct**


When an employee leaves without serving the full notice period (and without a buy-out arrangement), the employer has the right to recover the notice period shortfall. This is known as notice period recovery.


**6.1 Recovery Mechanism**


Employers have several mechanisms for recovering notice period dues:


1. Deduction from Full & Final (F&F) Settlement: The most common method. The employer deducts the unserved notice pay from the employee’s F&F amount, which includes pending salary, earned leave encashment, bonus, and other dues.


2. Withholding Relieving Letter: The employer can withhold the relieving letter and experience certificate until all dues (including notice recovery) are cleared. This is a powerful leverage point.


3. Civil Recovery: If the F&F settlement is insufficient to cover the recovery amount, the employer can pursue civil remedies under the Indian Contract Act, 1872 by filing a civil suit for breach of contract.


4. Background Verification Flag: Absence of a relieving letter is flagged by background verification agencies, which can impact the employee’s future job prospects.


**6.2 Recovery Calculation**


**Notice Period Recovery Formula** **Recovery = (Basic or Gross Salary as per contract / 30) x Unserved Days**


The exact salary component used for calculation (Basic Salary vs. Gross Salary) depends on the specific clause in your employment contract. In the absence of a specific clause, courts generally consider Basic Salary as the basis for recovery.


**Warning** Absconding from a job without serving any notice period can have serious consequences. Beyond financial recovery, it can result in being blacklisted by the company (and sometimes the entire group), negative references during background checks, and difficulty obtaining a relieving letter. Always try to negotiate an early release rather than absconding.


### **7. Garden Leave: Meaning, Rules & Comparison**


**Garden leave** (also called “gardening leave”) is a period during which an employee who has resigned or been terminated remains on the company’s payroll but is not required to attend office or perform any work. The term originates from the British practice of “sending the employee home to tend their garden.”


**7.1 Key Features of Garden Leave**


• The employee receives full salary and benefits during the garden leave period


• The employee cannot join another employer or engage in competing business activities


• Access to company systems, email, client lists, and confidential data is revoked


• Commonly used for senior employees in competitive industries such as IT, BFSI, and management consulting


• Duration is typically the full notice period or a significant part of it


• The arrangement is legally enforceable as part of the employment contract


**7.2 When Do Companies Use Garden Leave?**


• When a senior employee is joining a direct competitor


• When the employee has access to highly sensitive client data or trade secrets


• When there is a risk of the employee poaching colleagues or clients


• When the company wants to ensure confidential strategic plans remain protected


• When knowledge transfer is already complete, and physical presence adds no value


**7.3 Garden Leave vs Notice Period vs Paid Leave**

**Legal Note** Garden leave clauses are generally enforceable in India because, unlike non-compete clauses (which are void under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 post-employment), garden leave restricts activity during the employment period when the contract is still active and the employee is being compensated.


### **8. Early Release & Notice Period Waiver**


There are several scenarios where the full notice period may be shortened or waived entirely. Understanding these can help employees plan their transitions more effectively.


**Scenarios for Shortened Notice**


1. Mutual Agreement: Both the employee and employer agree to a shorter notice period. This is the most common and smoothest route to early release.


2. Employee Buy-Out: The employee pays for the remaining unserved notice days. The employer is not obligated to accept a buy-out, but most companies have a buy-out provision in their policy.


3. Employer-Initiated Early Release: The employer decides to release the employee early. In this case, the employer must pay salary for the full contractual notice period.


4. Medical Emergency: Employees facing genuine medical emergencies (with supporting documentation from a registered medical practitioner) are often granted early release on humanitarian grounds.


5. No Critical Handover Needed: If the employee’s role does not involve critical projects or client relationships, and a replacement is already available, the employer may waive the remaining notice.


6. Replacement Already Hired: If the company has already found and onboarded a replacement, the remaining notice period may serve no practical purpose and can be waived.


**Negotiation Tip** When requesting early release, frame it as a win-win. Offer to complete all handover documentation, train your replacement, and be available for questions via email or phone after your last day. Managers are more likely to grant early release when they feel confident that the transition will be smooth.


### **9. Legal Rights of Employees During Notice Period**


Employees in India have several important legal rights during their notice period. Understanding these rights ensures you are treated fairly and can make informed decisions.


1. Right to Resign: Every employee has the fundamental right to resign from employment. An employer cannot prevent an employee from resigning. The resignation takes effect once the notice period is served or bought out.


2. Right to Receive Full Salary: During the notice period, the employee is entitled to their full salary, allowances, and benefits as per their employment terms. The employer cannot reduce salary during notice.


3. Right to Use Earned Leave: Employees can utilise their accrued earned leave during the notice period, subject to company policy. Accrued earned leave is a statutory entitlement that cannot be forfeited.


4. Right to Receive a Relieving Letter: After serving the complete notice period and clearing all dues, the employee has the right to receive a relieving letter. Unreasonable withholding can be challenged in a Labour Court.


5. Right to Full & Final Settlement: The employee is entitled to receive their F&F settlement, including pending salary, leave encashment, bonus, and gratuity (if applicable), within 2 working days of their last working day under the new Labour Codes.


6. Right to Negotiate: Employees have the right to negotiate the notice period duration, request early release, or discuss buy-out terms. The employer is not obligated to agree, but negotiation is a legitimate right.


7. Right to Challenge Unfair Clauses: If the notice period clause in the employment contract is unconscionable or amounts to a restraint of trade, the employee can challenge it before a civil court or Labour Court.


### **10. Legal Rights of Employers**


Employers also have significant legal rights when it comes to notice periods. Understanding these rights helps employers protect their interests while ensuring compliance with labour laws.


1. Right to Enforce Contractual Notice: Employers can enforce the notice period as agreed in the employment contract. If the employee fails to serve notice, the employer can initiate recovery proceedings.


2. Right to Recover Notice Pay: If the employee leaves without serving the full notice, the employer can deduct the equivalent salary from the F&F settlement or pursue civil recovery.


3. Right to Withhold Relieving Letter: The employer can withhold the relieving letter and experience certificate until all outstanding dues, including notice period recovery, are cleared by the employee.


4. Right to Impose Garden Leave: Employers can place employees on garden leave during the notice period, restricting access to systems and clients while continuing to pay salary.


5. Right to Enforce Non-Compete During Notice: While post-employment non-compete clauses are generally void under Indian law, employers can enforce non-compete restrictions during the active notice period.


6. Right to Civil Remedies: For material breach of the employment contract (such as absconding or joining a competitor during notice), the employer can pursue civil remedies including injunctive relief and damages.


7. Right to Conduct Exit Process: Employers have the right to mandate exit interviews, asset return, knowledge transfer documentation, and security clearance as part of the notice period process.


### **11. Notice Period & Leaves: Can You Take Leave?**


One of the most frequently asked questions by employees serving notice is whether they can take leave during the notice period. The answer depends on company policy, the type of leave, and the terms of the employment contract.


**Types of Leave During Notice**


**Leave Type** **Generally Allowed?** **Impact on Notice Period**


Earned Leave / Privilege Leave Yes (or encashed) Does not extend notice (most companies)


Casual Leave Restricted by most companies May extend notice by equivalent days


Sick Leave (with medical cert.) Yes (genuine illness) May extend notice by equivalent days


Maternity / Paternity Leave Yes (statutory right) Runs concurrently or post-notice


Compensatory Off At employer discretion Does not extend notice


Leave Without Pay (LWP) Rare during notice Extends notice by equivalent days


**Key Rules**


• Most companies restrict casual leave and discourage sick leave during the notice period unless supported by a medical certificate from a registered medical practitioner.


• Accrued earned leave is a statutory right. The employer cannot force forfeiture of earned leave; it can either be utilised (if the company allows) or encashed as part of the F&F settlement.


• Some company policies state that any leave taken during notice extends the notice period by an equivalent number of days. Check your employee handbook or HR policy document for this clause.


• Public holidays and weekly offs do NOT extend the notice period as they are not classified as leave.


**Best Practice** If you plan to take leave during your notice period, communicate early with your manager and HR. Getting approval in writing (email confirmation) protects you from disputes about your last working day and F&F settlement date.


### **12. Relieving Letter: Importance & Rules**


The relieving letter is arguably the most important document issued at the end of an employment relationship. It serves as formal confirmation that the employee has been officially released from all duties and obligations.


**12.1 What a Relieving Letter Contains**


• Full name of the employee


• Employee ID or reference number


• Designation and department at the time of exit


• Date of joining and last working day


• Confirmation that all company assets have been returned


• Confirmation that all dues have been settled


• Statement that the employee has been officially relieved from duties


**12.2 Why is the Relieving Letter Critical?**


• Background Verification: Most reputable employers conduct background verification for new hires. The absence of a relieving letter from a previous employer is a major red flag that can lead to offer withdrawal.


• Future Employment: Many employers require the relieving letter as a mandatory joining document. Without it, the new employer may not process your onboarding.


• Legal Proof: The relieving letter serves as legal proof that you left your previous employment on good terms and fulfilled all contractual obligations.


• Professional Reputation: A clean exit with a proper relieving letter reflects professionalism and protects your long-term career reputation.


**12.3 What if the Employer Withholds the Relieving Letter?**


An employer cannot unreasonably withhold the relieving letter once the employee has served the notice period and cleared all dues. If the employer withholds without valid reason:


• Step 1: Send a formal written request (email with acknowledgement) to HR and your reporting manager.


• Step 2: Escalate to senior management with documented proof of notice served and dues cleared.


• Step 3: Approach the Labour Commissioner or file a complaint with the local Labour Court.


• Step 4: Courts can direct the employer to issue the relieving letter and may award compensation for the delay.

**Impact on Employees**


• Faster F&F Settlements: The 2-working-day mandate for F&F settlement ensures employees receive their dues promptly upon exit. Previously, F&F could be delayed for weeks or even months.


• Fixed-Term Clarity: Employees on fixed-term contracts now have clarity that the contract end date is the natural conclusion of the employment, and no separate notice period applies.


• Greater Protection for Workers: The increased retrenchment notice period (60 days for establishments with 100-299 workers) provides a longer buffer for affected workers to find alternative employment.


**Impact on Employers**


• Compliance Burden: The 2-day F&F settlement timeline requires employers to have robust exit management and payroll systems in place.


• Higher Retrenchment Threshold: The requirement of government approval for retrenchment in establishments with 300+ workers adds a procedural step that can delay workforce restructuring.


• Documentation Requirements: Employers must maintain detailed records of notice periods served, buy-out payments, and F&F settlements to demonstrate compliance during audits.

### **15. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)**


**Q: What is a notice period?**


A: A notice period is the duration between an employee’s resignation or termination and their last working day. It allows time for knowledge transfer, project handover, and replacement hiring. The duration is typically specified in the employment contract or offer letter.


**Q: What is the typical notice period in IT companies in India?**


A: Most large IT companies in India (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL) require 90 days (3 months) of notice for confirmed employees. Junior or entry-level employees may have 30-day notice periods, while mid-level roles typically require 60 days.


**Q: Can I resign without serving the notice period?**


A: Yes, you can resign at any time as resignation is a fundamental right. However, if you do not serve the notice period, the employer can deduct notice pay from your Full & Final settlement, withhold your relieving letter, and in extreme cases, pursue civil recovery under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.


**Q: What is notice period buy-out?**


A: A buy-out is when the employee (or their new employer) pays the current employer salary for the unserved notice days in order to leave early. The formula is: (Monthly Salary / 30) x Unserved Notice Days. The exact salary component (Basic or Gross) depends on the employment contract.


**Q: How is notice period recovery calculated?**


A: Recovery = (Basic Salary or Gross Salary as per contract / 30) x Number of Unserved Days. The employer deducts this from the F&F settlement. If the F&F amount is insufficient, the employer can pursue civil recovery.


**Q: What is garden leave?**


A: Garden leave is a period where the employee remains on the company payroll but does not work or attend office. The employee receives full salary but cannot join a competitor or access company systems. It is commonly used for senior employees in competitive industries to protect sensitive business information.


**Q: Can an employer force a 90-day notice period?**


A: Yes, if the 90-day notice period is specified in your signed employment contract or offer letter, it is legally enforceable. Courts have upheld notice periods of up to 90 days as reasonable. However, notice periods longer than 90 days may be challenged if deemed unreasonable.


**Q: Is a notice period mandatory during probation?**


A: Probation notice periods are typically shorter (7-30 days) and may even allow termination with 24 hours’ notice. The specific terms are defined in your appointment letter. If no notice period is mentioned for probation, a reasonable notice of 15-30 days is generally expected.


**Q: Can I take leave during the notice period?**


A: This depends on your company’s leave policy. Most companies restrict casual leave during notice but allow utilisation or encashment of earned leave. Sick leave with a valid medical certificate is usually permitted. Some companies extend the notice period by the number of leave days taken.


**Q: What if my employer does not give me a relieving letter?**


A: If you have served your complete notice period and cleared all dues, the employer cannot unreasonably withhold the relieving letter. You can send a formal written request, escalate to senior management, and if necessary, approach the Labour Commissioner or Labour Court for relief.


**Q: What are the notice period rules under the new Labour Codes?**


A: The new Labour Codes increased the retrenchment notice from 30 to 60 days for establishments with 100-299 workers, mandate F&F settlement within 2 working days, and clarify that fixed-term contracts end naturally without requiring separate notice. Government approval is needed for retrenchment in establishments with 300+ workers.


**Q: Can the notice period be negotiated?**


A: Yes, notice periods can be negotiated between the employee and employer. This can happen at the time of joining (negotiating a shorter notice clause in the offer letter) or at the time of resignation (requesting early release). However, the employer is not obligated to agree to a shorter notice period.
