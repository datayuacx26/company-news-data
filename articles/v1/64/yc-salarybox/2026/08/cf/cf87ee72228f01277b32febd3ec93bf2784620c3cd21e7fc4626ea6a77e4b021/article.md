You requested a background verification on a candidate. The report landed in your inbox. You open it and see a mix of statuses, document numbers, confidence scores, and verification outcomes. Some sections say “verified.” One says “unable to verify.” Another shows a partial match.


Now what? Do you hire the candidate? Do you reject them? Is that partial match a deal breaker or just a data entry error?


Most HR managers in India know that background verification is important. Far fewer know how to actually read and interpret the results. And that gap between running checks and understanding them is where bad hiring decisions get made. A report that shows a minor, explainable discrepancy gets treated as a rejection. A report with a subtle but serious red flag gets overlooked because the reader did not know what to look for.


This guide will teach you how to read a background verification report like a professional. We will cover what each section means, how to interpret different verification statuses, which discrepancies are harmless and which ones should stop a hire, and how to make confident, informed decisions based on verification data.


## The Anatomy of a Background Verification Report


While report formats vary between verification providers, most background verification reports in India contain the same core sections. Let us go through each one.


### Candidate Information Summary


This section lists the basic details of the candidate as provided by them or by your HR team. It typically includes full name, date of birth, contact details, position applied for, and the date the verification was initiated.


**What to check.** Confirm that the candidate information in the report matches the person you are verifying. Errors here mean the entire report may be about the wrong person.


### Identity Verification Results


This is the most fundamental section. It shows whether the candidate’s identity documents were successfully verified against government databases.


For a PAN card check, the report will show the PAN number that was submitted, the name and date of birth returned by the Income Tax Department database, and whether these match the candidate’s claimed details.


For a Voter ID check, it shows the EPIC number submitted, the name and details returned by the Election Commission database, and the match status.


For a Driving Licence check, it shows the DL number, the details from the VAHAN/SARATHI database including licence validity and vehicle class, and the match status.


**Possible statuses and what they mean:**


**“Verified” or “Match.”** The document number is valid and the details returned by the government database match the candidate’s claimed information. This is the best outcome.


**“Partial Match.”** Some details match but others do not. For example, the PAN number is valid and the name matches, but the date of birth is off by one digit. This requires investigation but is not necessarily a disqualifier. It could be a data entry error at the government end, a typo in the original document application, or a genuine discrepancy that needs explanation.


**“No Match” or “Mismatch.”** The details returned by the database do not match the candidate’s claims. The PAN number might belong to a different person, or the Voter ID might show a completely different name. This is a serious red flag that requires immediate attention.


**“Invalid” or “Not Found.”** The document number does not exist in the database. This could mean the document is fake, the number was entered incorrectly, or the document has been deactivated. Confirm the number with the candidate before treating this as a rejection.


**“Unable to Verify.”** The database was temporarily unavailable or the check could not be completed for technical reasons. This is not a negative result. Retry the check after some time.


### Cross Record Confidence Analysis


When multiple documents are verified for the same candidate, a good verification platform compares the details across all documents. ID Verify by SalaryBox provides this analysis automatically.


**What it checks:**


Does the name match across PAN, Voter ID, and Driving Licence? Do the dates of birth match across all documents? Are there any inconsistencies in the details returned by different government databases?


**How to interpret the results:**


**All documents consistent.** Name, date of birth, and other details match across all verified documents. This is strong confirmation of the candidate’s identity.


**Minor variations.** Small differences in name spelling (Rajesh vs Rajesh Kumar, or Sharma vs Sharman) are common and usually harmless. They often result from different transliteration conventions or data entry practices at different government offices. Note the variation but do not treat it as a red flag.


**Significant discrepancies.** Different names, different dates of birth, or different genders across documents are serious red flags. If the PAN card says “Amit Kumar born 1990” and the Voter ID says “Amit Singh born 1988,” you are either dealing with a data error that the candidate can explain or, more concerning, with documents that belong to different people.


### UAN Employment History


When a UAN check is run, the report shows the candidate’s employment history as recorded in EPFO’s database. This includes previous employer names (as registered with EPFO), dates when PF contributions started and stopped at each employer, and contribution amounts.


**How to read it:**


Compare the employment history shown in the UAN report with what the candidate listed on their resume. Check whether the employer names match. Verify that employment dates align (start and end dates at each company). Look for gaps between employments that the candidate did not disclose. Check if there are employers listed in the EPFO record that the candidate omitted from their resume.


**Common findings:**


**Resume matches UAN record.** The candidate’s claimed employment history aligns with their actual PF contribution history. This is the ideal outcome.


**Dates are slightly off.** A difference of a month or two between resume dates and PF contribution dates is common and usually harmless. Companies often start PF contributions a few weeks after joining, or continue contributions slightly after an employee’s last working day. A discrepancy of one to two months is normal.


**Employer name looks different.** Companies often register with EPFO under their legal entity name, which may differ from their trading name or brand name. “XYZ Technologies Private Limited” in the EPFO record might be the same company the candidate listed as “XYZ Tech” on their resume. Check before flagging this as a discrepancy.


**Missing employer.** The candidate listed a company on their resume, but it does not appear in the UAN record. This could mean the employer did not deduct PF (common in very small companies or informal employment), the candidate was a contractor or freelancer at that company (no PF applicability), or the candidate fabricated the employment. Discuss with the candidate to determine which scenario applies.


**Extra employer.** The UAN record shows an employer that the candidate did not list on their resume. This is more concerning. The candidate may have deliberately omitted an employer, possibly because they were terminated or had a negative experience there. Ask about it.


**Long gaps.** If the UAN record shows a gap of several months between two employers that the candidate’s resume does not reflect, they may have stretched dates to cover the gap. This is one of the most common forms of resume manipulation.


### Criminal Record Check Results


If a criminal record check was part of the verification, the report will show whether any court cases or criminal records were found.


**Possible outcomes:**


**“No records found.”** No criminal cases were found in the jurisdictions searched. Note that this is limited to the jurisdictions that were searched. India does not have a centralised criminal database, so a clean result only covers the districts and courts where the search was conducted.


**“Record found.”** A court case was found matching the candidate’s name and other details. The report should specify the nature of the case (criminal, civil, or both), the section of law involved, the current status (pending, disposed, convicted, acquitted), and the court and jurisdiction.


**How to interpret criminal record findings:**


Not every record is a disqualifier. A civil dispute is very different from a criminal charge. A traffic violation is different from a fraud case. An acquitted case is different from a pending charge.


Consider the nature of the offence and its relevance to the role. A theft charge is highly relevant for a role handling inventory. A traffic violation is less relevant for an office role. Consider how recently the offence occurred. A case from fifteen years ago may be less relevant than one from last year. Consider the current status. An acquittal means the person was found not guilty. A pending case means the matter is not yet resolved.


Always discuss criminal record findings with the candidate before making a decision. There may be context that the report does not capture.


### Address Verification Results


Address checks confirm whether the candidate’s claimed address is genuine.


**“Verified.”** The address exists and matches the candidate’s claim. In physical verification, a field agent confirmed the candidate’s residence at the address.


**“Partial match.”** The address exists but some details do not match perfectly. For example, the flat number might be slightly different, or the locality name might be spelled differently.


**“Unable to verify.”** The address could not be confirmed. This could mean the candidate recently moved, the address is in a newly developed area not yet in databases, or the address is incorrect.


### Education Verification Results


Education checks confirm whether the candidate holds the degrees and certifications they claim.


**“Verified.”** The educational institution confirmed that the candidate graduated with the stated degree and year.


**“Discrepancy.”** The institution’s records show different information than what the candidate claimed. This could be a different year of graduation, a different degree or specialization, or the candidate’s name not appearing in the institution’s records at all.


**“Unable to verify.”** The institution did not respond or records were unavailable. This is not a negative result but it means the education claim remains unconfirmed.


## The Decision Framework: When to Hire, When to Hold, When to Reject


Reading a verification report is only useful if you know how to turn the findings into a hiring decision. Here is a practical framework.


### Green Light: Proceed with Confidence


All identity documents verified with matching details. UAN employment history aligns with the resume. No criminal records found in searched jurisdictions. Cross record analysis shows consistent details across documents. Any variations are minor and easily explained (spelling differences, minor date variations).


### Yellow Light: Investigate Before Deciding


A partial match on one identity document that could be a data entry error. One employer on the resume not appearing in UAN records (could be legitimate if the employer was small or the work was informal). A minor, non violent, old criminal case that has been disposed of. An education check that returned “unable to verify” due to the institution not responding.


For yellow light findings, always discuss with the candidate before making a decision. Most yellow light situations have innocent explanations. The goal is to find those explanations, not to automatically reject.


### Red Light: Serious Concern, Likely Rejection


Identity documents show significant mismatches (different names, different dates of birth). PAN or Voter ID is invalid or belongs to a different person. Multiple employers on the resume are not found in UAN records. Recent criminal charges involving violence, fraud, theft, or sexual offences. Cross record analysis reveals inconsistencies that the candidate cannot explain. The candidate refuses to discuss discrepancies or becomes evasive.


Red light findings typically warrant not proceeding with the hire. However, even in red light situations, give the candidate an opportunity to explain before making a final decision. Document the findings, the candidate’s response, and your decision rationale.


## Common Mistakes When Reading Verification Reports


### Mistake 1: Treating Every Discrepancy as a Rejection


Not every discrepancy is fraud. Government databases in India contain errors. Data entry at Aadhaar centres, PAN offices, and election commission booths is done by humans who make mistakes. A name spelled slightly differently across two documents is far more likely to be a transliteration issue than identity fraud.


The question is not “is there any discrepancy?” but “is the discrepancy consistent with an innocent explanation?”


### Mistake 2: Ignoring Minor Discrepancies Entirely


The opposite mistake is equally dangerous. Dismissing all discrepancies as “probably just data errors” without investigating can let genuine fraud slip through. A one letter spelling difference is probably harmless. A completely different name is probably not. Use judgment, not blanket rules.


### Mistake 3: Not Discussing Findings with the Candidate


Verification reports provide data, not context. Only the candidate can provide context. Always discuss any concerning findings with the candidate before making a decision. Their response, whether it is a credible explanation, an implausible excuse, or outright evasiveness, is itself valuable information.


### Mistake 4: Making Decisions Based on Incomplete Checks


If a check returned “unable to verify” due to a technical issue, do not treat that as a clean result. Retry the check. An incomplete verification is not the same as a successful one.


### Mistake 5: Not Documenting the Decision


Whatever decision you make, document it. Record the verification findings, any discussion with the candidate, the candidate’s explanation for discrepancies, and the rationale for your hiring decision. This documentation protects you legally if questions arise later and helps maintain consistency across hiring decisions.

## Frequently Asked Questions


### What does “partial match” mean in a verification report?


A partial match means some details verified correctly while others did not match. For example, the name might match but the date of birth might differ by one digit. This could be a data entry error at the government end, a typo in the original application, or a genuine discrepancy. Discuss with the candidate to determine the cause.


### Should I reject a candidate if the UAN check shows an employer they did not list on their resume?


Not automatically. Ask the candidate why that employer was omitted. There may be a legitimate reason, such as a very short stint, a negative experience, or a freelance engagement that the candidate did not consider formal employment. However, if they deny ever working there despite EPFO records showing otherwise, that is a serious concern.


### How should I handle a criminal record finding?


Consider the nature of the offence, how long ago it occurred, whether it is relevant to the role, and the current case status. Always discuss the finding with the candidate before making a decision. An acquitted case or a minor, old offence may not be disqualifying. A recent, relevant, serious charge almost certainly is.


### Is it legal to reject a candidate based on background verification results?


Yes, provided the rejection is based on legitimate, role relevant findings. Fake identity documents, fabricated credentials, and concealed criminal records are valid grounds for rejecting a candidate. However, you cannot reject candidates based on protected characteristics (caste, religion, gender, disability) that may appear in verification results.


### What should I do if the verification report shows “unable to verify” for a check?


Do not treat it as either positive or negative. “Unable to verify” means the check could not be completed, usually due to a technical issue with the database or the institution not responding. Retry the check after some time. If repeated attempts fail, use alternative methods to verify the same information.


### How long should I keep verification reports?


Maintain verification reports for the duration of the employee’s tenure plus at least three years after they leave the company. This covers the typical limitation period for employment related legal claims and provides documentation if any issues surface after the employee’s departure.
