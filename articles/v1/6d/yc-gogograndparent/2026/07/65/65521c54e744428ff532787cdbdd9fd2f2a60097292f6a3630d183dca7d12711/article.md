# The Sandwich Generation: How to Care for Aging Parents and Kids at the Same Time

The sandwich generation is the group of adults caring for their own children and their aging parents at the same time, and if that's you, you're far from alone. In a 2021 survey by[Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/04/08/more-than-half-of-americans-in-their-40s-are-sandwiched-between-an-aging-parent-and-their-own-children/) , about 23% of U.S. adults were part of the sandwich generation, and among people in their 40s it was 54%.


Nobody signs up for two full-time caregiving jobs, and most people in this spot didn't see it coming. One day you're helping with homework, and the next you're also booking your dad's doctor visits and worrying about whether your mom should still be driving.


This guide is for the caregivers in the middle. It covers what the sandwich generation really is, why it feels so heavy, and the practical steps that make it lighter. The goal is simple. Caring for the people you love shouldn't mean losing yourself in the process.


## What is the sandwich generation?


The sandwich generation is anyone raising children while also caring for an aging parent, which leaves them "sandwiched" between two generations who both need them. That care can look like rides to appointments, help paying bills, managing medications, or just being the person everyone calls when something goes wrong.


It's more common than most people realize. In that same Pew survey, 23% of American adults were living it, and it peaks in midlife. About 27% of people in their 30s and 54% of people in their 40s had both a parent over 65 and a child they were still supporting.


The word makes it sound tidy. The reality rarely is.


## Why does caring for two generations feel so overwhelming?


Because the load is real, and the numbers back that up.


Across all family caregivers, not only the sandwich generation, people spend about 27 hours a week on care, according to[AARP's 2026 report](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/ltss/family-caregiving/valuing-the-invaluable-2026-update/) . That is most of a second job, on top of the one many caregivers already have. AARP counts 59 million family caregivers in the U.S., providing unpaid care valued at roughly $1 trillion a year. The sandwich generation is one slice of that huge, mostly invisible workforce, and if you're reading this, you're likely part of it.


The strain shows up in both your wallet and your health. A[University of Michigan study](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/sandwich-generation-study-shows-challenges-caring-both-kids-and-aging-parents) found that people caring for both a parent and a child were twice as likely to report financial difficulty, 36% compared to 17% of those caring only for an aging parent. They were also more likely to report serious emotional strain, 44% versus 32%.


Here's the part nobody says out loud. You can love your parents and your kids deeply and still feel exhausted, guilty, and stretched too thin. That doesn't make you a bad daughter, son, or parent. It makes you human, and it usually means something needs to give.


## How can you build support before you burn out?


The single biggest mistake is waiting until you're already in crisis, because support works best when it's set up early. Here's where to start.


- **Name the tasks.** Write down everything you do for your parent and your kids in a normal week, because seeing it on paper helps you spot what can be handed off.
- **Build a small care team.** You don't have to be the only one. A care team can include siblings, neighbors, a part-time aide, delivery services, and transportation help, and each piece you add gives you hours back.
- **Ask family to own a lane.** Instead of asking a sibling to "help more," give them one clear job, so that one person handles finances, another handles medical appointments, and another handles groceries.
- **Look into respite care.** Respite care gives you a short break while someone else steps in, and even a few hours a week can protect your health.
- **Automate the routine.** Recurring pharmacy refills, grocery orders, and scheduled rides can run in the background, so you're not managing every errand by hand.


Asking for help isn't failing. It's what makes the whole thing sustainable.


## What daily tasks can you take off your plate?


A lot of caregiving is really just logistics: getting your mom to dialysis, making sure your dad has groceries, picking up a prescription before it runs out. These are the tasks that eat your day, and they're also the easiest to delegate.


Transportation is usually the first bottleneck. When a parent no longer drives, that responsibility often lands on you, right in the middle of your workday or your kid's school pickup. Handing it off frees up more time than almost anything else. Services like GoGoGrandparent were built to carry that load, along with grocery delivery, meal delivery, and pharmacy delivery, none of which require you to be in two places at once.


The point isn't to do less for your family. It's to spend your limited energy on the parts only you can do, and let reliable help carry the rest.

## How do you take care of yourself in the middle of all this?


You can't pour from an empty cup, and caregivers run dry fast, so protecting your own health isn't selfish. It's what keeps you able to show up.


A few small habits go a long way:


- **Guard one thing that's yours.** A walk, a class, or coffee with a friend. Put it on the calendar and treat it like any other appointment.
- **Say yes to help when it's offered.** When someone asks what they can do, have an answer ready, whether that's a grocery run, an afternoon with your kids, or a ride for your parent.
- **Watch for burnout signs.** Constant exhaustion, a short temper, trouble sleeping, and pulling away from people are all signals to lean on your care team harder.
- **Talk to someone.** A support group, a counselor, or even one honest friend can lighten the mental load, because many people in the sandwich generation feel isolated and they don't have to.


Caring for two generations is one of the hardest things a person can do, so give yourself the same patience you'd give everyone else.
