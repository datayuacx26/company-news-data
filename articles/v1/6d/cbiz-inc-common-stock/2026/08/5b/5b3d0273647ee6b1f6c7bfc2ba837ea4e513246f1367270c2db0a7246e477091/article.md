# When Should an Employee Receive a Promotion?

## A Practical Three-Test Framework for in-Job Promotions


As organizations place greater emphasis on job architecture and career progression, we’ve seen a corresponding increase in client questions around when a promotion should take place.


While promotion philosophies vary, most employers are ultimately evaluating the same considerations. Before awarding an in-job promotion, organizations should be able to answer three questions:


- Has the employee developed?
- Has the work evolved?
- Does this fit our promotion philosophy?


These three questions can bring greater clarity and consistency to promotion decisions.


## Has the Employee Developed?


Organizations invest in training, development, and coaching to support employee growth and increase individual contributions. A promotion may be appropriate when that development produces sustained changes in how the employee performs the work.


**Evidence may include:**


- Greater independence
- More complex assignments
- Better judgment
- Broader impact
- Increased problem-solving responsibility
- Deeper technical or functional expertise


Strong performance at the current level is not necessarily evidence of readiness for the next level. Performance reflects how well an employee meets the expectations of the current role. Promotion reflects a meaningful and sustained increase in capability, responsibility, or contribution.


Recognition may be appropriate through pay, incentives, development opportunities, or expanded assignments rather than promotion for employees that are highly effective but not performing higher-level work.


## Has the Work Evolved?


Next, the organization must also determine whether there is an ongoing need for that capability in the employee’s current role. Relevant considerations include:


- Has the role shifted toward more complex or independent work?
- Is there enough higher-level work to support the promotion?
- Is the organization using the employee’s increased capability in the role?
- Is the higher-level work expected to continue?
- Does the work represent a meaningful part of the job rather than an occasional assignment?


Temporary projects, coverage for a staffing gap, or a short-term increase in workload may provide valuable development opportunities without permanently changing the level of the job.


## Does This Fit Our Promotion Philosophy?


The final consideration is the organization’s advancement philosophy. Some organizations place greater weight on employee readiness and promote once an employee demonstrates sustained next-level capability. Others place greater weight on business needs and require the role to support more complex work, broader responsibility, or greater impact.


Neither approach is inherently right or wrong. Each reflects a different balance between creating advancement opportunities and tying promotions to the work the organization needs to perform.


The important issue is consistency. Employees and managers should understand whether readiness alone can support advancement or whether promotion also requires a demonstrated need for higher-level work. Without a clear philosophy, similar cases may produce different outcomes based on the manager, department, or timing of the request. The organization’s approach should be deliberate, clearly communicated, and applied consistently.


## The Three-Test Framework in Practice


Promotion decisions are usually clearest at earlier career levels. The difference between job levels at the early career stage is often easier to identify because the employee is moving from learning the job to performing it with greater independence, consistency, and judgment.


Decisions become harder at higher levels. Continued progression depends less on basic competence and more on whether the work supports increased complexity, broader impact, greater influence, or deeper expertise.


Not every job family supports the same amount of in-job progression. Technical and specialized roles may allow for multiple levels of advancement because the work can continue to increase in complexity and value. Administrative and support roles may reach a practical ceiling sooner. Further growth may require a different role, expanded responsibilities, or movement into a management track.


## Make Promotion Decisions With Greater Consistency


In-job promotions are most defensible when the employee has developed, the work requires ongoing use of that development, and the decision aligns with the organization’s advancement philosophy.


A well-designed career framework should create opportunity while preserving meaningful distinctions between levels. Promotions are most effective when they recognize real development, reflect the work being performed, and are applied consistently across the organization.

## Two Types of Promotions


### In-Job Promotion


Advancement within an established job family or career path, typically reflecting increased capability, experience, or responsibility within the same general role.


**Example: Analyst → Senior Analyst**


### Role-Based Promotion


Advancement into a higher-level role that becomes available because of a vacancy, organizational growth, restructuring, or the creation of a new position.


**Example: Manager → Director**
