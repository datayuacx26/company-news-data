# How to Study for the SAT — A Proven 3-Month Study Plan

Preparing for the SAT can feel overwhelming, but a structured plan makes all the difference. This guide breaks down exactly how to study for the SAT over three months — the sweet spot for meaningful score gains without burning out.


## Step 1: Take a diagnostic test


Before you study anything, you need a baseline. Take a full-length, timed[SAT practice exam](https://miyagilabs.ai/sat/practice-exams) under realistic conditions. Your diagnostic score tells you two things:


- **Where you're starting** — your current Reading & Writing and Math section scores.
- **What to prioritize** — the question types and skills you miss most often.


You can also use an[SAT score predictor](https://miyagilabs.ai/sat/score-predictors) to get a data-backed estimate of where you stand and how far you are from your target.


## Step 2: Set a realistic target score


Look up the middle-50% SAT range for the colleges you're applying to. Your target should land at or above the 75th percentile of your top-choice schools. A realistic goal for three months of consistent prep is a **100–200 point improvement** .


## Step 3: Build a weekly routine


Consistency beats cramming. Here's a sample week-by-week structure:


### Weeks 1–4: Build fundamentals


- 30–45 minutes of[SAT practice questions](https://miyagilabs.ai/sat/practice) per day, focused on your weakest skills.
- Review every wrong answer until you understand *why* the correct answer is right.


### Weeks 5–8: Drill and reinforce


- Mix in timed[problem sets](https://miyagilabs.ai/sat/psets) to build pacing.
- Track your accuracy by category so you can see which areas are improving.


### Weeks 9–12: Simulate the real thing


- Take one full-length practice exam each week.
- Review thoroughly, then target the specific skills that cost you points.


## Step 4: Master your mistakes


The single highest-leverage habit in SAT prep is keeping an **error log** . For every question you miss, write down the concept tested and why you got it wrong. Patterns will emerge fast — and fixing patterns is how scores jump.
