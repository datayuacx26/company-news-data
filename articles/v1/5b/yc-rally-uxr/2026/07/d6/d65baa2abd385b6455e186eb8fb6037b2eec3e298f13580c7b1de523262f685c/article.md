## **Before You Start: Check What Already Exists**


The best research doesn't always require a new study. Start here:


**1.** *"Search all past research for findings related to \[topic or feature area\]. What has our team already learned about this? Include study names, who ran them, when, and how many sessions were completed."*


**2.** *"Are there any active studies currently running that touch on \[user behavior, workflow, or feature\]? I want to make sure I'm not duplicating something already in progress."*


**3.** *"What research has been run on \[user segment\] in the last 12 months? Summarize the key findings and flag any conflicting insights across studies."*


**4.** *"Pull the transcript and recording links from any prior sessions where participants talked about \[topic\]. I want to review what we already know before writing a new discussion guide."*


‍


## **Planning: Sharpen Your Question And Choose Your Method**


**5.** *"I'm trying to understand \[rough research question\]. Help me sharpen this into a specific, answerable research question and write a short research brief - including the decision it maps to, what we're assuming, recommended methodology, and participant criteria."*


**6.** *"I need to choose a research method. My goal is \[research goal\], my timeline is \[X weeks\], and I have capacity for \[N\] participant sessions. What method would you recommend and why? What are the tradeoffs?"*


**7.** *"Review these research questions and tell me: are any of them too broad to be answerable? Do any overlap? Which should we prioritize if we can only answer one this sprint?"*


**8.** *"We have three competing research questions and only the capacity for one study this quarter. Here's the context on each: \[paste questions and business context\]. Which would you prioritize based on decision impact, and what's the fastest method to answer it?"*


‍


## **Recruiting: Find The Right People Without Burning Your Panel**


Every prompt below checks your org's governance rules automatically - how often people are allowed to be contacted, if they’re on a blocklist, or if they’ve opted out. You'll only ever see participants who are free to contact.


**9.** *"Find participants in our panel who match these criteria: \[role, company size, product usage, or other filters\]. Show me anyone who is free to contact right now."*


**10.** *"I need \[N\] participants for a study on \[topic\]. Before you search, check what properties we track in Rally so you know what's available to filter on. Then find people who match \[criteria\].”*


**11.** *"Has \[participant name or email\] been in any prior studies? What did they say, when were they last contacted, and are they currently free to contact?"*


**12.** *"How many participants in our panel fit \[criteria\]? I want to know if we have enough to recruit from today or whether we need to build this panel before we can run the study."*


**13.** *"We're thinking about expanding research into \[new segment or market\]. What do we currently have in our participant pool that matches? Break it down by free to contact, in cooldown, and blocked."*


‍


## **Study Setup: From Idea To Live Study**


**14.** *"Set up a \[moderated interview / unmoderated usability test / survey\] study in Rally. The research question is \[question\]. Sessions should be \[X\] minutes, targeting \[N\] participants, from \[panel or segment\]. Use our standard consent form and set the incentive to \[$X\]."*


**15.** *"Build a screener for a study about \[topic\]. Qualifying criteria: \[list\]. Disqualifying criteria: \[list\]. Keep it under six questions and don't make the right answer obvious.”*


**16.** *"Draft a 45-minute discussion guide for a \[study type\] about \[topic\]. The core research question is \[question\]. I need to cover: \[list of areas\]. Keep questions open-ended and flag anything that reads as leading."*


**17.** *"Review this discussion guide and flag any leading questions, closed-ended questions, or questions that reveal my hypothesis before the participant answers. Suggest neutral alternatives for each one you flag."*


‍


## **Scheduling And Logistics: The Coordination Layer You Don't Want To Manage Manually**


Every study template in Rally can be configured with built-in automated reminder sequences. When a researcher launches a study from a template, participant reminders are already set up and scheduled to send automatically at the right intervals before each session.


**18.** *"Schedule \[N\] interviews for \[timeframe\]. Find participants who match \[criteria\] and are free to contact, send calendar invites with \[conference link\], and set up automated reminder emails 24 hours before each session."*


**19.** *"Send a reminder to all participants scheduled for \[study\] tomorrow. Include the conference link and a note that the session will be recorded with their prior consent."*


**20.** *"One participant hasn't confirmed their session. Draft a short, friendly reminder I can send them and suggest two alternative times if they need to reschedule."*


**21.** *"I have interviews coming up this week. For each scheduled session, give me the participant's name, timezone, conference link, and any prior study history or transcripts I should review before we speak."*


‍


## **Panel Health: Protect Your Most Valuable Research Asset**


**22.** *"Give me a panel health report. I want to know show rates, no-show rates, how many participants are in cooldown, how many are on the blocklist, and how many haven't been contacted in over six months. Flag anything that looks like it's trending the wrong way."*


**23.** *"How many participants in \[segment or population\] are currently free to contact? How many are in cooldown and when do those cooldowns expire? I want to understand our actual recruiting capacity before I kick off a new study."*


**24.** *"Which participants have been contacted the most in the last 90 days across all studies? I want to make sure we're not over-relying on the same people."*


**25.** *"Pull a list of participants who completed a study in the last six months but haven't been re-engaged since. Flag anyone who rated their experience positively - these are the people most likely to say yes if we reach out again."*


‍


## **Analysis And Synthesis: Turn Sessions Into Something Usable**


**26.** *"Analyze the transcripts from \[study\]. Identify the top themes, group them by how frequently they appeared across sessions, and pull two or three direct quotes for each. Flag anything that only came up in one session - I want to know what's significant versus what's a one-off."*


**27.** *"Across these sessions, where are the gaps between what participants said they do and what was actually observed? List them specifically."*


**28.** *"Rate the confidence level of each finding from this study - high, medium, or low - based on how frequently it appeared and whether it's consistent with prior research in our repository."*


**29.** *"Compare findings across \[study A\] and \[study B\]. Where do they align, where do they contradict each other, and what does that tell us? Flag anywhere the two studies point to different conclusions about the same user behavior."*


‍


## **Shareout: Get Findings To The People Who Need Them**


**30.** *"Generate a quarterly research program report for \[Q and year\]. Look across all studies in the workspace - active, closed, draft, and paused - and tell me how many studies ran, what types they were, who owned them, which researchers were most active, and what the headline findings were. Format it as a report I can share with leadership."*


**BONUS!** *"Write a one-paragraph research update I can post in our team Slack channel. Summarize what we learned in \[study\], connect it to the product decision it informs, and make it concrete enough that someone who wasn't in the sessions understands why it matters."*
