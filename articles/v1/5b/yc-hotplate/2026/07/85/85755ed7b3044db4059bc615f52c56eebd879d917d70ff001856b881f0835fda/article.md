# How to scale your recipes like a pro (with free Google Sheet template)

Let’s take a look at how recipe scaling works, when it’s straightforward, and when you’ll want to adjust instead of just multiplying.


## Why Recipe Scaling Matters for Food Businesses


When you move from cooking for friends to running a business - even if it’s just a small side hustle - accuracy matters.


Scaling helps you:


1.


**Control costs.** Every gram of flour and ounce of butter adds up. You need consistency to understand your true food costs.


2.


**Stay consistent.** Customers want the same product every time. A soggy empanada or too-dense cake on week two can turn off repeat buyers.


3.


**Save time.** Once you have a reliable system, you can prep without doing napkin math for every batch.


## The Math Behind Scaling a Recipe


At its core, recipe scaling is about proportions. You’re starting with:


-


**Base recipe yield.** What the recipe normally makes. For example, 24 empanadas, 1 loaf of bread, 24 cookies.


-


**Desired yield.** How much you actually need.


The formula is:


Scaling Factor = Desired Yield / Base Yield


Then, multiply each ingredient by your scaling factor.


### Example 1: Scaling Up Empanadas


Your base dough recipe makes **24 empanadas.** You need **60.**


Scaling Factor = 60 / 24 = 2.5


If your base recipe calls for:


-


1 kg flour


-


500 g butter


-


20 g salt


Your scaled recipe becomes:


-


2.5 kg flour


-


1.25 kg butter


-


50 g salt


### Example 2: Scaling Down Bread Dough


Your sourdough recipe makes **4 loaves.** You want to test **1 loaf.**


Scaling Factor = 1 / 4 = 0.25


If your base recipe calls for:


-


1000 g flour


-


700 g water


-


20 g salt


-


200 g starter


Your scaled recipe becomes:


-


250 g flour


-


175 g water


-


5 g salt


-


50 g starter


## When Scaling Isn’t Perfect


Some ingredients don’t play nicely when you scale linearly:


-


**Leavening agents (yeast, baking soda, baking powder):** Doubling or tripling may cause issues. Often you’ll use slightly less than the exact multiple when going big.


-


**Spices and extracts:** Intensity compounds. A scaled-up amount of cinnamon or vanilla can overwhelm. Start with slightly less than the full scaled amount, then adjust after a test batch.


-


**Cooking times:** Bigger batches don’t bake or cook in the exact same time. You’ll often need longer, and heat distribution may change, especially with vessels of different materials and sizes.


**Pro tip:** Always test a smaller scaled version first before committing to a large production run.


## Step-by-Step: How to Scale a Recipe


Here’s the process we recommend:


1.


**Record your base recipe.** Write down every ingredient with weights (grams/ounces), not cups or teaspoons. This is key for accuracy.


2.


**Note the base yield.** How many cookies, loaves, or empanadas does this recipe produce?


3.


**Decide your desired yield.** How many units do you actually need?


4.


**Calculate your scaling factor.** Desired yield ÷ base yield.


5.


**Multiply each ingredient by the factor.** Adjust for tricky ingredients (spices, yeast).


6.


**Document everything.** Write down your scaled recipe so you can repeat it later.
