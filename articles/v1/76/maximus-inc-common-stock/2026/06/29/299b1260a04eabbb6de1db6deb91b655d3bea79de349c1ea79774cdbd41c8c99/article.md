# Outcome-based contracting: Turning technology investment into measurable mission impact

## Key Takeaways


- **Technology deployment is not mission improvement** — agencies can adopt AI, automation, or cloud systems without ever measuring whether outcomes for citizens actually improve.
- **Outcome‑based contracting aligns incentives** — tying payment to measurable mission results ensures vendors are accountable for real‑world impact, not just system delivery.
- **Mission bottlenecks must define the work** — the most successful modernization efforts start with a specific operational constraint that the technology must remove.
- **Emerging tech enables auditable performance** — AI accuracy, automation throughput, and routing decisions generate data that can directly map to mission outcomes.
- **Shared outcome definitions are the foundation** — agencies and vendors must agree on what “success” means before drafting the contract.
- **Accountability must run both ways** — outcome‑based models require agencies to commit to transparency, data access, and acting on performance insights.


## From technology deployment to real-world results


Federal agencies are committing billions to AI, cloud migration, automation, and advanced data platforms under contracts designed for a different era. The contracting decisions made in this budget cycle will govern those deployments for years. Results must be demonstrated, not assumed. The contract determines whether agencies ever have to prove the technology worked.


Here is the structural problem: technology deployment and mission improvement are not the same thing. An agency can migrate applications to the cloud, stand up a machine learning pipeline, or automate a document workflow without ever measuring whether citizens receive faster decisions, whether fraud declines, or whether program integrity strengthens. The contracting model determines whether mission performance is tracked at all. Get the model wrong, and the technology investment produces activity without accountability.


### What measurable mission impact looks like


The clearest examples share a common starting point: a specific mission bottleneck that technology was held accountable for removing.


For the Department of Veterans Affairs, the bottleneck was evidence burden. Veterans' disability determinations require reviewers to navigate case files of 2,000 to 3,500 pages to surface critical medical evidence. We built a proprietary AI-powered medical records processing system on AWS GovCloud, achieving a 9x increase in processing efficiency, handling 8 to 10 million pages daily, and processing more than 400 million documents in under a year. VA decision-makers retained full authority over every benefit determination. The AI surfaces the evidence; the humans render the judgment. The contract did not measure whether a records system was deployed. It measured whether decision-makers could reach the right evidence faster to serve Veterans.


In the District of Columbia, the bottleneck was not a processing queue. It was the gap between residents enrolled in TANF and residents who found and kept sustainable employment — a gap shaped by education barriers, limited job market access, and the specific circumstances of the District's workforce. Our 25-year partnership in TANF job placement and work readiness was built around closing that gap. Since program inception, TANF customers have secured more than 9,000 jobs, with 68% retaining employment for more than 180 days. More than 4,000 DC residents have benefited from job training, education, and job readiness services. The contract measured whether people found and kept jobs, not whether a caseload was processed.


### Why contracting models determine whether technology investment succeeds


Each outcome traces back to the same decision: the contract required mission improvement, not system delivery.


Agencies face a fundamental misalignment between how they buy technology and how they measure whether it worked. Traditional contracts specify inputs: labor hours, systems delivered, and milestones checked. Performance-based contracts set service standards and penalize when contractors fall short. Outcome-based contracts go further. Payment depends on whether real-world results improve.


The path forward is an Outcome-Based Contract (OBC), a specific form of Performance-Based Contracting purpose-built for technology-driven modernization. Under this model, pricing and performance are tied to measurable mission outcomes, not service-level standards. The contractor does not earn payment because it deployed a platform or staffed an operation. The contractor earns payment because citizens received faster service, because fraud was prevented, because program integrity strengthened, or because case resolution improved. If the project fails, we fail together. There is no debate about whether it was a success.


This model works precisely because emerging technologies generate auditable, measurable data that connects directly to mission outcomes. AI accuracy rates determine whether benefit decisions are correct. Automation throughput determines whether backlogs shrink or grow. When contracts tie payment to those mission-mapped measures, agency and contractor share a single, verifiable definition of success. Accountability is built into the architecture of the contract itself.


The best outcome-based contracts function as live performance systems. Maximus operates 1-800-MEDICARE for the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, handling more than 36 million calls a year — and every one of those interactions generates data on access, resolution, and satisfaction that feeds performance accountability in real time. That discipline sustains 98% customer satisfaction on the program, and across the broader portfolio of government contact centers we operate, it has cut caller wait times by 65%. Those measures were not delivered in a quarterly report. They were the standard we were paid to meet.


For agencies considering this transition, the path is practical and incremental. It begins with one foundational step: establishing shared outcome definitions before a contract is drafted. The question is not "what will the vendor deliver?" It is "what will improve for the people this program serves, and how will we measure it?" The contract vehicle follows, built around those measures.


That path requires something from the agency as well. Outcome-based contracting works when agencies enter the process with shared definitions of success, the data access to measure them, and the willingness to act on what those metrics reveal — including when they surface performance gaps the organization would prefer not to see. Contractors who bear financial risk under OBC frameworks need agency partners who have made the same commitment. The accountability runs in both directions.


### The question every agency leader should ask


Agency leaders making technology investment decisions today should ask one direct question: does the contracting model for this investment require the vendor to demonstrate mission improvement, or does it only require delivery of a system?


When agencies adopt AI and automation under models that measure only deployment, they absorb the risk that the technology never produces value. Under outcome-based frameworks, accountability shifts: the vendor's financial success depends on the agency's mission success.


This is not a theoretical distinction. Across Medicare, Medicaid, and VA programs, Maximus performs more than 2.12 million independent clinical assessments a year under a human-in-the-loop (HITL) model built to address a specific mission bottleneck: assessment capacity that, left unaddressed, delays citizen decisions. AI organizes and prioritizes the evidence. Clinical reviewers make every final determination, and every case carries a complete, audit-ready record. The question is not what the tool can do. It is which bottleneck the tool removes, and whether the contract requires it to.


That is the difference between AI that measurably expands determination capacity and AI that generates dashboards no one acts on. It is the difference between automation that produces a complete, audit-ready record for every case and automation that creates new compliance risks. The technology is the same. The contracting model determines whether it produces mission impact.


Aligning incentives with mission performance


Maximus has operated across the full spectrum of government contracting for decades, from traditional service contracts to SLA-driven performance models to outcome-based frameworks where payment depends on results.


Across 100 million citizens served annually, the pattern is consistent: when contracts align incentives with measurable outcomes, services improve, accountability strengthens, and agencies deliver better results for the people they serve. Modernization should be measured by what improves. Outcome-based contracting makes that measurement the contract itself.
