# Government Technology Adoption Through a Federal AI Hackathon

## Key takeaways


Hackathons can bridge the gap between government innovation and slow technology adoption by providing a structured, real-world testing environment. When designed around actual mission problems, government constraints, and cross-functional evaluation, they allow agencies to quickly assess both the technical and operational readiness of emerging solutions. Crucially, their value depends on defined follow-through—from prototype to production—turning short-term experimentation into a repeatable pathway for modernization. As adoption urgency grows, well-executed hackathons help agencies identify capable partners early, reduce risk, and accelerate mission impact while maintaining required governance and oversight.


## Government agencies face a paradox: innovation is abundant, but adoption is slow. Commercial technology evolves in weeks.


Public sector certification, compliance, security review, and procurement cycles take far longer, and for good reason. These safeguards protect mission integrity. But they also create steep barriers for emerging technology partners eager to support federal programs.


Hackathons offer a disciplined way to close that gap. A federal AI hackathon gives agencies a controlled environment to evaluate new technologies and the partners behind them under authentic operational conditions. A recent collaboration with the Marine Corps, supported by leaders like Lane Johnson and Craig Clemons, Director of Data Analytics at Marine Corps Logistics Command (LOGCOM), reveals how these events turn experimentation into evidence. When engineered correctly, hackathons become a repeatable pathway for accelerating partner readiness and mission impact.


This operating model aligns with broader modernization patterns described in[The Government Front Door Is Becoming a Conversation](https://maximus.com/insights/government-front-door-becoming-conversation) , where agencies are redesigning constituent service delivery around measurable outcomes and governed technology deployment.


Before agencies can capture that value, they need a structure that turns experimentation into evidence. The design principles below show how to do that, and a recent event with the Marine Corps reveals what they look like in practice.


### Does the problem statement match the real mission?


Emerging technology partners need more than a demo. They need to prove mission fit. Effective hackathon challenges anchor directly to operational use cases: high-volume adjudication, document intelligence, and identity verification.


**Modern Day Marine Hackathon**


At the[Modern Day Marine Hackathon](https://mdm.hackathon-portal.maximus.com/events/modern-day-marine-hackathon) , nearly 100 participants from 18 teams spent two and a half days developing AI prototypes for real LOGCOM challenges. Lane Johnson, who helped facilitate the event, noted that many of these partners arrived with no prior military background. Despite that, Johnson observed they were "able to quickly learn what we're working with... and able to develop solutions for those problems." That is mission-fit tested in real time.


### Can the technology handle government's actual constraints?


Constraints are the truth-test. Hackathons should replicate the limitations that define government operations:


- Limited or synthetic datasets
- Policy and compliance boundaries
- Latency and throughput expectations
- Required audit trails and human-in-the-loop controls


> These events really simulate what it's like to be on an operational environment with limited time and resources.


Charles Ott Vice President of Applied Field R&D, Maximus


A prototype that survives constrained conditions is one that can survive procurement scrutiny. That same balance of speed and control is central to[Maximus contact center solutions](https://maximus.com/customer-experience/contact-center-solutions) , where automation is paired with clear escalation and oversight mechanisms.


### Does the evaluation go beyond technical feasibility?


No single team can determine mission readiness. Hackathons bring together program operators, cybersecurity engineers, product managers, AI architects, and mission owners. This cross-disciplinary structure ensures each solution is evaluated on two critical dimensions: technical feasibility and operational viability.


At the Marine Corps event, teams were judged against explicit criteria. As the event lead noted from the stage, teams were "judged based off of a number of criteria. One was security. One was the feasibility of implementation and obviously relevance to our mission." Security, feasibility, mission relevance, that is the same evaluation framework agencies apply in procurement, but in hackathons, it is compressed into two and a half days.


Partners get fast, direct insight into the security, architectural, and operational requirements that define federal missions, insight that typically takes months or years to gather through traditional channels.


### Is there a defined path from prototype to production?


A hackathon without follow-through delivers enthusiasm, not modernization. This is the common breakdown most events never address.


Agencies need a defined pathway to advance high-performing partners from prototype to production:


- Documented solution designs
- Architectural diagrams
- Security checkpoints
- Scoped pilots


Craig Clemons made this accountability explicit in his closing remarks:


> If these projects don't get put into production, we will have wasted our time. And we recognize that.


Craig Clemons Director of Data Analytics, Marine Corps Logistics Command


That acknowledgment, from the agency, not the contractor, is what a structured follow-through pathway makes possible. It shifts the event from a competitive showcase into the first gate of an adoption process.


### Why does the timing matter now?


Agencies face rising backlogs and increasing cybersecurity threats. Modernization cannot depend on multi-year evaluation cycles. Emerging technologies, especially AI and secure cloud platforms, are improving too quickly for slow adoption paths.


Hackathons flip the model. Across Maximus programs, rapid-test approaches have produced measurable results, including significant reductions in processing times and faster delivery of critical services. These outcomes demonstrate a consistent pattern: early validation under operational conditions accelerates mission performance.


For a concrete example of operational AI testing in government,[Using AI to deliver better customer experience in Michigan](https://maximus.com/case-studies/using-ai-to-deliver-better-customer-experience-in-michigan) , shows how agencies can improve speed and experience while preserving accountability in production environments.


The urgency is becoming institutional. In May 2026, the Secretary of the Army and nine major defense contractors announced the[Right to Integrate hackathon sprint](https://www.army.mil/article/292189/army_and_defense_sector_announce_right_to_integrate_hackathon_sprint_for_shared_technology) , described as potentially the largest in history, specifically to close the gap between siloed, proprietary systems and the speed modern operations demand. As Army CTO Dr. Alex Miller put it:


> We have seen standards come and go in the department for decades, but are still beholden to sub-par implementation.


Dr. Alex Miller Chief Technology Officer for the US Army


A federal AI hackathon model is increasingly how the DoD stress-tests its way out of that pattern. While the operational context differs, the integration imperative is the same.


### What do agencies actually gain?


By incorporating hackathons into their adoption strategies, agencies can identify high-value partners early, accelerate operational impact, and strengthen confidence in modernization of services.


As Craig Clemons put it: "We talk all the time... about implementing AI. This is exactly what it looks like."


The principles that make a hackathon effective, constrained testing, cross-disciplinary evaluation, structured follow-through, are the same principles that should govern how agencies approach any technology modernization decision. Whether the challenge is contact center transformation, benefits eligibility processing, or logistics automation, the question is always the same: has this partner proven they can perform under the conditions that matter?


The agencies that design these events with follow-through built in will set the pace for responsible modernization. The partners they select will have already proven they can perform under the conditions that matter most.
