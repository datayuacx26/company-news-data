The Founding Brothers of Graco


# Brothers in Business: How Leil and Russell Gray Built a Company that Endured a Century


Few companies survive their founders. Fewer still endure the friction and stubborn love that often define family partnerships. Yet Graco’s first century was forged by exactly that kind of relationship: two very different brothers who argued fiercely but shared the belief that being loyal to their company and generous to their employees could generate results beyond a spreadsheet.


The brotherhood between Russell Gray and Leil Gray became the engine behind a company that would grow from a single invention into a global industrial leader.


Seeing the Same Light, Different Colors


### The Brothers Assign Roles


Graco began with Russell’s idea: an air-powered grease gun that made it easier and faster to inject grease into a vehicle, solving a problem for service stations, oil companies and farmers. Gray Co. was incorporated in 1926—but Russell did not want to run a company.


“\[Leil\] didn’t know anything about \[the grease gun\],” said Russell Gray. “But he wanted to be an executive-type, so I said, ‘Here’s your chance to come over and be an executive.’”


The brothers assumed their company roles accordingly: Russell was the idea man—an exuberant, fun-loving inventor driven by ideas that seemed to spill out faster than they could be tested. And Leil was a perfectionist, tremendous marketer and natural leader who insisted that ideas only mattered if they could be executed and sold.


Longtime Graco engineer Charles Murphy, son of former CEO Harry Murphy, witnessed their dynamic firsthand. In a 1983 interview with the Minnesota Historical Society, he recalled how easily the brothers could clash. “They’d get into some real cat-and-dog fights, as brothers will that are of different natures,” said Murphy.


Their relationship was a solid one, though. Russell himself summed it up: “We were always a little bit at loggerheads because we saw the same light, but not the same colors.”


White-Glove Standards


### Leil as Leader


As president and CEO, Leil Gray ran Graco with precision. Every Friday afternoon, the Graco plant shut down 15 minutes early so employees could clean their areas. Leil would walk the facility conducting what became known as his “white-glove” tours, inspecting for dust, disorder or anything less than perfect.


That same meticulousness extended to decision-making. Charles Murphy recalled that even when Leil was outvoted, his judgment prevailed. “If it was six to one and he was the one,” Murphy said, “that was the decision.”


Mistakes—especially public ones—were not taken lightly. Murphy remembered a product demonstration for the first rotary Paintmaster that took an unexpected turn.


“Leil was meticulously dressed as always,” said Murphy. While Leil was testing the unit, a pinhole leak in the hose sprayed paint up Leil’s pant leg. “We got a lecture in no uncertain terms that we should never let that happen again,” Murphy said.


The incident triggered a major engineering effort to improve hose quality across the product line—a typical outcome of Leil’s intolerance for flaws.


Relationships and Reputation


### Opportunity at Every Mile


Leil’s intensity was matched by his commitment to selling. Despite having only an eighth-grade education, he understood that Graco’s future depended on relationships and reputation.


Even family vacations doubled as sales trips. On drives to and from Florida, Leil would stop at filling stations along the route, pitching Graco products to managers and attendants. “We might have to sit out in the car for four or five hours, with the doors open, fanning ourselves, while Dad would make his calls,” recalls Barbara Gray Koch, Leil’s daughter.


His devotion to the business expanded into other family occasions. “Our dinnertime centered around whether Graco had a good day or a bad day,” said Gray Koch. “If things didn’t go well, dinner might be at 7:30 or later.”


Restraint and Risk


### Russell the Inventor


Where Leil imposed order, Russell brought energy. Though he headed Graco’s engineering department, he openly admitted he “didn’t know anything about engineering” in the formal sense. What he did know was how Graco’s equipment worked—and how to get people excited about what it could do.


Russell demonstrated products himself, performing with the flair of a showman. His enthusiasm carried into sales meetings, where ideas were often launched without strategic planning or long-term analysis.


“Russ was a ‘can-do’ guy,” Murphy said. “There was no strategic planning. We just did it because Russ said, ‘This is a market, and we’re going to prove it, and we’ll do it.’ And we did it.”


That approach sometimes created chaos. Russell moved fast, pushed hard and left details behind. Murphy described him as “a bull in a china shop,” with others. “He would go out and do things and do it right now. But the follow-up, the cleanup and the details had to be done by somebody \[else\], and my dad \[Harry Murphy\] kind of evolved into that position,” said Murphy.


Leil frequently had to rein him in. Russell didn’t enjoy it, but he accepted it. Each brother understood his role, and each knew the company needed both restraint and risk.


Company Principles


### Generosity and Loyalty


Despite their clashes, the Gray brothers shared deep generosity and loyalty—to each other and to their employees. Workers who faced financial trouble could turn to either brother for help.


“If people had problems at home, they could talk to Leil, or Russ, or my dad and get a short-term loan,” said Murphy. “A lot of times, it wasn’t a loan. It was just a gift. Russ was great at impromptu incentive rewards for people. Somebody in engineering would do something \[and he would say\], ‘Gee, that’s great. Go and buy a new suit and bring me the bill.’”


That kindness, paired with Leil’s emphasis on cleanliness and high-quality products shaped Graco’s culture—principles that still define it today.


“\[Our products\] were not going to have defects. If we had to sell at a higher price than our competitors, so be it,” said Murphy. “That’s what we were going to base our reputation on.”


A Century Impacted by Brothers


### The Magic Combination


Russell Gray never doubted that Graco’s survival depended on the partnership. “He worked his fool head off,” Russell said of his brother, Leil. “If it hadn’t been for our combination, we wouldn’t have lasted five years.”


This year, Graco turns 100—a milestone few companies in manufacturing achieve. This anniversary reflects a century of resilience, innovation and leadership. From the Great Depression to digital transformation, Graco has adapted and thrived through every era. Our story spans global challenges and technology revolutions—emerging stronger every time. Today, we celebrate a legacy built on ingenuity and look forward to shaping the future.
