# Celebrating 100 Years of Graco: The Invention that Started a Legacy


In 1924, while leading a team at a busy service station in Minneapolis, 23-year-old Russell Gray had one persistent thought, “There has to be a better way to lubricate an automobile.”


The physically demanding labor was taking a toll on Gray and his crew. They parked vehicles on outdoor racks built over shallow pits where workers stood below and strained their arms to force thick grease into hard-to reach fittings using hand-operated push guns.


Minnesota’s extreme weather made the job even tougher, especially in frigid temperatures when frozen fingers were a job hazard and the grease could become too thick, like paste.


***“In real cold weather, the grease set-up was so nasty,” Russell recalled. “Oh, it was difficult to grease cars. It was really difficult.”***


The First Big Idea


### The World’s First Air-Powered Grease Gun


Gray knew a better grease gun could make the job easier. At nights, he tinkered in “a little laboratory” owned by a friend.


After a few months, Gray designed his first invention: an electric-powered grease gun. However, he abandoned the product after a crew member stepped into a puddle of water while using the gun and was knocked to the ground by an electric shock.


Gray kept working to find a solution, and after 18 months of work, he found the solution. “We took the same piece of equipment—almost identical—and converted it to air. It really made a splendid piece of equipment.”


The new device used compressed air—not muscle—to push grease from a wheeled tank through a hose and into the pistol-style gun. For workers, this invention was revolutionary.By 1926, Russell quit the service station to focus solely on manufacturing the new grease gun.


From Idea to Company


### Investors, Incorporation and the First Important Sale


By 1926, Gray stopped working at the service station to devote his time to making grease guns. Early investors contributed $500 each—the equivalent of about $9,100 today—to help Gray launch his business.


With the support of his father LaFayette “Lafe” Gray, **the Gray Co. was officially incorporated in April 1926.**


Using supplies from a friend at an iron works machine shop, Russell Gray and his team produced about 100 small grease guns and six large grease guns per day.


Pure Oil Co. was the first to make a significant purchase with six units. “That started things going,” said Gray, “so my oldest brother decided he’d like to join me.”


Brotherly Partnership


### Leil and Roland Join the Business


At the time,[Russell Gray’s brothers](https://www.graco.com/us/en/about-graco/news/articles/2026/q1/founding-brothers-of-graco-leil-russell.html) , Leil Gray and Roland (“Rollie”) Gray, ran a business that washed, steam-cleaned and removed paint from cars.


Russell had no interest in leading a company—he just wanted to create. So, the brothers decided that Russell would oversee Gray Co.’s manufacturing and product development. Older brother Leil (who had “more brains”) would manage the company.


Rollie didn’t want to take the risk of becoming a shareholder, so he became the leader of the manufacturing plant.


Dan Kvinge, a retired Graco engineer and company historian explained that once the brothers came together, they were, “off to the races.”


Transformation Across the Globe


### 100 Years of Innovation


Russell Gray just wanted an easier workday for him and his crew. Finding a solution to improve just one aspect of car maintenance has led to a catalogue of products used every day around the world.


In the last century, Graco has become one of the world’s top producers of products that paint houses, cars and airplanes; lubricate automobiles, farm equipment and large factory machines; squeeze tomato paste on pizzas, and peanut butter into jars; and so much more.


Our 100-year legacy is built on consistent growth, smart decisions, and a focus on delivering value to customers and communities. Powered by our people and a culture of ingenuity, we’ve stayed true to solving customer needs while advancing what’s possible. As we celebrate 100 years, we’re not just honoring our past—we’re building the future.
