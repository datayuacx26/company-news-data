# From Infrastructure Grants to Green Energy Tax Credits—Permit Data Reveals Which Cities Are Moving Fastest

*How real-time building permit intelligence uncovers local momentum in federal investment deployment.*


The United States is channeling unprecedented federal funding into infrastructure and clean energy through the[Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure_Investment_and_Jobs_Act) and the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA). These[initiatives](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/us-house-targets-big-climate-clean-energy-rollbacks-budget-proposal-2025-05-12/?utm_source=chatgpt.comhttps://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/us-house-targets-big-climate-clean-energy-rollbacks-budget-proposal-2025-05-12/) aim to revitalize the nation’s physical and energy infrastructure, with over[$230 billion in announced energy manufacturing investments and more than 920 new](https://www.energy.gov/invest) or expanded energy manufacturing plants across the country.


However, the pace at which cities and states are deploying these funds varies significantly. Building permit data serves as a leading indicator, revealing where projects are moving from planning to execution. By analyzing permit activity, stakeholders can identify which municipalities are effectively translating federal investments into tangible infrastructure and energy projects.


## **Federal Funding: A Catalyst for Local Development**


### **Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA)**


The IIJA allocates substantial resources to modernize transportation, water systems, and broadband infrastructure. For instance, in March 2024, the Biden administration announced $475 million in funding for projects in Arizona, Nevada, West Virginia, Kentucky, and Pennsylvania to build solar and geothermal power plants and energy storage on current and former mine lands .


### **Inflation Reduction Act (IRA)**


The IRA focuses on accelerating the transition to clean energy, offering tax credits and incentives for renewable energy projects. As of early 2025, the IRA has spurred over $230 billion in announced energy manufacturing investments, leading to the establishment of more than 920 new or expanded energy manufacturing plants.


## **Building Permit Data: A Window into Local Progress**


While federal announcements provide a macro-level view, building permit data offers granular insights into local implementation. Permits reflect actual construction activity, indicating where projects are breaking ground and progressing.


For example, cities like[Phoenix are actively considering zoning regulations to manage the placement of data centers](https://www.axios.com/local/phoenix/2025/05/13/phoenix-city-council-data-centers-zoning) due to their substantial energy consumption and space requirements. Such regulatory considerations often precede permitting activity, signaling upcoming construction trends.


Similarly, in Texas, over 50 solar and energy storage companies have urged Congress to[maintain federal clean energy tax credits,](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/business/energy/article/texas-solar-industry-congress-letter-20316152.php) highlighting the state’s reliance on these incentives for ongoing and future projects . An uptick in related permit filings would indicate the translation of these incentives into concrete developments.


Other examples include:


- As part of its[Green New Deal, Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_Green_New_Deal) has moved to ban fossil fuels in new city buildings and major renovations. This policy aligns with the city’s commitment to reducing carbon emissions and promoting sustainable building practices.
- In 2023, Illinois implemented a law that standardizes[siting, zoning, and setback requirements](https://www.mower.com/news/the-case-for-changing-the-way-renewable-energy-projects-get-approved) for wind and solar projects generating at least 0.5 MW. This legislation prevents local counties from imposing moratoriums on renewable energy projects, streamlining the approval process and encouraging consistent development across the state.
- Michigan’s Public Services Commission gained authority in 2023 to[oversee siting for substantial renewable energy projects](https://www.mower.com/news/the-case-for-changing-the-way-renewable-energy-projects-get-approved) , including solar facilities of 50 MW or greater, wind projects of 100 MW or more, and storage facilities with a discharge capability of 200 MWh or more. This move aims to standardize approvals and reduce local opposition that could hinder project progress
- [Texas House Bill 3356 and Senate Bill 715](https://www.houstonchronicle.com/business/energy/article/texas-solar-wind-damaging-bill-20303387.php) propose requiring existing and future wind and solar farms to provide backup power through natural gas plants or batteries. Non-compliance could lead to fines or shutdowns, potentially affecting thousands of megawatts of renewable capacity and altering the state’s energy landscape.
