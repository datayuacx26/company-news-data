# What Is a “Non-UPF” Claim?

Over the past few years, few food topics have generated as much discussion as ultra-processed foods, commonly referred to as UPFs.


Consumers are increasingly scrutinizing ingredient lists, questioning processing methods, and looking for foods they perceive as less processed. In response, a growing number of brands are beginning to market products using terms like "Non-UPF" or "Not Ultra-Processed."


But what does a Non-UPF claim actually mean?


Unlike claims such as USDA Organic or Non-GMO Project Verified, there is currently no universally recognized regulatory definition or certification program for Non-UPF foods.


That has created both opportunities and challenges for brands looking to respond to consumer demand while maintaining credibility.

##


What Does “Ultra-Processed Food (UPF)” Mean?


Most discussions about ultra-processed foods trace back to the


[NOVA food classification](https://www.news-medical.net/health/The-NOVA-Method-of-Food-Classification.aspx) system, a framework developed by researchers to categorize foods according to the nature, extent, and purpose of processing.


The NOVA system divides foods into four categories:


1.


Unprocessed or minimally processed foods


2.


Processed culinary ingredients


3.


Processed foods


4.


Ultra-processed foods


Under the NOVA classification system, ultra-processed foods are generally described as industrially formulated products that contain ingredients or substances not commonly used in home kitchens, often combined with multiple processing techniques.


Examples frequently cited include:


-


Soft drinks


-


Confectionery products


-


Packaged snack foods


-


Certain ready-to-eat meals


-


Some highly formulated bakery products

However, the ultra-processed food definition continues to be debated. Researchers, regulators, manufacturers, and nutrition experts do not always agree on where specific products should fall within the classification system. This lack of consensus is one reason why Non-UPF claims have become such a complex topic.


##


What Is a Non-UPF Claim?


A Non-UPF claim generally suggests that a product does not meet the criteria for being classified as ultra-processed. The challenge is that there is currently no universally accepted definition of a non-ultra processed food label.


Some brands rely on NOVA classifications, while others evaluate additional factors related to ingredients, formulation, and processing methods. Retailers may also establish their own requirements when evaluating products marketed as Non-UPF.


As a result, two products making similar Non-UPF claims may be using different methodologies to support those claims. This is one reason why the conversation has shifted beyond ingredient lists alone and toward the broader question of how these claims can be substantiated.

##


Are Non-UPF Claims Regulated?


Not at this time.


Unlike claims such as USDA Organic, there is currently no federal regulation in the United States that defines Non-UPF as a formal food labeling claim. There is also no government-managed certification program that determines whether a product qualifies as Non-UPF.


That does not mean companies can make misleading claims. Food labeling rules still require claims to be truthful and not deceptive.


At the same time, a third-party verification program has emerged to provide greater consistency around Non-UPF claims. The


[Non-UPF Verified™ program](https://www.nonultraprocessed.org/) establishes criteria that products must meet in order to carry a verified Non-UPF designation, helping bring greater transparency to an evolving category.


##


Can Non-UPF Claims Be Verified?


The emergence of the Non-UPF Verified™ Standard has established a framework for evaluating products against defined criteria related to ingredients, formulation, and processing methods associated with ultra-processing.


Verification requires clear, measurable requirements. Products seeking verification are evaluated against established criteria, which may include ingredient composition, processing methods, functional additives, formulation complexity, and supporting documentation.


As with any verification program, the credibility of the claim depends on the clarity, consistency, and transparency of the standard being applied.


Verification is not simply about determining whether a product is "good" or "bad." It is about evaluating whether a product meets a defined standard and whether that determination can be supported through documentation and review.

##


What This Means for Brands and Retailers


For brands, Non-UPF positioning represents both an opportunity and a responsibility.


Consumer interest in food processing, ingredient transparency, and product formulation is clearly growing. Products that align with those preferences may gain attention in an increasingly competitive marketplace. At the same time, the lack of universal agreement around ultra-processing creates challenges.

When claims are built on unclear or inconsistent criteria, it can become difficult for retailers, buyers, and consumers to understand what those claims actually mean.


[This is where transparent methodologies, clearly defined standards, and verification frameworks become increasingly important. Regardless of how the category evolves, claims should be supported by criteria that can be consistently applied, evaluated, and communicated.](https://www.nonultraprocessed.org/standard)


##


Looking Ahead


The conversation around ultra-processed foods is unlikely to disappear anytime soon. As consumer interest grows, more brands will likely explore Non-UPF positioning and seek ways to communicate how their products are formulated and processed.


For brands considering Non-UPF positioning, the focus should not simply be on making the claim. It should be on ensuring the claim can be clearly explained, consistently applied, and supported with evidence.
