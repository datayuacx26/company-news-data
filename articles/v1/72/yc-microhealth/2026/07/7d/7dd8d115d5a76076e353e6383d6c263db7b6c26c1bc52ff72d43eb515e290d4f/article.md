# Barriers to Effective Records Management

By **Morgan Dingle** | Jul 22, 2026


**Pop Quiz:** Your records management team identifies 47,000 patient files that have reached the end of their standard retention period. These files consume expensive storage space, but they may also be connected to legal, clinical, regulatory, or operational needs. What do you do?e.


1. Schedule disposal immediately—the retention policy is clear
2. Check with legal counsel first
3. Review for any active litigation, investigations, or audits
4. Both B and C


If you answered D, you understand that records management is not just about storing files or following a retention schedule. It requires knowing what records exist, why they matter, who needs access, when they must be preserved, and when disposal is appropriate as part of a controlled lifecycle.


The email arrives on a Tuesday afternoon with an urgent flag. Subject line: “STOP ALL RECORD CHANGES IMMEDIATELY.” Inside, legal counsel explains that those 47,000 patient files scheduled for lifecycle review next week—files that took months to identify, catalog, classify, and prepare—cannot move forward. Not yet. Maybe not for years.


A former patient filed a lawsuit yesterday. The regulatory investigation that seemed to be resolved last quarter has been reopened. An auditor requested files from that exact time. What started as a routine records management project following approved retention schedules has transformed overnight into a legal, operational, and compliance challenge where one wrong move could cost the organization millions in sanctions, penalties, or adverse judgments.


This isn’t an isolated incident. Records managers across healthcare face this scenario regularly: the retention policy says one thing, legal says hold certain records, compliance says some records have different requirements, clinical teams may still need access, and operations says storage costs are unsustainable. Everyone is right, and everyone is frustrated.


Effective records management brings structure to that complexity. It helps organizations classify records, apply retention rules, preserve information when required, maintain access for care and operations, and dispose of eligible records securely when the time is right. Disposal is an important outcome, but it is only one part of a broader lifecycle that must be governed, documented, and defensible.


### The Records Management Foundation: Where It All Begins


Before organizations can make confident decisions about retention, access, preservation, or disposal, they need a solid records management foundation. Certain compliance documentation must be maintained for at least six years from the date of creation or last effective date, while medical record retention is often governed by state law, payer rules, accreditation requirements, and clinical needs. That means records management must account for multiple authorities, record types, jurisdictions, and business functions.


- State laws vary dramatically


- Federal programs add complexity


- Specialized regulations create additional requirements


Organizations must develop comprehensive retention schedules covering all record types—medical records, billing records, personnel files, contracts, correspondence, administrative documents, system logs, and supporting compliance documentation. But the schedule is only the beginning. Strong records management also requires clear ownership, reliable indexing, secure storage, access controls, documented review processes, and the ability to preserve, transfer, archive, or dispose of records based on their status and purpose.


### When Legal Holds Change the Lifecycle


Active or anticipated litigation creates one of the most common interruptions in the records management lifecycle, and the obligations start earlier than many teams realize. Once litigation is reasonably anticipated, organizations have a duty to preserve relevant records. That duty requires teams to identify affected information, suspend routine lifecycle actions, maintain access controls, and document preservation decisions until the matter is resolved.


**Legal holds must cover:**


- Obvious documents such as, medical records and incident reports


- Emails discussing the incident or patient


- Time-to-complete critical processes


- System logs showing who accessed information


- Metadata revealing when documents were created or modified


- Any other records potentially relevant to claims or defenses


Determining the scope requires legal analysis of potential claims, defenses, and discovery likely to be requested. These holds remain in effect until litigation concludes—through trial, appeal, and final resolution. For complex cases, this can mean preserving records for years beyond normal retention periods.


The practical challenges are significant. Many organizations use automated workflows for retention review, archiving, storage transfers, and secure disposal. Legal holds require suspending those actions for affected records while still managing the rest of the record inventory efficiently, consistently, and securely.


Large organizations often juggle multiple simultaneous legal holds covering different matters, time periods, and record types. A single record might be subject to multiple holds from different matters, and no final lifecycle action should occur until all holds are released. This requires coordinated tracking, defined accountability, and constant vigilance across legal, compliance, IT, clinical, and records management teams.


### Clinical Considerations That Shape Records Management


Clinical needs often shape how records are retained, accessed, archived, or eventually disposed. Patients who have not been seen recently may return for care, and inaccessible or prematurely disposed records can create clinical risks and potential liability. This can be particularly true for patients with chronic conditions who benefit from historical information even after long gaps in care.


Pediatric records provide valuable information when patients transition to adult care. That childhood asthma diagnosis, that early developmental delay, that family history documented during pediatric visits—all of this information can be clinically relevant decades later.


Some records contain information valuable beyond the individual patient. Genetic test results or familial disease patterns may have value for patients’ relatives. A patient’s genetic testing revealing hereditary cancer risk doesn’t just affect that patient—it has implications for siblings, children, and extended family.


Stepping back, healthcare records cannot be managed by schedules alone; clinical context must remain central to every lifecycle decision. When historical information may support patient care, continuity, or family health insights, preserving records is not just a compliance choice—it is a clinical responsibility.


### Practical Strategies for Stronger Records Management


Organizations can manage this complexity through systematic approaches that balance compliance, access, preservation, security, disposal, and operational efficiency.


#### Centralized Legal Hold Management


**Implement systems that:**


- Track all active legal holds with matter names, hold dates, affected record types, and custodians


- Notify records custodians of holds automatically


- Suspend automated disposal processes for affected records


- Flag affected records in systems to prevent inadvertent disposal


- Document all hold-related decisions and actions


Conduct periodic reviews of active holds with legal counsel to determine if holds can be released. Establish clear procedures for releasing holds, including legal approval requirements, custodian notifications, and resumption of normal retention schedules.


#### Disposition and Secure Disposal as a Lifecycle Step


Disposal still matters, but it should happen as the result of a controlled records management process—not as a standalone goal. Before records are destroyed, organizations should confirm that retention requirements have been met, no legal holds or investigations apply, clinical needs have been considered, and the decision has been documented.


In essence, organizations need clear escalation criteria for records tied to incidents, complaints, audits, unusual circumstances, or other preservation obligations. When records are eligible, secure disposal should follow approved methods and leave an audit trail that demonstrates what was destroyed, when, why, and by whom.


#### Technology Enablement


**Deploy systems that support the full records management lifecycle:**


- Records management systems that track retention schedules, ownership, access, storage location, and legal holds


- E-discovery platforms that quickly identify and preserve records subject to legal holds


- Automated workflows that flag records for review, preservation, archiving, transfer, or secure disposal


- Comprehensive audit trails documenting retention decisions, access, preservation actions, and final disposition


**Technology enables better management, but it’s not a substitute for human judgment.** Systems can flag potential issues, automate routine workflows, and preserve audit trails, but people must make final decisions about whether records should be retained, escalated, or destroyed.


#### Cross-Functional Collaboration


**Establish committees including:**


- Legal counsel


- Compliance officer


- Records management professionals


- IT representatives


- Clinical leadership


- Privacy officers


These committees review retention, preservation, access, archiving, and disposal decisions; resolve complex issues; and ensure awareness of investigations, litigation, clinical considerations, and other matters affecting records. Train staff on legal hold obligations, preservation duties, retention procedures, access controls, and proper disposal practices to prevent records from being lost, mishandled, or destroyed prematurely.


### The High Cost of Getting It Wrong


Improper records management creates serious consequences that extend far beyond storage problems or missed retention deadlines.


**Spoliation consequences can include:**


- Adverse inference instructions


- Monetary penalties


- Case dismissal or default judgment


- Program exclusion for repeated violations or poor documentation controls


- Expensive reconstruction attempts from other sources


- Reputational damage affecting patient trust and referrals


Courts take preservation obligations seriously, and sanctions can be severe. Destroyed records can’t defend against claims, meaning organizations that destroy records improperly may face liability they could have avoided.


### Moving Forward with Confidence


Records management is not simply a back-office function or a storage policy. It is a coordinated lifecycle process that helps organizations know what they have, apply the right rules, preserve what must be kept, make records available when needed, and securely dispose of information when it is truly eligible.
