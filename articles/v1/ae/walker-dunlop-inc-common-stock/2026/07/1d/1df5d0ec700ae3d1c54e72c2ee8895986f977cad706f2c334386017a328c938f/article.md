# Why social license has become a competitive advantage in data center development

Power availability, access to capital, and construction timelines have become defining considerations in modern[data center](https://www.walkerdunlop.com/specialties-sectors/data-centers) development. As the industry continues to grow, another factor is emerging as equally important: community trust.


Developers are beginning to recognize that successful projects require more than securing land and financing. They also require earning a social license to operate, the confidence of local communities that a project will create long-term value while being a responsible neighbor.


That shift reflects the growing maturity of the data center industry. As projects become larger and more visible, community engagement has become an important part of development strategy.


## Growth has brought greater public scrutiny


Demand for digital infrastructure continues to expand. As development accelerates, communities are asking understandable questions.


- How will projects affect power infrastructure and electricity costs?
- What impact will they have on water resources?
- Will they create long-term economic value?


These questions have become more prominent as local governments evaluate new development proposals. Addressing them requires transparency, education, and meaningful engagement throughout the development process.


## Communities want partners, not just projects


One of the strongest themes to emerge from[Walker Webcast's conversation with Compass Datacenters Founder and CEO Chris Crosby](https://www.walkerdunlop.com/webcast/chris-crosby) was that successful developers should think beyond project delivery and focus on long-term partnership. Rather than approaching communities as places to build, developers should approach them as places to invest for decades.


That philosophy influences decisions ranging from infrastructure investments to workforce development and community improvements. It also encourages developers to think about how projects will operate long after construction is complete.


For owners planning to hold assets over the long term, those investments can strengthen relationships while supporting sustainable growth.


## Transparency matters more than ever


As data center development has accelerated, public discussion has often focused on electricity demand and water consumption.


In reality, the industry continues to evolve. Many developers are investing in alternative cooling technologies, supporting grid modernization, and working with utilities to improve infrastructure planning. Others are partnering with communities on workforce development, transportation improvements, and public infrastructure.


Sharing those efforts openly helps communities better understand both the opportunities and tradeoffs associated with new development.


Transparency builds confidence, and confidence helps projects move forward.


## A competitive advantage that extends beyond approvals


Community engagement is often viewed through the lens of entitlement and permitting.


In practice, its value extends much further.


Developers who establish strong local relationships may be better positioned to navigate future expansion opportunities, recruit talent, build lasting partnerships, and maintain operational continuity over decades of ownership.


For institutional investors, those relationships can also contribute to greater confidence that projects are positioned for long-term success.


In that sense, social license is not separate from execution. It has become part of it.


## Building for the next generation


Data centers have become critical infrastructure supporting artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and the broader digital economy. As investment continues to grow, the industry's relationship with communities will become increasingly important.


The developers who succeed over the long term are likely to be those who pair technical expertise with meaningful local engagement, investing not only in digital infrastructure but also in the places where that infrastructure operates.
