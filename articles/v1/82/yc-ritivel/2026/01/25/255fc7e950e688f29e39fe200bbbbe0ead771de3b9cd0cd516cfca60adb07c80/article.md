# Clinical Trial Success Rates: An Analysis of 450,000+ Trials

A recent study published in[Nature Communications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12572394/) analyzed **453,366 clinical trials** to calculate success rates across phases, therapeutic areas, and time periods. The dataset represents one of the most comprehensive analyses of clinical trial outcomes to date.


The data offers a detailed view of where drugs succeed and fail in the development pipeline. Below, we summarize the key findings and what they suggest about the current state of pharmaceutical R&D.

The most striking finding? **Phase II is where drugs go to die.** With a success rate of just 32.4%, it represents the most challenging hurdle in drug development—often called the "valley of death."


💡


Why Phase II is so difficult


Phase II is the first time a drug is tested for efficacy in patients with the target disease. Many compounds that showed promise in safety trials fail to demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit. This is where the "biology meets reality" moment happens.

## Success Varies Dramatically by Disease Area


Not all therapeutic areas are created equal. The data reveals striking differences in success rates across disease categories:

LOA = Likelihood of Approval. Source: ClinSR.org database analysis.


**Hematology and infectious disease** lead the pack, with Likelihood of Approval (LOA) rates of 22% and 19% respectively. Meanwhile, **oncology** —despite receiving the most R&D investment—has one of the lowest success rates at just 5.3%.


Why such variation? Several factors contribute:


- **Biological complexity:** CNS disorders involve the blood-brain barrier and complex neural circuits
- **Endpoint clarity:** Infectious diseases have clear, measurable outcomes (pathogen elimination)
- **Patient heterogeneity:** Cancer is really hundreds of different diseases
- **Regulatory pathways:** Some areas have more established approval frameworks


## Key Findings from the Research


#### Key Findings


##### Orphan drugs show higher success


Drugs targeting rare diseases have significantly higher approval rates, benefiting from smaller trial requirements and supportive regulatory pathways.


##### Biomarkers boost approval odds


Trials using biomarker-based patient selection demonstrate 2-3x higher success rates, validating precision medicine approaches.


##### Success rates are improving


Overall clinical trial success rates have increased over the past two decades due to better trial design and patient selection.


##### Phase II remains the bottleneck


With only 32.4% success, Phase II is where most drugs fail—the critical transition from safety to efficacy demonstration.

⚠️


The cost of delay


Every month of delay in drug development has real consequences. For a drug treating a serious disease, delays mean patients who could have been helped are instead waiting—or worse. This isn't just about business metrics; it's about lives.


## Implications for Regulatory Operations


Given these success rates and timelines, where can efficiency gains realistically be made? Clinical trial duration and regulatory review times are largely determined by scientific and procedural requirements. However, document preparation—the compilation of Clinical Study Reports, CTD modules, and supporting documentation—represents a significant time investment that is more amenable to optimization.


The regulatory submission process involves substantial manual work: cross-referencing across documents, ensuring consistency in terminology, compiling tables and figures, and formatting to regulatory standards. These tasks, while necessary, don't require the same scientific judgment as trial design or data interpretation.


This distinction matters when considering where automation and AI tools can appropriately be applied. Tasks that are rule-based, repetitive, and verifiable are candidates for automation. Tasks requiring scientific interpretation, regulatory strategy, or clinical judgment are not.


> The question for regulatory teams isn't whether to use new tools, but which parts of the workflow can be made more efficient without compromising quality or regulatory acceptability.
