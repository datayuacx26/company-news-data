# What Is a Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC) and How Do They Work?


For many homeowners, the equity they’ve built in their home is one of their most valuable financial tools, especially for home improvement projects, consolidating debt, and emergency funds.


In simple terms, a home equity line of credit (HELOC) gives you a flexible way to tap into that equity when you need it. However, before you leverage your equity, it’s always best to do a little research.


“How does a HELOC work?” and “When is the best time to use a HELOC?” are questions potential borrowers should be able to answer before making any financial maneuvers.


This guide breaks it down in simple, practical terms so you can decide whether it’s the right fit for your financial goals.


## Home Equity Line of Credit Explained


A HELOC is a revolving line of credit that lets you borrow against the equity in your home. Instead of receiving a lump sum upfront, you’re approved for a maximum credit limit and can borrow from it as needed.


Think of it as a credit card backed by the value of your home. However, unlike a credit card, a HELOC typically has lower interest rates and more predictable terms.


HELOCs are popular because they offer flexibility. You can borrow, repay, and borrow again during the draw period, making it easier to manage ongoing or unexpected expenses.


### How Does a HELOC Work?


A HELOC has two main phases: the draw period and the repayment period.


#### Draw Period


During the draw period, you can access your available credit whenever you need it. Most banking and lending institutions offer a draw period of 10 years.


Many homeowners use this time to fund projects in stages, cover large purchases, or consolidate higher‑interest debt. You’ll typically make interest‑only payments during this phase, which helps keep monthly costs manageable.


#### Repayment Period


Once the draw period ends, the remaining balance must be repaid—but how that happens can look different from one loan to another. Some HELOCs, like at Landmark, require a balloon payment at the end of the loan term. Other HELOCs automatically transition into a repayment period after the draw period ends. During this phase, the remaining balance is repaid over a set number of years through regular monthly payments that include both principal and interest.


#### Variable Interest Rates


Most HELOCs come with variable interest rates, meaning your rate can change over time based on market conditions. This can be beneficial when rates are low, but it’s important to understand how rate changes may affect your future payments.


U.S. prime rate, inflation trends, housing market conditions, your credit score, and other relevant factors influence HELOC interest rates.


### When to Use a HELOC


A HELOC can be a smart financial tool when used thoughtfully and with a reliable repayment strategy in place. Homeowners often turn to a HELOC for:


#### Home Improvements


Projects like kitchen remodels, roof replacements, or energy‑efficient upgrades come with significant upfront costs, but will ultimately increase your home’s value. A HELOC gives you the flexibility to fund these projects as they progress.


#### Debt Consolidation


If you’re carrying high‑interest credit card or personal loan debt, using a HELOC to consolidate balances may help lower your interest costs and simplify monthly payments.


#### Emergency or Unexpected Expenses


####


Unfortunately, surprise expenses and medical situations can catch families off guard. A HELOC can serve as a financial safety net for hospital bills, major repairs, or other unplanned expenses.


#### Education or Large Purchases


Some homeowners use a HELOC to help cover tuition, weddings, or other significant life events.


### Benefits of a HELOC


A HELOC may not be the best fit if you prefer a fixed interest rate, need a one‑time lump sum, or are concerned about future rate changes. However, HELOCs remain a popular borrowing option because they:


- Offer flexible access to funds
- Typically come with lower interest rates than credit cards
- Allow borrowers to only borrow what they need when they need it


## Home Equity Borrowing Options


A HELOC isn’t the only way to borrow against your home’s equity. Homeowners often make the HELOC vs home equity loan comparison to determine which option best fits their needs. Understanding the differences can help you make a confident, informed decision.


### HELOC vs Home Equity Loan


While both options use your home’s equity as collateral, they work differently and are ideal in certain scenarios.


**Characteristics of a HELOC:**


- Revolving line of credit
- Borrow as needed
- Variable interest rates
- Flexible repayment structure
- Ideal for ongoing or unpredictable expenses


**Characteristics of a home equity loan:**


- Lump‑sum loan
- Fixed interest rate
- Predictable monthly payments
- Best for one‑time, well‑defined expenses


If you’re planning a single project with a clear budget, such as replacing your HVAC system, a home equity loan is likely the best option. If you want ongoing access to funds or expect costs to change over time, such as with a large-scale home improvement project, a HELOC offers more flexibility.


## How to Get a HELOC


Applying for a HELOC is similar to applying for other types of loans. Lenders review your financial profile to determine how much you can borrow and what terms you qualify for. While every lender has its own process, the general steps include reviewing your home’s value, verifying your income, and assessing your credit history.


### HELOC Key Qualifications


Although requirements vary by banking institution, most lenders look for:


**Sufficient home equity:** You typically need to have built up a certain amount of equity in your home. Lenders often allow you to borrow a percentage of your home’s value minus your remaining mortgage balance.


**Reliable income:** Lenders want to ensure you can manage monthly payments, so they’ll review your income and employment history.


**Good credit history:** A strong credit score can help you qualify for better rates and terms.


**Responsible debt management:** Your debt‑to‑income ratio helps lenders understand how much of your monthly income goes toward existing debt.
