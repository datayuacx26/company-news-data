# Vision Statement Examples for 2026 to Inspire Your Company's Growth

**Key takeaways:**


- We pulled every statement below from the organization's own live page in August 2026 and labeled it the way that organization labels it, because a lot of what circulates as famous "vision statements" is actually a mission, and some of it is years out of date.
- Amazon is the clearest case. Its published mission today is "to make customers' lives better and easier every day by relentlessly inventing on their behalf," not the customer-centric line that roundups still quote.
- Gallup surveyed 4,475 U.S. workers in August 2025 and found only 18% describe their job as having a purpose they personally believe in, while 45% work primarily for the paycheck.
- The statements that hold up share one trait: they describe a finished world, not a company activity. "A world where everyone has a decent place to live" survives a strategy change. "Be the leading provider" does not.


There is a strange thing about vision statement roundups: most of them quote statements the company no longer uses.


We checked. Fetching each organization's own page in August 2026 turned up a live Amazon mission that reads nothing like the version repeated across dozens of listicles, plus several statements filed under "vision" that the company itself files under "mission." So every example below carries the label its owner gives it.


Jim Collins and Jerry Porras set the standard in[Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision) in 1996, splitting a real vision into a core ideology that stays fixed and an envisioned future the organization is reaching for. Thirty years on, that split still explains why some of these work and others are wall art.


It matters more than it sounds.[Gallup surveyed](https://news.gallup.com/poll/697403/purposeful-work-boosts-engagement-few-experience.aspx) 4,475 U.S. working adults in August 2025 and found just 18% describe their current job as having a purpose they personally believe in, against 45% who work primarily to collect a paycheck. Among employees with a strong sense of purpose at work, 50% were engaged; among those with little, 9% were.


We're Awesomic, and a lot of the branding work that comes through us starts with a founder trying to turn a vague ambition into something a designer can actually build a brand around. The ones who arrive with a sharp statement get to the good work faster, so we pay attention to what separates them.


## What separates a vision statement from a slogan


A vision statement describes the world once you have succeeded. A slogan describes how you would like to be perceived while trying. The difference shows up in whether the sentence would still be true if you doubled your product line tomorrow.


Test any candidate against four questions. Could a competitor put their name on it? Does it name an end state or an activity? Would an employee know what to stop doing because of it? Is there anything in it a person could disagree with?


Here is what each of those looks like when a statement passes, with the example on this list that shows it best.

A statement that fails all four is not harmless. It absorbs a strategy offsite, gets printed on a wall, and then quietly teaches everyone that the words leadership uses do not mean anything.


## Mission vs vision, and why half of these get mislabeled


The clean version of the distinction: a mission says what you do every day, a vision says what the world looks like when you have done it long enough. Mission is present tense and internal. Vision is future tense and external.


LinkedIn is the tidiest live example, because it publishes both and labels them. Its vision is about the world it wants; its mission is about the work it does. Most organizations publish only one and call it whichever word feels bigger, which is why mission and vision statement examples get muddled in every roundup, including the ones that rank first.


We are not going to pretend the labels are cleaner than they are. Each entry below says which word the organization actually uses, and where a company publishes a mission and no vision, we say so rather than promoting it to fill a slot. That is also why the vision and mission statement examples here are not split into two neat lists: most organizations do not publish two neat statements.


## How we picked and verified these 14


The list spans large companies, nonprofits, a church, product teams, and individuals, so it covers what people actually search for. The sourcing rules were simple:


- Every organizational statement was fetched from that organization's own live page in August 2026, not from another roundup
- Each one is quoted exactly and labeled vision, mission, or business idea to match the source
- Where the live wording differs from the version in circulation, we use the live wording and flag the difference
- Product vision and personal vision entries are marked as worked examples, because organizations and individuals almost never publish these
- No statement was included on reputation alone

## Company vision statement examples


Large companies are useful here precisely because their statements have survived contact with real strategy for years. These five are quoted from live pages, and two of them are labeled mission by their owners even though they show up in vision roundups constantly.


### 1. LinkedIn


**Label used:** Vision, alongside a separate mission | **Sector:** Professional networking


LinkedIn publishes both halves, which makes it the reference case for the whole distinction.


> Create economic opportunity for every member of the global workforce.


The vision names an end state that LinkedIn will never fully reach, which is exactly right. Its separate mission, to connect the world's professionals to make them more productive and successful, describes the daily work that moves toward it. Read together they explain why a jobs marketplace and a learning library sit in the same company.


**Steal this:** Publish the mission and the vision side by side so nobody has to guess which one your strategy is answering.


### 2. IKEA


**Label used:** Vision, with a separate business idea | **Sector:** Home furnishing


IKEA's vision is deliberately about people rather than furniture.


> To create a better everyday life for the many people.


"The many people" is the phrase doing the work. It rules out a premium-only strategy, which is a real constraint on a furniture company, and its business idea spells the constraint out: a wide range of well-designed, functional products at prices so low that as many people as possible can afford them. The vision tells you what IKEA will not do.


**Steal this:** Write a vision that closes a door, then check whether anyone in your company would notice if you walked through it anyway.


### 3. Microsoft


**Label used:** Mission | **Sector:** Software and cloud


This is the single most-quoted line in the genre, and Microsoft files it as a mission.


> To empower every person and every organization on the planet to achieve more.


The scope is enormous, which is the point and the risk. It has enough direction to justify accessibility work and enough elasticity to justify almost anything else, so it functions less as a filter than as a permission slip. It works for Microsoft because the four commitments published underneath it do the narrowing.


**Steal this:** If your statement is broad, publish the two or three commitments that narrow it, or it will not constrain a single decision.


### 4. Amazon


**Label used:** Mission | **Sector:** Ecommerce and cloud


Here is the one that most roundups still get wrong, and it is worth checking yourself.


> Amazon's mission is to make customers' lives better and easier every day by relentlessly inventing on their behalf.


The line circulating in almost every vision statement listicle is an older customer-centric formulation. The current wording is more specific about method: the promise is not just customer focus but invention on the customer's behalf, which is a claim Amazon can be held to. That specificity is why it reads as a real mission rather than a value.


**Steal this:** Check your own about page before you quote yourself, because the statement in your deck is often two revisions behind the one on your website.


### 5. Khan Academy


**Label used:** Mission | **Sector:** Education nonprofit


A short statement that has not needed rewriting in fifteen years.


> Khan Academy's mission is to provide a free, world-class education to anyone, anywhere.


Three constraints in eleven words: free, world-class, and universal. Each one is falsifiable, and the organization publishes numbers against them, reporting availability in more than 190 countries and over 55 languages with 227 million registered users. A statement you can audit is rarer than it should be, and it is the reason this one has not needed a rewrite while the product changed underneath it repeatedly.


**Steal this:** Build your statement out of two or three adjectives you would be embarrassed to fail on, then publish the number that proves each one.


## Vision statement examples for nonprofits and churches


Nonprofits tend to write the sharpest statements in this genre, because their end state is genuinely imaginable and their donors ask about it directly. These five come from live pages, and the first shows the cleanest vision-versus-mission split on the whole list.


### 6. Habitat for Humanity


**Label used:** Vision, alongside a separate mission | **Sector:** Housing nonprofit


Nine words that describe a finished world and nothing else.


> A world where everyone has a decent place to live.


No activity, no organization name, no method. The mission carries all of that separately, describing bringing people together to build homes, communities and hope, so the vision is free to stay a destination. Notice that the word "decent" is quietly doing enormous work, since it sets a standard without specifying a building, and it lets the organization count a repaired roof as progress rather than only a new house.


**Steal this:** Write your vision so that your own organization's name would be redundant in it.


### 7. Feeding America


**Label used:** Mission | **Sector:** Hunger relief


A newer, more operational statement than the version many people remember.


> Our mission is to advance change in America by ensuring fair access to nutritious food for all in partnership with food banks, policymakers, supporters, and the communities we serve.


It names the partners explicitly, which for a network organization is a strategic statement rather than a courtesy. "Fair access" and "nutritious" are also narrower than "no one is hungry," and that narrowing reflects a real shift toward policy and nutrition quality rather than volume alone.


**Steal this:** If you work through partners, name them in the statement, because it tells everyone the work is not yours to do alone.


### 8. Alzheimer's Association


**Label used:** Mission | **Sector:** Health research and support


A mission written as a leadership claim rather than a description of services.


> The Alzheimer's Association leads the way to end Alzheimer's and all other dementia.


The full published statement continues into three specific methods: accelerating global research, driving risk reduction and early detection, and maximizing quality care and support. Splitting the sentence that way gives the organization an end state and a set of testable programs in one breath, which is what most one-line statements fail to do.


**Steal this:** Follow your end-state clause with the two or three methods you have chosen, so the statement doubles as a budget argument.


### 9. charity: water


**Label used:** Organizational description plus a stated goal | **Sector:** Clean water


This one is interesting because the formal statement is plain and the ambition sits next to it.


> charity: water is a nonprofit organization bringing clean and safe water to people around the world.


The page pairs that description with a single stated goal of ending the global water crisis, then immediately backs it with counts: water projects funded, countries reached, and people served, all shown as live figures. Putting proof beside ambition is what stops a big claim from reading as bluster.


**Steal this:** Place your most ambitious sentence directly next to your hardest number, and let the number carry the credibility.


### 10. Life.Church


**Label used:** Mission | **Sector:** Church


Church statements are their own genre, and this is the most widely copied one in it.


> To lead people to become fully devoted followers of Christ.


It is unusually specific for a church statement because of the word "fully." A church that only wanted attendance would not have written it, and the statement rules out measuring success by Sunday headcount alone. That is what makes it arguable, and therefore real. It also explains a great deal about where the organization puts its money, since discipleship programs are harder to fund than services and easier to cut.


**Steal this:** Find the one adjective that raises your bar past the easy metric, and put it in the sentence.


## Product vision statement examples


Product vision statements almost never get published, so these two are worked examples rather than quotations. Both use Geoffrey Moore's positioning template from Crossing the Chasm, which forces a target, a need, a category, and a differentiator into one sentence and stops teams writing a feature list.


### 11. Scheduling tool for home services


**Label used:** Worked example | **Framework:** Moore positioning template


Written for a team that kept describing its product by its integrations.


> For dispatchers at 5 to 50 truck home services companies who lose an hour a day rebooking around cancellations, our scheduler is a routing tool that rebuilds the day's route in one tap, unlike calendar apps that only show the conflict.


The differentiator clause is the whole value of this format. Naming what the product does that calendars do not gives design, sales, and support the same sentence to work from.


**Steal this:** Force yourself to finish the "unlike" clause with a real category of competitor, not a strawman.


### 12. Onboarding for a fintech app


**Label used:** Worked example | **Framework:** Moore positioning template


The same template applied to a consumer product with a compliance constraint.


> For first-time investors under 30 who abandon account opening at identity verification, our onboarding is a guided flow that completes verification in under four minutes, unlike bank apps that hand users a document upload and no feedback.


Putting the abandonment point and the time target inside the vision keeps the team honest about what success means. A product vision that contains a number is much harder to quietly reinterpret two quarters later.


**Steal this:** Put the one metric your vision lives or dies on inside the sentence itself.


## Personal vision statement examples


Personal vision statements are private by nature, so these are worked examples too. The useful ones are uncomfortably specific about trade-offs, because a personal vision that costs nothing is a mood board.


### 13. A designer building toward independent practice


**Label used:** Worked example | **Horizon:** Five years


Written to name what gets given up, not just what gets gained.


> In five years I run a two-person practice doing brand systems for climate companies, earning enough from four retainers a year that I never take work I would not show anyone, and I accept that this means turning down the agency roles that pay more.


The final clause is the reason this works. Naming the sacrifice converts a wish into a decision rule you can apply the next time a recruiter calls.


**Steal this:** End your personal vision with the sentence "and I accept that this means," then finish it honestly.


### 14. A founder after an exit


**Label used:** Worked example | **Horizon:** Ten years


A longer horizon needs a harder constraint, not a vaguer one.


> In ten years I have built one more company to profitability without outside capital, I work four days a week, and I measure the decade by whether my team stayed rather than by what the company sold for.


Choosing the measure in advance is the move. Anyone can say they value team retention; writing down that it beats exit value is a statement you can be held to when the offer arrives.


**Steal this:** Name the metric you will judge yourself by before you are in a position where a different metric would flatter you.


## Patterns worth copying across all 14


Read the 14 in a row and three habits repeat. The strongest statements describe a finished world rather than a company activity. They contain at least one word that closes a door, like IKEA's "the many people" or Life.Church's "fully." And the ones that survive a decade are short enough to be quoted from memory by someone who does not work there.


The failure mode repeats too, and it has less to do with wording than with process. In a thread on[Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/nonprofit/comments/1kqooxw/strategic_planning_makes_me_want_to_quit_the_board/) about a nonprofit board's strategic planning, one commenter described spending three months with a facilitator to get their mission statement down from a novella to a run-on sentence, then admitted nobody on the board could recall what it said.


Another called the exercise a timesuck for organizations that treat planning as a substitute for deciding. It is anecdotal, but it names the real risk: the words get workshopped and the decisions do not change.


That is the test worth applying to your own. If the statement went up on the wall and no project was cancelled, no hire was reconsidered, and no pitch was turned down, it did not do anything.

## Where to start on yours


Write the world, not the company. One sentence, present tense, describing what is true once you have succeeded, with your organization's name deliberately left out of it.


Then run the two tests that most statements fail. Could your closest competitor sign it without changing a word? If yes, add the constraint that only applies to you. And what does it tell you to stop doing? If the answer is nothing, you have written an aspiration rather than a vision.

## Frequently asked questions


### What is the difference between a mission and a vision statement?


A mission describes what your organization does now and for whom; a vision describes the world once you have done it long enough to matter. Mission is present tense and internal, vision is future tense and external.


LinkedIn publishes both, with a vision about economic opportunity for the global workforce and a mission about connecting professionals, and that pairing is the clearest way to see it. Most mission vs vision statement examples get muddled because organizations publish only one and pick whichever word sounds bigger.


### How long should a vision statement be?


Short enough that an employee who has worked there six months can quote it without checking. In practice that is one sentence, usually under 20 words. Habitat for Humanity's runs to nine. Length is a symptom rather than a rule, though. A long statement is almost always a sign that several stakeholders each got a clause, which is exactly the process failure that produces statements nobody remembers.


### What should a vision statement for a nonprofit include?


An end state the organization would genuinely go out of business achieving, phrased without the organization's own name in it. Strong vision statement examples for nonprofits describe a changed world rather than a program: a world where everyone has decent housing, an end to a specific disease, universal access to something. Keep the methods in the mission. Donors read the vision to understand what you are for, and they read the mission to understand what their money buys.


### Can a small business use the same approach as a large company?


Yes, and small businesses usually do it better, because they have fewer stakeholders adding clauses. The one adjustment worth making is scope. Most useful vision statement examples for business at a small scale are geographically or vertically bounded, describing a changed situation for a specific customer type or region rather than for the planet. A statement you can plausibly reach is more motivating than a global one nobody believes.


### What makes a good vision statement for a church?


Specificity about what change is being asked for, and a bar that a full building alone would not clear. Life.Church's line about leading people to become fully devoted followers is copied so widely because the word "fully" sets a standard that attendance numbers cannot satisfy. Good vision statement for church examples tend to name a depth of commitment rather than a level of participation, which gives leadership something to plan programs against.
