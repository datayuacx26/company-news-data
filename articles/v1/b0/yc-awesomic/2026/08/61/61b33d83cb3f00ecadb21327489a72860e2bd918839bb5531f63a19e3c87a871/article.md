## What a video animation agency actually makes


"Video animation" covers at least five distinct products, and mixing them up is the most common reason a project costs double what the client expected.


The explainer sits at the center: 60 to 120 seconds, narrated, usually 2D, aimed at making an abstract product legible. Around it sit product demo animation built from real interface recordings, motion graphics and kinetic typography for social and ads, brand films that carry emotional weight rather than information, and technical or 3D animation for physical products and processes.


Each one has a different cost base. A 3D technical animation involves modeling and rendering that a 2D explainer never touches, which is why a single "animated video" line item in a brief is close to meaningless.


Before you contact anyone, write down which of the five you actually need, how long it runs, and where it will play. Studios quote wildly differently on the same brief, and a large part of that spread comes from them guessing at answers you did not give.


Naming the format also tells you which kind of supplier to approach, since a specialist animation house and a broader[video production service](https://www.awesomic.com/blog/video-production-services) are priced and staffed for different work.

## Why animation explains things text cannot


There is real cognitive science behind the explainer format, and understanding it changes how you brief one.


In[Educational Psychologist](https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/TC510-Fall2011/MayerMoreno9WaysToReduceCognitiveLoad.pdf) , Richard Mayer and Roxana Moreno set out three findings about how people process multimedia: we have separate channels for verbal and visual material, each channel holds only a limited amount at once, and learning requires active processing in both. Narration and animation together use two channels in parallel, which is why a well-made 90-second video can land an idea that a page of text does not.


The same research explains the most common way explainer videos fail. If your narrator reads text that is simultaneously on screen, you are loading the same information into both channels and crowding out the actual explanation.


So the brief matters more than the style. Ask your studio to keep on-screen text minimal where narration is carrying the point, and to use the visual channel to show the thing being described rather than to repeat the words.


Reach is the other half of the argument. The Pew Research Center's[social media fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/social-media/) puts YouTube in front of 84% of US adults, ahead of every other platform it tracks, so video is where your audience already is even in unglamorous B2B categories.


## How to brief an animation studio without paying twice


Rework is where animation budgets die, and almost all of it traces to approvals given too late. Lock each stage before the next one starts.


1. Write the single idea the video must land, in one sentence, before you contact anyone.
2. Approve the script in full, because changing a line after animation begins means re-recording and re-timing.
3. Approve the storyboard and style frames, since this is the last cheap moment to change the look.
4. Record final voiceover only after the script is signed off, then animate to that track.
5. Reserve your revisions for the animation stage, and ask up front how many rounds are included.


That sequence is the actual product a good studio sells you, and it's the discipline we apply to[video production](https://www.awesomic.com/video) work too. If an agency offers to skip storyboarding to move faster, treat it as a cost risk rather than a favor.


One thing worth checking before you approve any style direction: whether the aesthetic already looks dated. In a r/MotionDesign discussion on whether the format has run its course, a designer working in-house at a large B2B company said their team still relies on explainers to get complex solutions across, while the top reply noted[on Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/MotionDesign/comments/1jl75ju/are_explainer_videos_dead_or_will_they_never_die/) that clients no longer want "generic animated explainers with a ukulele playing in the background." That's practitioner opinion rather than research, but a pitch built on flat corporate illustration and stock acoustic backing is a fair signal to keep looking.

## How long an animated video takes


A standard 60 to 90-second explainer runs four to eight weeks with most studios: roughly one to two weeks on script, one to two on storyboard and style frames, two to three animating, and a final week for revisions and delivery.


Brand films and 3D technical work run longer, often three to four months once modeling, sound design and multiple review layers are involved. At the other end, our Parcha launch video reached final approval in 27 days by running a hybrid AI production, and studios using similar workflows now quote comparable timelines.


The variable you control is approval speed. A studio can hold a four-week schedule only if script and storyboard sign-off come back in days, which is why naming a single decision-maker at kickoff shortens projects more reliably than paying a rush fee, something worth settling before you[hire a motion design expert](https://www.awesomic.com/blog/how-to-hire-a-motion-design-expert---a-comprehensive-guide) or an outside studio.

## FAQs


### How much does a 60-second animated explainer video cost?


Yans Media's survey of 110 studios found an average of $8,746, with real quotes spanning $950 to $250,000. Most businesses land between $4,000 and $10,000, and startups typically find the best value between $4,000 and $7,000. Location moves the number more than most buyers expect, with US studios averaging $12,667 against $5,662 in Europe for the same brief.


### How long does it take to produce an animated video?


Budget four to eight weeks for a standard 60 to 90-second explainer, split across script, storyboard, animation and revisions. Brand films and 3D technical animation commonly run three to four months. Studios using AI-assisted workflows are delivering faster, and our own Parcha launch video was approved in 27 days, but the real limiter is usually how quickly your team signs off each stage.


### Should I hire an animation studio or a design subscription?


It depends on frequency. A project studio gives you a director's attention on one film and is the right buy for a flagship piece. A subscription makes more sense when video recurs monthly and each one drags a landing page, social cutdowns and a deck along with it, a pattern visible across our[case studies](https://www.awesomic.com/case-study) . Teams commissioning one video a year should almost always use a studio.


### What is the difference between an explainer video and motion graphics?


An explainer is a narrated piece, usually 60 to 120 seconds, built to make a product or idea understood. Motion graphics is a technique, animated type and graphic elements, used inside explainers, ads, social posts and title sequences. You can buy motion graphics without buying an explainer, and studios price them very differently, so name the one you mean in your brief.


### How do I judge an animation studio's portfolio?


Look for work in your specific format and your level of complexity rather than the most beautiful thing on their reel. A studio with stunning brand films may never have made a software walkthrough. Check that recent work is genuinely recent, ask which pieces the team assigned to you personally animated, and be wary of a reel built on a single visual style if that style does not suit your brand.
