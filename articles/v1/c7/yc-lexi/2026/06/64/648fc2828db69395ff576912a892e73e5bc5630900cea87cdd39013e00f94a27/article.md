# How Is AI Changing the Legal Profession?


Naresh Garg


June 2, 2026


10 min read


How is AI changing the legal profession? It is speeding up drafting, research, review, and case organization while making lawyer oversight more important.


AI is changing the legal profession by speeding up drafting, research, document review, and case organization, while making human verification, judgment, and ethics more important, not less. In practice, that means lawyers in many jurisdictions can complete routine work faster, serve more clients efficiently, and focus more of their time on strategy, advocacy, and client counseling. The shift is real, but it is not a license to stop thinking: every output still needs a lawyer's review.


I come from a family of lawyers. My father practised long before any of this technology existed, and I watched him build a career the hard way through patience, thorough preparation, and hours of work that most people outside the profession never see. I started the same way. Somewhere between his generation and mine, the mechanics of legal work began to change.


That puts me in an unusual position. I have practised in a world without AI, and I am practising now in a world with it. Both experiences are real. Neither is theoretical. So when someone asks how AI is changing the legal profession, I do not give a think-piece answer. I give a practitioner's answer from the court floor, where the work actually happens.


Here is what I have seen, what I believe, and what I think lawyers need to hear right now.


## What legal practice looked like before AI


Before we talk about change, it is worth being honest about what the work traditionally demanded.


Drafting a strong legal document, whether a petition, notice, submission, or contract, took hours. Not because lawyers were slow, but because doing it properly required time. You worked through precedents, notes, prior files, statutory text, and client facts. You drafted, reviewed, revised, and then revised again.


Research could take even longer. Finding authority that genuinely supported your position often meant long hours searching databases, checking references, and verifying that a case said what someone claimed it said. In smaller practices and busy trial environments, that burden often sat directly on the lawyer's shoulders.


Those hours were not meaningless. They built discipline and legal instinct. But much of that time went into tasks that required effort more than judgment. That distinction matters, because AI is changing exactly that layer of work.


## What AI has actually changed in legal work


### The shift from hours to minutes


The first visible change is speed. Work that once required long stretches of concentrated effort can now be organized, summarized, or drafted in a fraction of the time. A contract can be redlined faster. A large file can be summarized faster. A first draft can appear quickly enough to change how a lawyer structures the day.


That does not mean the machine is replacing legal reasoning. It means the machine is reducing the administrative and mechanical burden around that reasoning.


For lawyers who want a practical example, this is why teams increasingly use tools for[real legal workflows](https://www.getlexi.io/blog/how-lawyers-actually-use-ai-in-practice) rather than as a novelty. The value is not magic. The value is time.


### What this means on the ground


Consider a familiar scenario: a client arrives under time pressure, documents are scattered across emails and attachments, and the legal issue needs quick analysis. Previously, a lawyer might spend much of the day simply organizing material before even beginning the real legal task.


With the right system, that first pass becomes faster. Documents can be grouped, summarized, and turned into a usable starting point. The lawyer still has to inspect the facts, confirm the law, adjust the framing, and decide strategy. But the path to a polished work product is shorter.


That efficiency matters whether you are in a solo practice, a litigation team, a corporate legal department, or a larger firm. It is one reason legal AI is being adopted across[law firms](https://www.getlexi.io/solutions/law-firms) ,[litigation teams](https://www.getlexi.io/solutions/litigation) , and[in-house legal departments](https://www.getlexi.io/solutions/in-house) .


## The biggest risk is not replacement. It is uncritical reliance.


Here is the point I think the profession still does not say loudly enough: the biggest danger is not that AI will replace lawyers. The biggest danger is that lawyers will start trusting fluent output more than their own professional responsibility.


AI can produce text that sounds authoritative and still be wrong, incomplete, jurisdictionally irrelevant, or unsuited to the client's facts. This is not a theoretical concern. In 2023, the sanctions decision in *Mata v. Avianca* in the Southern District of New York became a widely discussed example of lawyers submitting AI-generated citations that did not exist. The lesson was simple and global: if it goes out under your name, you own it.


That is why the right question is not whether AI can generate legal work. The right question is whether the lawyer using it has a process to verify every important point. ## Will AI replace lawyers?


Not in the way people usually mean the question. Law is not just document production. It is judgment, context, persuasion, ethics, negotiation, and trust. Clients are not paying only for words on a page. They are paying for someone to understand risk, advise on consequences, and stand behind the recommendation.


What AI can do well is handle parts of the process that are repetitive, time-consuming, or structurally pattern-based. That can make lawyers more productive, and in many cases more responsive to clients. Lexi customers, for example, use AI to move work forward faster across drafting, review, and research workflows, with reported outcomes including 45% more cases per attorney and 10+ hours saved per lawyer per week.


So the better framing is not replacement versus survival. It is redistribution of work. The repetitive layer shrinks. The judgment layer becomes more visible. That is why I see AI as amplification, not substitution. ## The profession is splitting, and not only by age


People often describe AI adoption as a generational divide, and there is some truth in that. But in my experience, the deeper divide is between lawyers who understand the tool's limits and lawyers who do not.


### Why some experienced lawyers resist it


Many senior lawyers built excellent careers without any of this. Their skepticism is understandable. They know from experience that legal quality comes from careful reading, hard-earned instinct, and responsibility that cannot be outsourced. They are right to protect those standards.


But outright refusal can become its own problem. If a lawyer ignores tools that reduce mechanical work, the result may be unnecessary cost, slower turnaround, and less time for the work clients actually value most.


### Why some younger lawyers use it too quickly


At the other end, some younger lawyers adopt AI enthusiastically before developing the habits that make its output safe. Fast drafting can feel like competence, but speed without verification is not professional strength. It is exposure.


This is especially important in research and review. A summary is not a substitute for reading. A suggested authority is not a substitute for checking the source. That is why workflow discipline matters as much as software quality.


### Where the profession needs to land


The lawyers who will benefit most are the ones who combine traditional rigor with modern efficiency. They use AI to accelerate the first pass, not to eliminate legal thinking. They treat it as a capable assistant, not as a final decision-maker.


## How AI is changing access to justice and client service


One of the most important effects of AI may be outside elite practice. In many jurisdictions, legal help remains too expensive, too slow, or too hard to access for ordinary people and small businesses. When routine legal work becomes faster, the cost structure of service can change too.


That does not mean AI alone solves access-to-justice problems. It does not. But it can help lawyers deliver more help with the same number of hours, and it can make basic legal information easier to organize and explain. In multilingual markets especially, better drafting assistance, summarization, and translation support can reduce friction for both lawyers and clients.


The opportunity is large, provided lawyers remain clear about what the tools can and cannot do. If a system helps someone understand documents, prepare questions, or organize facts before meeting counsel, that can be valuable. If it encourages blind trust in automated answers, that is a different story. ## How lawyers should use AI well


If you are going to use AI in practice, the goal is not just to use it. The goal is to use it responsibly.


- **Give it precise inputs.** Vague prompts usually produce vague results. Include the facts, document type, objective, jurisdiction, audience, and tone.
- **Verify every important output.** Check citations, quoted language, procedural assumptions, and legal conclusions. In many jurisdictions, professional duties of competence and confidentiality still apply regardless of the tool used.
- **Use it for the first pass, not the final call.** Let it organize, summarize, compare, or draft. Then apply legal analysis yourself.
- **Protect client information.** Understand your vendor's privacy and security controls, and check your local bar rules, court guidance, or client outside-counsel guidelines before uploading sensitive material.
- **Build a repeatable review process.** The safest use of AI is not casual. It is structured.

## A final word from one practising lawyer to another


I am not interested in telling lawyers that the old way of working had no value. It did. That work built us. It trained attention, patience, and seriousness. Those qualities still matter, and they will matter even more if the first draft now comes from a machine.


But it is also true that too much legal time has always been spent on work that did not need a lawyer's full mind. If AI can remove part of that burden, then the profession has an opportunity to become better at its highest-value work: advising, arguing, negotiating, deciding, and taking responsibility.


That is how AI is changing the legal profession. It is not making lawyers irrelevant. It is forcing the profession to separate mechanical effort from legal judgment, and to decide which one clients should really be paying for.


## FAQ


### How is AI changing the legal profession for small law firms?


It helps small firms handle drafting, review, summarization, and file organization faster, which can improve turnaround time and free lawyers to focus on advice and advocacy. The key is to keep a lawyer in the loop for verification and final judgment.


### Can AI do legal research reliably?


AI can speed up legal research by surfacing relevant authorities and summarizing materials, but reliability depends on verification. Lawyers should always confirm the source, check the jurisdiction, and read the underlying authority themselves.


### What is the safest way for lawyers to use AI?


The safest approach is to use AI as an assistant for first drafts, summaries, and issue spotting while maintaining a structured human review process. Lawyers should also review confidentiality obligations and check applicable local professional guidance before using any tool with client data.
