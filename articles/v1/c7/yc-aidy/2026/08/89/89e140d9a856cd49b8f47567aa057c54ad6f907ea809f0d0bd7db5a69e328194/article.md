# Lichtiger Score for Severe Ulcerative Colitis Explained

The Lichtiger Index is an eight-part clinical score used in ulcerative colitis studies, especially[severe or hospitalized disease](https://aidyhq.com/learn/acute-severe-uc-what-happens-in-the-hospital) . It records diarrhea, nighttime diarrhea, visible blood, fecal incontinence, abdominal pain or cramping, general well-being, abdominal tenderness, and antidiarrheal-drug use. The score itself contains no CRP, ESR, hemoglobin, albumin, or fecal calprotectin. It is sometimes called a modified Truelove and Witts index, which creates a naming trap: the classic Truelove and Witts criteria used to define acute severe UC include systemic and laboratory findings ([IBD activity-index review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938394/) ;[acute severe UC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11307437/) ).


## What the Lichtiger score asks


The index assigns points to daily stool frequency and adds separate weight for[nocturnal diarrhea](https://aidyhq.com/learn/nighttime-ibd-symptoms-what-to-track-and-when-they-matter) . Visible blood is graded by its proportion of stools. Incontinence, cramping, and antidiarrheal use add points, while well-being and abdominal tenderness are graded by severity. The total ranges from 0 to 21, with higher scores representing greater clinical activity.


Trials have used thresholds and reductions in the score to define response or remission, but definitions should be read from the protocol. A hospital team may collect the components daily to follow a trajectory. The direction and speed of change can be more useful than attaching a generic label to one total.


Several inputs can be influenced by problems other than mucosal inflammation.[Infection, medication effects](https://aidyhq.com/learn/ibd-flare-vs-infection-vs-food-reaction-a-practical-comparison) , reduced intake, and other gastrointestinal conditions can affect stool frequency and pain. Antidiarrheal use is particularly contextual because these medicines may be inappropriate in some severe-colitis situations. Do not take one to improve a score without the treating team’s guidance.


## Why this is not the same as the ASUC definition


Classic Truelove and Witts severe colitis requires frequent bloody stools plus at least one sign of systemic toxicity, historically including fever, tachycardia, anemia, or raised ESR. Modern practice may use CRP in related assessments. That is a definition used to identify acute severe ulcerative colitis, a medical emergency requiring hospital care, not the same calculation as the symptom-focused Lichtiger total ([UC severity review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7510557/) ;[acute severe UC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11307437/) ).


Hospital teams also use day-three prediction tools to estimate risk of intravenous-steroid failure. The Oxford criteria combine stool frequency and CRP; other tools can incorporate albumin or colonic dilation. Those scores answer a treatment-response question and should not be mislabeled as Lichtiger.


## Where labs and endoscopy fit


Although labs are absent from Lichtiger,[CBC, CRP, electrolytes](https://aidyhq.com/learn/understanding-your-uc-lab-results-crp-calprotectin-and-more) , renal function, and albumin can be crucial in severe colitis. Stool testing can look for infection. Imaging may assess dilation or complications, and limited endoscopic evaluation can help confirm severity and collect biopsies. Guidelines and reviews emphasize this multi-part inpatient assessment rather than relying on one symptom index ([acute severe UC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11307437/) ).


If a Lichtiger score appears in a record, save the date, individual answers, and whether the patient was hospitalized. Add vital signs, stool-test results, hemoglobin, CRP, albumin, electrolytes, imaging, and treatment timing. Rapidly worsening bloody diarrhea, fever, fast heart rate, fainting,[severe pain or swelling, or dehydration](https://aidyhq.com/learn/when-to-go-to-the-er-for-a-uc-flare-red-flags-what-theyll-check) warrants urgent evaluation. The score can structure clinical change, but it does not replace the laboratory and bedside assessment that makes severe UC safe to manage.


## Questions that make the number more useful


Before comparing this result with an older one, confirm that both records used the same named instrument, version, scoring range, and assessment window. Ask whether the total represents the worst segment, a sum across segments, an average, or a single day. If a clinician or study used a modified version, record exactly what was removed or added. Also check whether treatment, bowel preparation, infection, missed diary entries, or incomplete examination affected the inputs. A small apparent change may reflect measurement conditions rather than a meaningful change in disease.


At a visit, useful questions include: What domain does this score measure? Which component drove the result? What would count as response or remission in this setting? Does the number need confirmation with blood work, stool testing, endoscopy, biopsy, or imaging? Is the goal to follow symptoms, current inflammation, structural damage, treatment response, or quality of life? Asking these questions turns an unfamiliar total into a specific clinical conversation.


For[personal tracking](https://aidyhq.com/learn/does-symptom-tracking-actually-help-with-ibd) , avoid combining unlike scores on one graph. Keep each instrument in its own series and place objective tests on parallel timelines. Save the source report because the component details may matter later. Note both the sample or procedure date and the date you received the result. Trends are easiest to interpret when the method and timing stay consistent. If the number conflicts with how you feel or with another test, bring the disagreement to your care team instead of choosing the result that seems most reassuring. Discordance is often the reason to look more closely.
