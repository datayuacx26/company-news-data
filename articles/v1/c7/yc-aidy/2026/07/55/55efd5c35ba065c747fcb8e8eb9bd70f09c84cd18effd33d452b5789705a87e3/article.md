# Ulcerative Colitis Mayo Score: Full, Partial, and Modified

The phrase “Mayo score” can refer to three related ulcerative colitis measures. The full Mayo score has four components and ranges from 0 to 12. The partial Mayo removes endoscopy and ranges from 0 to 9. The modified Mayo removes the physician global assessment and also ranges from 0 to 9. Those two 9-point totals are not the same. None of the versions includes CRP, ESR, hemoglobin, albumin, or fecal calprotectin in its calculation ([Mayo-score comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9802037/) ;[IBD activity-index review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938394/) ).


## Which components are in each version


The full Mayo score assigns 0 to 3 points each for stool frequency, rectal bleeding, endoscopic appearance, and physician global assessment. Stool frequency is judged against the person’s normal pattern rather than a universal count. Rectal bleeding ranges from none to blood alone. The endoscopic component is the[Mayo Endoscopic Subscore](https://aidyhq.com/learn/mayo-score-explained-what-03-means-in-ulcerative-colitis) , or MES, and the physician assessment integrates the clinician’s overall impression.


The partial Mayo includes stool frequency, rectal bleeding, and physician global assessment. It is useful between colonoscopies because it does not require a scope. The modified Mayo includes stool frequency, rectal bleeding, and endoscopy, eliminating the physician global assessment to reduce subjectivity. Modern trial descriptions may specify central endoscopy reading or altered definitions, so check the stated protocol rather than assuming every “modified Mayo” was calculated identically ([Mayo-score comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9802037/) ).


An isolated “Mayo 2” needs clarification. It might mean a total score of 2 or an MES of 2. The latter indicates moderately active endoscopic disease under common definitions, but a full Mayo total of 2 tells a different story. Save the component values and version with the total.


## How remission and response are defined


Trials often define clinical response as a specified reduction in total score plus improvement in rectal bleeding.[Clinical remission](https://aidyhq.com/learn/clinical-remission-vs-biochemical-remission-ibd) typically requires a low total and very low component values. Endoscopic improvement is reported from the MES component. Exact definitions have changed across drug-development programs, and newer trials may use the modified Mayo and stricter bleeding requirements. Comparing remission percentages across studies therefore requires checking the score version and endpoint definition.


Partial Mayo can follow symptoms during routine care, but it cannot establish mucosal healing because there is no endoscopy input. Modified Mayo includes the scope but omits the clinician’s global assessment. Full Mayo includes both. A structured review of endoscopic indices describes MES separately from[UCEIS, another UC scope score](https://aidyhq.com/learn/uceis-explained-another-uc-endoscopic-score-you-might-see-in-your-chart) with different descriptors ([endoscopic activity review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9329646/) ).


## Where lab results fit


[CRP and fecal calprotectin](https://aidyhq.com/learn/calprotectin-vs-crp-for-uc-which-one-reflects-colon-inflammation-better) remain separate objective measures. AGA guidance recommends combining symptoms and biomarkers for UC monitoring and uses fecal calprotectin thresholds in specific clinical situations, but those values do not add points to Mayo ([AGA UC biomarker guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-biomarkers-for-the-management-of-ulcerative-colitis-uc/) ). Treat-to-target recommendations likewise distinguish symptom remission, biomarker normalization, and endoscopic healing ([STRIDE-II](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33359090/) ).


[Track daily stool frequency](https://aidyhq.com/learn/ulcerative-colitis-symptom-diary-track-stool-frequency-blood-urgency-pain-and-medication) relative to your own normal and rectal bleeding consistently. Keep the exact Mayo version, each component, scope date, preparation quality, medication timing, CRP, calprotectin, CBC, and albumin together. Do not use a self-calculated value to change treatment. Heavy bleeding, fainting, severe abdominal pain or swelling, fever, dehydration, or very frequent worsening diarrhea should prompt urgent clinical advice. The Mayo family becomes far more useful once the version is explicit and the lab and endoscopy context travels with the number.


## Questions that make the number more useful


Before comparing this result with an older one, confirm that both records used the same named instrument, version, scoring range, and assessment window. Ask whether the total represents the worst segment, a sum across segments, an average, or a single day. If a clinician or study used a modified version, record exactly what was removed or added. Also check whether treatment, bowel preparation, infection, missed diary entries, or incomplete examination affected the inputs. A small apparent change may reflect measurement conditions rather than a meaningful change in disease.


At a visit, useful questions include: What domain does this score measure? Which component drove the result? What would count as response or remission in this setting? Does the number need confirmation with blood work, stool testing, endoscopy, biopsy, or imaging? Is the goal to follow symptoms, current inflammation, structural damage, treatment response, or quality of life? Asking these questions turns an unfamiliar total into a specific clinical conversation.


For personal tracking, avoid combining unlike scores on one graph. Keep each instrument in its own series and place objective tests on parallel timelines. Save the source report because the component details may matter later. Note both the sample or procedure date and the date you received the result. Trends are easiest to interpret when the method and timing stay consistent. If the[number conflicts with how you feel](https://aidyhq.com/learn/when-an-ibd-symptom-does-not-match-your-gut-activity) or with another test, bring the disagreement to your care team instead of choosing the result that seems most reassuring. Discordance is often the reason to look more closely.
