# SCCAI Score for Ulcerative Colitis: What the Questions Mean

The Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index, or SCCAI, summarizes the daily symptom burden of ulcerative colitis. It asks about daytime and nighttime bowel movements, urgency, blood, general well-being, and specified symptoms outside the bowel. It does not include a colonoscopy, CRP, ESR, CBC, albumin, or fecal calprotectin. A low SCCAI can therefore indicate limited symptoms without proving that the colon is healed, while a higher score can be influenced by problems other than active UC ([IBD activity-index review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938394/) ).


## What the six SCCAI areas mean


Daytime stool frequency and nighttime stool frequency are scored separately. That distinction captures a burden missed by tools that ask only for a daily total. Urgency is graded from none through immediate need or incontinence. Rectal bleeding is graded by frequency and amount. General well-being records the person’s overall sense of health. The final area adds points for selected[extraintestinal features](https://aidyhq.com/learn/uc-beyond-the-gut-joint-pain-skin-eye-issues) , such as joint, eye, or skin manifestations.


Because wording and response options matter, use the actual questionnaire rather than reconstructing it from memory. Record whether a night stool woke you from sleep, whether blood was streaked, obvious, or passed without stool, and whether urgency caused leakage. Infection, bowel preparation, laxatives, menstrual symptoms, pelvic-floor dysfunction, and other conditions can affect answers.


Common research interpretation treats scores of 2 or less as remission, with increasing ranges used for mild, moderate, and severe activity. However, cutoffs vary across validation studies and uses. A change over time on the same version is often more informative than applying an isolated internet label.


## SCCAI versus Mayo and UC PRO-2


SCCAI contains more symptom detail than UC PRO-2. UC PRO-2 focuses on stool frequency and rectal bleeding, while SCCAI also captures nighttime stools, urgency, general well-being, and extraintestinal manifestations. Partial Mayo uses stool frequency, rectal bleeding, and a physician global assessment. The scales are not interchangeable even when they move in the same direction ([PRO-2 and mucosal-healing study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34286411/) ;[Mayo-score comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9802037/) ).


SCCAI is noninvasive and convenient for repeat measurement. Its deliberate lack of endoscopy also limits what “remission” means. Endoscopic activity is scored separately with MES or[UCEIS](https://aidyhq.com/learn/uceis-explained-another-uc-endoscopic-score-you-might-see-in-your-chart) , and biopsy activity with a histology index such as Nancy or Robarts ([endoscopic activity review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9329646/) ).


## Why labs remain separate


Fecal calprotectin and CRP provide objective inflammatory context but do not alter SCCAI points. AGA guidance recommends[combining symptoms with biomarkers](https://aidyhq.com/learn/treat-to-target-ibd-what-patients-need-to-know) during UC monitoring, including when symptoms and inflammation may disagree ([AGA UC biomarker guidance](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-biomarkers-for-the-management-of-ulcerative-colitis-uc/) ). STRIDE-II similarly treats symptom response, biomarker change, and healing as distinct targets ([STRIDE-II](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33359090/) ).


Keep the six component answers, date, medication changes, and unusual circumstances beside the total. Add calprotectin, CRP, CBC, albumin, endoscopy, and biopsy results from the same period.[Contact your care team promptly](https://aidyhq.com/learn/when-to-call-your-gi-visit-urgent-care-or-go-to-the-er-for-ibd-symptoms) for rapidly increasing bowel frequency, substantial bleeding, fever, severe pain or swelling, fainting, or dehydration. SCCAI makes the patient experience visible and repeatable. It becomes safer and more clinically useful when it is read as a symptom score, not a substitute for inflammatory testing.


## Questions that make the number more useful


Before comparing this result with an older one, confirm that both records used the same named instrument, version, scoring range, and assessment window. Ask whether the total represents the worst segment, a sum across segments, an average, or a single day. If a clinician or study used a modified version, record exactly what was removed or added. Also check whether treatment, bowel preparation, infection, missed diary entries, or incomplete examination affected the inputs. A small apparent change may reflect measurement conditions rather than a meaningful change in disease.


At a visit, useful questions include: What domain does this score measure? Which component drove the result? What would count as response or remission in this setting? Does the number need confirmation with blood work, stool testing, endoscopy, biopsy, or imaging? Is the goal to follow symptoms, current inflammation, structural damage, treatment response, or quality of life? Asking these questions turns an unfamiliar total into a specific clinical conversation.


For personal tracking, avoid combining unlike scores on one graph. Keep each instrument in its own series and place objective tests on parallel timelines. Save the source report because the component details may matter later. Note both the sample or procedure date and the date you received the result. Trends are easiest to interpret when the method and timing stay consistent. If the number[conflicts with how you feel](https://aidyhq.com/learn/when-an-ibd-symptom-does-not-match-your-gut-activity) or with another test, bring the disagreement to your care team instead of choosing the result that seems most reassuring. Discordance is often the reason to look more closely.
