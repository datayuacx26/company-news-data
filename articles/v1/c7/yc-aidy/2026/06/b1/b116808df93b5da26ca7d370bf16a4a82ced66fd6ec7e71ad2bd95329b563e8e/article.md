# What Your GI Needs to Know When You Report a Flare

When you suspect a flare, the message you send your gastroenterologist is the raw material for the decision they make about your care. A vague note like "I think I'm flaring" forces the office to reply with questions, and each round of back-and-forth delays the moment someone can actually help. That gap is common. In a 2024 survey of inflammatory bowel disease patients, only 32 percent felt completely satisfied that their needs were addressed after a gastroenterology visit, and[tools to recognize and communicate flares ranked among the top unmet information needs](https://badgut.org/wp-content/uploads/Unmet-Needs-of-IBD-2024.pdf) . Reporting a flare well is a skill you can prepare for. Treat the report as a clean data handoff, and your gastroenterologist can respond on the first read instead of the third.


## Why "I'm flaring" is not enough on its own


Gastroenterologists grade disease activity using measurable changes, not adjectives. For ulcerative colitis, severity is scaled by concrete numbers such as stool frequency and how often blood appears, where[more than six bloody bowel movements a day signals severe disease and more than ten signals fulminant disease](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/ulcerative-colitis/symptoms-causes) . Crohn's disease is tracked through[diarrhea, abdominal cramping, weight loss, fatigue, and fever](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/crohns-disease/symptoms-causes) . Your clinician cannot plug "worse than usual" into any of these frameworks. When you translate how you feel into counts, dates, and comparisons against your normal state, you hand over information the office can act on directly.


## Describe the change from your baseline


The single most useful thing you can report is the contrast between your usual state and now. Your care team relies on you to track disease between visits so they can see how it is changing, and the Crohn's & Colitis Foundation recommends[documenting symptoms with a tracker and staying in close communication, more often during a flare](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-07/managing-flares-brochure-final-online.pdf) . Give your baseline first, then the current number. For example, note that you normally have two formed stools a day and are now having eight loose stools with visible blood. Cover stool frequency, blood, urgency, waking at night to use the bathroom, abdominal pain and its location, appetite, and any weight loss. Then anchor it to a timeline: when the change started and whether it is worsening, steady, or improving. A three-day arc tells a very different story than a three-week one.


## Include your recent labs and biomarkers


Numbers from stool and blood tests help your gastroenterologist decide whether your symptoms reflect active inflammation or something else. Fecal calprotectin is the most useful of these. AGA guidance treats[a fecal calprotectin below 150 micrograms per gram, along with a normal C-reactive protein, as evidence against active inflammation in ulcerative colitis](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-biomarkers-for-the-management-of-ulcerative-colitis-uc/) , and applies[the same calprotectin threshold of 150 micrograms per gram and a C-reactive protein under 5 milligrams per liter in Crohn's disease](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-biomarkers-for-the-management-of-crohns-disease/) . If you have had any of these tests recently, report the value and the date. When symptoms are significant and biomarkers are elevated, that combination can point toward a treatment adjustment, whereas mild symptoms with unclear markers often prompt a scope instead. Sharing the numbers you already have can spare you a redundant test and speed the decision.


## Report your medication history and possible triggers


Your gastroenterologist needs to know what you are actually taking, which can differ from what was prescribed. State each IBD medication, the dose, and honestly whether you have missed doses, run out, or stopped. A flare during a lapse in therapy leads to a different plan than a flare on full adherence. Then list anything that could be driving symptoms that is not a flare. Recent antibiotics matter, because AGA guidance directs clinicians to[test for Clostridioides difficile infection in any IBD patient presenting with a flare](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/management-of-clostridium-difficile-infection-in-inflammatory-bowel-disease-ibd/) , and a new infection can mimic disease activity. Note new nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, recent travel, and any foods that reliably worsen your symptoms, which the Foundation advises you[raise with your doctor or dietitian](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-07/managing-flares-brochure-final-online.pdf) . These details help your clinician separate a true flare from an infection, a food reaction, or a medication effect.


## Flag the symptoms that change the urgency


Some findings move your report from routine to urgent, so state them plainly at the top rather than burying them. The Crohn's & Colitis Foundation lists warning signs that warrant urgent attention:[a fever above 100.3 degrees Fahrenheit, an inability to keep liquids down because of nausea, vomiting, or pain, rectal bleeding with blood clots, and constant pain](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-07/managing-flares-brochure-final-online.pdf) . For people with Crohn's disease, crampy abdominal pain paired with vomiting and bloating can indicate an intestinal obstruction, a described[complication that may require prompt treatment](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/legacy/assets/pdfs/intestinalcomps.pdf) . If any of these apply, say so first and follow your action plan for emergencies rather than waiting for a routine reply. Being explicit about severity helps the office triage your message correctly.


## A message template you can adapt


Assemble the pieces into one message so nothing gets lost. A workable structure reads like this:


> Name, diagnosis, and current medications with any missed doses. My baseline is X; over the past N days it has changed to Y. Specific symptoms: stool frequency, blood, urgency, nighttime episodes, pain and location, weight change. Recent labs: calprotectin or CRP value and date, if available. Possible triggers: recent antibiotics, NSAIDs, travel, illness. Urgent flags: fever, vomiting, inability to keep liquids down, blood clots, or constant pain.


Keep it factual and specific. One complete message that answers the questions your gastroenterologist would otherwise have to ask is worth more than a long narrative about how difficult the week has been.


## Bringing it together


A good flare report is built from a handful of concrete elements: the change from your baseline, a timeline, your recent lab numbers, your true medication and trigger history, and any urgent warning signs stated up front. Each of these maps onto how clinicians actually measure and manage disease activity, which is why a report organized this way lets your gastroenterologist respond meaningfully the first time. Gathering the information takes a few minutes, and it turns an anxious guess into a document your care team can use to decide what happens next.
