# When to Call Your GI, Visit Urgent Care, or Go to the ER for IBD

Deciding whether inflammatory bowel disease symptoms warrant a message to your gastroenterologist, a trip to urgent care, or a visit to the emergency room is one of the hardest judgment calls people with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis face. Symptoms overlap heavily across an ordinary flare, an infection, a food reaction, and a serious complication, which makes the decision genuinely confusing in the moment. This guide organizes IBD symptoms into three escalation tiers so you can recognize the general pattern each one tends to follow. It is educational information, not personalized medical advice, and it does not replace the specific instructions your own care team has given you. When you are unsure about severe symptoms, the safest course is to get evaluated rather than wait.


## Routine concerns worth a message to your GI team


Many shifts in IBD symptoms can wait for a non-urgent message through your patient portal. A flare is the reappearance of disease symptoms such as[frequent or urgent bowel movements, diarrhea, bloody stool, and abdominal pain](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-02/Managing-flares.pdf) , sometimes with fatigue and reduced appetite. Diarrhea,[rectal bleeding, abdominal cramping, and tenesmus](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/ulcerative-colitis/symptoms-causes) are among the core symptoms of ulcerative colitis, so their mild return is often expected rather than alarming. Mild, gradual changes, questions about medications, or a sense that your usual routine is slipping are appropriate reasons to contact your physician, who generally recommends[reaching out when symptoms worsen or when you have concerns about your treatment](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-02/Managing-flares.pdf) . Your team may respond by checking objective markers of inflammation. The American Gastroenterological Association recommends monitoring ulcerative colitis with[fecal calprotectin and C-reactive protein alongside symptoms](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-biomarkers-for-the-management-of-ulcerative-colitis-uc/) , where a calprotectin below 150 micrograms per gram helps exclude active inflammation. Similar[biomarker-based monitoring applies to Crohn's disease](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/the-role-of-biomarkers-for-the-management-of-crohns-disease/) . This kind of measured follow-up fits well with the[twice-yearly monitoring visits](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-02/Managing-flares.pdf) typical during remission.


## Symptoms that warrant an urgent call the same day


A middle tier of symptoms deserves a prompt call to your gastroenterology team rather than a routine message, even if you are not yet in crisis. Increasing frequency of bloody stools, a rising fever, early signs of dehydration, or a flare that is not responding to your usual medications all justify same-day contact. A worsening flare in Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis can also signal a new infection rather than the disease itself. Because the two look almost identical, the American Gastroenterological Association advises that clinicians[test patients presenting with an IBD flare for Clostridioides difficile infection](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/management-of-clostridium-difficile-infection-in-inflammatory-bowel-disease-ibd/) . A timely call lets your team arrange stool testing, adjust treatment, or decide whether you need to be seen in person before symptoms escalate. If you cannot reach your team and symptoms are climbing, do not simply wait until the next business day.


## Emergency red flags: when IBD symptoms mean the ER


Some symptoms point toward a medical emergency and warrant going to the emergency room. The Crohn's and Colitis Foundation advises seeking immediate care for[heavy and persistent diarrhea, rectal bleeding with blood clots in the stool, constant pain, and high fever](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/2019-02/Managing-flares.pdf) . Severe abdominal pain, significant rectal bleeding, and signs of dehydration such as dizziness, very dark urine, or an inability to keep fluids down belong in this category as well. Ulcerative colitis that becomes acutely severe is a well-defined emergency. Acute severe ulcerative colitis is diagnosed when a person passes[six or more bloody stools per day along with systemic signs](https://academic.oup.com/bmb/article/151/1/3/7686450) such as fever, a pulse of ninety beats per minute or higher, or anemia, and it requires hospital admission for intensive treatment and monitoring. If your bleeding, pain, or bowel frequency reaches this level, the hospital is the right destination.


## Recognizing obstruction and toxic megacolon


Two structural complications deserve particular attention because they can progress quickly and may require surgery. Intestinal obstruction is the[most frequent complication of Crohn's disease](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/legacy/assets/pdfs/intestinalcomps.pdf) and results from swelling and scar tissue that narrow the bowel. Warning signs include crampy abdominal pain, often with vomiting and bloating, and an inability to pass stool or gas. Toxic megacolon is described as the[most serious complication](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/legacy/assets/pdfs/intestinalcomps.pdf) , involving severe dilation of the colon along with high fever and abdominal pain and tenderness. Clinically, it is defined by[colonic dilation greater than six centimeters plus systemic findings](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547679/) such as a temperature above 38 degrees Celsius, a heart rate over 120 beats per minute, or a high white blood cell count. Both conditions require immediate medical attention, so go to the emergency room if these patterns appear.


## Urgent care versus the emergency room


Urgent care and the emergency room are not interchangeable for IBD. Urgent care is reasonable for a minor problem such as a mild upper respiratory infection or a question that your GI office cannot answer quickly, and some centers can provide intravenous fluids for early dehydration. It generally cannot arrange the advanced imaging, surgical consultation, or inpatient care that a serious IBD complication demands. Because[obstruction and toxic megacolon](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/sites/default/files/legacy/assets/pdfs/intestinalcomps.pdf) may require imaging, decompression, or surgery, red-flag symptoms should send you straight to the emergency room rather than a walk-in clinic. When you arrive at urgent care and staff recognize a potential emergency, they will refer you onward, which costs valuable time. For severe pain, high fever, heavy bleeding, persistent vomiting, or suspected obstruction, choose the emergency room first.


## Preparing for whichever step comes next


Whichever tier your symptoms fall into, a clear account of what is happening helps your clinician act faster. Note when symptoms began, how many bowel movements you are having, whether there is blood or clots, your temperature, the location and severity of pain, and how much you have been able to drink and eat. Keep your gastroenterologist's contact details and after-hours instructions somewhere easy to find, since many practices have specific guidance for weekends and holidays. The general principle is straightforward. Gradual, mild changes usually fit a portal message or an urgent call, while severe abdominal pain, high fever, heavy rectal bleeding, persistent vomiting, or signs of obstruction call for emergency evaluation. Learning these patterns will not remove every uncertainty, but it can help you respond with less panic and more confidence when symptoms change.
