# Your First IBD Medication List: What to Record and Keep Updated

When you are newly diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease, one of the most useful documents you can build is a clear, current medication list. Your gastroenterologist makes treatment decisions based on what you have already taken, how you responded, and what you are taking now, so the accuracy of that record directly shapes your care. Federal safety agencies already recommend the habit. The[CDC advises keeping a written list of your medicines, vitamins, and supplements](https://www.cdc.gov/medication-safety/about/index.html) and making sure every provider knows about all of them. This guide explains what belongs on an IBD medication list template and how to keep it from going stale between appointments.


## Why an accurate IBD medication list matters


An incomplete list is a genuine safety problem, not a paperwork chore. In the United States,[adverse drug events cause more than 1.5 million emergency department visits each year and nearly 500,000 hospitalizations](https://www.cdc.gov/medication-safety/data-research/facts-stats/index.html) . These errors cluster at transitions of care. A patient safety review found that[more than 40 percent of medication errors stem from inadequate reconciliation during admission, transfer, and discharge](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK2648/) , and that omission of a drug the patient was actually taking was the single most common discrepancy. Medication reconciliation,[the formal comparison of what you take against what a clinician has ordered](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/match/intro.html) , only works when your side of the record is complete. For IBD specifically, poor tracking carries consequences: a review of adherence obstacles reported[non-adherence rates ranging from roughly a quarter to more than two-thirds of patients across countries and tied poor adherence to disease exacerbation, higher costs, and treatment failure](https://www.dovepress.com/obstacles-to-medication-adherence-for-patients-with-inflammatory-bowel-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-PPA) .


## What to include in your IBD medication list


Build a template with one row per medicine and a column for each detail a clinician actually asks about. At minimum, record the drug name in both brand and generic form, the dose, the route, the frequency, the start date, and the prescriber. IBD treatment spans several classes, and the[NIDDK notes that corticosteroids should only be used short term while immunosuppressants, biologics, and newer small-molecule medicines are used for longer control](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/crohns-disease/treatment) . Because steroid courses are meant to be brief, dose and date columns matter for tracking a taper. For infused or injected biologics, add the infusion or injection dates and the dosing interval, since your team needs to know exactly when you last received a dose. A row structure like this doubles as an ulcerative colitis medication tracker or a way to keep track of Crohn's medications without rewriting the whole history each visit.


## Recording past therapies and why they still count


Your history of discontinued drugs is often as important as your current regimen, because prior biologic exposure changes how well the next therapy is likely to work. A 2025 analysis found that IBD patients[who stopped a biologic because of intolerance responded far better to their next treatment than those who stopped because the drug failed](https://academic.oup.com/ecco-jcc/article/19/3/jjae151/7762524) , with clinical remission in ulcerative colitis reaching 25 percent after intolerance versus 11 percent after failure. Recording whether you stopped a drug because of a side effect or because it lost effectiveness gives your team that exact signal. For each past therapy, note the drug, the approximate dates you took it, and the reason you stopped. It also helps to log any therapeutic drug monitoring results, because AGA[suggests reactive testing of drug levels and antibodies when an anti-TNF biologic is not controlling active disease](https://gastro.org/clinical-guidance/therapeutic-drug-monitoring-in-inflammatory-bowel-disease-ibd/) .


## How to record side effects, reactions, and allergies


A reaction column turns a vague memory into a usable clinical fact. For each drug you reacted to, write what happened, how severe it was, and roughly when it occurred, then separate a true allergy from an expected side effect. That distinction guides whether a medicine can ever be tried again or must be avoided entirely. Being specific here matters because reconciliation reviews find that[patients and family members are often imperfect historians of their own medication records](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK2648/) , and details fade quickly once a flare passes. Knowing how to record medication side effects in the moment, rather than reconstructing them months later, keeps the record trustworthy. Capture the symptom, whether it required stopping the drug, and any treatment you needed. These notes protect you across every future handoff between clinicians.


## Supplements and over-the-counter products belong on the list


Many people leave off vitamins, minerals, and over-the-counter drugs, yet the[AHRQ reconciliation toolkit specifically calls for capturing prescription, over-the-counter, supplement, and herbal products together](https://www.ahrq.gov/patient-safety/settings/hospital/match/intro.html) . IBD makes this category clinically relevant rather than optional. The[Crohn's & Colitis Foundation explains that iron deficiency is common from inflammation and intestinal blood loss, that vitamin D is recommended for all IBD patients, and that vitamin B12 can run low after ileal disease or surgery](https://www.crohnscolitisfoundation.org/patientsandcaregivers/diet-and-nutrition/supplementation) . The Foundation also warns that medicines such as sulfasalazine and methotrexate can interfere with nutrient absorption, which is one more reason your supplement column and your prescription column need to sit side by side. Listing doses for each supplement lets your provider spot gaps and interactions in a single view.


## Keeping the list current between appointments


A medication list only helps if it reflects today, not your last flare. Update it whenever a dose changes, a drug is added or stopped, or you notice a new reaction, and bring the current version to every appointment. The CDC frames a shared, up-to-date list as a core tool for preventing harmful interactions and duplicate therapy. Because so many gaps come from information that never made it into the chart, a living document you revise in real time avoids the staleness that undermines a paper form filled out once and forgotten. Deciding on a single source of truth, whether an app or a printed sheet you reprint after each change, prevents the confusion of several conflicting versions. An IBD medication list template earns its value through maintenance, and the few minutes you spend updating it translate directly into safer, faster decisions the next time your treatment history is reviewed.
