# Common Deductions and Compliance Issues in Health, Beauty, and Wellness

In this article, learn about:


-


Why health, beauty, and wellness brands lose 5 to 7% of revenue to retail and distributor deductions


-


The category-specific factors driving that exposure, from shade complexity to gifting windows


-


How to prevent avoidable charges and recover the ones you're actually owed


---


Ask a finance leader at a health, beauty, and wellness (HBW) brand what share of revenue disappears into retailer and distributor deductions each year, and most won't have a precise number. Instead, what they'll have is a feeling: the charges are painful, but normal.


The real figure is closer to five to seven percent of revenue, which is money written off to fees, shortages, and chargebacks that land on a remittance statement and get absorbed as the cost of doing business.


But treated as a line to manage instead of a line to accept, deductions become one of the most direct levers a brand has to protect profitability, without touching price, cost of goods, or headcount.


Not every deduction is the same kind of problem. Some are preventable compliance failures caused upstream, inside your own operations. Some are legitimate adjustments you already agreed to. And some are invalid charges that were never owed in the first place. Telling these three apart (preventable, legitimate, and invalid) is where margin protection actually starts.


## Anatomy of an HBW Deduction


Every deduction lands the same way: a charge on a remittance statement, a dollar figure subtracted from what you expected to be paid. But the three types of deductions behind that pattern are not the same problem, and treating them as one is exactly what keeps them unmanaged.


### Preventable Compliance Failures


These would include a late or inaccurate ASN, a mislabeled case, a routing guide off by one step, or an OTIF window missed by a day. These are real costs, but they are self-inflicted ones. This is the cost of doing business *only* in the sense that it is avoidable, not inevitable. Every dollar here traces back to a process gap your team can close.


### Legitimate Adjustments


These would include events such as a real short-shipment acknowledged on receipt, a trade allowance you negotiated, or terms you already agreed to. These deductions are working as intended. They are not disputable, and they should not be treated as if they were. Chasing a legitimate adjustment wastes analyst time and can strain the retailer relationship for no return.


### Invalid Charges


These would include deductions for things like duplicate claims, chargebacks coded to the wrong reason, or stale post audit charges with no valid basis. This is the category worth fighting for, and it is larger than most finance teams assume. Roughly 70% of invalid deductions go untouched industry-wide, not because they are unrecoverable, but because there was no process built to catch them.

## Why HBW Is Different: Category-Driven Exposure


The reasons health, beauty, and wellness brands carry heavier deduction exposure than most categories trace directly back to what makes the category what it is.


-


**Shade and SKU depth** : A single product line is often fragmented into dozens of shades, sizes, and formulations, each with its own UPC, pack configuration, and item attributes. That density is where item-data mismatches and barcode errors creep in, especially during high-velocity new-item setup.


-


**Dated, perishable formulations** : Minimum-remaining-shelf-life requirements at receiving mean a shipment that is perfectly good on the truck can still get rejected if it arrives inside the retailer's cutoff window.


-


**Fragile liquids, glass, and aerosols** : These carry a higher damage-claim rate than sturdier goods, and that sensitivity extends into packaging and routing compliance more broadly.


-


**Gifting and seasonal windows** : Holiday, Mother's Day, Valentine's Day, and similar gifting occasions compress the sell-through period into a few short weeks. An OTIF miss or a late delivery lands harder in HBW than in a category with a twelve-month sell cycle, because a shipment that arrives after the window closes is not just late, but is unsellable at full value.


-


**Distributor dependency** : Heavy reliance on specialty distributors like[UNFI](https://www.spscommerce.com/community/articles/how-to-become-a-supplier-for-unfi) and[KeHE](https://www.spscommerce.com/community/unlock-your/kehe-supplier-technology-guide) adds a distinct compliance layer on top of direct retailer requirements: their own routing guides, their own scorecards, their own fine structures, running in parallel with whatever the retailer itself requires.


## Preventing Avoidable Deductions in HBW


Because avoidable deductions are self-inflicted, they are also fully within a brand's control to close. Preventing them is less about any single fix and more about tightening the handful of processes that generate most of the exposure.


-


**ASN timing and accuracy** : Of everything in this list, a late or inaccurate advance ship notice is the single highest-impact fix available. It touches nearly every downstream compliance check a retailer or distributor runs, and it is one of the most common single causes of a deduction in the first place.


-


**Label and routing guide compliance:** This gets harder as a brand adds trading partners, since distributor routing guides and retailer routing guides do not always agree. A carton correctly labeled for one partner can still violate another's requirements on the same pallet.


-


**Item data governance for shade- and variant-heavy catalogs:** Attributes, UPCs, and pack configurations need to be right at setup. The cost of catching a wrong attribute during new-item onboarding is a fraction of the cost of catching it after a deduction notice arrives.


-


**OTIF as a leading indicator:** Most brands look at OTIF after the fact. Treated instead as an early warning, especially heading into a short gifting window, it becomes something a team can act on before the delivery date, not something they explain afterward.


Underneath all four of these is the same requirement: visibility into fill rate, OTIF, and ASN accuracy before those numbers become a deduction rather than after.


## Recovering Invalid Deductions in HBW


Invalid charges exist because most retailer and distributor deduction systems generate the charge automatically, at the point of issue, with no person reviewing it before it lands in a supplier's remittance.


That is why the volume is high, and why catching it requires a process on the receiving end to match. Most HBW brands handle this in one of two ways, and both leave something on the table.


The first is writing it all off. A meaningful share of invalid charges would not hold up if anyone actually challenged them, which means every deduction absorbed without question is recoverable revenue left behind. The second is disputing everything on principle. That approach burns analyst hours on claims that were never winnable and spends down the retailer goodwill a brand needs for the disputes that actually matter.


The alternative to both approaches is a process in three parts:


-


Centralizing documentation across every retailer and distributor portal instead of logging into each one separately.


-


Checking each claim against the original documentation before a dispute is filed, so the decision to dispute is based on evidence rather than instinct.


-


Routing disputes through each partner's specific codes, formats, and deadlines rather than a single generic process.


A dispute that recovers this quarter's charge and a fix that prevents next quarter's charge are two different jobs. Most HBW brands only have someone staffed for the first one, usually a compliance or AP analyst working portal by portal. That is the real constraint: that recovering lost money and fixing what caused the deductions compete for the same few hours in the same week.


## How To Tell a Legitimate Adjustment From an Invalid Charge


A legitimate adjustment and an invalid charge can arrive looking almost identical. The difference is in whether the documentation behind it holds up.


A real shortage claim should be traceable end to end: a bill of lading and packing list that match the shipped quantity, a signed proof of delivery showing what the retailer or distributor actually received, and a claim filed within the partner's stated window. When those three line up and the numbers genuinely differ, the shortage is real, and it is not worth disputing.


A stale or duplicate charge tends to be missing at least one of those pieces, or contradicts them outright. What it requires is someone checking the documentation before deciding whether to pay or fight the chargeback.


The other piece that trips brands up is that the line between the two moves. Retailer and distributor compliance rules change often enough that a deduction code that was valid last quarter can become disputable this quarter, and vice versa: a routing requirement that used to be optional can become mandatory with a single routing guide update. A compliance calendar, tracking each partner's rule changes as they are published, is what keeps that judgment call current instead of six months stale.


## What Protecting a Margin in HBW Requires


Everything in this article collapses into one plain requirement: prevent upstream, recover downstream, and do both at the same time. Margin protection is the two working together, not a choice between them.


A short way to check where you actually stand:


-


Are deduction types separated (preventable, legitimate, invalid) before anyone decides whether to accept or fight a charge?


-


Is there a standing, repeatable dispute process, or does recovery happen only when someone has spare time?


-


Is documentation centralized across retailer and distributor portals, or does each dispute start from scratch?


-


Does a recovered deduction ever get traced back to a process fix?


-


Is there a compliance calendar tracking retailer and distributor rule changes?
