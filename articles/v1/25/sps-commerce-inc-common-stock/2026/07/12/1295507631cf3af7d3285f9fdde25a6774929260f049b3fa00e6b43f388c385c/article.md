# How Sustainable Consumer Behavior Is Changing Across the U.S.

In this article, learn about:


-


Why sustainability has shifted from a marketing trend to an operational capability


-


How packaging, pricing, and product transparency influence purchasing decisions


-


What retailers and suppliers can do to turn sustainable demand into measurable retail performance

A decade ago, sustainable shopping was often treated as a niche market. Brands launched environmentally focused product lines, retailers created dedicated sections, and consumers seeking sustainable options knew where to find them.


Today, sustainability is no longer a separate shopping category. It has become one of many factors consumers weigh alongside price, quality, convenience, availability, and trust.


The question is no longer whether consumers care about sustainability. Deloitte found that[nearly half of consumers (47%) purchased a sustainable product](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/environmental-social-governance/sustainable-consumption-trends.html) within the previous four weeks despite ongoing economic pressures. Packaging, pricing, product data, inventory availability, and fulfillment all shape whether sustainable intent translates into a sale. Environmental commitments may influence consideration, but retail execution determines the outcome.


For retailers and suppliers, sustainability has become less of a marketing initiative and more of an operational capability. The organizations creating long-term advantage are those embedding sustainability into everyday business decisions rather than treating it as a standalone program.


## Sustainability Has Become Part of the Value Equation


Consumers are increasingly incorporating sustainability into purchasing decisions, but rarely in isolation.


A parent may choose reusable food containers because they last longer. A homeowner may invest in an energy-efficient appliance to lower utility bills. A shopper may purchase secondhand clothing to access a premium brand at a lower price. Each decision has an environmental benefit, but the immediate motivation is often practicality.


This distinction matters because it changes how retailers and suppliers should think about demand. Consumers are not necessarily looking for the "greenest" product. They're looking for products that deliver the best overall value, with sustainability becoming one component of that equation.


Recent[GlobeScan research](https://globescan.com/2025/05/14/insight-of-the-week-more-americans-say-they-are-buying-sustainable-products-in-2025/) found that 49% of Americans purchased an environmentally friendly product within the previous month in 2025, up from 43% the year before. Another 36% said they wanted to make a sustainable purchase but were held back by price, limited awareness, or product availability.


Most consumers do not wake up intending to make an unsustainable purchase. They simply buy what is available, affordable, and easy to trust. Every point of friction pushes good intentions a little farther down the shopping list.


Better product information, improved assortment planning, reliable inventory, and thoughtful pricing can all help bridge the gap between consumer intent and purchasing behavior.


Durability has become part of that conversation as well. Consumers increasingly evaluate products based on how long they last, whether they can be repaired, and whether they retain value over time. A product designed for longevity often delivers both financial and environmental benefits, making sustainability easier for consumers to justify without asking them to compromise elsewhere.

## Packaging Is Where Sustainability Becomes Visible


Consumers can't easily evaluate a company's manufacturing practices or supply chain, but they can immediately evaluate its packaging.


Oversized boxes, unnecessary plastic, and unclear recycling instructions create a visible disconnect between sustainability messaging and the customer experience. Conversely, packaging that uses materials efficiently, protects the product, and clearly communicates disposal or recycling guidance reinforces a brand's credibility.[McKinsey's research on U.S. packaging preferences](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/packaging-and-paper/our-insights/do-us-consumers-care-about-sustainable-packaging-in-2025) found that while price, quality, and convenience remain the strongest purchase drivers, recyclability is the sustainability attribute consumers value most in packaging.


The lesson goes beyond the idea that every package requires a dramatic redesign. Incremental improvements often create meaningful value. Reducing excess material, improving package fit, or providing clear recycling guidance gives consumers something tangible to understand. Broad claims like "better for the planet" fail to carry the same weight.


Packaging decisions also extend beyond the consumer experience. Changes to carton dimensions can alter case-pack quantities, pallet configurations, freight costs, warehouse storage, retailer item setup, and transportation efficiency. Alternative materials may reduce environmental impact but increase product damage if they fail to protect the product during distribution.


Sustainable packaging succeeds when environmental improvements support operational performance rather than compete with it. The strongest strategies balance product protection, transportation efficiency, retail compliance, merchandising requirements, and consumer expectations throughout the product's lifecycle.


## Trust and Price Still Determine the Purchase


Consumers consistently express interest in sustainable products, but that interest has practical limits.


[PwC's 2024 Voice of the Consumer Survey](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/issues/c-suite-insights/voice-of-the-consumer-survey/2024.html) found that consumers are willing to pay an average premium of 9.7% for sustainably produced or sourced goods, suggesting that sustainability is staying important even as higher prices make purchasing decisions more deliberate.


Consumers may accept a modest premium when they clearly understand the benefit, whether that's longer product life, lower operating costs, or responsibly sourced materials. They are less likely to pay significantly more for vague environmental claims.


Trust is equally important. As sustainability messaging has become more common, skepticism has grown alongside it.[Blue Yonder's 2025 research](https://sustainabilitymag.com/articles/blue-yonder-consumer-sustainability-survey-2025) found that while many consumers are willing to spend more on sustainable products, only a small percentage fully trust the sustainability claims brands make which directly creates both a marketing challenge and an operational one.


General statements like "eco-friendly" or "responsibly made" offer little guidance when consumers are comparing products. Specific, verifiable information is far more persuasive. Claims supported by measurable product attributes, recognized certifications, or transparent sourcing help shoppers make informed purchasing decisions while strengthening brand credibility. Consumers have gone weary of promises and now[expect transparency](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/delivering-on-promise-and-purpose-in-the-retail-supply-chain) .

## Sustainable Demand Shows Up in the Data


Understanding consumer preferences is really only the beginning. Capturing the demand depends on operational execution.


Retailers and suppliers need visibility into where sustainable products are performing, where demand is growing, and where operational issues are preventing sales. The same principle applies to[assortment planning](https://www.spscommerce.com/community/articles/what-is-assortment-planning) , product content, pricing, and[seasonal merchandising](https://www.spscommerce.com/community/articles/understanding-calendars-and-seasons-in-merchandising) .


Demand for sustainable products varies by geography, household demographics, local infrastructure, as well as shopping behavior. Organizations that rely solely on national trends risk overlooking meaningful opportunities within those individual markets.


Store-level sales data, inventory performance, product attributes, online conversion rates, and replenishment metrics provide a much clearer picture of where sustainable demand is translating into revenue and where operational improvements are needed. The same is true across regions: A product that sells well in one climate or demographic may stall in another for reasons that have nothing to do with consumer interest.


## Frequently Asked Questions


### What is sustainable consumer behavior?


Sustainable consumer behavior refers to purchasing decisions that consider a product's environmental or social impact alongside traditional factors such as price, quality, convenience, and availability. Consumers may prioritize products with recyclable packaging, longer lifespans, energy efficiency, responsible sourcing, or refill and repair options, but these benefits must also deliver practical value.


### Are consumers willing to pay more for sustainable products?


Many consumers are willing to pay a modest premium for products they perceive as more sustainable, particularly when the environmental benefits are clear, and the product delivers comparable quality and convenience. However, price remains one of the strongest drivers of purchasing decisions. Retailers and suppliers are often more successful when they reduce the tradeoff between sustainability and affordability rather than relying on sustainability alone to justify higher prices.


### How does sustainable consumer behavior affect retailers and suppliers?


Sustainability increasingly influences decisions across the retail supply chain, from product design and packaging to inventory planning and assortment strategy. Retailers and suppliers that align sustainability initiatives with operational execution, including accurate product data, reliable inventory, and efficient packaging, are better positioned to meet consumer expectations while improving retail performance.


### Why is packaging important to sustainable purchasing decisions?


Packaging is one of the most visible ways consumers evaluate a brand's sustainability efforts. Efficient packaging, recyclable materials, and clear disposal instructions help reinforce environmental claims, while excessive packaging or vague messaging can reduce consumer trust. Effective packaging strategies balance sustainability goals with product protection, transportation efficiency, and retail compliance.


### How can retailers turn sustainable demand into higher sales?


Retailers can improve sustainable product performance by ensuring products are consistently in stock, providing accurate product information, tailoring assortments to local demand, and communicating sustainability benefits clearly. Consumer interest is often already present; success depends on removing the operational barriers that prevent shoppers from completing the purchase.


## Sustainability Requires Operational Excellence


Sustainability has become part of consumers' baseline expectations, but it hasn’t replaced the fundamentals of retail. Shoppers are still expecting competitive prices, reliable availability, and clear information. A durable product that's also recyclable doesn't ask a shopper to choose between product value and their personal values.


For retailers and suppliers, the opportunity is not simply to promote sustainable products. It is to build the operational capabilities that consistently deliver on those promises. Organizations that connect sustainability with packaging decisions, product data, inventory planning, assortment strategy, and supplier collaboration will be better positioned to convert consumer intent into measurable retail performance.


Consumers increasingly reward brands that make responsible choices practical, accessible, and easy to understand. In today's retail environment, sustainability may earn consideration, but operational excellence is what earns the sale.
