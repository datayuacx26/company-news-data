# MJF vs. FDM 3D Printing

When selecting a plastic additive manufacturing process, engineers often compare Multi Jet Fusion (MJF) and fused deposition


modeling (FDM). Both technologies produce durable thermoplastic parts without tooling, but they differ significantly in mechanical performance, feature resolution, surface quality, throughput, and cost.


Understanding those differences early in product development can help you avoid unnecessary design compromises and


identify the most efficient path from prototype to production.


At


Protolabs, both technologies support rapid iteration and functional testing. However, each process is


optimized for different applications and manufacturing requirements.


---


## How MJF and FDM Work


MJF and FDM both build parts layer by layer, but the underlying processes are fundamentally different.


### Multi Jet Fusion (MJF)


MJF uses an inkjet array to selectively deposit fusing and detailing agents onto a powder bed of nylon material. Infrared energy then fuses the selected regions. After each layer is completed, a new layer of powder is spread and the process repeats.


Because the surrounding powder supports the part during printing, MJF does not


require dedicated support structures. This allows for complex geometries, internal channels, lattices, and nested assemblies.


Learn more about


[MJF 3D printing](https://www.protolabs.com/services/3d-printing/multi-jet-fusion/) .


### Industrial FDM


Industrial FDM builds parts by extruding heated thermoplastic filament through a nozzle. Material is deposited layer by layer until the part is complete. Depending on geometry, support structures may be


required and removed after printing.


FDM supports a wide range of engineering-grade thermoplastics, including ABS, polycarbonate,


Ultem™ and carbon-fiber-filled materials. This makes it attractive for applications requiring specific thermal, chemical, or flame-retardant properties.


Learn more about


[FDM 3D printing](https://www.protolabs.com/services/3d-printing/fused-deposition-modeling/) .

## Material Options


Material


selection is often one of the first considerations when evaluating additive manufacturing technologies.


### MJF Materials


MJF is primarily associated with nylon-based materials, including:


- PA 12


- PA 11


- Glass-filled nylon options


- Flame-retardant nylon grades


These materials offer an excellent balance of strength, toughness, fatigue resistance, and dimensional stability.


### FDM Materials


Industrial FDM offers a broader material portfolio, including:


- ABS


- ASA


- Polycarbonate (PC)


- PC-ABS


- Ultem 9085


- Ultem 1010


- Carbon-fiber-reinforced thermoplastics


If your application requires high heat resistance, aerospace compliance, flame retardancy, or specific chemical resistance, FDM may provide more material flexibility.

## Mechanical Performance


For many engineers, mechanical performance is the deciding factor.


### MJF Strength and Isotropy


One of the key advantages of MJF is its ability to produce parts with more uniform mechanical properties. Because the material is fused throughout each layer within the powder bed, MJF parts


exhibit greater isotropy than FDM parts, where material is deposited as individual extruded roads.


This results in more consistent strength across multiple axes, improved impact resistance, and better fatigue performance under repeated loading conditions. MJF parts are also less susceptible to layer separation, which can be a concern for load-bearing components manufactured with some additive processes.


As a result, MJF is often selected for functional prototypes and end-use components that require reliable mechanical performance, including housings, brackets, clips, snap-fit assemblies, and other parts subjected to regular stress during operation.


### FDM Strength Considerations


FDM parts can achieve excellent strength, particularly when using high-performance materials. However, strength varies depending on build orientation because bonding between layers is typically weaker than strength within a layer.


Engineers often need to carefully consider print orientation when designing FDM parts to


optimize structural performance.

## Surface Finish and Appearance


Surface quality can significantly influence both functionality and customer


perception.


### MJF Surface Finish


MJF produces a


relatively uniform matte finish with fine feature detail and minimal visible layer lines.


Benefits include:


- Improved cosmetic appearance


- Better feature resolution


- Easier post-processing


- More consistent surfaces across complex geometries


MJF parts are


frequently used for customer-facing prototypes and production components where appearance matters.


### FDM Surface Finish


FDM parts typically display visible layer lines and extrusion paths.


While post-processing can improve appearance, as-printed surfaces are


generally rougher than MJF.


For purely functional applications, this may not be a concern. For presentation models or consumer-facing components,


additional finishing steps may be


required.

## Design Freedom and Complexity


Both technologies support geometries that would be difficult


or impossible with traditional manufacturing methods, but MJF typically offers greater design freedom.


---


## Advantages of MJF


MJF offers significant design freedom because the surrounding powder supports parts throughout the build process. This


eliminates the need for dedicated support structures and allows engineers to create complex internal channels, lattice structures, organic shapes, intricate undercuts, and even moving assemblies in a single build. MJF also supports efficient production because multiple parts can be densely nested within the build chamber, helping maximize throughput and reduce cost per part.


---


## Advantages of FDM


Industrial FDM is often a strong choice for larger components, straightforward geometries, and low-volume functional prototypes. It is also well suited for applications that require specialized engineering thermoplastics, including high-temperature, flame-retardant, or chemically resistant materials. While support structures can introduce some design constraints and post-processing requirements, FDM


remains a versatile and reliable solution for many engineering and manufacturing applications.


---


## Dimensional Accuracy


Both MJF and FDM


are capable of producing parts with production-quality tolerances, but they achieve dimensional accuracy in


different ways. MJF


generally delivers higher feature resolution, greater dimensional consistency, and more


accurate reproduction of small features and


fine details. These characteristics make it particularly well suited for parts with intricate geometries or tight tolerance requirements.


FDM can also achieve excellent accuracy, particularly on larger features and simpler geometries. However, dimensional variation may be influenced by factors such as thermal shrinkage, support removal, and the layer-by-layer deposition process. For applications that require precise small features or highly consistent dimensions across production runs, MJF often provides a measurable advantage.


---


## Cost Considerations


Cost depends on part size, geometry, quantity, and material requirements.


### When MJF Is Typically More Economical


MJF often becomes the more cost-effective choice as part quantities increase or geometries become more complex. Because parts can be efficiently nested throughout the build chamber, manufacturers can maximize machine


utilization and reduce cost per part. MJF is particularly


advantageous for low- to mid-volume production runs where consistent quality, minimal post-processing, and efficient batch production help offset initial manufacturing costs. For complex components that would require extensive support structures or finishing operations with other technologies, MJF can deliver


additional cost savings.


### When FDM May Be More Economical


Industrial FDM can offer a cost advantage for larger parts,


very low production quantities, and applications that require specialized engineering thermoplastics. It is also


frequently used during the


early stages of product development when engineers need functional prototypes quickly and economically. For simple geometries and one-off builds, FDM often provides a lower overall manufacturing cost, making it a practical choice for design validation, fit-and-function testing, and low-volume production needs.

## MJF vs. FDM: Which process should you choose?


Choose


MJF


when you need:


- Strong, isotropic mechanical properties


- High-quality surface finish


- Fine feature detail


- Complex geometries


- Production-ready nylon parts


Choose


FDM


when you need:


- Specialized thermoplastic materials


- High-temperature performance


- Flame-retardant materials


- Large part sizes


- Cost-effective functional prototypes

## Moving From Prototype to Production


For many product development teams, MJF serves as a bridge between prototyping and full-scale manufacturing. Its combination of repeatable quality, mechanical performance, and production scalability makes it well suited for low-volume production and bridge manufacturing.


FDM continues to be a valuable tool for concept validation, fixture development, manufacturing aids, and applications requiring specialized materials.


The best choice ultimately depends on your performance requirements, material needs, geometry complexity, production volume, and budget.

## Frequently Asked Questions


---


### Is MJF stronger than FDM?


*expand_less* *expand_more*


In many applications, MJF parts offer more consistent mechanical properties because the material is fused throughout the powder bed rather than deposited as individual extruded layers. This results in greater isotropy, improved impact resistance, and better fatigue performance. However, industrial FDM can achieve excellent strength when using high-performance materials such as polycarbonate or Ultem™, particularly when parts are properly oriented during the build process.


### When should I choose MJF over FDM?


*expand_less* *expand_more*


MJF is often the preferred choice for complex geometries, functional prototypes, end-use parts, and low- to mid-volume production runs. It delivers excellent surface finish, fine feature resolution, and consistent mechanical performance. FDM may be a better fit when your application requires specialized engineering thermoplastics, larger part sizes, or lower quantities where material selection is the primary design consideration.


### Is MJF or FDM more cost-effective?


*expand_less* *expand_more*


The most cost-effective process depends on part geometry, quantity, material requirements, and size. MJF typically becomes more economical for production quantities and complex parts because multiple components can be nested efficiently within a single build. FDM is often more cost-effective for large parts, one-off prototypes, and applications that require specialty thermoplastics. Comparing quotes for both processes can help determine the best option for your specific application.
