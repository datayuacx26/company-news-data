We are honored to share that Democracy Earth received a USD $228k grant from the[Templeton World Charity Foundation](https://earth.us13.list-manage.com/track/click?u=301becd69e00afa63c21122f3&id=49194a3a51&e=788477408c) (TFWC) in order to continue developing our efforts on Machine Learning resistant[Turing Tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_test) that can be used as proofs of identity.


As you may know, our research throughout the years points to the development of a secure identity protocol as a crucial step to enable sovereign governance on blockchain networks. TWCF is an unmatched supporter for this effort. They fund research and development in some of the most reputable institutions around the world, tackling relevant themes for us such as artificial intelligence, information theory, individual freedom and free markets. Being among the organizations supported by them is an enormous validation and a true honor.


With TWFC's grant our goal is to build a protocol able to model a one-human-one-node graph that can be a source of legitimacy in the governance of digital networks. We don’t expect this protocol to narrow down an identity to a one-dimensional number (username, surname, etc), but instead to leverage the *social aspect* of identity — the different groups and social ties that form it. In doing so, we won't be limited to the governance of specific organizations or institutions, but instead create a path to signal voter rights that can benefit all networks.


As a hypothetical use-case, let's imagine that tomorrow a decision held by[MakerDAO](https://makerdao.com/en/) — a ~$400M credit smart contract network that regularly has its community voting over interest fees — can be contrasted against a probabilistic consensus that defines how democratic these decisions were: then we can signal *legitimacy* within that decision. If we have an effective way of proving in the future that MakerDAO is a more relevant “Central Bank” to Venezuelans than the Central Bank of Venezuela itself, then this fact can exert political power even in legacy Venezuelan politics.


The key challenge to achieve this is to formalize humans in decentralized networks. In order to do so, we must prevent one single node from controlling multiple entities, a problem known in distributed systems as the[Sybil Attack (Douceur 2002)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-sybil-attack/) . On the Web 2.0, traditional solutions to this kind of problem are variations of the CAPTCHA challenge, which employs an algorithm to determine if there is a real human behind the screen or if it's a bot. However, employing an algorithm to block another algorithm creates a structural vulnerability: the pattern of defense can always be learned and hacked by the pattern of attack. Therefore, in order to protect the network from artificial intelligence exploits our protocol aims to validate singular humans with something far more powerful than computation processing: *attention, a.k.a.* human processing *.*


This a significant element, because it is a design that places network controls with its members, instead of a third-party entity such as Facebook or governments.


### Machine Learning resistant Turing Tests


In order to join the network, members must submit a proof that they are really a human. A machine’s perception threshold can be measured using[Turing Tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_test) , tasks designed to tell robots and humans apart. So a human-based consensus that can't be gamed by software, by definition requires participants to submit Machine Learning resistant Turing Tests that can be uses as proofs of identity. Attestation mechanisms that go beyond pattern recognition and can place human intelligence at the center of network governance - in other words, they must be hard for computers but easy for brains to process. These attestation mechanisms will enhance our ability to measure the information threshold needed to establish a political border between human and machine intelligence.


A couple of examples are videos (such as[the blockchain-based birth certificate of Roma Siri](https://www.coindesk.com/meet-the-dad-who-registered-his-daughters-birth-on-the-blockchain) , daughter of Democracy Earth co-Founders Santiago Siri and Pia Mancini), and some variations of[Winograd Schema Challenges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winograd_Schema_Challenge) . However, Machine Learning resistant Turing Tests can only help us solve part of the problem: to prevent a single node from controlling multiple identities we also need to employ reputation algorithms that can grant attestation rights to the nodes able to earn more trust from the network. That can serve as a protection to the network in that members will be able to stake their reputation on each other. Finally, to prevent the risks of bribes and centralization (inherent to reputation algorithms), we will implement randomized voting through the use of cryptographic lotteries that can incent users to attestate family and their social environment first.


Stay tuned for our first MVP: we are looking forward to begin testing with our community over the next few months!


### Citizenship in a Networked Age @ University of Oxford


As a part of our collaboration with TWFC, our team also had the honor of joining the Citizenship in a Networked Age conference, which took place in April 2019 at the University of Oxford. The conference was centered around the question “What makes a good citizen in the age of decision-making through algorithms and artificial intelligence?”.


In order to search for answers, TWFC and the University of Oxford gathered some of the leading minds in academia and industry for 2 days of enriching exchanges. We learned enormously, and also brought our own perspective on the key role that identities play in our networked societies, and the importance of creating independent means for citizens to identify each other.
