# Referendum for the Chief Executive in Hong Kong with Civic Data Hong Kong and Pop Vote — 2017

### **Referendum for the Chief Executive in Hong Kong with Civic Data Hong Kong and Pop Vote — 2017**


Today we celebrate the[landslide victory](https://edition.cnn.com/2019/11/24/asia/hong-kong-district-council-elections-intl/index.html) of Hong Kong pro-democracy parties in the District Council Elections — taken as the de facto protest referendum. We take this opportunity to share information about the 2017 referendum that we participated in and the different kinds of cyberattacks suffered by the initiative, in hope that they can be learned from and new efforts can improve upon the experience we had.


The shadow referendum for the Chief Executive in Hong Kong was an initiative implemented by[Civic Data HK](http://civicdata.hk/) and[PopVote](http://popvote.hk/) in partnership with Democracy Earth Foundation, in 2017. The official Chief Executive Election is largely considered illegitimate by the population of Hong Kong. Participation, including nominations and voting, is restricted to a small committee — at the time, 1200 people or 0.03% of the registered voters, who amounted to 3.7 million. It has also been historically perceived by civil society as a major vulnerability, as most of its nominees tend to be loyal to Beijing and thus can threaten Hong Kong’s autonomy.


Since its implementation in 1997, activists have been battling to bring universal suffrage to this election, and in 2013 a referendum led by PopVote was held to elect proposals on new ways to elect the Chief Executive, garnering an astounding number of 800,000 participants — one quarter of the registered voters. That event was closely tied with the beginnings of the[Occupy Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupy_Central_with_Love_and_Peace) and[Umbrella movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbrella_Movement) , a 79-day occupation of the city demanding more transparent elections, which began 4 months after the referendum was held. The proposals were generated by local parties and civil society, and the winning choice was that nominees for Chief Executive of Hong Kong should have the support of at least thirty five thousand registered voters, or one percent of the voting population — otherwise, all parties could elect a nominee given they had at least five percent of the votes in the previous legislative election. The referendum received considerable coverage from the western media as it received[one of the largest known DDoS attacks to date](https://www.forbes.com/sites/parmyolson/2014/11/20/the-largest-cyber-attack-in-history-has-been-hitting-hong-kong-sites/) , executed by the Chinese government.


Drawing from this experience,[Civic Data HK](http://civicdata.hk/) and[PopVote](http://popvote.hk/) partnered with Democracy Earth Foundation to develop a voting mechanism based on encrypted infrastructure. The aim was to provide a robust defense from Chinese attacks against a new upcoming referendum: an open primary process where citizens could nominate civilians for the Chief Executive Election. Anyone could participate by submitting a letter of intent, and whoever got one percent of the registered votes on the platform would enter the list of candidates, therefore implementing what had been democratically decided in 2014.


Due to a smear campaign (discussed below) our team decided to not implement the blockchain-based cryptographic key architecture that was initially devised for the referendum, but the code is available on Github for anyone willing to look into it:[https://github.com/lucasisasmendi/r2bot-family](https://github.com/lucasisasmendi/r2bot-family) .


Instead, together with our partners we designed a simpler infrastructure utilizing the Telegram app, due to its encryption and polling functionalities. Additionally, Telegram presented a higher difficulty target for attacks as you need a phone number to register, and after downloading the app, additional information was required in order to prevent duplicate votes. Citizens could vote from their phones, or from polling stations that were set up around the city, to ensure all could participate.


#### Cyberattacks


As the campaign unfolded, the referendum received four types of cyberattacks:


1 — The first one was targeting the accounts of the organizers. We suspect that during the time period of the election there was a hack within the telecommunication operator that sent SMS confirmations for resetting Telegram accounts. By intercepting those SMS messages, hackers were able to access the accounts of some of the organizers.


2 — There were attacks targeting the physical polling stations, with interferences in the Wi-Fi signal causing slow connection. It took a long time for tablets to record new votes, leading to long lines and a constant need to reset the Wi-Fi.


3 — Since Telegram was not available in Chinese, our team built a modified front-end for the app in the local language. The third kind of cyberattack was an exploit at this modified front-end that bypassed the phone verification process in order to create fake accounts, thus delegitimizing the initiative.


4— After we were able to provide a fix for the phone verification exploit, attackers managed to elaborate botnets that would send more API requests to the app than Telegram allowed for. Thankfully, the Telegram team did not shut down the services — instead, their team was extremely responsive, helping us screen bots and offering unlimited API requests during the referendum.


In parallel to those four cyberattacks, the most important strike to the referendum was a smear campaign that derived from concerns raised by the local IT Security Officers. They questioned the security of the voting information on the modified Telegram client, and false rumors quickly spread on national media saying that sensitive voter data, such as Hong Kong ID numbers, was leaked. Organizers went before the Privacy Commissioner for personal data of Hong Kong providing every necessary evidence that no such leak had happened, yet still the rumors undermined confidence in the process leading to small participation: over 65.000 participants, or 1.75% of the registered voters.


The vote enabled both positive and negative signaling: the winning candidate, John Tsang, received 90.7% of positive votes, while Carrie Lam received 95.3% of the negative votes. In contrast to the popular sample, Carrie Lam was officially elected Chief Executive of Hong Kong — by 777 Election Committee Members.


Kudos to our team Virgile Deville, Lucas Isasmendi, & CivicDataHK and PopVote for the incredible efforts. We invite cryptographers to continue developing methods that can enable disenfranchised populations to signal their preferences under secure, incorruptible and privacy preserving methods.
