## 2. Speechling


Speechling is another strong option for learners focused on improving their spoken English. The app emphasizes daily[speaking practice](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/english-pronunciation-practice) , encouraging users to record their voice regularly and compare it to native speakers. The format is simple: listen, repeat, record, and receive feedback.


‍


What makes Speechling useful is its structure—it provides curated phrases and sentences spoken by native[American English](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/standard-american-accent) speakers, helping you internalize the flow of natural speech. There’s also the option to submit recordings to a coach, who offers written corrections and guidance.


‍


However, Speechling doesn’t go very deep into pronunciation theory or mechanics. If you’re looking for in-depth explanations of vowel sounds, IPA symbols, or mouth placement, this may not be the right tool. It’s a good daily companion for shadowing and repetition, but not a standalone solution for mastering the American accent.

## 3. Accent Training by CanarSpeech


Accent Training by CanarSpeech is designed for learners working on their pronunciation. The app includes[vowel](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/vowel-sounds-in-english) and[consonant](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/consonant-clusters-in-english) drills, real-time waveform analysis, and visual feedback that shows you how closely your pronunciation matches the target.


‍


The connection to American English is clear—many of the sound targets and exercises are built around American pronunciation. You can isolate individual sounds, practice[minimal pairs](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/improve-english-minimal-pairs-pronunciation-exercises) , and see your accuracy reflected on screen. It’s especially helpful for self-motivated learners who want visual proof of progress.


‍


That said, the app’s interface and user experience aren’t as polished or intuitive as others on this list. There’s limited guidance or context beyond the drills themselves, so if you’re someone who learns best with structured explanations or engaging lessons, you may find it lacking.


‍


## 4. English with Emma


English with Emma is a well-known YouTube channel run by an American ESL teacher who offers casual, conversational lessons. While not technically a standalone app, her channel and associated mobile resources are often used by learners around the world to speak English like an American.


‍


Her content is highly approachable and focused on natural American English. Lessons cover things like[commonly mispronounced words](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/commonly-mispronounced-english-words) , reductions in connected speech,[idiomatic expressions](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/50-american-idioms) , and[real-life phrases](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/advanced-english-phrases) . It’s great for getting a sense of how native American speakers actually sound in day-to-day conversations.


‍


The main drawback is structure. English with Emma isn’t a formal course, and there’s no diagnostic feedback or progress tracking. You’re on your own when it comes to deciding what to learn next, and the lack of speech analysis makes it difficult to know whether you're improving. For mid-advanced learners who enjoy casual exposure to native speech, it’s a nice complement—but it can’t replace a focused, interactive app.


‍


## 5. Say It: The Pronunciation App


Say It is a pronunciation app built around one core function: helping learners hear, see, and fix their English sounds. Using recordings from native speakers, the app lets you listen to a word, record yourself, and view visual feedback comparing your pronunciation to the model. You can slow down playback, isolate[syllables](https://www.boldvoice.com/blog/rules-for-stressed-syllables-in-english) , and get a clear view of where you’re going wrong.


‍


It’s especially helpful for tackling tricky vowel sounds and consonants common in American English. The app includes audio from dictionaries like Oxford and offers phonemic transcriptions to guide your mouth positioning.


‍


However, Say It focuses on individual words, not full sentences or natural rhythm. It doesn’t teach connected speech, intonation, or sentence-level flow, which are essential for speaking English like an American.
