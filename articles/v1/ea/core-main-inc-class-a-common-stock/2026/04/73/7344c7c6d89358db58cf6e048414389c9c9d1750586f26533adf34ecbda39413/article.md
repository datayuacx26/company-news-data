This article outlines the key factors driving I&I and SSO conditions and highlights the practical tools and tasks operators rely on to manage them effectively.


## **What is Inflow and Infiltration?**


Inflow and Infiltration describe unintended sources of water that enter a municipal sanitary sewer collection system and increase flows beyond what the system was designed to manage. Inflow refers to the direct and rapid entry of surface water into the sewer system, most often during rainfall or snowmelt events. This typically occurs through improper connections such as roof downspouts, sump pumps, and yard drains, or through openings in manholes located in low‑lying areas. Because inflow enters the system quickly, it often causes sharp, short‑term spikes in sewer flow during storms.


Infiltration, by contrast, occurs when groundwater slowly seeps into the sewer system through structural defects in pipes and manholes. Cracked or deteriorated pipes, leaking joints, and deteriorated manhole walls allow groundwater to enter, especially when groundwater levels are high. Unlike inflow, infiltration tends to be continuous or gradually increases over time rather than occurring in sudden bursts.


Once excessive I&I enters the network, operators face higher pumping volumes, increased energy use, and unpredictable system behavior during wet-weather events.

## **Challenges Operators Face in the Field**


Operators and maintenance crews work within an increasingly complex environment shaped by operational demands, environmental conditions, and safety requirements. Many systems rely on aging infrastructure that require continuous monitoring and reactive repairs, often with limited visibility into underground assets until failures or performance issues emerge. These challenges are intensified by staffing shortages, which can prolong response times and extend maintenance cycles.


External factors further complicate operations. Seasonal weather patterns—such as freeze-thaw cycles, elevated groundwater levels, and storm surges—place additional stress on systems already operating near capacity. At the same time, increased solids loading from modern consumer products and non-dispersible materials accelerates wear and raises the likelihood of blockages. Overlaying all of this is growing regulatory pressure, which demands detailed documentation, accurate flow data, and thorough incident reporting, adding administrative burden to already stretched teams.


Despite these challenges, operators continue to deliver essential services that protect public health and the environment—often with minimal recognition and substantial physical demands.
