# What Paper Records Really Cost a School District

Every district has a room like this. Maybe it is a converted classroom behind the main office. Maybe it is a basement with a dehumidifier running in the corner, or a rented storage unit across town that shows up on a monthly invoice nobody questions. Inside are the cume folders, the special ed files, the personnel records going back to administrators who retired before anyone currently in the building was hired.


Ask what that room costs and you will probably get a number from the copier contract. Paper, toner, service calls.


That is not the expensive part.


## The registrar problem


In August, a family moves into the district. The registrar needs the incoming student's file from their previous district, and a former student who just got into a nursing program needs a transcript from 2011, and there is a new bus driver whose paperwork has to go somewhere.


On paper, each of those is a trip. Not a metaphorical trip, an actual walk to a cabinet, a search through a drawer, a photocopy, a walk back. Some of them are two trips because the file was not where it should have been. Occasionally one of them is a forty-minute problem because a folder got put back in the wrong year and now three people are looking for it.


None of this is dramatic. That is the issue. It is fifteen minutes here and ten minutes there, spread across the registrar, the special education secretary, the HR generalist, and the front office aide, and it never gets logged anywhere. There is no line item called "staff time spent walking to filing cabinets," so it has never once come up in a budget discussion.


It adds up to real money. Bloomington Public School District 87 timed their student record transfers before they digitized and found the process took up to ten minutes per student, counting the gathering, the review, the scanning, and the emailing. Afterward the same transfer ran in under thirty seconds. That is one workflow out of a dozen.


If you want a number for your own district, pick your worst process and have someone time five instances of it. Multiply by how many you handle a year. Put your loaded hourly rate against the total. It is usually a bigger figure than people expect, and unlike most budget arguments, it is yours rather than a vendor's.


#### Retention schedules are much harder to follow on paper


Districts are not short on records obligations. FERPA governs how student information is safeguarded and prohibits the destruction of records while a request to inspect them is pending, as covered in the Department of Education's[guidance on data retention and destruction](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/training/data-retention-and-data-destruction) . Special education files add another layer:[34 CFR 300.624](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/subtitle-B/chapter-III/part-300/subpart-F/subject-group-ECFR1d2a18271819883/section-300.624) requires districts to notify parents when personally identifiable information is no longer needed for services and to destroy it if the parent requests it.


Most of the day-to-day detail, though, comes from the state. Ohio districts work from a retention schedule approved by their records commission, the State Archives, and the Auditor of State, and the Ohio History Connection publishes a[model schedule for school districts](https://www.ohiohistory.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/School_Revised_20240530.pdf) to build from. Other states run something similar.


Now think about what following one of those schedules actually requires when the records are on paper. The schedule says a given record series gets destroyed after six years, or after audit, or after a student turns a certain age. Executing that means a person physically pulling the right folders out of the right drawers on the right timeline, in every building, every year, and documenting that they did it.


Almost nobody does this. What happens instead is that districts fall behind, decide it is safer to keep everything, and end up storing forty years of records under a schedule that says they should be storing six. Then an auditor asks about it, or a public records request lands, and the response takes three weeks, and the gap becomes visible.


Going digital does not change the obligation. It changes whether you can realistically meet it, because retention rules can run against records that are actually indexed instead of against a room.


#### The room is worth something


Space is the cost most people can see and still somehow discount. A five-drawer cabinet needs roughly sixteen square feet once you count the clearance to open it, and no district has just one.


Whether that matter depends on your building. In a district with flat enrollment and spare space, reclaiming a storage room is nice but not urgent. In a district that is running a program out of a repurposed closet, or paying for offsite storage every month, or planning a levy partly around space constraints, it is a live argument. Worth knowing which one you are before you bring it up.


#### The one that is hard to think about


Paper can be destroyed all at once. A pipe breaks over a long weekend, or a roof fails, and decades of files are gone with nothing behind them.


The odds in any given year are small. The problem is what it looks like afterward: former students who cannot get transcripts, records requests you cannot answer, retention requirements you cannot demonstrate compliance with because the evidence no longer exists. It is not a recoverable situation the way most facilities problems are.


Districts already carry insurance against this category of risk everywhere else on campus. Records tend to be the exception, mostly because nobody has framed them as an asset.


#### Where this usually stalls


The reason records digitization dies in budget season is rarely that people disagree about the value. It is that the request shows up as a technology line item and lands next to devices, network upgrades, and instructional software, which is a fight it will lose most years.


It is better positioned as operations and risk. The savings are in staff hours and facilities, and the exposure is compliance and continuity, none of which competes with a Chromebook refresh.


The other thing that helps is scope. You do not have to do the whole district at once, and districts that get approval usually do not. They pick one series, often inactive student records or special ed files, and start there. It turns an intimidating capital ask into something a business official can slot into an existing budget without a board conversation.
