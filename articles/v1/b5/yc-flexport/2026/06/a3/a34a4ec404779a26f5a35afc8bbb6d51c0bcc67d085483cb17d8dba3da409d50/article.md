Update: June 15, 2026


The U.S. and Iran both said they reached an interim agreement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and end the blockade of Iranian ports. They’re expected to sign the memorandum Friday; it would allow commercial vessels to begin transiting the strait again, while negotiations continue over details including Iran’s nuclear program, sanctions, and long-term control of the waterway.


Update: June 11, 2026


The U.S. military announced Tuesday, June 9 that it had begun strikes against Iran after a U.S. Army Apache helicopter crashed while patrolling over the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump blamed Iran for the incident, and U.S. officials said the helicopter went down after colliding with an Iranian drone. Both aviators aboard were safely rescued in what marked the first known drone boat rescue operation in U.S. military history. U.S. Central Command described the strikes as a proportional response to what it called unjustified Iranian aggression, while Iranian state media reported explosions on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's foreign minister pushed back on Trump's accusations, warning that foreign forces near Iranian territory face constant risk and suggesting they leave the region.


Meanwhile, more tankers carrying Iraqi crude are moving through the Strait of Hormuz. Iraq has shipped or loaded about 7 million barrels so far in June, matching its combined April and May volumes, as more vessels exit the Gulf and producers use discounts and ship-to-ship transfers to keep crude moving.


However, that flow is now in jeopardy: on June 11, Iran’s top joint military command announced that it had closed the Strait of Hormuz to oil tankers and commercial ships, warning that any vessel attempting passage would be fired upon.


Update: June 2, 2026


Tensions around the Strait of Hormuz continued to rise over the weekend, with another round of military exchanges between U.S. and Iranian forces. U.S. forces struck Iranian radar and drone control sites on Goruk and Qeshm Island after a U.S. drone was shot down over international waters. Iran's IRGC responded by firing ballistic missiles at U.S. forces in Kuwait, where no American personnel were harmed. Iran's IRGC also claimed responsibility for the June 1 strike on Mediterranean Shipping Company's (MSC) Sariska V container ship in the port of Um-Qasr, Iraq. MSC said all crew were safe and characterized the strike as an unprovoked attack on a neutral commercial carrier with no U.S. or Israeli affiliation.


The strikes came as peace talks stalled. Trump convened his national security team Friday, May 29 to make a "final determination" on a ceasefire framework, but the meeting ended without a decision. The U.S. has insisted any deal must include the reopening of the Strait, Iran renouncing nuclear weapons development, and the removal of its enriched uranium stockpile. Iran has repeatedly rejected those terms.


By Monday, June 1, Iran's state-affiliated outlet Tasnim reported that Tehran was halting negotiations entirely and had resolved to fully close the Strait of Hormuz and “activate other fronts including the Bab al-Mandeb Strait,” a critical shipping lane linking the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden.


Oil prices jumped more than 7% following Tasnim’s report.


Update: May 27, 2026


Overnight Monday, May 25 into Tuesday, May 26 U.S. forces conducted strikes on Iranian boats that it said were attempting to lay mines, and on missile launch sites near Bandar Abbas, a southern Iranian port city on the Strait of Hormuz. Iran called it a ceasefire violation and threatened retaliation. Despite the exchange, Iran's top negotiator remained in Qatar for negotiations.


Shipping remains severely disrupted:
It’s reported that only three large commercial vessels transited the Strait overnight (May 25-26), all with AIS tracking switched off, while three IRGC speedboats patrolled nearby.
A tanker also reported an external explosion approximately 60 nautical miles east of Muscat in the Gulf of Oman; crew was safe but bunker fuel was discharged into the sea.
The U.S. Navy is escorting a small number of vessels through the Strait, including a Greek supertanker carrying two million barrels of crude to India; the vessel had been stranded since early March.


Even if Iran and the U.S. negotiate an end to hostilities, analysts warn it could take months for normal traffic to resume in the Strait. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has said the Strait will open "one way or another" and must be toll-free, a position a senior Iranian lawmaker involved in negotiations pushed back against.


Update: May 5, 2026


Yesterday, the U.S. military stated that two U.S.-flagged vessels had successfully transited the Strait of Hormuz.


This transit does not indicate a general reopening of the Strait, and its practical impact on the market appears limited. At this stage, the release of additional capacity is less impactful than the sustained rise in fuel costs, which is proving increasingly difficult for carriers to absorb—particularly for smaller, less fuel-efficient vessels that are being deployed on major East-West trade lanes, where most of the largest ships are deployed.


Update: May 4, 2026

Update: April 23, 2026


At least two commercial vessels were attacked in the Strait of Hormuz yesterday.


- The UKMTO reported that an Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) gunboat opened fire on a container ship approximately 15 nautical miles northeast of Oman, causing significant damage to the ship’s bridge. All crew members were reported safe.
- Three hours later, the UKMTO reported a second incident in the Strait of Hormuz. A cargo ship located roughly 8 nautical miles off the Iranian coast indicated that it had been fired upon and came to a stop in the water. The crew was unharmed.
- [Flexport Atlas](https://atlas.flexport.com/) showed two container vessels stationary near the Strait as of yesterday morning: the Panama-flagged MSC Francesca, and the Greek-operated Epaminondas. These vessels had previously broadcasted positions within the Persian Gulf.

Update: April 18, 2026


Less than 24 hours after Iran declared the Strait of Hormuz "completely open," the Strait is closed again.


This morning, Iran's military announced that the Strait of Hormuz has reverted to "strict control," blaming the U.S. for failing to uphold its obligations under the ceasefire. Passage through the Strait now once again requires Iranian approval.


The announcement came after a morning in which a minor convoy of tankers managed to exit the Persian Gulf following a brief overnight wait, which was the first meaningful commercial traffic since yesterday's opening announcement.


This reversal will likely erase the market relief from yesterday, when oil prices plunged 11% following Iran's foreign minister declaring the Strait "completely open." Brent crude had fallen to approximately $89 per barrel on April 17, its lowest level since March 10, but is expected to surge again. Goldman Sachs had previously warned that another month of Hormuz closure would keep Brent above $100 throughout 2026.


The U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in effect. President Trump stated yesterday that the blockade would remain until a deal is "100% complete," and warned that attacks could resume if no agreement is reached before the Israel-Lebanon ceasefire expires on April 26—now just eight days away.


Impacts:


- Ocean:


Vessels in or approaching the Strait of Hormuz should expect delays or denial of transit. Any transit windows may be short-lived and subject to sudden reversal.
- Air:


No new direct impacts on air space, though elevated regional risk continues to affect carrier scheduling and fuel surcharges. Jet fuel prices, which had begun to ease on yesterday's oil drop, are expected to resume upward pressure.
- Bunker Fuel and Surcharges:


Emergency bunker surcharges remain in effect across major trades. Any easing in bunker fuel costs anticipated from yesterday's crude price drop is now unlikely to materialize.

Update: March 17, 2026


Air:


The situation in the Middle East has shifted from “concerning” to “critical” in the last 24 hours, between the latest Israeli strikes on Iran and the resulting wave of air space closures.


As of March 17, total air space closures remain in effect across Iran, Iraq, and Kuwait. Even hubs that are “open,” like Israel, Qatar, and Bahrain, are under restricted access with Prior Permission Required (PPR), meaning every flight needs special permission to move.


The strike near Dubai International yesterday resulted in massive ripple effects. While UAE carriers like Emirates are back in the air, many others are still diverting to DWC, the cargo-only airport in Dubai, or sitting on the tarmac in neighboring countries.


The immediate impact:


- Global air cargo has been squeezed by 12% this week. In the Middle East and South Asia, that drop is even sharper—down 36%.
- If you’re moving freight from India to Europe, expect rates to be up about 80%. Hong Kong to Europe has already cleared the $5.15/kg mark.
- The big players are hurting. Emirates is at about 60% capacity, while Etihad has pulled back significantly, operating at just 15% of their pre-conflict levels.


Jet fuel jumped 58% in a single week. Because of this, carriers like Oman Air are rolling out weekly fuel and war risk surcharges starting tomorrow. To keep cargo moving, airlines are adding “technical stops” in places like Jeddah just to leapfrog the restricted zones, which adds time and cost to every mile.


Additionally, all major EU and U.S. carriers (Lufthansa, Cargolux, United, AFKLM, etc.) have not resumed operations and will proceed with cargo embargoes to and from the region until March 28.


With over 25,000 regional flights canceled, potential alternatives for clearing the backlog and/or avoiding new consequences include:


- Leveraging hubs in Oman and Saudi Arabia, the primary workarounds for UAE-origin freight
- If it doesn't have to be in the air the whole way, using road feeder services through Saudi Arabia to bypass flight cancellations


This is a 24/7 situation that may evolve by the hour.

Ocean Freight: Suez Canal and Strait of Hormuz Closures


The closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the suspension of Suez Canal transits remove the option for transiting Suez used by some Ocean carriers for Indian Subcontinent to Europe and US East Coast, while simultaneously blocking access to key Gulf hubs.


What makes this disruption particularly significant is that some Asia–Europe services had only recently returned to Suez.


On the Asia–North Europe trade, CMA CGM had officially resumed Suez transits for its flagship FAL 1 and FAL 3 services, connecting Shanghai, Ningbo, and Yantian with Southampton, Hamburg, and Rotterdam. The return to Suez reduced full-loop transit times by seven days, from 105 days down to 98 days. That efficiency gain has now been reversed, with both services pulled back to Cape routing.


Within the Gemini Cooperation, Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd had previously announced that AE12 and AE15 services were next in line for a structural return to Suez, following initial adjustments on ISC-focused services. Those plans have now been formally rescinded.


On the Asia–Mediterranean trade, CMA CGM’s MEX service, linking Asia with Valencia and Fos-sur-Mer, had briefly returned to the Suez route earlier this year. It has now reverted to the Cape. Similarly, Gemini’s SE1 and SE3 services were being upgraded to 20,000 TEU vessels with the intent to route via Suez and improve reliability in the Adriatic and Spanish regions. Those structural changes remain in place, but the Suez routing component has been withdrawn.


For Transpacific services between Asia and the U.S. East Coast, the impact is more limited. Most services were already avoiding Suez, with the exception of certain Indian-Subcontinent-linked routings. The broader Transpacific network remains largely unchanged from a routing perspective, though equipment imbalances and capacity tightening may create secondary effects.


Across alliances, the response has been consistent. Vessels scheduled for Suez are being rerouted around Africa. Far East–Red Sea services are suspended. Cargo destined for Gulf ports is being held at transshipment hubs or diverted to alternative Mediterranean and African gateways. Some carriers have also suspended new bookings to Persian Gulf destinations until safe feeder or land-bridge solutions can be confirmed.


For shipments currently inside the Persian Gulf, the situation is more immediate. Vessels have been instructed to take shelter at major hubs such as Jebel Ali, Abu Dhabi, and Doha. Cargo on those vessels is effectively paused until safe passage can be reestablished.


Example: CMA CGM SAN ANTONIO left Jebel Ali on February 28, and is now unlikely to arrive to Mundra, India by Monday, March 2.


The operational consequences are significant.


Transit times between Asia and Europe, and on certain Asia–U.S. East Coast lanes, are expected to increase by 10 to 14 days as vessels detour around the Cape of Good Hope. That extension places additional pressure on equipment availability, schedule reliability, and network capacity.


At the same time, we anticipate:


- Emergency diversion fees and war risk surcharges
- Spot rate increases driven by capacity tightening
- Port congestion at alternative Mediterranean and Southeast Asian gateways


As carriers discharge Gulf-bound cargo elsewhere and rebalance networks, secondary bottlenecks are likely to emerge.


Air Freight: Widespread Airspace Closures


The disruption is not limited to ocean freight.


On the same day, coordinated military strikes between the United States, Israel, and Iran led to the immediate closures of sovereign airspace across Qatar, the UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Kuwait and immediate suspension of operations to and via the Middle East for most airlines.


The closures of Middle East hubs (Doha, Dubai, Riyad, Bahrain) reduces global capacity and specifically impacts Asia - Europe, Asia - Middle East and Europe - Asia Air Trade.


Several carriers have suspended regional operations to, from and via the Middle East and invoked force majeure. Middle East carriers which represent 13.6% of global capacity (source:[IATA](https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2025-releases/2025-11-28-01/#:~:text=Table_title:%20Trade%20Lane%20Growth%20Table_content:%20header:%20%7C,%7C%20Market%20share%20of%20industry*:%207.0%25%20%7C) ) in particular are unable to operate the majority of their flights. Their operations will be disrupted beyond the trade between Asia - Middle East - Europe.


With airspace inaccessible and safety risks elevated, most shipments originating in, destined for, or transiting through the Middle East are being grounded or diverted. Service levels and transit guarantees are currently suspended by the airlines.


The Middle East carriers stoppage, combined with the longer and more fuel-intensive rerouting required by European and Asian airlines, is reducing effective global capacity and increasing costs to operate. We expect immediate volatility, including a sharp increase in airfreight spot rates, suspension of longer term rate agreements under force majeure and growing backlogs on key Far East corridors.


Even lanes not directly tied to the Middle East will feel secondary pressure as aircraft and crews are redeployed across longer routes.
