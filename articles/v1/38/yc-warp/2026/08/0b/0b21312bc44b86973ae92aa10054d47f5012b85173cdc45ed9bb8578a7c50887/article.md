# HR Burnout Is Higher Than Ever. Is AI the Cause?

Searches for “HR burnout” have jumped about 3,500% over the past year, according to Google Trends. That climb lines up almost exactly with AI’s rapid arrival inside HR’s job description, both as a tool HR is expected to use and as a rollout HR is expected to lead for everyone else. When you see these trends together, the question asks itself: Is AI the reason?


## **HR professionals are experiencing burnout at higher rates than the employees they support**


Mind Share Partners’ 2025[Mental Health at Work Report](https://www.mindsharepartners.org/blog/hr-burnout-the-hidden-crisis-behind-the-people-profession) , run with Qualtrics, found that 54% of HR professionals report moderate to severe burnout, compared to 48% of non-HR employees.[Lattice’s](https://lattice.com/state-of-people-strategy/2026) 2026 State of People Strategy Report found that 48% of U.S. HR practitioners are considering leaving the field entirely, citing the emotional toll of managing employee issues, feeling undervalued, and the collapse of work-life balance.


So what’s driving this?


## **Examining AI’s Role in HR Burnout**


On paper, AI looks like exactly what tipped this over.[OpenAI’s research](https://openai.com/index/how-ai-is-expanding-what-people-do-at-work/) on how AI is expanding what people do at work analyzed over 800,000 ChatGPT conversations from U.S. workers and found that HR has one of the highest rates of “task crossover” of any function measured, with 69% of its AI use going toward work *outside* HR’s traditional scope. HR is already the department stretching furthest, covering ground a helpful tool was supposed to close.


And what AI actually handed HR was not small.[The Coders Guild](https://thecodersguild.org.uk/) found that 95% of HR leaders report rising workloads, and 91% say their responsibilities grew over the past year specifically because of AI-related work: workforce planning, employee training, governance, recruitment redesign, and organizational change. Rather than simply adopting a new tool, HR professionals have effectively taken on a second job.


## **Blame the tool, and you’ll fix the wrong thing**


Here is where the easy answer of “blaming AI” falls apart.[Deloitte’s 2026 Human Capital Trends](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/topics/talent/human-capital-trends.html) found that companies taking a technology-first approach to AI are 1.6 times more likely to miss the returns they expected than companies that lead with a human-centric approach.


[Prosci’s research on AI change management](https://www.prosci.com/ai-change-management) backs this up from the failure side: 43% of organizations point to insufficient executive sponsorship as the reason their AI rollout stalled, 38% point to inadequate training, and 63% cite human factors, not technical ones, as the primary obstacle.


Taken together, these findings shift the focus. The issue is not AI itself, but insufficient leadership during implementation. Companies have overlooked the human aspects of the rollout, leaving HR to manage both the resulting gaps and consequences.


## **HR is leaning in anyway**


Despite these challenges, HR professionals remain optimistic about AI. Lattice found that 83% of HR professionals feel excitement, hope, or optimism about delegating tasks to AI, and 42% use it regularly. Even still, The Coders Guild reports that only 23% of HR departments have received AI-specific training. HR continues to lead in adoption, yet receives very little support.


[HR should keep leading AI adoption](https://www.warp.co/blog/hr-digital-transformation-ai-era) ; what needs to change is taking away some legacy work, like[compliance paperwork](https://www.warp.co/compliance) , so new responsibilities don’t compete with old ones.


## **So, is AI the cause?**


Not quite. The data points to something more specific: when AI is added without executive support, training, or reducing old workloads, it’s not a productivity tool. It’s just another job. Giving this extra work to a group already facing 54% burnout, without easing their original duties, makes the huge jump in “HR burnout” searches less of a mystery and starts looking like the most predictable workplace outcome.
