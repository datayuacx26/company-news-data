# 36 Team Building Activities for Work

**TL;DR:** The team building activities that held up all shared three things.


1. Everyone has a reason to talk to someone outside their usual team.
2. The stakes are low enough that people relax but high enough that people stay engaged.
3. The event produces a story people are still telling months later.


Team building activities have a bad reputation, and mostly a deserved one. David Jacobson has produced corporate team-building events professionally for twenty years, first as a scavenger hunt creator, then with trivia events at[TrivWorks](https://trivworks.com/) , and he's blunt about why most of them fail: forced interaction, cheesy activities that don't match the group's culture, compulsory public speaking, and vague claims about building teamwork skills with no evidence attached. Done badly, he says, people "walk away feeling less connected, motivated and engaged, which is the exact opposite of what you want."


Done well, it's a different story. "The key to creating awesome events with meaningful impact is a shared positive experience," Jacobson says. "When there's a sense of accomplishment with a fun activity, especially one involving good-natured competition, people naturally feel more comfortable with each other, and teams can form bonds in a new way." His other rule of thumb: give people a say in the activity instead of just telling them what's happening. Planners get far more buy-in that way.


So instead of another generic list, every activity below is a real account: from founders, HR leaders, and event organizers who actually ran it, plus an internal survey of our own team at Warp.


## **Competitive and high-energy activities**


**1. Go-karting.** The single most-cited favorite in our internal survey of the Warp team. Employees pointed to it as "a great way to learn about everyone's competitive style."


**2. Paintball.** David Salamon, President of[eCopier Solutions](https://www.ecopiersolutions.com/) , still calls a corporate paintball afternoon the best team-building day his company has run: "Out in the woods nobody cares about your title, and the quiet guy from accounting turns out to be a tactical genius while the VP gets tagged in the first thirty seconds." His measure of success: whether people were still ribbing each other about it in November.


**3. Wreck 'Em Rally (remote-control car battles).** James Bennett, CEO of[Firefly Team Events](https://fireflyteamevents.com/) , runs this event with teams armed with RC cars and balloons taped to the back, battling to pop each other's balloons. "It takes a novel task, makes it playful, raises the stakes, and requires everyone on your team to collaborate to find success."


**4. Cornhole tournament.** Mike Terry of[Lynx SEO](https://www.lynxseo.com/) calls the cornhole ladder tournament from his team's cottage weekend one of the easiest wins of the whole event: "Simple, competitive, and got everyone, even the less 'gamey' people, fully invested." It works indoors or outdoors, and scales from head-to-head matchups to a full bracket.


**5. Timed obstacle course with multiple stations.** Conductor ran this with its Customer Success team, about 30 people rotating through short stations, including cornhole, mini-golf, and trivia; fastest times won. It's a good format for a mid-size team, since nobody has to be good at just one thing.


**6. Escape room.** Marc Dal Cin, Founder of[Calibre Connect](https://calibreconnect.com.au/) , mixed field and admin staff who rarely interact. With no assigned roles, "you could see within the first 10 minutes how the group naturally self-organized."


## **Cross-functional activities that break down silos**


**7. Role swap day.** Tom Bukevicius at[Scube Marketing](https://www.scubemarketing.com/) had employees temporarily trade roles with someone from another department for a few hours. "People became more thoughtful when making requests because they understood the workload on the other side. We saw fewer communication bottlenecks and better collaboration on client campaigns afterward."


**8. Reverse mentoring workshop.** Kristiyan Yankov of[Above Apex](https://aboveapex.com/) paired newer employees with executives to trade ideas in both directions. "Team members became more comfortable sharing ideas during meetings, and managers actively asked for input from people who had previously stayed quiet."


**9. Customer Safari (field research challenge).** Pavel Ufimtsev at[Pikadil](https://pikadil.com/) sent cross-functional teams out to talk to real customers instead of running an internal exercise at all. "Designers, developers, and marketers all came back with a much deeper appreciation for the customer perspective. It also gave quieter team members a chance to contribute."


**10. Cross-functional product hackathon.** Peter Barnett at[Action1](https://www.action1.com/) ran a two-day, 45-person hackathon mixing product, engineering, support, and marketing around real customer problems. A few of the resulting ideas made it onto the actual product roadmap.


**11. Business recovery simulation.** David Pickard at[Phonexa](https://phonexa.com/) gave mixed 22-person teams two hours to save a fictional client account about to cancel a $250,000 contract. "Engineers learned why sales made certain promises. Sales saw the technical constraints behind feature requests."


**12. One-day campaign sprint.** Colleen Barry at[Ketch](https://www.ketch.com/) had 25 people build a full marketing campaign in one day, then introduced surprise customer feedback halfway through to force a real-time strategy shift.


## **Unconventional and immersive experiences**


**13. Fire-breathing workshop.** Nikita Khandheria, Founder & CEO of[ERIA](https://www.eriaevents.co/) , has run this for executive teams under trained instruction. "People who had worked together for years discovered a new side of each other in the span of an hour."


**14. Hawaiian dance workshop.** Also from ERIA: teams learn choreography together and perform as a group. Khandheria's takeaway applies to both events: "Memorable team building isn't about competition, it's about creating experiences people would never have on their own."


**15. Collaborative mural painting.** Tanya Slyvkin, Founder of[Whitepage](https://www.whitepage.studio/) , had her four-person team paint sections of one large canvas without seeing the full design, then joined the pieces together. "The effect can be felt only when all elements perfectly fit into each other."


**16. Scavenger-hunt offsite.** Michael Kovnick of[Culture Discovery Vacations](https://www.culturediscovery.com/) built an annual leadership offsite in Portugal around paper maps and no phones, splitting the team into groups that each carried a piece of a final celebration dinner that only came together when all three groups converged.


**17. Themed cook-off ("Guac Off").** One Warp team member's pick: an avocado-themed cook-off, starting with avocado trivia, then small teams compete to make the best guacamole, each choosing one secret ingredient. Proof that a genuinely odd format can outperform something more conventional.


**18. Puppy bonding experience.** Echo Wang, CEO & Founder of[Yoga Kawa](https://www.yogakawa.com/) , arranged small groups to lead puppies through an obstacle course, then unwind over pastries and coffee while petting them. "Animals have this ability to help people feel comfortable. People talk easily, laugh freely, and forget about hierarchy and saying the 'right' thing." She calls it one of the most memorable corporate wellness events she's arranged, because the connection between people was genuine rather than performed.


**19. Learn how to DJ.** Another pick from our internal Warp Survey: a professional DJ walks the group through mixing and beatmatching, then lets everyone take a turn behind the decks building a short set. It's playful rather than competitive, and works well as a change of pace for a team that's already done the usual trivia and games rotation.


**20. Paint and sip.** Also from the Warp team picks: a guided painting class paired with wine or another drink of choice. No artistic skill required; the low pressure is the point, and everyone leaves with a finished piece (maybe even something they want to display at their desk).


## **Outdoor skill-building**


**21. Axe and tomahawk throwing.** Joanne Dewberry, founder of[Networking Ninja](https://www.joannedewberry.co.uk/) , runs monthly outdoor sessions, including tomahawk and axe throwing, for local small business owners built around meeting new people and learning a new skill together, not just networking over coffee.


**22. Archery.** Dewberry's favorite of the bunch: "There's always a lot of laughter, especially as some people are really good and others not so much."


## **Purpose-driven activities**


**23. Community build day.** Michael Benoit, Founder of[ContractorBond.org](http://contractorbond.org/) , had about 15 staff spend a Saturday helping build a house for a family through a local housing nonprofit. "By the end no one recalled the job titles, they only recalled who gave them a hammer when they needed one."


**24. Culture camp.** Matt Bowman of[Thrive Local](https://thriveagency.com/thrive-local/) built a 125-person week that combined training, department meetings, and a community outreach project to pack school supplies, with a virtual track for anyone who couldn't travel.


**25. Volunteer days, team's choice of cause.** Jess Munday, Co-Founder and People & Culture Manager at[Custom Neon](https://customneon.com/) , lets the team pick the cause: kids' Christmas gifts, beach clean-ups, tree planting. "Hierarchy disappears. Nobody is anyone's manager when you're all holding shovels, or picking up litter." One year, a quiet team member who "wasn't somebody you'd immediately peg as a leader" put forward the tree-planting idea, then coordinated the entire day. "She came back into the office with completely different confidence, and just bloomed."


## **Industry-specific activities**


**26. Timed pipe assembly challenge.** Emily Demirdonder of[Proximity Plumbing](https://proximityplumbing.com.au/) mixed plumbers, office admin, and new hires into small teams racing to assemble pipe correctly. "Face to face, people were able to coach each other through it in real time. One person would pick up a wrench the wrong way and another team member would jump in right there and show them the better way."


**27. Warehouse packing competition.** Manny Soto of[Burning Daily](https://burningdaily.com/) built a competition around real shift tasks instead of pulling staff off the floor, keeping accuracy rules in place so speed never came at the cost of quality. Packing speed went up afterward and order accuracy held steady.


**28. Shingle and nail relay.** John Franco of[GreenTek Roofing & Solar](https://greentekroofing.com/) ran this with two ten-person crews competing to gather, transport, and place roofing materials correctly. "The most valuable resource a crew has is not the tools they bring to the job, it's how they flow together in the heat of the moment."


## **Quick, low-effort activities**


**29. Weekly wins recognition program.** Eric Turney of[The Monterey Company](https://montereycompany.com/) recognizes a 12-person sales team's KPI wins every week with gift cards. "It builds camaraderie without forcing everyone into a long team-building event... it creates friendly competition in a positive way."


**30. Swift Swap.** Mia Morin of[Intimeros](http://www.intimeros.com/) described this five-minute game: one team studies the other's appearance, then spots what changed after a quick swap of shoes, hair, and positions. "It was low stakes, required no materials, and took less than 30 minutes. That was a good match-up for a midday break."


**31. Rose and thorn of the week.** Gabriella Garcia, COO & CPO at Warp, has her team share their high and low points of the week, from a successful project to an unforeseen roadblock. It's a low-lift way to make wins and setbacks visible across a team without waiting for a formal retro.


## **Virtual and hybrid activities**


**32. Code Break (remote logic puzzles).** Dmitrii Malashkin, Founder & CEO of[Born to Move](https://www.goborntomove.com/) , built this fully remote event with three days' notice for a 30-person distributed team ahead of a holiday weekend, proof that having one flexible virtual format on hand matters as much as picking the perfect one.


**33. "Guess Who" fact game.** Margo Kashuba, CMO at[Devart](http://www.devart.com/) and TMetric, ran this with unexpected facts about colleagues, guessed anonymously by a 40-person group. A simple format that expanded how people saw coworkers they see daily.


**34. Company fantasy football league.** Joe Williams of[Arcane Marketing](https://www.arcanemarketing.com/) credits this 14-person league with helping maintain culture after a shift to fully remote work.


**35. Hybrid escape room.** Caitlin Agnew-Francis of[Desky](http://desky.ca/) split this challenge between an in-office team and a remote team on video, each dependent on the other to find and interpret clues. "It became impossible to accomplish anything unless there was collaboration between teams."


**36. Trivia.** The second-most-cited favorite in our internal Warp poll, called out as "easy for people to get into" because everyone has their own specialty to contribute.


## **How to pick the right one for your team**


Three questions decide which activity on this list fits best.


1. **Team size.** Under 15 people can do something intimate and conversational (reverse mentoring, puppy bonding); 200-plus needs something that scales or can be easily split into groups.
2. **Format.** In-person activities that rely on physical materials or shared meals don't translate to video calls; virtual activities need built-in participation (breakout groups, time limits) or they turn into a few people talking while everyone else watches.
3. **Goal.** Breaking down silos, building trust on a team that already works closely, giving a distributed team a shared reference point, or just giving people a reason to laugh together during a hard quarter. The goal should decide the activity, not the other way around.


David Jacobson's advice applies across all of these: let the group have a say in the choice. An activity picked for the team lands very differently than one picked by the team.

## **FAQ**


### **What's the best team building activity for a small team?**


For teams under 15 people, format matters more than budget. Reverse mentoring, a collaborative mural, and a puppy bonding experience all gave everyone a chance to speak. Larger group formats like a quiz or fantasy league tend to lose intimacy at this size.


### **What team building activities work for fully remote teams?**


The best virtual formats force participation instead of allowing people to sit back and watch. A remote logic puzzle event, a "Guess Who" fact game, and a hybrid escape room all held up well. Avoid anything that relies on one person presenting to a passive group.


### **How do you do team building on a tight timeline or budget?**


Code Break was planned in three days, and Swift Swap takes under 30 minutes with no materials. The common thread is low setup: a format the organizer already knows how to run, kept on hand for exactly this situation.


### **Do team building activities actually improve performance?**


Several activities above point to measurable, lasting change: faster cross-team communication after a hackathon or role swap, fewer client escalations after a business simulation, steadier packing accuracy after a warehouse competition, and, per Jess Munday's account, a quiet team member finding real leadership confidence after running a volunteer day.


### **How often should a company run team building activities?**


There's no universal cadence, but mixing formats works best: a small, low-effort activity on a regular basis, paired with a larger event once or twice a year.


### **What team building activities should companies avoid?**


David Jacobson, who has produced corporate events for twenty years, points to forced interaction, cheesy or non-customized activities, compulsory public speaking, and vague claims about building teamwork skills with no evidence any of it actually happened. The common thread: these formats create a performance of bonding rather than a real reason for people to talk to each other.
