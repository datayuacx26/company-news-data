# Federal Funding Should Support Communities, Not Political Agendas


Congressional investments should reach the communities they were intended to serve.


Linda Morris ,
ACLU Women's Rights Project


Amanda Walgrove


**Share This Page**


July 20, 2026


Researcher Katie Edwards has dedicated her career to[reducing violence among LGBTQ+ and Indigenous youth](https://www.aclu.org/news/racial-justice/devastated-and-hopeless-researchers-speak-out-on-funding-cuts) , building programs that help young people feel safe and supported. Josefina Navar, deputy director of HOPE Fair Housing (HOPE), works to[help families across Illinois access safe and stable housing](https://www.aclu.org/news/racial-justice/how-eviction-brands-renters-with-a-scarlet-letter) . Adam Odsess-Rubin helped found the National Queer Theater to[amplify the voices of LGBTQ+ artists facing censorship](https://www.aclu.org/news/free-speech/revisiting-the-nea-four-free-speech-battles-in-the-arts) abroad. These organizations **,** and countless others throughout the country, could soon risk losing the funding they need to deliver critical resources that millions of Americans rely on.


On May 29, 2026, the U.S. Office of Management and Budget (OMB) and 40+ agencies proposed sweeping changes to[overhaul the set of rules governing federal grants](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/29/2026-10817/regulation-for-federal-financial-assistance) to nonprofits, state and local governments, and other grantees. If implemented, this proposal will unlawfully reshape how federal funding is awarded and administered, putting essential services at risk.


“Families could lose access to early childhood education programs,” said Linda Morris, senior staff attorney in the ACLU Women’s Rights Project, “survivors of abuse could face greater barriers to support, researchers could lose funding for lifesaving scientific work, and health care providers could face new obstacles to delivering care.”


---


## Weaponizing Federal Grants for Trump's Political Agenda


The proposed rule would give the Trump administration sweeping authority to approve, deny, or cut off funding to organizations based on the government’s political agenda, not community needs. This blatant power grab undermines longstanding civil rights protections and threatens to suppress viewpoints the government disagrees with by leveraging financial resources.


In fact, the rule would impose unlawful restrictions on funding related to transgender people, gender-affirming care, addressing racial health disparities and reproductive health. It would also impose unlawful bans on diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility and so-called “gender ideology” that undermine civil rights and civil liberties.Individuals who rely on federally funded programs will be penalized for their views or their identity.


It’s clear that this is a rule designed to lock up taxpayer funds that previously went to programs that supported all Americans and redistribute those funds to programs that politically align with the current administration’s priorities.


---


## Putting Vulnerable Communities and Critical Services at Risk


If the proposed rule goes into effect, every single American could feel the impact of these haphazard funding bans. Children won’t receive high-quality education that Head Start previously would have been able to provide in their first language, survivors of domestic violence will face increased barriers in receiving essential shelter and services, Americans’ access to HIV treatment will be limited, and gender-affirming care will be harder to receive.


The ripple effect will be undeniable. Any organization that receives federal funding will be faced with unnecessary red tape, added costs, and administrative burdens — adding significant financial risks that many may not be able to overcome. Essential services will be disrupted. Scientific research will be altered or halted. And viewpoints that don’t match the administration’s agenda will be suppressed.


Disruptions to essential federal programs worsen inequality and have an outsized impact on households of color, immigrant families, people with disabilities, and LGBTQ+ individuals, who receive a disproportionate amount of the affected services and programs due to the ongoing systemic inequality and discrimination they face.


---


## Protecting Programs That Serve the Public


The ACLU proudly represents many clients who would be negatively impacted by this proposed rule, including nonprofit organizations serving transgender and gender nonconforming people, researchers and scientists, community health centers, artists and arts organizations, educational programs, and other recipients of federal funding.


[We urge](https://www.aclu.org/documents/aclu-comment-on-omb-nprm-on-federal-financial-assistance) OMB and all participating federal agencies to withdraw the proposed rule in its entirety and preserve a federal grants system that is transparent, lawful, and focused on serving the public rather than advancing a political agenda.

### ACLU and ACLU of North Dakota File Brief in Support of Right to Protest on Public Roads


ST. LOUIS — The American Civil Liberties Union and the ACLU of North Dakota filed an amicus brief today with the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit in Thunderhawk v. Kirchmeier, a case that challenges the government’s decision to close a public road during a Standing Rock protest, preventing people from assembling to express their opposition to the Dakota Access Pipeline. In the brief, the ACLU argues that roads of every kind — including rural roads, multi-lane roads and high-speed roads — have served as sites of protest throughout United States history, from the civil rights marches and anti-war demonstrations of the 1960s and ’70s to more recent protests, including marches in opposition to abortion, in support of rural healthcare and against police brutality. Courts across the country have long recognized that roads are quintessential traditional public forums and open for public assembly, debate, and the communication of ideas. “The government’s argument disregards our history of protest in the United States and misinterprets our fundamental First Amendment rights. Our right to assemble and protest in the streets should never depend on the type of roadway chosen,” said Andrew Malone, senior staff attorney for the ACLU of North Dakota. “The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that protesting in public streets is a time-honored American right. It is long overdue for police officers, prosecutors and government officials to uphold this constitutional standard.” For nearly eight years and through multiple rounds of dismissals and appeals, the government has argued that some streets, including rural roads, don’t belong to the community and that the road at issue in this case doesn’t deserve the same protection afforded to other public spaces. But, the ACLU argues in the brief, as one of the few communal spaces in rural areas, these roads are uniquely positioned to offer rural communities – and those wishing to address them – a public space in which to associate, communicate thoughts and discuss public issues. “Protesting on public roads is as American as apple pie, and that doesn’t change whether the road is a rural highway in North Dakota or 42nd St in New York City,” said Vera Eidelman, senior staff attorney with the ACLU’s Speech, Privacy, and Technology Project. “People can turn the page in a magazine or change the channel on TV, but streets remain one of the few places where we have to confront ideas we might otherwise tune out—and that’s why our rights to protest on streets, sidewalks, and in public parks are so important.”


**Court Case:** Thunderhawk v. Morton County

### ACLU Reacts to Draft Bipartisan AI Bill That Would Preempt State Laws


WASHINGTON — Today, Congressman Obernolte and Congresswoman Trahan released a draft regulatory framework for Artificial Intelligence, which they may soon introduce in the House of Representatives. This draft bill – if enacted into law – would largely prohibit states from regulating AI developers. It would block states from enforcing existing state protections and from enacting new protections This could include anything from privacy regulations to antidiscrimination requirements to AI safety laws. Congress has twice overwhelmingly rejected legislative proposals to block states from regulating AI, also known as “preemption.” This includes a 99-1 Senate vote last year against an amendment that would have created a 10-year moratorium on state AI regulation. Seventeen Republican governors joined the bipartisan opposition to that failed amendment. In response, Jina John, senior policy counsel for AI, privacy and technology at the ACLU released the following statement: "This draft bill fails to learn from Congress’s previous attempts to block state AI regulations. States must be able to protect their own residents from harm, hold tech companies accountable, and ensure that AI is safe and trustworthy. "Governors, attorneys general, and lawmakers from both parties have opposed the efforts to push states aside for good reason. AI touches all aspects of people’s lives in this country, including employment, healthcare, lending, education, and so much more. So far, it has been governors and state legislatures—and largely not Congress or the president—who have been trying to protect the American people from harms that AI may cause. The federal government must not grant tech giants a blank check to deploy these technologies without the oversight of states."
