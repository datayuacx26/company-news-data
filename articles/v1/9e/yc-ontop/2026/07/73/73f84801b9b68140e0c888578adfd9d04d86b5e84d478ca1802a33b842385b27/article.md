## How do you classify a contractor in Latin America?


Classify by how the person actually works, not by the label on the contract. A genuine contractor sets their own hours, uses their own tools, and can work for other clients. If you control their schedule and methods like an employee, most Latin American labor authorities will treat them as one, with back taxes attached.


### What happens if you get it wrong?


Misclassification can trigger retroactive social contributions, fines, and severance claims in the worker's country. It is the risk that turns a cheap hire into an expensive lesson.


## What are the penalties for getting classification wrong in Latin America?


Steep, and rising. In Brazil, treating an employee as a contractor can cost 3,000 to 400,000 reais per worker (Atlas HXM and Skuad, 2026), and across the region a reclassification brings retroactive social contributions, severance, and fines. The savings from calling someone a contractor disappear the moment a labor authority disagrees, so classify honestly and keep the relationship genuinely independent.


## What should a contractor agreement include?


A strong agreement names the scope, deliverables, payment terms, currency, and confidentiality in writing. State the amount, the currency, and the payment date explicitly, because ambiguity on payday is the fastest way to lose trust with a new contractor.


Keep it simple and specific. A one-page agreement everyone understands beats a ten-page template nobody reads.


## What documents do you need to onboard a contractor?


Three things, on file from day one: a signed contractor agreement, the tax or identification documentation the contractor's country requires, and a clean invoicing process so every payment has a paper trail. Assembling these after an audit begins is far harder than collecting them up front, and a missing document is what turns a routine review into a problem.


## How do you stay compliant when paying contractors abroad?


Collect the right tax documentation for cross-border work, keep clean records of every payment, and adapt to each country's rules. Requirements differ across Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, and Brazil, so a process that flexes per jurisdiction keeps you audit-ready without a finance team in every market.

## What is the best way to pay a contractor in Latin America?


The best method pays in full, on time, in a currency the contractor controls. International wires are familiar but slow and heavy on fees. Payout platforms are faster but often convert against the worker. A workforce platform pays into an account the contractor holds, so they decide when to convert.
