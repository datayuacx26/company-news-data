### How do different countries classify workers?


Countries classify workers in different ways. Some view individuals as *self-employed* , while others expect employers to withhold taxes. Even though tax treaties are in place, they are complicated and applied inconsistently. You might qualify for **reduced withholding rates** under a bilateral agreement, but only if both sides file the correct forms on time. If you miss a deadline or misunderstand a provision of the agreement, you could end up paying double taxes until the issue is resolved, which often requires costly professional assistance.


### What challenges do international wire transfers present?


International wire transfers pass through correspondent banks, each of which charges a fee and slows the process. A payment sent from New York to Nairobi might go through three or four intermediary institutions before it arrives. Each institution charges fees and adds a day or two to the process. What should be a quick transaction becomes a week-long waiting game, leaving you unsure where the funds are or when they will clear.


In some countries, it's hard to get access to stable-currency accounts or international payment platforms. Depending on where you live, opening a USD or EUR account locally can be tough or even impossible. This makes you depend on local-currency accounts, which can expose you to inflation risk, or on third-party services that add additional fees and exchange spreads. The infrastructure gap isn't your fault, but you're the one who ultimately bears the cost.


### Why is worker classification a risk?


Are you an employee or a contractor? The answer depends on the laws of the country you are in. In one place, they might look at your work hours and decide that you are really an employee. This can lead to **payroll taxes** and benefits that the company did not expect. On the other hand, in another location, they might see the same situation as independent contracting, which means the employer has no responsibilities. This confusing area creates risk for both parties; however, contractors typically have **less leverage** to negotiate clarity up front.


Misclassification isn't always intentional. Companies that are growing globally often do not realize that definitions of contractors can be different by country. They often use the same contract template across all locations, believing it is legally acceptable. When a disagreement arises or a tax authority reviews the relationship, the contractor is left trying to prove their status and protect their income.
