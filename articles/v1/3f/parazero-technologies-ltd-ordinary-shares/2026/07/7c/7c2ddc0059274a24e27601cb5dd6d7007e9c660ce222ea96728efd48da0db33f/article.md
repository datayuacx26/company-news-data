###### Small Drones, Big Problem Report Cover


*New US counter-UAS doctrine puts net capture in the same defeat category as interceptor aircraft and directed fragmentation, and immediately warns about the collateral risk of everything else on that list.*


Ask most people what “counter-drone” means and they will describe the same thing: shooting a drone out of the sky. The US Department of War’s new counter-UAS playbook, “Small Drones, Big Problems,” published in June by Joint Interagency Task Force 401, agrees that this is what people picture. Then it flips the assumption. Defeat, the playbook says, is “probably what most people think of when they hear ‘countering drones.’ Paradoxically, it should also be the last of several steps you’ve taken.”


### **Rethink Your CAUS Playbook**


Put more bluntly: the moment the last step is all a defender has left is the moment everything else has already failed. So when that moment arrives, what does doctrine say to actually use?


The playbook defines four defeat mechanisms: link disruption, navigation disruption, software disruption, and kinetic defeat. The first three attack the drone’s dependencies. The fourth removes it from the air. Here is how the document describes it: “when immediate removal from the air is required, kinetic defeat provides a hard-kill option. This can involve intercepting drones with other aircraft, using explosives or directed fragmentation, capturing them with nets, or physically striking them by other available means.”


Read that list again. Nets are not described as a police curiosity or a niche accessory. They sit in official US doctrine in the same hard-kill category as explosive interceptors and directed fragmentation.


The playbook reinforces the point when describing a joint counter-drone exercise at Eglin Air Force Base in October 2025. Fiber-optic drones, which trail a physical cable and “circumvent many countermeasures,” were among the threats tested. The answer was layered sensing that cues “lasers, intercepting drones, small arms, and nets” to neutralize them. When jamming has nothing to jam, the effectors that remain are physical ones.


###### Ilustration from “Small Drones, big problems”


But the most important sentence in the defeat chapter is not the list of options. It is the warning that follows it. Kinetic methods, the playbook cautions, “carry higher risks of collateral damage, making careful consideration of the environment and safety of civilians essential.”


Consider what that caveat means in practice. An explosive interceptor stops the drone, but it sends fragments and a burning airframe into whatever sits below the engagement: a flight line, a fuel depot, a crowd, a city street. Directed fragmentation has the same problem by design. Gunfire adds rounds that keep traveling past the target. The playbook is candid about how marginal improvised solutions are, noting from training that “shotguns may be useless, and rifles may have a hard time hitting drones at night.” It is equally candid that nets require training and realistic practice to employ well. No effector is free.


### A Hard-Kill Option Built for Sensitive Environments


The doctrine states its own goal in six words: “Protect what matters. Minimize unintended effects.” Most of the hard-kill menu makes the second half of that sentence difficult. Within the playbook’s own kinetic category, net capture is the option that delivers immediate removal from the air without explosive effects, without fragmentation, and without rounds downrange. It is the hard-kill mechanism suited to exactly the environments the playbook worries about: air bases, urban areas, critical infrastructure, and anywhere people stand under the engagement.


There is a second argument in the document that most defense architectures overlook. “Disabling a drone without destroying it can be just as valuable as bringing it down,” the playbook notes. “Recovering a system may reveal who flew it, how it was controlled, and what it was meant to accomplish. That information feeds back into detection, denial, and disruption, strengthening your drone protection plan over time.”


A drone destroyed by explosive effects leaves little to examine. A drone brought down without them leaves far more: the airframe, the payload, the control method, and sometimes a path to the operator. In a threat landscape defined by rapid adversary adaptation, that feedback loop is not a side benefit. It is a capability.

### Still, When It Comes to Drone Defense – No Silver Bullet


None of this makes nets a silver bullet. The playbook’s foreword, signed by Brigadier General Matthew Ross, is explicit that no such thing exists and that the answer is layered defense. Nets are one layer. What has changed is that this layer now has doctrinal standing, and defense buyers building layered architectures have official language telling them the defeat layer needs a low-collateral, non-explosive option.


This is the design logic behind ParaZero’s DefendAir family, from handheld net launchers to drone-mounted interceptors: hard-kill defeat built for environments where explosive effects are unacceptable. By creating a non-explosive last line of defense for dismounted units, armored vehicles, or strategic sites, operators can trust that if all else fails to engage the threat, a layer of protection still exists.


The playbook says defeat should be your last resort. It has now also made clear what that last resort should look like when the ground below you matters.
