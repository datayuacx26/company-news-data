# Why Chatbots Aren't Enough in PE

A CIM lands in the inbox on a Monday afternoon, and the AI sits there waiting to be asked. An associate can pull a teaser summary out of it in seconds, which feels like help. Then the actual work starts: the first-pass screen, the add-on fit check, the CRM history, the read on the banker, the prior theses this might collide with, the questions worth raising before the first call. The model handled one step of that. The associate still carried everything else.


So the firm didn't really hand off the work. It handed off a single task buried inside the work, and mistook the difference for progress.


## Adoption was the easy part


By the numbers, private equity has already embraced AI.[Deloitte's 2025 M&A study](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/what-we-do/capabilities/mergers-acquisitions-restructuring/articles/m-and-a-generative-ai-study.html) found that 86% of corporate and PE dealmakers have folded generative AI into their deal workflows, and 88% of the PE firms it surveyed have committed at least a million dollars to it.


The gains at the task level are real, too, not hype. When Harvard Business School[put 758 BCG consultants through a controlled experiment](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2025.21838) , the ones working with GPT-4 finished assignments a quarter faster and produced work judged 40% better, at least on the tasks the model was suited to. Whatever the old skepticism was, the question of whether AI helps with individual pieces of work is settled.


What's strange is how little of that climbs upward.[MIT's NANDA initiative looked at 300 enterprise AI deployments](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/) and found that roughly 95% of them moved the P&L not at all. The culprit wasn't the models. Generic chat tools stall inside an organization because they never learn how the organization actually works; they make people individually quicker and leave the institution exactly where it was.


[McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) reached the same place from the opposite direction. Across 25 organizational traits it tested against AI's bottom-line impact, the one that mattered most wasn't budget or choice of model. It was whether the firm had bothered to redesign its workflows around the tool.


Which points at the real trap. Task speed has become a commodity; everyone has it now. The advantage lives in the workflow itself, and almost no one has touched that.


## The chatbot bottleneck


None of this is an argument against chat. A partner should be able to fire off a question in Teams. A principal should be able to forward a deck and ask for a quick screen. Those are the channels deal teams already live in, and meeting people there is just sensible.


The error is mistaking the chat window for the workflow.


When an associate has to prompt every step of a new-deal routine by hand, the firm hasn't removed coordination work; it has invented a new kind and wrapped it around the model. Each individual task does run faster. But someone still has to hold the sequence in their head, remember what comes next, check what came back, and decide where the output goes. The labor doesn't disappear. It migrates, from doing the work to orchestrating it.


PE firms hardly need more of that. Deals arrive over email. Context gets dropped in Teams. A banker phones the one person they happen to know. Portfolio signal hides in board decks, and the firm's real theses are scattered across old memos, CRM comments, diligence folders, and half-remembered conversations from a year ago. A chat assistant can genuinely help once someone has pulled those threads together. What it cannot do is notice, on its own, that they need pulling.


That is what makes prompt dependence such a quiet way to fail. Every task gets shorter, so it reads as momentum, right until you recognize the shape of it: MIT's failed 95%, with quicker people and an unchanged firm.


## What a teammate actually does


Consider how you hand work to a colleague instead. They've learned the process. They know where things live and what a fresh CIM demands without being walked through it. You don't re-explain the job every time a deal comes in; you trust them to pick it up.


That's the posture AI keeps missing. A real AI teammate works where the deal team already works, across email, Teams, Slack, CRM tasks, forwarded decks, calendars, and data rooms. It understands the firm's standard procedures, watches for the moments those procedures are meant to kick in, gets the routine prep moving, and pulls a human in the instant real judgment is called for. That is the model Zarna is built on: a layer of teammates inside the systems where deals already happen, rather than one more destination investors have to visit to chat with a machine.


Direct instruction still has its place. A partner can drop a note:


"We just saw this company. Check it against our add-on theses across the portfolio, pull the relevant history, and rough out the follow-up questions."


A VP can tag the teammate on a CRM task:


"Get me the banker relationship history before tomorrow's call: last touch, open loops, companies we should be asking about."


That's exactly how the exceptions and the judgment calls should be assigned. The routine, though, ought to be underway before anyone reaches for the keyboard.


Go back to Monday's CIM. For a teammate, the deck landing is the trigger, and nothing else needs to happen. By the time the associate opens it, there's a first-pass screen drafted against the firm's own criteria, a flag on two earlier theses that brushed the same market with links back to the original memos, a summary of the banker's last three interactions lifted from the CRM, and a working list of questions for management already in the thread. Nobody asked for any of it. The associate's first real task isn't assembly. It's the call: does this earn a partner's time this week?


## Where it adds up


The pattern repeats across the firm, and the gap widens with the weight of the prep.


In sourcing, a chatbot will write the outreach email once you remember to ask for it. A teammate is already watching the relationship history, has already caught the follow-up that went cold six weeks back, and already has the next touch drafted. Coverage stops living or dying by one person's memory of who's owed a reply.


Diligence is where the prep runs heaviest, so the daylight between the two is widest. A chatbot summarizes whatever CIM you hand it. A teammate sees the CIM arrive, builds the screen, pulls comparable deals, tests the company against old theses, and drafts the management questions while the thing is still sitting in the inbox. The associate shifts from chasing the work to reviewing it, and the firm's accumulated memory shows up before the first call instead of after.


Portfolio support tells the same story. A chatbot explains a KPI swing once you've pasted in the figures. A teammate is watching for the drift, has retrieved last year's board context, has set the pattern against the rest of the portfolio, and hands the operating partner a prepared set of questions ahead of the review. The cost of that prep is higher than most firms admit:[EY documented a North American mid-cap fund](https://www.ey.com/en_ch/insights/strategy-transactions/ai-in-private-equity) that compressed portfolio reporting from four person-days to under an hour once the data ran through an AI layer instead of analysts stitching it together by hand. The conversation with the CEO still belongs to a person. They simply stop walking into it cold.


## When proactive AI goes wrong


Skepticism toward proactive AI is warranted, and I'd be wary of anyone who didn't feel it. A system that fires on everything is just noise. A system that acts with confidence on the wrong trigger is genuinely worse than a chatbot, because a bad answer in a chat window costs you a minute, while a bad email to a banker can cost you the relationship.


That caution shows up in the data.[Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be scrapped by the end of 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) , blaming runaway costs, fuzzy value, and weak risk controls. Of the thousands of vendors waving the "agentic" banner, Gartner reckons only about 130 are the real thing. The rest it calls "agent washing": a chat assistant wearing a teammate's clothes.


What separates the version that works is unglamorous discipline. The triggers stay narrow and tied to procedures the firm has already decided it wants run, so the system only ever starts work the firm meant to happen. Every claim traces back to its source document, so a human can check it in seconds rather than take it on faith. Anything that leaves the building, or that turns on judgment, stops at an approval gate. The teammate does the preparation, and the person makes the decision. A firm shopping for vendors should treat "fully autonomous" with exactly the suspicion it brings to "just ask our chatbot."


## The only test that counts


In the end the measures that matter are narrow: how fast you can deploy capital, IRR and MOIC, DPI, the confidence of your LPs. Hours saved is something a vendor counts. These are what a fund counts.


Deployment velocity feels it first. A firm that can screen every credible inbound and keep banker and founder relationships warm without lowering its standards simply sees more of the market than one triaging from memory. IRR and MOIC shift further upstream, where sharper context changes which questions get asked and which risks surface before the money is committed. DPI improves when a portfolio problem appears in month two instead of month seven and the follow-through actually happens.


LP confidence is the slowest to earn and the easiest to fake badly. LPs read the same surveys everyone else does; at 86% adoption, "we use AI" buys nothing. What a GP can genuinely put on the table is the thing McKinsey's data singled out: workflows that were rebuilt, with evidence that sourcing, diligence, and portfolio monitoring run on a system rather than on heroics.


## The choice in front of PE firms


Most firms will buy the tools and keep the old routine. Associates will lean on them for research, partners will ask for memo summaries, and the firm will bank some real task-level efficiency and call it a transformation. That's the well-worn road straight into MIT's 95%.


The better firm makes a harder, narrower set of decisions: which workflows the teammates should own, which channels they watch, which procedures they follow, where the approval gates belong. Then it teaches the team to treat them as colleagues who handle the routine and raise a hand at the exceptions, and holds them to the standard you'd hold a first-year associate to: show your work, and ask before anything goes out the door.


Nearly every PE firm is going to use AI. The question that separates them is whether it stays a window you type into or becomes a layer of teammates working inside sourcing, diligence, portfolio support, and the firm's own memory. That is the line between a tool you operate and a colleague who works alongside you.
