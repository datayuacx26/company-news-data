# How to turn flat lay photos into on-model images for e‑commerce

Flat lay photos are a practical way for small and mid-size businesses to photograph clothing catalogs. One overhead setup can cover an entire product line, and these images show the item’s color and design details clearly, which is critical for selling soft goods like[clothing and accessories](https://www.photoroom.com/ai-product-photography/clothing) online. But they don’t show how the garment falls on a body, and traditional shoots with fashion models require more production time and a much larger budget.


[On-model images](https://www.photoroom.com/blog/ai-virtual-models) provide the necessary context to help buyers picture themselves wearing the clothes. In this guide, we’ll walk through how to turn flat lay photos into on-model images, so budding fashion brands and e‑commerce sellers can create catalog-ready visuals from the assets they already have. Throughout, we’ll explore how Photoroom’s[Batch edit](https://www.photoroom.com/batch) capabilities, alongside[Flat Lay](https://www.photoroom.com/tools/flat-lay) and[AI Virtual Model](https://www.photoroom.com/tools/virtual-model) tools, help small teams convert those images at scale.

## What’s “flat lay to on-model” photography, and why do teams use it?


Converting a flat lay to an on-model image puts an existing top-down garment photo into context by dressing a model with the item. Flat lay shots are important for online catalogs because they display details like stitch quality and overall garment shape, while on-model images help buyers judge the fit, length, and overall silhouette.


Teams turn flat lay images into model photos for several reasons.


### Higher trust, lower return rates


Finding a cute shirt or nice pair of low-rise jeans in an online storefront is one thing, but understanding how the shirt’s fabric drapes or where the jeans sit makes a bigger impact on a buyer’s purchase decision. Since e‑commerce shoppers can’t physically try on clothing, it’s important that they can see the product in context by putting it on a body. Buyers who can see how a garment could fit via an on-model image are more likely to visualize how that fit will translate on their body. That stronger visualization means they’ll have higher trust in the clothes they’re buying, and you’ll see fewer returns after delivery.


### Show the cut when worn


A flat lay can show that a shirt has dropped shoulders, but it doesn’t reveal where the shoulder seam falls or where the hem lands when someone wears it.


On-model product photography makes those proportions visible and demonstrates how the material behaves. For example, a structured blazer keeps a firm line, while a soft dress falls closer to the body. This way, shoppers get realistic information about the silhouette before they place an order.


### Display fit on different body types


Flat lays show the general shape of an item, but shoppers may struggle to picture that shape against their own proportions. Only using one model also gives a limited view when a brand serves customers with a range of body types.


Photoroom lets teams generate the same apparel on pre-set AI fashion models with a range of body types without booking a separate shoot for each one. You can also upload your own model images, which are saved in[Brand Kit](https://www.photoroom.com/brand-kit) so your team can use the same model for catalog continuity in later product drops.


### Add on-model shots after the campaign shoot


A campaign fashion photography shoot can only cover the products brought to set that day. When last-minute product additions miss the shoot, the team must book another session or publish a product listing with fewer useful images.


A flat lay to model workflow starts with the inventory photos the team already has. Fashion sellers can create an on-model image with just a phone, natural lighting, and a clean background, even when the official shoot has wrapped.


### Scale on-model photography without losing consistency


A production team can keep one photoshoot visually consistent. The challenge starts when a growing catalog requires different photographers and locations, or when edits stretch over longer periods of time and editors join and leave the team. All of these small changes introduce variations in your images, which makes your catalog look disconnected. Inconsistent catalogs, in turn, lower customers’ confidence that what they see is what they’ll get.


Flat lay to model gives teams more control over the process. They can photograph every garment with the same flat lay setup, then use Photoroom to place each item on the same AI fashion model. Teams can also reuse the approved pose and background broadly across up to 250 SKUs with Photoroom’s Batch edit feature, so new additions stay visually consistent without another round of creative alignment.


Enterprise teams can extend the same workflow on a larger catalog through existing workflows using the[AI Fashion Model API](https://www.photoroom.com/api/virtual-model) . For example, Marketplace GoodBuy Gear[increased conversion rates by 23%](https://www.photoroom.com/customer-stories/goodbuygear) after adopting Photoroom’s API.


## How to turn a flat lay into an on-model image in 6 steps


Producing on-model images through traditional photoshoots requires extensive budget planning and collaborations, especially when catalogs grow. Photoroom combines bulk image preparation with virtual AI models for e‑commerce in one platform, so teams can turn existing assets into on-model visuals while reducing editing costs,[like the marketplace Selency did](https://www.photoroom.com/customer-stories/selency) .


Here’s how to use Photoroom to turn flat lays into on-model images in just six steps.


### 1. Upload and prepare your flat lay


Open Photoroom and upload a sharp image that shows the complete garment. If you’re starting from an existing product image, select “Flat Lay,” then add any specifications like image size or styles from your Brand Kit.


Once you have a flat lay image ready in Photoroom (directly uploaded or created via the Flat Lay tool), you can use integrated[editing tools](https://www.photoroom.com/blog/edit-product-images) like[AI Background Remover](https://www.photoroom.com/tools/background-remover) to remove the original background when it blends into the clothing or adds clutter around the edges and[Edit with AI](https://www.photoroom.com/tools/edit-with-ai) to make changes through text prompts. If wrinkles distort the outline, use the[Ironing tool](https://www.photoroom.com/tools/remove-wrinkles-clothes) to smooth them without flattening every natural fold.


### 2. Open AI Fashion Models


From your Photoroom Space, select AI Fashion Models. You’ll need to pick at least one image to start, but if you’d like to display more than one item on a model at a time, you can add up to four total at this stage.


Listings that need a product-focused image without a visible person should use[AI Ghost Mannequin](https://www.photoroom.com/tools/ghost-mannequin) to create an invisible-mannequin view from a flat lay or hanger shot. You’ll skip steps three and four, but the rest are similar for both tools.


### 3. Choose a preset or custom model


Select a model from Photoroom’s preset library. You can also upload custom model photography that matches your existing campaign. Photoroom saves all models in AI Fashion Model and can add them to your[Brand Kit](https://www.photoroom.com/brand-kit) , which makes it easier for other team members to reference and use the same model later.


Photoroom’s library includes 12 models with different body types and skin tones. Including a range of appearances emphasizes inclusivity and gives more shoppers relevant body references across the collection, ultimately supporting higher conversion potential from a broader audience. According to the[Unstereotype Alliance’s 2026 report](https://www.unstereotypealliance.org/en/resources/research-and-tools/state-of-the-industry-2026-five-year-retrospective-report) , created in partnership with Oxford University’s business school, brands who used inclusive models found about 3.5% higher short-term conversion rates and 16% in the long term.


### 4. Set the pose and background


Choose a pose from Photoroom’s list that[keeps the apparel visible](https://www.photoroom.com/blog/clothing-photography) , such as a hand in pocket for a blouse or arms crossed for jeans, then select a background or upload your own (including images you’ve already generated in Photoroom, or new images you’d like to create).


Alongside these generation settings, you can also use text prompts to[refine the model’s position or crop](https://www.photoroom.com/blog/retouch-product-photos-for-e-commerce) , such as showing only the upper half of the model, to minimize the number of edits you have to make later.


### 5. Select the image size and quality


Pick an aspect ratio that matches the platform where you plan to publish the on-model image. Some marketplaces require specific aspect ratios, like 16:9, while others have pixel minimums, such as a 500px minimum on the longest side. Don’t worry about matching every marketplace requirement at once—you can use Photoroom’s Batch edit interface to[automatically resize and reformat](https://www.photoroom.com/use-cases/multichannel-product-image-optimization) up to 250 images at a time.


Finally, select the output quality. Many major marketplaces recommend at least 1,000 x 1,000 pixel resolutions, but Photoroom can generate images in resolutions up to 4K for products that need sharper textures or larger zoom views.


### 6. Generate, review, and set up batch processing


Select “Generate” to get your on-model image.


Before exporting, compare the original flat lay to model AI output. Check the color and fit first, then review details such as logos or prints, zippers and buttons, and tags.


Once you feel confident with your result, use Batch edits to apply the same AI generated model and setup across multiple product images and lookbooks. You can add identical edits on up to 250 images at a time for a consistent catalog, no matter what listing you’re looking at. Batch processing cuts a lot of time off your editing process, too. When Italian art marketplace intOndo started using Photoroom’s Batch mode, they[saved 25 minutes](https://www.photoroom.com/customer-stories/intondo) for every 100 images edited—equivalent to 125 hours across their 30,000-item catalog.
