# Accrued Expenses Journal Entry Examples & How-To (July 2026)

Payroll runs after the month ends. The utility invoice arrives two weeks late. The auditors billed for work they finished in December but won't invoice until January. Every one of those is an accrued expense, and each one needs a journal entry dated to the period it belongs to. Here's how to record them so your balance sheet and income statement actually match what happened.


**TLDR:**


- Record accrued expenses by debiting an expense account and crediting a liability account on the last day of the period.
- Accrued expenses sit under current liabilities on the balance sheet; they are liabilities, not assets.
- Skipping reversals causes the same cost to post twice, once as an estimate and once when the invoice arrives.
- The IRS requires businesses exceeding an inflation-indexed gross receipts threshold to use the accrual method. The TCJA set the base at $25 million, adjusted annually for inflation, so the exact figure changes each year.

## What Are Accrued Expenses


Accrued expenses are costs incurred in a given period that haven't been paid or billed yet. The work happened, the service got used, or the obligation was created, but no cash moved and no invoice arrived. Under accrual basis accounting, you record the expense when it's incurred, not when it's paid.[Investopedia's overview of accrued expenses](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/accruedexpense.asp) outlines how these obligations appear on financial statements before payment occurs.


Skip this step and the numbers mislead you. Expenses land in the wrong period, profit looks better than reality, and liabilities stay off the balance sheet until the bill finally shows up.


## Accrued Expenses vs. Accounts Payable


Accounts payable and[accrued expenses journal entry](https://doublehq.com/blog/accrued-expenses-journal-entry/) examples often get lumped together because both represent money owed. The difference comes down to documentation and timing.


Accounts payable exist because a vendor invoice has already arrived. The amount is fixed and the ledger reflects an exact figure someone sent. Accrued expenses have no invoice yet. The obligation gets recorded based on estimation because the cost was incurred before paperwork arrived, as noted in[AccountingTools' overview of accrued expenses](https://www.accountingtools.com/articles/what-are-accrued-expenses.html) .


That distinction affects confidence in the number. Accounts payable is a known quantity. Accrued expenses require judgment, and that judgment gets corrected once the real invoice lands.


## Accrued Expenses vs. Prepaid Expenses


Accrued expenses and prepaid expenses run in opposite directions. One recognizes a cost before cash leaves the business. The other recognizes cash leaving before the cost is used up.


An accrued expense means the service happened but payment hasn't. A prepaid expense means payment happened but the benefit hasn't been used yet, like six months of insurance paid upfront in January. The reverse concept,[deferred revenue](https://doublehq.com/blog/deferred-revenue-meaning-definition-examples/) , applies when cash is received before the service is delivered. The timing mismatch sits on opposite sides of the balance sheet.


Both entries correct for timing. Accrued expenses catch up on unbilled costs. Prepaid expenses spread a payment across the periods it covers.


## Common Accrued Expense Examples


Every close month brings a familiar set of costs that showed up before the paperwork did. A few examples cover most of what you will see:


- **Wages and salaries payable** : employees work through the end of the period, but payroll runs after the close date, so the days worked get accrued.
- **Accrued interest on loans** : interest builds daily on outstanding debt even though the lender bills monthly or quarterly.
- **Utility expenses** : electricity, water, and gas get used all month, but the invoice arrives weeks into the next period.
- **Accrued rent** : a tenant occupies the space before the landlord sends a bill, or a landlord earns rent before collecting it.
- **Accrued taxes** : payroll tax, property tax, and income tax obligations build up before the filing or payment date arrives.
- **Professional services fees** : legal, consulting, or audit work gets performed under an engagement, but the firm invoices after the period closes.


## Accrual Accounting vs. Cash Basis Accounting


Cash basis accounting only records a transaction when cash physically moves. Accrual accounting records it when the expense is incurred or the revenue is earned, cash timing aside. Under cash basis, there is nothing to accrue. A bill sitting unpaid at month end simply doesn't show up until the check clears.


The matching principle drives this. Expenses need to land in the same period as the revenue they helped produce, so a December sale tied to a December commission gets recorded together, even if the commission check goes out in January.


This isn't optional for every company. The IRS requires businesses exceeding an inflation-indexed gross receipts threshold to use the accrual method. The TCJA set the base figure at $25 million, adjusted annually for inflation, so the exact number shifts each year. Consult your tax advisor for the current threshold, and note that[general ledger reconciliation](https://doublehq.com/blog/general-ledger-reconciliation/) becomes critical to verify those accrual entries are correct.


## Double-Entry Bookkeeping for Accrued Expenses


Every accrued expense entry debits an expense account, raising cost on the income statement, and credits a liability account, raising what's owed on the balance sheet. Both sides move by the same amount.


The common mistake is reversing the sides. Cost incurred, so expense rises. Nobody paid yet, so liability rises.


Breaking "accrued liabilities" into specific accounts like Wages Payable, Interest Payable, and Utilities Payable gives cleaner tracking and makes it easier to match each balance against the underlying obligation at close.


## How to Record an Accrued Expense Journal Entry


Recording an accrued expense follows the same four step logic every time, no matter the account involved.


1. **Identify the expense and its period.** Confirm the cost was incurred before period end, even without an invoice.
2. **Estimate the amount.** Use historical bills, contract terms, or usage data for a reasonable figure.
3. **Record the adjusting entry.** Debit the expense account, credit the matching liability account, dated to the last day of the period.
4. **Document the calculation.** Attach the math behind the estimate so it holds up under audit.


Materiality matters. Per AccountingTools, immaterial accrued expenses may skip recording, since accruing every small, recurring cost keeps staff busy near period end. Setting thresholds also helps manage[variance accounting](https://doublehq.com/blog/variance-accounting-guide/) when estimates differ from actuals. Set a dollar threshold and skip anything below it.

## Journal Entry When an Accrued Expense Is Paid


When the invoice arrives and cash goes out, the accrual closes. The entry debits the accrued liability and credits cash, settling the balance instead of creating one. No expense account is touched, since the cost was recognized when the accrual was booked.


If the actual invoice differs from the estimate, adjust the gap against the expense account at payment. A $2,100 utility accrual settled by a $2,180 bill adds $80 to Utilities Expense, the kind of period-over-period shift that[flux analysis](https://doublehq.com/blog/flux-analysis-explained-complete-guide/) surfaces during review.


## Accrued Expense Reversal Journal Entry


Reversing an accrual matters because skipping it can double count the same cost, once as an estimate and once when the invoice lands. Post the reversal on the first day of the new period: debit the accrued liability and credit the expense account for the accrued amount.


QuickBooks and NetSuite let you flag entries to auto reverse on a set date, cutting the manual step, though[managing accruals manually in Excel](https://doublehq.com/blog/stop-managing-accruals-manually-excel/) remains a common pain point worth tackling on its own. Fixed recurring accruals, like a level lease payment, typically skip reversal since the same figure repeats each period.


## Accrued Income Journal Entry


Accrued income mirrors an accrued expense on the revenue side. Work gets delivered or interest accumulates, but no invoice or cash has moved yet, so the entry debits an accrued income asset account and credits revenue, keeping recognition in the correct period.


A $50,000 note earning 6% annually generates $750 in quarterly interest. The entry debits Interest Receivable $750 and credits Interest Income $750.


For services, a consulting firm delivering January work but invoicing in February debits Accrued Revenue $6,000 and credits Service Revenue $6,000. Once cash arrives, debit Cash and credit the accrued income account, clearing the receivable without touching revenue again.


## Accrued Expenses on the Balance Sheet


Accrued expenses sit under current liabilities on the balance sheet, since the obligation settles within twelve months. They appear either as their own line item, such as Accrued Wages or Accrued Interest, or rolled into a single Accrued Liabilities balance depending on chart of accounts granularity. A[month-end close checklist](https://doublehq.com/blog/month-end-close-checklist/) helps confirm all accruals are posted before financials finalize.


Before the adjusting entry, the trial balance understates liabilities and expenses, since the unbilled cost hasn't been recorded. Posting the accrual brings both into balance before financial statements finalize.


Accrued expenses are liabilities, never assets. The related asset-side counterpart, accrued income, sits in receivables instead.


## Common Mistakes When Recording Accrued Expenses


Five errors account for most of the distorted financials practitioners find during review.


- **Forgetting to reverse.** The estimate stays on the books after the real invoice posts, double counting the expense until someone catches the duplicate.
- **Wrong estimate, no true-up.** A stale amount gets booked and never gets corrected against the actual bill, so small variances quietly pile up across periods.
- **Wrong period.** An expense incurred in January gets booked in February because the invoice arrived late, pushing cost into a period it does not belong to.
- **Confusing accruals with accounts payable.** Booking an estimate to the AP account instead of a dedicated accrued liability account muddies which balances are confirmed.
- **Stale accruals.** An old accrual sits on the balance sheet long after the obligation settled, overstating liabilities.


The fix stays the same each time: match the accrual against the actual invoice every period and clear anything outdated before close.
