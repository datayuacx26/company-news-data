# How to Start a Microschool as a Teacher: From Teacher to Microschool Founder in 2026

If you’re thinking about starting a microschool, you’re probably clear on why you want to do it. What’s less clear is how. At KaiPod Learning, we’ve supported 150+ educators through this transition, and the path looks different for everyone. What most founders share, though, is the same starting point: a vision for something better, and a lot of questions about the practical side.[According to a 2025 RAND report](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA3698-1.html) , between 1 and 2 million U.S. students now attend microschools full-time, and the majority of founders come from education backgrounds. This guide covers what the transition actually involves, what skills most educators need to develop, and how to think about getting support.


## **Why Educators Choose to Start Microschools**


Many teachers came into the profession with a clear vision: classrooms where students are genuinely known, instruction that adapts to each individual, and relationships at the center of everything. Starting a microschool is often about finding a way to build that vision in full, rather than in pieces.


[According to the American Microschools 2025 report](https://22424203.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/22424203/American%20Microschools%202025.pdf) , over 70% of microschool founders have an education background, which means this is a path being led largely by people who came up through classrooms.


As Amar Kumar, KaiPod’s founder and CEO, shared in a[recent fireside chat with Michelle Rhee](https://youtu.be/HRVFiD5SdZE?si=BGbTVSsCztWXEEGT) : “There are so many educators across the country who are frustrated for different reasons with the bureaucracy of the school. They love the kids, they love teaching, but they’re tired of being in the system.”


You choose the curriculum, set the schedule, and decide how to work with families. You get to build something that reflects your philosophy of education.


Mercedes Grant, who went through our first KaiPod Catalyst cohort and now mentors other founders, explained her motivation in[this webinar](https://youtu.be/NUW1yj1jknY?si=sfKWV6SAvYIndkBR) : “I knew why I joined the education system. I wanted to help kids. I wanted to love kids. I wanted to make the world different. I wanted to create a space where students were successful… \[But\] my impact is so limited to 50 minutes a day inside my math classroom.”


That desire to expand impact and build something aligned with your values is what drives most of the founders we work with. That might mean focusing deeply on 10-15 students instead of managing 30 at a time,, or serving a community that traditional schools tend to overlook.


## **What Is a[Microschool](https://www.kaipodlearning.com/start-a-microschool/) ?**


A microschool is a small learning environment, typically serving 10-15 students with one educator or a small team. Each microschool can be designed around a specific educational philosophy, student population, or community need. They operate under a range of legal structures, including homeschool co-ops, private schools, and hybrid models, depending on state regulations.


For a deeper look at the different models and what distinguishes microschools from other educational options, see our full guide on[what is a microschool](https://www.kaipodlearning.com/what-is-a-microschool/) .


## **How to Know If You’re Ready**


Most educators thinking about this transition know their why long before they know their how. The free[Microschool Founder Decision Guide](https://www.kaipodlearning.com/microschool-decision-guide/) includes a scored self-assessment and planning worksheet designed to help you get clearer on both. Here are four areas worth sitting with first.


**Do you have a clear educational vision?** The guide asks you to rate how clearly you can articulate what makes education better, who you want to serve, and what philosophy will shape your school. Knowing what problem you’re solving for families in your community is what makes everything else actionable.


**Are you ready to take initiative and communicate your vision?** Building enrollment before you have anything polished requires showing up, telling your story, and inspiring trust in families who haven’t seen your school yet. This is often where founders feel the biggest gap, and it’s one of the most important things to think through early.


**Do you have an entrepreneurial mindset?** As[Mercedes Grant](https://youtu.be/NUW1yj1jknY?si=sfKWV6SAvYIndkBR) puts it: “I know how to educate kids. I’ve always been a phenomenal educator… but I had to figure out how to actually run a business and run a successful business.” The good news is these skills can be learned. As Amar Kumar noted, “Teachers, I think, oftentimes underestimate how many transferable skills they have. A lot of those characteristics that make a great teacher also make for a great entrepreneur.”


**Are you practically ready?** The guide asks about time availability, access to funding or personal resources, and whether your family situation supports this commitment. Many founders choose to reinvest revenue back into the school in year one rather than drawing a salary. Knowing that going in shapes how you plan.


## **The Skills Gap: What Teachers Don’t Learn in Education Programs**


You already bring curriculum design, lesson planning, classroom management, and family communication to this work. What most education programs don’t cover is the business side of running a school, and that’s the area where new founders most often feel unprepared.


The main areas you’ll develop:


- **Regulations and licensing:** Requirements vary significantly by state and structure. You may need to navigate private school licensing, daycare regulations, zoning requirements, or homeschool co-op rules. Connecting early with others who have opened schools in your area is worth the time.
- **Financial management:** As a founder, you’ll be thinking about budgets, tuition pricing, and cash flow in ways classroom teaching doesn’t ask of you. In some states, Education Savings Account (ESA) programs allow families to direct public funds toward alternative education options, though availability and eligibility vary considerably by state and are not guaranteed. Understanding your funding model early shapes everything else.
- **Marketing and enrollment:** This is often the biggest mindset shift. Telling your story, connecting with families, and building trust before your school opens looks different from anything most teachers have done before. Personal connection consistently outperforms advertising.
- **Business operations:** Thinking like a founder means making strategic decisions, setting goals, tracking expenses, and planning for contingencies. These are skills you’ll add to what you already do well.


## **Finding Your Focus, Building Your School, and Getting Support**


### **Identify your niche**


One of the most common patterns we see in early-stage founders is trying to appeal to every family, and it almost always makes things harder than they need to be. The microschools that gain real traction tend to start focused.


A clear focus makes your outreach more specific, helps families self-select based on fit, and lets you build genuine expertise in serving your particular community. That might mean focusing on gifted learners who need more challenge, students with learning differences who thrive with individualized attention, families seeking outdoor or nature-based education, a specific curriculum approach, or a particular age group or grade range.


### **What to expect in year one**


Most microschools start with 5-15 students and grow from there. The founders we see build something durable tend to share a few things in common. They can articulate clearly who they serve and why their approach matters. They treat their first enrolled families as partners, because those families become their best word-of-mouth. And they stay focused on delivering an excellent experience for the students in front of them rather than growing too quickly.


[Mercedes Grant](https://youtu.be/NUW1yj1jknY?si=sfKWV6SAvYIndkBR) started with modest intentions: “I intended for my micro school to be at my house alongside my daughter with maybe six other kids. That was my dream.” She now has over 70 students in her program, won a $250K Yass Prize grant, and is expanding to more locations.


### **Finding support**


You don’t have to figure this out alone. A growing number of training programs, networks, and founder communities now exist specifically to help educators make this transition. They vary considerably in their approach, what they ask of founders, and what kind of school you’d be building.


Some programs are model-specific, meaning you’d be adopting a particular curriculum or operating philosophy as part of joining. Others function more like franchises, where the school carries the network’s brand and structure rather than your own. If owning your school fully, including its name, philosophy, and curriculum, matters to you, it’s worth understanding those distinctions before choosing a program.

## **Is This Path Right for You?**


For educators who want more ownership over how they teach, who see a gap in what their community has access to, or who want to build something that reflects their values, this path offers something meaningful. There is real work involved, and the learning curve in year one is genuine. But that’s true of anything worth building.


As one microschool founder described it after returning to education: “This redesigned role has given me a greater purpose in supporting children, and the job is far more sustainable than prior teaching roles.”


With expanded school choice programs, growing family demand, and more support for founders than has existed before, more teachers are making this transition now. The skills and experience you already have are a real foundation. The rest can be learned.


## **Frequently Asked Questions**


### **How long does it take to start a microschool?**


Many founders who begin at the start of the year have enough time to open by the following back-to-school season (August/September). The timeline depends on your state’s requirements, your location, and how quickly you build enrollment.


### **Do I need a teaching credential to start a microschool?**


Requirements vary by state and how your school is structured. Some states require credentials; others don’t, especially if you operate under homeschool laws. KaiPod Catalyst walks founders through the specific requirements for their state.


### **How much does it cost to start a microschool?**


Startup costs vary widely depending on location, whether you rent space or use a home-based model, and equipment needs. Catalyst helps you develop a realistic budget for your specific situation.


### **Can I start a microschool while keeping my current teaching job?**


Most founders begin planning while still employed, but launching typically requires full commitment. Enrollment outreach in particular depends on consistent community presence.


### **What if I complete KaiPod Catalyst but decide not to open a microschool?**


If you complete Catalyst but ultimately decide that opening a microschool isn’t right for you, there’s no further financial obligation beyond the initial $249 program fee.


### **How do I find my first students?**


Early enrollment comes from personal relationships, not advertising. Show up at community events, have one-on-one conversations with families, and build trust before you have everything polished. Word-of-mouth is your most powerful tool.
