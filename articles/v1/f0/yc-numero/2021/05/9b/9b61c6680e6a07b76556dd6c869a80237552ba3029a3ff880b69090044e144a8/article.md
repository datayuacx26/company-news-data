# How to Run for Office in Florida

The state’s political landscape is more charged than ever—debates around affordable housing, reproductive rights, and climate resilience have taken center stage. Then there’s women’s rights, LGBTQ rights, climate change, and so on.


The urgency around these issues and others in post-2016 politics has inspired a whole new group of people to launch political careers. Maybe you’re one of them?


If so, we’re glad you’re here! We support your aspirations to run for office. We have put together this guide to answer all your first-time candidate questions—equipping you with the knowledge you need to operate a political campaign in your home state.

## **What are the politics in Florida like? How are people voting?**


As of the end of 2024, the government in Florida leans heavily Republican. Rick DeSantis is the Governor, and Republicans hold the majority in the state legislature. Traditionally, however, Florida has been a swing state.


Florida tends to be competitive for a few reasons. One is that the state has a diverse population since people move to Florida from around the continental United States, Puerto Rico, and Cuba. According to the *Washington Post* ,[Florida has six distinct political states](https://www.washingtonpost.com/graphics/2020/politics/florida-political-geography/) , or six different regions with their own socioeconomic and political leanings.


A breakdown of party affiliations across the state also shows why election results in Florida can be so close.


Florida has more than 14.5 million registered voters. Of these, 5.2 million voters are registered Democrats, 5.1 million are registered Republicans, and 4 million are registered as either minor or nonaffiliated parties.


(The Florida Division of Elections offers a handy table of[voter registration data by county](https://dos.myflorida.com/elections/data-statistics/voter-registration-statistics/voter-registration-reportsxlsx/voter-registration-by-county-and-party/) if you want to look further into the numbers.)


### **What did voting look like in 2020 and 2022?**


The[DailyKos reported](https://www.dailykos.com/stories/2021/1/15/2009489/-Trump-s-gains-with-Latinos-were-most-pronounced-in-three-heavily-Cuban-districts-in-South-Florida) that, despite Biden’s 2020 outperformance of Clinton’s 2016 numbers, Trump actually gained support in the state in 2020, especially within a pocket of Cuban communities in Southern Florida. In Florida, Republicans also flipped two U.S. House of Representatives seats in the 2020 elections.


In the 2022 midterms, Florida further cemented its red-leaning status. Governor Ron DeSantis won re-election significantly, which marked a shift from the narrow victories seen in prior statewide races. Additionally, Republicans made gains in the state legislature, expanding their majorities, and Senator Marco Rubio won re-election against Democratic challenger Val Demings.


### **What does this mean for upcoming elections?**


Notably, many independent voters account for almost as many votes as those affiliated with the two major parties. Independent voters tend to be more persuadable and less partisan than those who declare for one of the main parties. Why does this matter in Florida? Well, more voters identify as independent, which means this group of voters is likely to have more sway on election outcomes (they made up 23% of Obama voters in Florida in 2008).


Information about voter affiliations and the different political climates found within the state will help focus your campaign’s[GOTV efforts](https://www.numero.ai/blog/voter-engagement-gotv-field-events) .


## **What office can I run for?**


Figuring out what offices you can run for in a specific state can be tricky as political offices vary from state to state. Plus, the more local you get, the harder it is to learn what offices are elected positions.


Florida has a somewhat confusing but ultimately informative system that helps you determine what office to run for and identify who already holds that office. The Florida Division of Elections website provides[links to connect you to](https://www.dos.myflorida.com/elections/contacts/elected-officials/) the Governor and your U.S. representatives, U.S. senators, and state-level representatives.


### **Finding what local offices to run for**

### **What else should I know about the competition?**


Once you’ve found the office you want to run for and know who already holds that office, find out what their[campaign budget](https://www.numero.ai/blog/political-fundraising/) looks like. Remember, their campaign was successful, so the amount they raised while running for office indicates pretty accurately what kind of budget your campaign will need.


The Federal Election Commission (FEC) website is a great place to start looking for[finance information on competitor campaigns](https://www.fec.gov/data/browse-data/?tab=candidates) . Search by candidate to find the money raised and spent by the campaign. Information on a current candidate’s cash on hand and acquired debts is also available through this same search.


You can even[browse through individual contributions](https://www.fec.gov/data/receipts/individual-contributions/?two_year_transaction_period=2022&min_date=01%2F01%2F2021&max_date=12%2F31%2F2022) and[candidate receipts](https://www.fec.gov/data/receipts/?data_type=processed&two_year_transaction_period=2022&min_date=01%2F01%2F2021&max_date=12%2F31%2F2022) .


Why is this information helpful? Well, you can use it in two key ways: to see the kind of[campaign budget](https://www.numero.ai/blog/political-campaign-budget) you’ll need and to research potential donors.


## **How do I start my political campaign?**


Now that you’ve decided on the office you want to run for, it’s time to[set up your campaign](https://www.numero.ai/blog/how-to-start-a-political-campaign) .


The first three steps to do exactly that are detailed below.


### **Forming a Political Committee**


The very first step is registering as a political candidate. You’ll need to register before applying to appear on the ballot. Registering as a candidate also allows you to form a political committee, which signals to banks and vendors that you’re legitimate and a valid organization that’s ready to take contributions.


### **Getting ready to fundraise**


[Fundraising often begins well before the election cycle begins](https://www.numero.ai/blog/political-fundraising/) . This reality is especially true of larger, national campaigns, but even small campaigns for[local offices](https://www.numero.ai/blog/local-offices-to-run-for/) need to raise money in order to run.


When running a political campaign,[campaign finance laws](https://www.numero.ai/blog/campaign-finance-laws/) dictate how your fundraising takes shape, mainly through a series of laws with which you’ll need to stay in compliance. To start, you’ll need a few things before accepting any contributions to ensure you stay compliant with federal campaign finance laws:


1. **An**[Employer Identification Number (EIN)](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/political-organizations/employer-identification-number-political-organizations) **:** Political campaigns can get an EIN through the IRS. EINs are used for tax and financial reporting purposes.
2. **A banking account specific to your campaign:** All campaign finances should be kept separate from personal finances.
3. **A way to keep your fundraising efforts organized:** Companies like[Numero](https://www.numero.ai/) and[ActBlue](https://secure.actblue.com/) offer payment processing, allowing you to accept donor contributions.
4. **A donor management system:** Fundraising is time-consuming and has many moving parts. You’ll need to reach out to existing contacts, expand your list of contacts and reach out to them, and follow up with people you’ve contacted. You’ll also need to follow up with people you haven’t even managed to reach yet. Without a system, it can be overwhelming. Luckily,[Numero’s Calltime app](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/numero-calltime/id1545066954) can help you organize donor lists and coordinate fundraising efforts across your entire campaign staff from campaign manager to finance associate.

### **Getting on the ballot**


Florida’s Division of Elections has a page dedicated to information about[qualifying to run for office](https://dos.myflorida.com/elections/candidates-committees/qualifying/) . This page includes up-to-date information about important dates and necessary materials (such as the[Candidate Petition Form](https://files.floridados.gov/media/693291/dsde104.pdf) ). It also has the contact information for Florida’s Qualifying Office to help you find more information.


Running for a special election? Visit the Division of Elections[Special Elections](https://dos.myflorida.com/elections/for-voters/special-elections/) page.

You’ll need to keep a couple of things in mind with federal political campaign contribution limits:


1. Contribution limits per election refer to each type of election: primary, general, run-offs, and special elections. The limits are renewed for each type of election.
2. These limits are calculated for the 2021/2022 election cycles and will be adjusted for inflation in odd years.
