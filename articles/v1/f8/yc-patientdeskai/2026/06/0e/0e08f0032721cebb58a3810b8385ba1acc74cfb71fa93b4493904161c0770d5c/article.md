# EHR Systems in Dentistry: $21.2B Market & What's Next

Electronic health records have quietly become one of the most consequential technology decisions a dental practice can make. They shape how your team documents care, how patients experience your practice, how you bill, and increasingly, how AI tools plug into your workflows. Yet for many practice owners, EHR systems remain a source of frustration — clunky interfaces, interoperability dead ends, and mounting cybersecurity concerns.


The numbers tell a story of rapid growth and rising stakes. According to[IBISWorld's 2026 industry analysis](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-states/industry/electronic-health-records-systems/4172) , the U.S. Electronic Health Records Systems industry reached **$21.2 billion in revenue in 2026** , growing 5.0% in 2025 alone. ## Why Dental EHR Is Different From Medical EHR


### The Unique Complexity of Dental Records


It's tempting to assume that dental practices can simply adopt the same EHR frameworks used in medicine. In practice, that assumption creates real problems. Dental records involve a fundamentally different data structure — charting tooth-by-tooth conditions, periodontal measurements, radiographic findings, and procedure codes that don't map cleanly onto the ICD-10 and HL7 standards that power most medical EHR systems.


The American Dental Association addressed this directly in June 2025, filing comments with the FDA highlighting the unique challenges of standardizing and integrating electronic dental records into national research systems. As the ADA noted:


> "While medicine has made significant progress in using structured data from electronic health records, dentistry has unique challenges that make this more complicated." —[American Dental Association, ADA News, June 2025](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2025/june/integrating-electronic-dental-records-into-research-systems-complicated)


This isn't a minor technical footnote. It means that dental practices operating within larger health systems — or those hoping to contribute data to population health initiatives — face structural barriers that won't be resolved by simply upgrading software.


### Interoperability: The Persistent Gap


A[peer-reviewed systematic review published in the Journal of Clinical & Community Practice](https://jccpractice.com/article/electronic-health-records-in-dentristry-a-systematic-review-340) found that while EHR systems significantly improve clinical efficiency, patient data management, and interdisciplinary communication in dental settings, **interoperability limitations remain one of the most persistent challenges** . Dental EHR platforms often can't share data seamlessly with hospital systems, specialist offices, or pharmacy networks — creating friction in care coordination and limiting the value of the data practices collect.


For DSO operators managing multiple locations across different software platforms, this interoperability gap compounds quickly. A patient record created in one system may not transfer cleanly to another, creating duplicate data entry, documentation errors, and compliance risk.


### The Integrated EHR Advantage


Despite these challenges, the market is moving decisively toward integrated solutions. According to[Fortune Business Insights](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/electronic-health-records-ehr-market-102660) , the **integrated EHR segment is expected to account for 83.73% of the global EHR market in 2026** , driven by growing adoption of integrated software among healthcare providers. Integrated platforms — those that combine clinical documentation, billing, imaging, scheduling, and patient communication in a single environment — reduce the friction of managing multiple disconnected tools and create a more complete picture of each patient's care journey.

## Cybersecurity: The Growing Threat Landscape


### Why Dental Practices Are Targets


EHR systems store some of the most sensitive data a business can hold: protected health information, insurance details, Social Security numbers, and financial records. This makes dental practices attractive targets for cybercriminals — and the threat environment in 2026 is more sophisticated than ever.


According to[Schultz Technology's analysis of dental practice technology challenges in 2026](https://www.schultztechnology.com/blog/dental-practice-technology-challenges) , dental practices face **AI-powered phishing threats, unreliable network infrastructure causing EHR lag and imaging slowdowns** , and the pressure to adopt new digital tools while managing overhead costs. These technology challenges don't exist in isolation — they compound existing staffing and insurance pressures, creating a perfect storm for practices that haven't invested in robust IT infrastructure.


### What a Breach Actually Costs


A ransomware attack or data breach affecting a dental EHR system can result in HIPAA penalties, patient notification costs, reputational damage, and extended downtime that disrupts scheduling and billing. For a single-location practice, even a brief EHR outage can mean thousands of dollars in lost production per day.


The[systematic review in the Journal of Clinical & Community Practice](https://jccpractice.com/article/electronic-health-records-in-dentristry-a-systematic-review-340) specifically identified **cybersecurity threats as one of the persistent challenges** in dental EHR adoption — alongside interoperability limitations, financial constraints, and user resistance. Practices that treat cybersecurity as an afterthought rather than a core component of their EHR strategy are taking on significant financial and legal risk.


### Building a Defensible EHR Environment


Mitigating EHR-related cybersecurity risk requires a layered approach: regular software updates, multi-factor authentication, encrypted backups, staff training on phishing recognition, and a documented incident response plan. Cloud-based EHR platforms often provide stronger baseline security than on-premise systems, since vendors invest heavily in infrastructure security at scale. However, cloud migration doesn't eliminate risk — it shifts some responsibilities to the vendor while creating new ones around access control and third-party integrations.

Not all EHR systems are built for dentistry. General medical EHR platforms adapted for dental use often lack the specialty-specific features that dental workflows require: periodontal charting, tooth-by-tooth procedure coding, integration with digital radiography systems, and dental-specific billing workflows. When evaluating platforms, practices should prioritize systems built from the ground up for dentistry — or those with robust dental-specific modules.

## Key Takeaways for Dental Practice Owners and DSO Operators


The EHR landscape in 2026 is more dynamic than it's ever been. Here's what matters most for practice decision-makers:


- **The market is growing fast.** The U.S. EHR industry hit $21.2 billion in 2026, and the dental practice management software segment is on track to more than double by 2035. Investment in this space is accelerating, which means more innovation — but also more complexity in vendor selection.


- **Dental EHR has unique challenges.** Interoperability gaps, non-standard data structures, and the difficulty of integrating dental records into national health frameworks are real constraints. Choose platforms built specifically for dentistry, not adapted from medical systems.


- **AI integration is no longer optional.** AI-powered imaging analysis, predictive scheduling, and automated patient outreach are becoming standard features in leading dental EHR platforms. Practices that don't adopt these capabilities will fall behind on both clinical quality and operational efficiency.


- **Cybersecurity is a board-level issue.** AI-powered phishing, ransomware, and network vulnerabilities are active threats to dental practices in 2026. EHR security must be treated as a core operational priority, not an IT afterthought.


- **Integration ecosystem matters.** Your EHR is only as valuable as the tools it connects to. Prioritize platforms with robust integration ecosystems — and pair them with AI-powered patient engagement tools that extend EHR data into action.


- **Cloud is the direction of travel.** Cloud-based dental EHR platforms offer stronger security baselines, easier multi-location management, and faster access to new features. The migration from on-premise to cloud is well underway and accelerating.


The practices that will lead in 2026 and beyond are those treating their EHR as the intelligent core of a connected, AI-augmented practice — not just a place to store patient records. The technology is ready. The question is whether your practice is positioned to use it.
