# MVNO in a Box: The smartest way to launch a mobile service

From fintechs to airlines, HR platforms to smartwatches, more and more companies are embedding connectivity in their products. Described by some as an ‘MVNO in a Box’, these brands are launching premium mobile services fast, without the cost or complexity of building their own wireless infrastructure.


This article dives into the MVNO in a Box model, exploring why MVNOs are on the rise, how tech brands are redefining the telecom industry, and why it’s easier than ever before to launch a mobile service.


Let’s get started.


## **The rise of modern MVNOs**


First, let’s cover the basics. A Mobile Virtual Network Operator (MVNO) is a company that offers mobile services without owning the wireless infrastructure. Instead, MVNOs lease network access from major carriers (MNOs) and resell it under their own brand.


Historically, MVNOs focused on offering low-cost mobile plans, targeting price-sensitive customers with a cheap product. But today, the landscape is changing fast.


Leading brands like


[Nubank](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/28/more-neobanks-are-becoming-mobile-networks-and-nubank-wants-a-piece-of-the-action/) ,


[Revolut](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/02/12/revolut-launches-travel-esim-phone-plans-in-the-uk.html) , and


[Wealthsimple](https://gigs.com/press/wealthsimple-debuts-global-travel-esims-powered-by-gigs) are now offering world class mobile services embedded seamlessly in the platforms we use daily. Far from the “low cost, low quality” MVNO model, these brands provide:


-


**Exceptional mobile experiences:** High-speed data and nationwide 5G coverage bundled with great perks targeted to their audience.


-


**World-class UX:** Fully digital sign-ups, one-tap eSIM activation, and intuitive subscription management.


-


**Tailored plans** : Custom offerings that fit users’ needs, rather than rigid one-size-fits-all contracts.


By offering users a better way to stay connected at home and abroad, these brands aren’t just earning revenue, they’re building real customer love and lasting platform loyalty.


## **The old way: Why launching an MVNO was so difficult**


Before we uncover what’s driving the boom in


[embedded connectivity](https://gigs.com/blog/embedded-connectivity) services, it’s worth exploring how companies launched mobile services in the past.


Back in the day, delivering a telecom product was a massive undertaking, full of complexity and cost. Companies had to spend months negotiating contracts with


[MNOs](https://gigs.com/blog/mobile-network-operator-mno) , navigating complex integrations, building user interfaces from scratch, and tackling an intricate web of compliance regulations—before they even got their mobile service off the ground. It was a process that required substantial time, expertise, and upfront investment.


Here are just some of the factors businesses had to consider when launching an MVNO:


-


**Network access:** Negotiating contracts with multiple carriers across markets could take years, with no guarantee of competitive commercials or plan flexibility.


-


**Upfront commitments:** Large carriers demand millions of dollars in volume commitments paid upfront, meaning new MVNOs had to take on considerable risk.


-


**Integration with carriers:** Because legacy telecom systems aren’t designed for modern platforms, companies had to put hours of costly development work into building complex integrations.


-


**Billing, SIM-provisioning, and customer support** : Companies either had to partner with multiple


[MVNEs](https://gigs.com/blog/mobile-virtual-network-enabler-mvne) or spend millions of dollars developing solutions in-house.


-


**Compliance:** Telecom is a highly regulated industry, with laws differing across countries and states. MVNOs must adhere to local rules on consumer protection, data privacy, service standards, taxes, and fees, or face significant fines.


For years, these high barriers to entry dissuaded innovative companies from entering the telecom market, while ongoing operational costs made it difficult for those who tried to turn a profit.
