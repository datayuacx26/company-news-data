Yes, a person’s hair type can change over time. The sharper answer: what changes may be your curl behavior, strand thickness, density, oiliness, dryness, frizz, breakage, or the way your lengths hold shape—not always one neat switch from one “type” to another. Hair quality and type can change with time, and aging hair changes can be influenced by factors such as environment, general health, hormones, inflammation, genetics, grooming habits, stress, smoking, and diet.\[10\]


That distinction matters. A new wave pattern at the roots calls for a different read than straight, frayed ends after years of heat styling. OLAPLEX looks at the foundation first: what is happening to the hair fiber, where the change starts, and how your routine can support the hair you have now.


## **What “Hair Type Change” Can Actually Mean**


When people say their hair type changed, they may be talking about several different traits at once:


**What changed?** **What it may point to**


New curls, waves, or straighter roots


A shift connected to follicle behavior, age, hormones, or life stage


Hair feels finer or less dense


Changes in strand diameter, hair density, growth cycle, or breakage


Hair feels coarse, dry, or less silky


Lower oil production, dryness, damage, or buildup


Curls look frizzy or less defined


Damage, dryness, friction, split ends, or styling routine mismatch


Ends look thin, see-through, or uneven

Hair type and curl pattern are closely tied to follicle shape, and follicle shape can shift slightly as follicles change with age.\[10\] At the same time, scientific reviews describe hair as dynamic across life stages, with changes in diameter and pigmentation from childhood through puberty, adulthood, and older age.\[5\] In plain language: your hair is not frozen in time. Neither is your routine.


## **The Quick Diagnosis: Check New Growth Versus Ends**


Start with the simplest mirror test: compare your new growth to your mid-lengths and ends.


### **If the change starts at the roots**


Changes in new growth can suggest that the hair being produced now is different from the hair produced years ago. That can happen as follicles, strand diameter, oil production, density, or the hair cycle change over time. Hair type and texture can also be influenced by hormones, general health, and life stage, so the roots can tell a useful story.\[10\]


Look for cues like:


- New waves or curls at the root


while older lengths still hang straighter.


- Finer-feeling new growth


compared with older hair.


- Less volume at the scalp


that feels different from simple product buildup.


- Drier roots or scalp changes


that affect how hair behaves from the start.


### **If the change mostly shows up on the lengths and ends**


Changes concentrated from mid-lengths down often point to the hair shaft—the part of hair that has already grown out and lived through brushing, heat, color, sun, friction, and styling. Damaged hair is more fragile and tends to break, which can leave hair looking frizzy and less healthy.\[8\]


Look for cues like:


- Curls that spring at the roots but fall flat at the ends.


- A rough or straw-like feel


after heat styling, bleaching, coloring, or chemical services.


- Frizz that appears even when the weather is calm.


- Split ends, snapping, dullness, or uneven density


at the perimeter.


Fine hair may show damage as limpness or flyaways. Thick hair may show it as bulk without definition. Curly and coily hair may show it as dryness, tangles, frizz, or stretched-out ends. Straight and wavy hair may show it as dullness, roughness, or a shape that no longer falls cleanly. The goal is not to force your hair into an old category. It is to read what it is asking for now.


## **Why Age And Hormones Can Make Hair Feel Different**


Aging can change more than hair color. A scientific review of hair aging notes changes in growth, pigmentation, sebum production, fiber diameter, and density.\[4\] Another review notes that anagen—the active growth phase—can become shorter with aging, more hairs can remain in the resting phase, and hair density can decline with age.\[5\]


That can translate into changes you can feel and see:


- Hair may feel finer.


Individual strand diameter can decline with age, making hair feel less thick and more prone to damage.\[10\]


- Hair may feel drier or coarser.


Aging follicles can produce less sebum, the natural oil that helps keep hair from feeling dry and brittle.\[10\]


- Hair may look less dense.


Density changes can make a once-full shape feel lighter or less voluminous.\[5\]


- Hair may reflect light differently.


Changes in pigment, graying, dryness, and surface condition can affect shine and softness.\[4\]


Hormones can also play a role, but this is where precision matters. Menopause and related hormone-level changes can affect the hair cycle and common hair conditions.\[6\] The American Academy of Dermatology also notes that menopause can bring noticeable hair changes as hormone levels fall.\[7\] Some research suggests estrogen is involved in hair growth, with hair diameter changes observed around pregnancy and the perimenopausal transition, but that should be framed carefully—not as a guarantee that hormones will change everyone’s curl pattern.\[5\]


So, can hormones change hair texture? They may contribute to changes in hair amount, growth behavior, dryness, and perceived texture. But a texture change alone does not diagnose a hormone issue, and products should not be framed as changing hormones, genetics, or follicle biology. If you are concerned about thinning or hair loss, or your routine changes are not helping, seeing a dermatologist is recommended.\[8\]


## **How Damage Can Mimic A New Hair Type**


Damage can make hair act like it has become a different type because damage changes the condition of the hair shaft. Hair that used to curl may look limp at the ends. Hair that used to feel smooth may feel rough. Thick hair may feel puffy instead of polished. Fine hair may seem even finer because breakage removes length and density.


Common damage triggers include:


- Frequent heat styling or high heat settings.


- Bleach, color, relaxers, perms, or other chemical services.


- UV and environmental exposure.


- Friction from towels, pillows, hats, or tight hairstyles.


- Rough brushing, detangling, or stretching when hair is vulnerable.


Excessive heat can damage hair regardless of hair type, so limiting blow-drying and hot tools and using lower settings is a smart routine move.\[9\] If damaging practices continue, hair may eventually appear thinner or develop more serious breakage patterns.\[8\]

## **FAQ**


### **Can Straight Hair Become Curly Or Curly Hair Become Straighter Over Time?**


Yes, some people notice curl-behavior changes over time. Hair type and curl pattern are connected to follicle shape, and follicle changes with age can slightly affect that shape.\[10\] But not every curl change starts at the follicle. If new growth is curling differently, age, hormones, or life stage may be part of the picture. If only the ends look straighter, frizzier, or less defined, damage is a more likely suspect.


### **Can Hormones Change Hair Texture?**


Hormone shifts can contribute to changes in the hair cycle, hair amount, dryness, and perceived texture. Menopause and related hormone changes have been associated with effects on the hair cycle and common hair conditions.\[6\] Still, hormones should not be treated as a guaranteed explanation for curl-pattern changes. If hair changes are sudden, heavy shedding appears, or thinning concerns you, a dermatologist can help identify what is really happening.\[8\]


### **Can Damaged Hair Look Like A Different Hair Type?**


Yes. Damage can make hair look and feel like a different type because it affects the hair shaft. Fragile hair tends to break, and breakage can create frizz and an unhealthy-looking finish.\[8\] Curly hair may lose definition. Fine hair may look thinner. Thick hair may feel rougher or puffier. Straight or wavy hair may look dull, uneven, or harder to smooth.


### **Can Products Change My Natural Hair Type?**


No cosmetic product should be framed as changing your genetics, follicle shape, hormones, or natural hair type. Products can support the condition of the hair fiber—strength, softness, hydration, shine, smoothness, manageability, and damage repair—within their supported claims. That is the smart line: care can help hair look and feel better, but it does not rewrite the biology that creates new growth.


## **The Bottom Line**


Hair can change over time, and the reason is not always one thing. Age can affect growth, pigment, density, oil production, and strand diameter.\[4\] Hormone shifts may influence hair cycles and perceived texture.\[6\] Damage can make the lengths look frizzy, weak, dull, broken, or less defined.\[8\] Your best first move is simple and powerful: check whether the change starts at the roots or lives mostly on the ends, then build a routine for the hair in front of you.
