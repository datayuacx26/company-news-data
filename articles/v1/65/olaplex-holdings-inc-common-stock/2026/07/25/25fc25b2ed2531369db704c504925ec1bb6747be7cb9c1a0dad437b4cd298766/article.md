# Olaplex guide — when and how to use conditioner: roots vs mid-lengths

Conditioner placement is the difference between polished lengths and roots that feel like they quit early. The short version: for a standard rinse-out conditioner, focus on the mid-lengths and ends. Your scalp and roots are usually the shampoo zone; your lengths are where conditioner does its best work.


That does not mean conditioner can never go higher. Product directions, hair type, scalp-specific formulas, co-washes, and stylist guidance can all change the move. But if you are building a classic shampoo-and-conditioner routine at home, start with this OLAPLEX-clear rule: cleanse at the scalp, condition through the lengths, give the ends extra attention.


## **The Short Answer: Where Conditioner Goes**


For most standard rinse-out conditioners, apply conditioner from the mid-lengths to the ends, then rinse well. Long hair can often be conditioned from around chin level downward, with extra focus on the ends rather than the scalp.\[4\]


Why skip the roots most of the time? Roots are closest to the scalp’s natural oils, while ends are farther away and more exposed to daily wear, tangling, heat styling, brushing, and color or chemical services. Dermatology guidance also separates shampoo and conditioner placement by role: shampoo is focused on the scalp, while conditioner placement changes by hair type, with fine or straight hair generally focusing on the ends and dry or curly hair often conditioning more of the length.\[6\]


The exception is simple: follow the label. If a product is designed for the scalp, a co-wash routine, a leave-in format, or a stylist-directed treatment, the application area may be different. For a regular rinse-out conditioner, though, mid-lengths and ends are the power zone.

A clean routine has a clean division of labor. Shampoo is for the scalp and roots; conditioner is for the hair shaft, especially the lengths and ends. Conditioner helps moisturize, detangle, and make hair easier to manage after washing.\[6\]


There is also a science reason placement matters. Conditioners act on the hair shaft surface: they can reduce friction and static between hair fibers and improve ease of combing.\[3\] Other hair-care research describes conditioners as improving softness, gloss, frizz, manageability, and combing by working on the hair fiber surface rather than biologically renewing living scalp tissue.\[2\]


Translation: conditioner is built for the strand. Put it where the strand needs polish, slip, and support.


## **How To Build The Routine Step By Step**


Here is the routine to use when you want clean roots and conditioned lengths.


1. Shampoo the scalp and roots.


Massage shampoo at the scalp, where oil, sweat, and styling-product buildup collect. Rinse thoroughly before conditioning. A standard rinse-out conditioner routine is shampoo first, rinse shampoo, then condition.\[4\]


2. Let excess water run off.


Hair should be wet, not streaming. Gently squeeze the lengths so conditioner lands where you place it instead of sliding straight down the drain.


3. Apply conditioner to mid-lengths and ends.


Start lower than you think if your hair gets oily or flat easily. For longer hair, chin level down is a smart starting point.\[4\]


4. Detangle gently.


Work conditioner through the ends with your fingers or a wide-tooth comb, moving slowly and avoiding aggressive pulling.\[4\]


5. Leave it on for the label time.


Conditioner is not a guess-and-hope step. The product’s directions control timing.


6. Rinse well.


Thorough rinsing helps avoid a greasy, weighed-down, or coated feel; not rinsing fully is a common conditioner mistake.\[7\]

## **How Hair Type Changes Placement**


The best conditioner placement is not one-size-fits-all. Use your hair type and how your roots behave as the filter.


### **Fine, Straight, or Oily-Prone Hair**


If your hair gets flat quickly, keep conditioner lower. Apply a smaller amount through the ends, then lightly through the mid-lengths if needed. Dermatology guidance supports applying conditioner to the ends for fine or straight hair.\[6\]


This is where the “don’t condition your roots” advice comes from, but make it practical rather than extreme. You are not banned from ever touching the upper lengths. You are simply avoiding heavy placement at the scalp when your roots already have enough oil or your style needs lift.


### **Dry, Curly, Thick, or Coarse Hair**


Dry or curly hair may need conditioner through more of the length rather than only the ends.\[6\] Thick or coarse textures may also need more even distribution through the mid-lengths for slip and manageability.


Still, keep the label in charge. Unless the product is designed for scalp use, start through the lengths and avoid massaging regular conditioner directly into the scalp.


### **Oily Roots With Dry Ends**


This is one of the most common routine mismatches. Treat each zone differently: shampoo the scalp and roots, then condition the mid-lengths and ends only. Do not punish dry ends because the scalp gets oily. Separate the jobs and the routine gets smarter fast.

## **Common Conditioner Mistakes To Avoid**


Even a strong conditioner can underperform if the placement is off. Skip these routine traps.


- Applying from root to tip by default.


Applying conditioner from root to tip is a common mistake; for most routines, concentrate it where it is needed most: the lengths and ends.\[7\]


- Using too much at the scalp.


Applying conditioner to the scalp can weigh hair down and make roots appear greasy.\[7\]


- Skipping the ends.


Ends are the area most likely to feel dry, rough, tangled, or worn, so give them the most attention.


- Rinsing immediately when the label calls for a wait.


If the product says to leave it on for a set time, respect the timing. For Nº.5, that timing is 1–3 minutes.\[1\]


- Not rinsing thoroughly.


Rinse until the hair feels conditioned but not coated. Incomplete rinsing can contribute to buildup and a greasy or weighed-down feel.\[7\]


- Treating every conditioner the same.


Rinse-out conditioner, deep conditioner, leave-in conditioner, scalp products, and co-washes can have different instructions. The label wins.

## **FAQ**


### **Can I Put Conditioner On My Roots?**


Usually, skip the roots with a standard rinse-out conditioner, especially if your hair gets oily, flat, or weighed down easily. Roots are typically the shampoo zone, while conditioner belongs through the mid-lengths and ends. The exception: if the product label says it is meant for scalp use, a stylist recommends it, or your specific routine calls for it.


### **Should Conditioner Go On Soaking-Wet Or Damp Hair?**


Apply rinse-out conditioner after shampooing and rinsing. Your hair should be wet, but it helps to squeeze out excess dripping water first so conditioner stays concentrated through the lengths instead of being diluted immediately. If your product gives specific directions, follow those directions.


### **How Long Should I Leave Conditioner In?**


Follow the product label. Many rinse-out conditioners are designed for a short wait time, while masks and deep conditioners may require different timing. For Nº.5 Bond Maintenance® Conditioner, leave it on for 1–3 minutes, then rinse well.\[1\]


### **What If I Have Oily Roots And Dry Ends?**


Split the routine by zone. Shampoo the scalp and roots; condition the mid-lengths and ends. This lets you keep the roots fresh without leaving the ends under-conditioned.
