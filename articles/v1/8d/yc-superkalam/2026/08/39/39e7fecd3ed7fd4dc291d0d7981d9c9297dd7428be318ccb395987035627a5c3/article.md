## Context


***Fig:** Meghalaya's landscape faces ongoing friction between customary tribal land governance under the Sixth Schedule and environmental protection mandates.*


Rat-hole coal mining in Meghalaya persists despite judicial bans. Unique constitutional protections under the Sixth Schedule grant customary land and subsoil mineral ownership to tribal communities rather than the sovereign state. This arrangement creates regulatory friction: local autonomous authorities lack technical enforcement capacity, yet they rely heavily on mineral royalties.


This structural gap allows informal extraction to endure across vulnerable terrain. Thin, irregular coal seams render industrial mechanisation commercially unviable.


Key regulatory milestones include:


- **2014 NGT Ban:** The National Green Tribunal halted rat-hole mining over severe environmental degradation and safety risks.
- **2019 Supreme Court Ruling:** The apex court reaffirmed the ban while setting statutory conditions under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957.
- **Ongoing Oversight:** The Justice B.P. Katakey Committee highlights that unsealed shafts, Acid Mine Drainage, and illegal transit remain pressing governance hurdles.


Sustainable resolution demands reconciling constitutional tribal autonomy with mandatory federal environmental standards.


## Why the Ban on Rat-Hole Mining is in the News


The Meghalaya High Court observed in PIL No. 2 of 2022 that law enforcement agencies were failing to fix accountability on recorded landowners hosting illegal operations. As of April 2026, judicial scrutiny over illegal coal extraction across the State of Meghalaya has intensified.


The 39th Interim Report of the Justice B.P. Katakey Committee highlighted critical monitoring findings:


- Unsealed Shafts: An estimated 26,000 unclosed rat-hole mine shafts remain unsealed across the region, creating persistent drowning and collapse hazards.
- Missing Inventory: A discrepancy of 28,369.44 metric tonnes of coal missing from designated storage depots requires formal explanation from state mining authorities.


These findings show that unregulated extraction continues despite repeated prohibitions by the Supreme Court of India and the National Green Tribunal.


## What is Rat-Hole Mining and How Does It Work?


Rat-hole mining is a manual coal extraction method practiced in Meghalaya where deep vertical or horizontal pits give way to narrow subterranean tunnels. Workers first sink vertical shafts ranging from 100 to 400 feet deep into the earth. They then excavate horizontal tunnels barely 2 to 4 feet high along thin coal seams. Because spatial dimensions inside these tunnels are extremely constricted, physical navigation is hazardous and arduous.


Extraction generally follows two distinct structural methods based on local topographical conditions.

## Background of the National Green Tribunal Ban


The National Green Tribunal instituted a complete interim ban on rat-hole mining across Meghalaya on April 17, 2014, in the All Dimasa Students Union case. The judicial intervention responded to widespread environmental destruction and recurring fatal disasters.


Key directives and findings under Original Application No. 73 of 2014 included:


- Extraction and Transit Ban: The order prohibited both active coal extraction and the illegal transportation of mined coal throughout the state.
- Unscientific Operations: The bench found that the practice was entirely unscientific and unregulated.
- Fatal Shaft Flooding: Monsoon rainwater frequently caused fatal inundations, trapping workers inside flooded subterranean shafts without emergency escape mechanisms.


## The Sixth Schedule and Autonomous District Councils: Ownership vs Regulation


The Sixth Schedule of the Constitution of India governs Meghalaya's tribal areas under Article 244(2) and Article 275(1) to protect customary land rights. In most Indian states, subsoil minerals vest in the sovereign government under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957. However, private and tribal landholders in Meghalaya retain customary ownership over both surface land and subsoil minerals.


This legal framework creates a complex governance dynamic:


- **Customary Ownership:** Tribal landholders retain rights over surface land and subterranean minerals.
- **Land Allotment Powers:** Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) hold legislative authority under Paragraph 3 of the Sixth Schedule, yet lack technical mining oversight mechanisms.
- **Royalty Sharing:** Under Paragraph 9 of the Sixth Schedule, ADCs receive a share of mineral royalties collected by the state government.


This financial reliance on royalty revenue creates an institutional disincentive for ADCs to enforce strict extraction limits within their tribal jurisdictions.

## Economic Realities and Livelihoods in Meghalaya


Commercial coal extraction expanded rapidly across the Jaintia and Garo Hills during the 1980s, creating an extensive informal political economy. Coal deposits occur in thin, interstratified, and irregular seams that make industrial longwall or opencast mining commercially unviable. Consequently, mine operators rely heavily on low-cost manual rat-hole operations.


Key drivers and structural impacts of this informal economy include:


- **Political Influence:** High profitability allowed prominent mine owners to gain significant political representation within the state legislature, shaping policy enforcement.
- **Human Rights Vulnerabilities:** The narrow 2-to-3-foot horizontal shafts encourage child labour and child trafficking, as smaller frames navigate subterranean tunnels more easily.
- **Downstream Demand:** Audits submitted to the Katakey Committee indicate unverified coal continues to supply coke oven plants, ferro-alloy plants, and cement factories across the state.

## Environmental and Safety Costs of Illegal Mining


Meghalaya coal contains high concentrations of pyritic sulfur ranging between 2% and 8% total sulfur content, driving severe environmental degradation. When exposed to air and water, pyritic sulfur oxidises to form sulfuric acid through Acid Mine Drainage (AMD).


The major environmental and safety impacts include:


- **Water Acidification:** AMD from unscientific stockpiles has reduced water pH levels to 2–3 in major rivers such as the Kopili, Lukha, and Myntdu, rendering water toxic for drinking and irrigation.
- **Ecological Destruction:** Acidic runoff flowing downstream into Assam's Dima Hasao district triggers massive fish mortality and collapses regional aquatic ecosystems.
- **Hazardous Openings:** The lack of a comprehensive mine closure policy leaves an estimated 26,000 unclosed rat-hole mine shafts across the landscape, posing ongoing drowning and collapse risks.

## The Way Forward for Sustainable Resource Governance


The Supreme Court of India established a legal framework for sustainable mining in its 2019 State of Meghalaya judgment. On July 3, 2019, the apex court held that unscientific rat-hole mining is illegal. However, it clarified that coal extraction on private or customary land is permissible if operators comply with statutory federal mining laws.


The Supreme Court framework mandates the following directives:


- **Statutory Clearances:** Future mining requires explicit leases and environmental clearances under the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 and the Mines Act, 1952.
- **Restoration Fund:** Meghalaya was ordered to transfer Rs 100 crore from the Meghalaya Environment Protection and Restoration Fund to the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB).
- **Auctioning Stockpiles:** All illegally extracted coal stockpiles must be handed over to Coal India Limited for inventorying and public auctioning.


On March 24, 2022, the state government notified the **Meghalaya Minerals (Prevention of Illegal Mining, Transportation and Storage) Rules, 2022** to strengthen regulatory oversight against illegal transit. Achieving long-term compliance requires a multi-pronged strategy. State authorities must equip Autonomous District Councils with technical monitoring tools, enforce a strict mine closure policy, and foster sustainable alternative livelihoods.


## Key Takeaways


- **Mechanics of Extraction:** Rat-hole mining relies on sinking deep vertical shafts (100–400 feet) and excavating narrow horizontal tunnels (2–4 feet) along thin coal seams.
- **Judicial Ban Rationale:** The National Green Tribunal banned rat-hole mining in 2014 due to severe environmental degradation, water pollution, and fatal shaft flooding hazards.
- **Constitutional Conflict:** Article 244(2) and the Sixth Schedule preserve customary land and subsoil rights, while Paragraph 9 royalty revenue creates institutional disincentives for Autonomous District Councils to restrict illegal extraction.
- **Ecological Destruction:** High pyritic sulfur (2%–8%) causes Acid Mine Drainage, lowering river pH levels to 2–3 in the Lukha, Kopili, and Myntdu rivers.
- **Statutory Path Forward:** The Supreme Court's 2019 ruling permits mining on customary land only under strict compliance with the MMDR Act, 1957 and Mines Act, 1952, backed by CPCB restoration funds and state transit rules.
