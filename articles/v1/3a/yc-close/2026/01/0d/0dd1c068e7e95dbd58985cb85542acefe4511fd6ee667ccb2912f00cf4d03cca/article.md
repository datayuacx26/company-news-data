# A Practical Guide to Not Lighting Your Sales Budget on Fire With AI

AI tools are everywhere right now. And that’s great…when they’re helpful.


But what happens when “[AI](https://www.close.com/blog/sales-teams-ai-skills) ” just becomes shorthand for “dartboard we like to throw money at?” That’s when you start to feel like your business needs more tools, *any* tools, just to keep up.


Enter FOMO: fear of missing out. Rather than slow down and deliberate on an AI strategy, many sales teams just pile tools on top of tools. Soon, AI “solutions” become AI problems:


Bloated tech stacks, overlapping subscriptions, nonsensical workflows.[And wasn’t AI supposed to simplify our lives](https://www.close.com/blog/ai-everyday-tools) ?


To prevent these problems, you need a practical framework for being more deliberate about how you choose AI tools. That way, you can avoid overspending on them—and pick up the tools that feel like the perfect fit for your sales team. We’ve got a few ideas to help you along.


## Start With the Problem, Not the Tool


The challenge here is that for many sales teams,[AI is a solution in search of a problem](https://www.close.com/blog/ai-voice-agent-build-vs-buy) . No bueno. What you need is a *problem* for which an AI tool is designed as a clear *solution* .


Otherwise, you’ll just be fixing what isn’t broken.


With AI tools, the strongest use cases tend to involve work that follows a particular set of patterns:


- Repetitive, time-consuming work, like data entry
- Work with well-defined inputs and outputs, like lead scoring
- Tasks that fit into an existing workflow, like automatically assigning leads within a CRM


In other words, you need a job to do *before* you acquire the tool. Don’t invest in the hammers until you’re sure you’ve got a lot of nails to pound in.


[If AI is so great](https://www.close.com/blog/ai-for-sales-glossary) , why not just throw money at it in hopes it will all work out? Because your ROI will depend on your needs.[According to McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/the-economic-potential-of-generative-ai-the-next-productivity-frontier) , for example, one company with lots of customer service to automate was able to build out 5,000 AI agents. It led to tangible results: AI increased issue resolution by 14% per hour.


But what was really enlightening about the company’s experience was the tool-specific improvements:


- AI helped identify issue resolution during initial contacts much faster
- AI reduced the overall response time for incoming customers
- AI also helped customer support increase sales


Because so many of its workflows went through customer support, scaling to 5,000 customer service agents made sense.


But what about *your* business? Maybe you’re not worried about 5,000 customers reaching out every week.


Look around in your existing workflows. Workflows that depend on software are prime candidates:


- Lead routing
- Follow-up email drafting/scheduling
- Data cleanup
- Implementing customer research into[CRM workflows](https://www.close.com/blog/how-to-use-close-forms-with-workflows)


If it’s a w[orkflow that fits inside your CRM](https://www.close.com/blog/automate-follow-ups-with-close-workflows) , it’s a candidate for[AI automation](https://www.close.com/blog/difference-between-ai-automation-and-machine-learning) . That makes it a candidate for generating instant ROI when you implement it, preventing frustration and the feeling that you just plopped down a lot of money for a tool with only marginal benefits.


What if you *don’t* have jobs like those ready to go? Hit “pause.” Try to let the feelings of FOMO wash right over you. Because if you don’t, throwing money at AI tools can only introduce *more* friction and *more* decisions, eventually adding to the frustration of getting work done.


If your workflows are still unclear, the safest move right now is not to buy a tool at all.


## Get Your House in Order Before You Buy AI


Don’t be fooled by the phrase “artificial intelligence.” It’s not yet “artificial wisdom” or “artificial creative thinking.”[AI isn’t a catch-all tool just yet](https://www.close.com/blog/best-practices-training-ai-sales-agent) , and it’s not going to fix your broken workflows for you.


AI systems are essentially algorithms. And they only *inherit* the quality of the data and the workflows you feed them. If you have messy CRM data or inconsistent workflows, AI might not have clear answers for you.


This is the kind of scenario that makes business leaders say: “AI? Tried that. Overrated.”


The thing is, it’s *not* overrated. If you can overcome these challenges and give it clean data and predictable workflows, you’ll be amazed at how quickly it works. You just need clarity in your processes before you automate.


Take the company above. They invested in 5,000[AI customer service agents](https://www.close.com/blog/custom-ai-tool-for-customer-support) , but they already had systems in place to measure how quickly they were responding to customer queries. Those agents fit into a preexisting customer service workflow.


That’s what you need before you invest. Don’t build the skyscraper without digging the foundation first.


## Match the Job to the Right AI Category


Many business leaders evaluate software purchases based on the vendor. “Well, so-and-so has a good reputation. Let’s get a subscription.”


That might not be a great approach with AI tools.


AI tools make more sense when you evaluate them by *category* , not because you like a particular vendor. Common categories include:


- Content generation tools
- [Workflow automation](https://www.close.com/blog/what-is-n8n-sales-automation)
- Decision support
- Data hygiene and analytics
- Customer interaction


Some AI tools fit multiple categories. And your needs are multiple, too.


But ideally, you’ll know which *category* you need most, like a[CRM](https://www.close.com/ai-crm) . This will narrow the field of potential tools.


And more importantly, it will prevent your team from getting distracted with add-on features that bloat the tech stack without making a meaningful impact.


## Define ROI Before the Demo


You should get a feel for which AI tools are a fit. But you still need one thing:


A mindset shift.


Ask yourself: *how can we hold both ourselves and the tool accountable* ? This sets your ROI standards before you opt for a demo or a free trial.


To hold the tool accountable for future evaluation, focus on a few variables:


- **Who owns the outcome?** Which team is getting assigned the AI tool? Which will use it the most? They’ll have the most critical feedback on how well it performs.
- **When will you review the results?** Think about the sample size. If you have 5,000 customer interactions a day, that sample size grows quickly. If you automate a workflow that only triggers once every couple of days, you might need a longer period.
- **How do you measure success?** Can you track time saved? Money saved? Or is it simply a “feel” in which you interview your sales reps and ask them if they feel like the AI tool is helping them or not?


Set your metrics now, because that will put you ahead of the 66% of companies that struggle to define ROI before they[unleash their AI initiatives](https://www.fullview.io/blog/ai-statistics) .


(Oh, and by the way: 42% of projects get abandoned due to implementation complexity. Another case for keeping your tools simple.)


Whether you define your ROI in terms of revenue gained, costs reduced, time saved, or bottles of Excedrin still full, you need to know whatyou’re trying to accomplish first.


When you’re looking at a potential AI tool, you’ll see a lot of buttons: *sign up for a demo* . So you do. It’s a good idea, too, as you evaluate tools.


But remember that these demos are designed to impress you.


They don’t address your specific business needs. They don’t frame your ROI for you. They don’t (always) tell you which metrics to watch. You’re the one who has to set these standards in advance.


## How to Make the Decision Before You Commit to AI


### Evaluate AI Tools Like You’d Hire a Teammate


Buying AI software is less like installing an add-on feature than it is hiring a “digital coworker.” Before you buy, write down three key variables:


- What the tool “owns” (which process? Which metric?)
- What the tool isn’t responsible for
- What success looks like (ROI, revenue generated, time saved, etc.)


Like actual hires, your AI's ability to onboard also matters. You don’t want a tool to get you stuck in weeks of setup and fine-tuning. (Just like a hire who takes too long to figure out what you’re doing is potentially a red flag.)


And don’t forget to factor in the cost of “babysitting” the tool. Someone on your team will have to implement it, check its outputs, and correct any[mistakes](https://www.close.com/blog/sales-mistakes) . That adds to the time cost, which will affect your ROI if your key variable is “time saved.”


### Run Small, Time-Boxed Experiments


You don’t have to do everything at once. In fact, it’s better if you don’t.


AI tools are easiest to evaluate when you give them small, minimalist experiments to pass first. Call it a “pilot program.” Focus on one team or a straightforward workflow, rather than throwing it in the deep end and seeing if the “intelligence” can keep up.


Give it two to four weeks. That’s usually enough time to see if your experiment is actually saving time or improving outcomes.


But one little tip: *do* define your criteria for success before you start this test.


### Prevent Tool Sprawl Before It Starts


The simple fact is that the best AI tools *reduce* the burden on your decision-making. They shouldn’t create more.


But “AI tool sprawl” sets in when you throw more and more tools at the many decisions of your workflows. Over time, this sprawl creeps in, and teams thinking “just one more tool” will solve their problems.


Then they’re surprised when tools overlap. (Yeah, shocker.)


To prevent tool sprawl, give yourself a basic working document to track all of the things you’d *like* to automate, then assign:


- What the tool does
- Which workflow the tool supports
- Who “owns” the tool internally
- What it costs
- The ROI and how you measure it


## A Simple Framework to Say “No” to Shiny Objects


And now, what you’ve been waiting for: permission to say “no” to your FOMO.


How can you be sure you’re not missing out? Here’s a framework we like that can help you say no to the shiny objects of AI tools that won’t help you:


- **Remember, cool demos aren’t cool ROI.** Not every AI tool deserves a place in your tech stack just because it *seems* fancy. It’s only worth it if it delivers real results.
- **Bias towards “quiet effectiveness.”** We expect AI to be impressive and spectacular. Instead, it works best when it’s just quietly humming and slicing through our old, boring, manual, repetitive tasks.
- **Treat AI as a productivity expander, not a panacea.** Evaluate it in terms of *improvements* , not total fixes. Does it *shorten* your follow-up time, rather than completely outsource your human emails? Does it automatically assign leads, but still let humans deal with them? Don’t expect it to do everything just yet.
- [Run small experiments](https://www.close.com/blog/ai-for-small-business) **.** You never know if a tool truly fits until you’re actually hammering in those nails.
