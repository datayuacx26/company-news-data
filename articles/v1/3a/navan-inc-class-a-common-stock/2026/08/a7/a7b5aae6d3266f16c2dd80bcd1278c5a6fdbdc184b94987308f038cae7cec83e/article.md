# What is expense management? A practical guide for finance leaders

Finance teams are less concerned with whether or not employees bought lunch. They are more concerned that any lunches bought were policy-compliant, properly coded, reconciled against the card statement, and sitting in the general ledger before leadership asks about burn rate. Expense management is the discipline and the tooling that connects those dots, and this guide covers the process, the technology, the challenges, and much more.


## What is expense management?


Expense management is the end-to-end process organizations use to track, control, approve, reimburse, and reconcile employee-initiated business spending.\[2\] It includes setting spending policies, capturing receipts, routing approvals, auditing transactions, issuing reimbursements, and syncing data with accounting systems.


Today expense management functions as a closed-loop spend control system. Policy is enforced at the point of purchase, with an automated audit before approval, and reconciliation happens before month-end. For example, if travel bookings, corporate card charges, and expense reports live in separate systems, finance teams reconcile three data streams that don't always match up.


Organizations generally start evaluating the[best expense management software](https://navan.com/blog/best-expense-management-software) options once they outgrow spreadsheets and email approvals. It's important to understand the complete process before selecting a tool.


## What counts as a business expense?


According to the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), business expenses are the costs of carrying on a trade or business that are ordinarily deductible when the business operates for profit.\[3\] In practice, company policies cover a broader set of day-to-day purchases that employees make on the company's behalf.


These kind of common, reimbursable categories include things like:


- **Travel:** Airfare, hotels, ground transportation, and mileage for business trips
- **Meals and client entertainment:** Team lunches, client dinners, and event sponsorships within policy limits
- **Operations:** Office supplies, software subscriptions, and professional development
- **Remote work:** Home internet stipends and equipment allowances where policy permits


Policies also define what is excluded, such as personal upgrades, commuting between home and a primary office, and expenses without business purpose documentation. Having clear category rules reduces disputes at approval and protects accountable plan status for tax-advantaged reimbursement.\[3\]


## What are the compliance and tax requirements for expense management?


Expense management isn't only an operational workflow. It's a compliance system that determines whether reimbursements stay tax-advantaged and audit-ready.


Under the rules of Internal Revenue Service (IRS) accountable plan, reimbursements must have a documented business purpose, be substantiated with receipts, and be submitted within a reasonable timeframe — or they may be treated as taxable wages.\[9\] Therefore, finance teams need centralized records that tie each transaction to policy, the relevant approver, and the accounting code.


The compliance requirements that finance leaders watch closely include:


- **Substantiation:** Itemized receipts above policy thresholds, business purpose on every line item
- **Timely submission:** Deadlines that support month-end close and accountable plan rules
- **Record retention:** Documentation stored for audit periods your jurisdiction requires
- **Policy consistency:** The same rules applied across roles, including executive travel
- **Segregation of duties:** Submitters, approvers, and auditors are different people for high-risk transactions


Public and pre-IPO companies face additional Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) expectations around approval trails and access controls.\[2\] Manual spreadsheets rarely satisfy auditors who need to be able to easily search through the history of thousands of transactions.

## How does the expense management process work?


The expense management process follows a repeatable sequence. Each step has an owner, but the point of handoffs is where manual systems could lose data quality. Here's how the process looks:


- **Employee incurs spend:** Purchase is made on a corporate card or with personal funds for an approved business purpose.
- **Capture and categorize:** Receipt is attached, cost center assigned, and business purpose documented.
- **Submit for approval:** Report is routed according to policy — by amount, department, or category.
- **Manager review:** Approver confirms policy compliance or returns for any correction.
- **Finance audit:** Sample or full audit for exceptions, duplicates, or fraud indicators.
- **Accounting sync:** Approved transactions post to the general ledger or accounts payable.
- **Reimbursement:** Out-of-pocket amounts are repaid within the committed timeline.
- **Reporting and analysis:** Finance reviews trends, exceptions, and impact to the budget.

## What is travel and expense management?


Travel and expense (T&E) management is the subset of expense management that covers costs employees incur on business trips. This includes flights, hotels, ground transport, meals, per diems, and client entertainment on the road. A single trip often generates multiple expense types. Some charges are made via a corporate card, whereas others are paid out of pocket. Receipt rules differ by category, and per diem may apply where itemized receipts aren't required.


T&E is harder to manage than desk-side purchases because spend happens across days or weeks, often in multiple currencies, before finance sees a complete picture. When booking tools, card programs, and expense reporting all live in separate systems, the lag between the actual trip and the reconciliation can stretch across weeks. Finance teams rebuild trip context line by line instead of reviewing a trip-level summary.


Unified travel and expense management software closes that gap by capturing booking data at the time of purchase, and matching card charges to itinerary context automatically. Trip-level reporting groups the expenses by journey rather than by submission date, which makes it easier to assess the true cost of the trip, and enforce per-diem rules by destination. Organizations managing a lot of business travel often evaluate[travel and expense management software](https://navan.com/blog/travel-and-expense-management-software) once report volume and booking fragmentation outpace manual reconciliation.

## Why does expense management matter for businesses?


Strong expense management protects cash flow, compliance, and employee trust all at the same time. It helps maintain:


Financial visibility: Real-time spend data supports forecasting and budget adjustments before quarter-end surprises. Finance teams can't manage what they reconcile thirty days late.


Policy compliance: Written rules mean little without enforcement. Systems that flag violations before submission reduce after-the-fact disputes and protect accountable plan requirements.\[3\]


Fraud and error reduction: The Association of Certified Fraud Examiners (ACFE) reports that 13% of occupational fraud schemes involve expense reimbursements.\[6\] Centralized audit trails and duplicate detection catch issues manual review misses.


Employee experience: Slow reimbursement erodes trust, and manual expense processing can be time consuming and frustrating. Skift and Navan's 2025 survey found that 71% of respondents spend 30 or more minutes on expense reports — time that an automated process can help reclaim.\[7\]


Mid-market and enterprise teams scaling beyond manual processes often need[enterprise expense management software](https://navan.com/blog/enterprise-expense-management-software) once the volume of reports calls for more finance headcount.


## What are the biggest expense management challenges?


Manual expense management can create predictable challenges. Finance leaders generally cite the same patterns regardless of industry:


Receipt chasing: Missing documentation delays approvals and month-end close. Finance teams can spend hours on follow-up instead of analysis.


Policy drift: Hybrid work, international travel, and subscription sprawl overtake outdated policy documents. Employees guess, and approvers apply inconsistent standards.


Reconciliation gaps: Card statements, booking records, and expense reports rarely align when systems don't share data. AP teams rebuild context line by line.


Approval bottlenecks: Email chains and unclear escalation paths stretch reimbursement cycles. Employees remain out of pocket longer than the policy promises.


Audit readiness: Spreadsheet archives lack the centralized documentation that SOX and tax audits require.\[2\]


Center's 2025 Expense Management Trends Survey cited manual reporting, credit card reconciliation, and time to close the books as the top challenges mid-market finance teams face.\[8\] Naveen Singh, CEO of Center, noted that the gap between corporate booking tools and employee behavior creates ongoing T&E control problems for growing companies.\[8\]

## 5 steps to develop and update an expense management policy


A written policy is the operating system for every other step in this guide. Without it, approvers apply inconsistent standards and audits fail on documentation.


Here's a recommendation to building a new policy:


Step 1: Inventory spend categories: Travel, meals, software, stipends, client entertainment, and remote-work allowances


Step 2: Set limits by category and location: Benchmark against industry norms and regional cost differences


Step 3: Define approval chains: Route by amount, department, and risk level


Step 4: Document substantiation rules: Receipt thresholds, business-purpose fields, and submission deadlines


Step 5: Publish where employees actually look: HRIS, expense app, and onboarding — not a buried PDF


### How to update an existing policy


If you're not starting from scratch, review your[expense policy](https://navan.com/resources/glossary/expense-policy) annually at the least, and after material changes like new office locations, remote-work shifts, international expansion, or IRS guidance updates. Cross-functional review (finance, HR, legal) catches gaps before auditors do, and be sure to communicate changes with real examples, not policy memos alone.


## 5 expense management best practices?


Effective expense management programs combine clear policy, visible enforcement, and technology that meets employees where they spend. Here are 5 rules of thumb you can follow:


- **Write a policy that employees will read:** Short documents with real examples outperform forty-page PDFs no one opens. Update annually and after major work-model changes.
- **Enforce at purchase, not just at audit:** Flag out-of-policy choices during booking or card authorization so spend is controlled before reimbursement disputes begin.
- **Integrate cards, travel, and expense:** Disconnected systems force finance to reconcile three sources. Unified platforms capture context at transaction time.
- **Train and remind:** Onboard new hires to your policy, and remind teams before peak travel seasons.
- **Audit consistently:** Random monthly samples keep compliance visible without being confrontational.


Software can enforce the policy you build. Best practices can fail if the written rules and the approval system tell different stories.
