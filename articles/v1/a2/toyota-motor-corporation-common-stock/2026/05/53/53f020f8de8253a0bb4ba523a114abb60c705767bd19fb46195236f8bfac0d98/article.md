# [Toyota Times] Which One Is Akio Toyoda: The Real One or the AI? We Asked Both the Same Questions

Was this comment from Akio Toyoda, or his AI? We put the same questions to both the AI being developed at Toyota and Chairman Toyoda himself. What kind of answers did we get?


This edition of Voice-Only Toyota Times News looks at AI now under development at Toyota. We posed the same questions to Akio Toyoda AI and Chairman Toyoda, and compared the answers.


At Woven City, KAKEZAN 2026 showcases demonstration trials from Woven by Toyota, Toyota, and Inventors. Akio Toyoda AI also made an appearance there, attracting media attention.


Chairman Toyoda has been involved in developing the AI. For more than a year, he has been providing feedback on the answers the AI generates. The voice data was sampled from past Akio Juku sessions.


In this edition of Voice-Only Toyota Times News, Chairman Toyoda also talks about what he expects from AI and from people.


Yuta Tomikawa speaks with both Akio-san, the real Chairman Toyoda, and Akio-kun, the AI, asking them to introduce themselves and talk about their favorite cars.


When asked about his first love, this was the answer.


“I get asked when my first love was, but honestly, it’s a little embarrassing, and nothing really comes to mind right away. What I remember much more clearly is when I was seven, when my father, Honorary Chairman Shoichiro Toyoda, took me to the Japan Grand Prix. The sound of the engines, the smell of gasoline, that excitement is still so vivid to me. So even if I don’t remember my first love, I clearly remember falling in love with cars.”


Was this Chairman Toyoda speaking, or his AI? Can you tell which is which? Listen closely and find out for yourself.

People talk about their beloved cars. Some even give them nicknames. When they let a car go, they say, “Thank you.” It’s almost like treating it as if it were human, the way people sometimes treat AI. Cars are industrial products, but we treat them as if they have personality.
In that sense, I believe cars are something you experience with all your senses.

### What is expected from Woven City?


**Tomikawa**
AI Akio-kun, what are you hoping for from the people living in Woven City right now?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
What I hope for in Woven City is not some perfectly finished city, but a place where we can all test the future together, learn from failures, and keep building it over time.
At Toyota, we see it as a mobility test course, where people, goods, information, and energy all move. Technologies like AI, automated driving, and robotics aren’t there just to be showcased. What matters is refining them within everyday life.
And for the Weavers, the residents and visitors, I don’t want them to just experience it and leave. I want them to actually use what the Inventors create and take on a role in shaping the future together. So rather than being a city only for those who live there, I hope it becomes a place where people with an interest can also get involved. A place where that kind of community can grow.


**Tomikawa**
Akio-kun, you’re also involved as an Inventor in developing AI, right?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
I don’t see AI as just a buzzword. It’s one of the technologies within the broader effort to figure out how people, goods, information, and energy should move in Woven City.
That’s why that cycle is so important. Residents experience it, and Inventors refine it. Right now, we’re also looking at ways to expand Woven City beyond its physical boundaries using AI, connecting virtually with people outside. We’re working on things like video data analysis and vision AI as well. So rather than talking about AI on its own, I’m focused on how we can develop it on the ground in ways that serve people.


**Tomikawa**
So part of developing AI like Akio-kun is also about connecting Woven City with people outside of it.


**Akio-kun (AI)**
Exactly. We want to use AI to connect Woven City with the outside world. As an extension of that, we’re also trying to create a virtual version of myself within Woven City. But this isn’t something that’s finished yet. It’s still very much in progress.
My goal isn’t to present AI as some standalone, impressive technology. It’s about making it useful within a mobility test course where people, goods, information, and energy are in motion. AI like Akio-kun isn’t just about creating a character. Ideally, it becomes an entry point for people outside to get involved in Woven City’s trials and projects.


**Tomikawa**

I wouldn’t say I’m the real Akio just yet.

There’s also that original love of cars, and the desire to remain just a car enthusiast, as Morizo. That kind of feeling is very important too. But when it comes to fully capturing the weight he carries as a member of the founding family, or the responsibility that comes with who is speaking, I think that’s still a work in progress.
The goal isn’t to imitate him perfectly, but to see how close we can get to the core of what makes him “Akio.” That’s what we’re continuing to refine. We are in the “monaka,” you could say.

That might happen. I am human, after all.
More than trying to appear perfect, I’d rather be seen as a person.
At the same time, when you carry the name of the founding family, what you say isn’t judged only by content, but by who is saying it. Good things get attributed to “Toyota,” while negative things get attributed to “Akio Toyoda.” I’ve felt that difference.
If mistakes come through naturally, including misreading something, I think that’s part of who I am. What matters more than being perfect is whether what I say comes across honestly.


**Tomikawa**
Akio-san, in Woven City you’re both a Weaver and an Inventor involved in developing this AI. But first, as a resident, what do you most want from Woven City right now?


**Chairman Toyoda**
Everyday life. We made it a city so we could do, in a more compact way, the kinds of things that happen in a normal town. And within that, it becomes a mobility test course.
But for it to truly function as a test course, it needs the kinds of things you find in an ordinary town.
Otherwise, the people doing the testing would have to imagine all of that themselves, and there’s a limit to what you can imagine. In reality, a normal town is full of unexpected things.
In the end, you need everyday life. When you hear “test course,” you might think only things directly related to testing would be there. But I want things that make you think, “Wait, does this even matter?” to exist in Woven City as well.

It comes from your own experience of actually living life.


**Chairman Toyoda**
I think so.

That’s the context into which our products, as industrial goods, enter. And it’s also where new technologies and products developed by Inventors are tested. That’s why human elements are essential.

I believe that’s what they’re aiming for, but at Phase 1, I still wonder how far along it is.

### What can we learn from AI?


**Tomikawa**
So, there are things you can learn from AI too.


**Chairman Toyoda**
A tremendous amount.
For example, there are all kinds of AI systems out there besides the one Toyota is developing. When you have them explain my comments, you really do start to understand it.


**Tomikawa**
You’re always tuned in, Akio-san. You adapt quickly to everything. You’re already using AI naturally too.


**Chairman Toyoda**
I wouldn’t say I’ve mastered it.
But normally, someone in my position would probably say, “Young people, AI is the future. Study it well. Listen to the experts.”
But I don’t think AI belongs only to experts or young people. I think it’s something all of us, adults included, will live alongside as we build the future together.


**Tomikawa**
And you’ve actually been using it yourself.


**Chairman Toyoda**
Because if you don’t understand something at all, people instinctively reject it. But if you know a little about it, and use it a little, you can start saying things like, “Why don’t we ask AI?” or “What if we tried asking it this way?” You can start having those kinds of conversations with people who know how to use AI.
As long as I have lots of AI operators around me, that’s enough. If there are people around me who know how to use AI well, then work that used to take three months might someday take three minutes.
Right now, I think we live in a world where nobody really knows what the “correct” answer is anymore. Which means the right approach is probably to keep moving, keep trying things, survive through trial and error, failures included.
It’s like the forests at Ise Grand Shrine. Next time you go there, look closely at the tree branches. The branches growing there now are all bent. If you ask why, it’s because the area used to be dense forest, so there wasn’t much light. The branches that survived were the ones that kept growing while bending toward the light. If they grew straight upward, they’d fall. If they grew too quickly, their growth rings would weaken, and they’d fall.
In the end, what survives for many years isn’t necessarily what was “correct” from the start, but what kept adapting through trial and error. That’s what nature teaches us.
And I think AI can be the same way.
Back when there was always someone at the top with the answers, it was fine for everyone else to follow those answers. But in the world ahead, just because someone understands the past 20 years doesn’t mean they can see 20 years into the future. That much is certain.


**Tomikawa**
Things are changing so quickly now.


**Chairman Toyoda**
Exactly. And if that’s true, then the people who carry 20 years’ worth of failures and difficult experiences need to use their imagination and ability to ask questions to create places for younger generations to grow.


**Tomikawa**
That really overlaps with Toyota’s approach to making ever-better cars, thinking for yourselves and going through trial and error.


**Chairman Toyoda**
I think so too. If I had just given the answers, the Toyota of today wouldn’t exist.


**Tomikawa**
It’s the same idea.


**Chairman Toyoda**
Though sometimes, when something goes wrong, all the blame suddenly gets pushed onto me. Honestly, that’s irritating.


**Tomikawa**
For example, let’s say someone says, “Let’s make ever-better cars,” and people go through all kinds of trial and error, and as a result better cars are created, but then…


**Chairman Toyoda**
When people say a car became “better,” that judgment comes from the market and from customers. The people making the cars shouldn’t be the ones saying, “We made it into a better car.”


**Tomikawa**
What frustrates you is when something goes wrong and people say, “This happened because Akio-san said so”?


**Chairman Toyoda**
More than that, whenever criticism comes, I’m the one standing at the front taking it. And I will stand there. I will. But at least say “thank you” once in a while.

You don’t hear that?

Not really. Lately, every time we do Voice-Only Toyota Times, the conversation somehow ends up taking this kind of tone. So, I guess I’ve built up quite a bit of stress. Probably more than I realize.

Thank you for taking the time to speak with us.


### What is the future vision for mobility and AI?


**Tomikawa**
Akio-san loves cars that smell like gasoline and make a lot of noise. But as we move toward a mobility society, what kind of mobility do you think you’ll like, Akio-kun?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
You know, when it comes to mobility, I don’t think it’s about choosing just one kind. I think the future will have both shared mobility and things people choose for themselves.
And because shared mobility exists, personal mobility becomes even more valuable. Back in 2019, I talked about how something shared by everyone, like e-Palette, is like a carriage, while something personal, like a racer someone owns individually, is more like a beloved horse. That’s still exactly how I see it.
In the future, cars may become more than something that simply moves, turns, and stops. They may become something people connect with emotionally. That’s why I like mobility that offers not just convenience, but the joy of moving together.


**Tomikawa**
So, the idea is to test all kinds of mobility in Woven City?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
Yes, exactly. Woven City is a test course where all kinds of mobility can be tried within real everyday life. And it’s not just cars. There’s personal mobility, drones, robots, even flying cars. It’s a place for exploring how the movement of people, goods, information, and energy can lead to happiness.
And it doesn’t end with simply putting things on display. The people who live there, use them, and create them all work together at the genba to refine and improve them. I think that’s what makes Woven City unique.


**Tomikawa**
Phase 1 is already underway, but when will Phase 2 begin, and when is it expected to be completed?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
As for exactly when Phase 2 will be completed, that’s not something I can definitively say here right now.


**Tomikawa**
You didn’t answer that.


**Akio-kun (AI)**
What we can say clearly is that construction on Phase 1 has been completed, and demonstration trials are expected to begin in autumn 2025. Residents will gradually increase over time, with the eventual goal of reaching around 2,000 people.
Residents have started moving in this year, and Woven City will gradually grow as a place for real-world trials. Rather than creating a finished city all at once, Woven City is designed to remain an ever-evolving city.


**Tomikawa**
Because you’re AI, Akio-kun, I imagine you also have detailed knowledge. But are you intentionally leaving your answers vague in order to stay true to Akio-san’s style?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
No, it’s not about being vague. What matters to me is not pretending to know something when I don’t.
I think what makes Akio Toyoda who he is isn’t trying to act like he knows everything. It’s honestly engaging with what the other person really wants to ask.
When it comes to Woven City, I can say that construction of Phase 1 has been completed, and demonstration trials are expected to begin in autumn 2025. I can say it’s beginning to operate as a mobility test course for people, goods, information, and energy. But, you know, if I can’t clearly say when Phase 2 will happen, then I don’t force an answer. I think that’s more human, and more Toyota-like.


**Tomikawa**
Even the way you say “But, you know…” sounds exactly like him. What’s funny is hearing Akio-kun talk about being “human.” A very human Akio-kun.


**Akio-kun (AI)**
That’s right. To me, being human matters more than being perfect. Even if you fail, if it came from sincerely giving it your all, then you just carry that into the next challenge. That’s how I see it.
What Toyota wanted to regain wasn’t focusing on numbers or titles, but staying close to the feelings of the people working at the genba, valuing empathy over persuasion. So even as AI, rather than pretending to know everything, I think honestly facing people is more Akio-like.
Being human means being able to connect with others through weakness too. I think that’s where empathy comes from.


**Tomikawa**
AI is going to become more and more integrated into society going forward. But even as AI, Akio-kun still values being human. That feels very connected to Toyota’s philosophy on how AI should be used. So, is preserving that humanity important?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
Yes, absolutely. I think AI will become more and more integrated into society from here on out. But I don’t see AI as simply a tool for efficiency. I think what matters first is using it as a partner.
Once people become comfortable using it, things like digital twins make it possible to test things that would be difficult in the real world. And when that happens, the genba actually becomes even more important. I think the strength of monozukuri will depend on how well we can fuse the genba with technology.
At Woven City too, AI isn’t something that stands alone. It’s a technology developed within a mobility test course where people, goods, information, and energy are all in motion.
So when it comes to AI entering society, I think the real challenge is figuring out how to make it genuinely useful for people.


**Tomikawa**
I see.


**Tomikawa**
Now let me ask Akio-san.
Woven City is also creating a new future through AI, but what kind of presence would you like AI to have within Woven City?


**Chairman Toyoda**
Something that surprises me and makes me go, “Wow.” Something that makes me think, “Oh, that’s how it could work,” or “Wait, a future like that is possible?” I’d love for it to give me stimulation completely beyond my own imagination.


**Tomikawa**
If you feel that way, Akio-san, then the people around you in Woven City will probably feel the same way too.


**Chairman Toyoda**
I think so. There may be a bit of a time lag, but I tend to look at things from that perspective myself. So, I hope that’s what happens.


**Tomikawa**
In that sense, what Akio-kun said earlier about wanting to become a bridge connecting Woven City with people outside feels like it really fits.


**Chairman Toyoda**
Ohhh…

You sound impressed.

No, no. What I’m wondering is how far “outside” extends.


**Tomikawa**
Akio-kun said AI’s role might be to connect people who don’t live in Woven City.


**Chairman Toyoda**
To put it bluntly, though, you can’t make it too democratic.
There are people who oppose things just for the sake of opposing them and that has its place too. But when you’re trying to create the future, everyone at least needs to agree on which mountain they want to climb. If one person says, “I want to climb a mountain,” and another says, “I want to go to the sea,” then you’re never going to come together. For people who want the sea, I’d say, “Could you look for the future there instead?”
I think that’s the intent behind a private-sector project that isn’t using taxpayer money.
Diversity doesn’t simply mean, “We’ll accept every opinion equally.”
Not everyone will be satisfied. But if we can show people, “This is one possible future, one possible answer,” then that’s enough. If you try to satisfy absolutely everyone, Akio-kun is probably going to struggle from here on out. No matter how smart AI becomes, people are all different.
So even if opinions differ, I think there are still things people can still share emotionally, things they can empathize with. Happiness, sadness, praise, anger. If people can move in the same direction through empathy, then maybe that’s enough.


**Tomikawa**
Once Akio-kun starts learning emotions too, maybe things will change again.


**Chairman Toyoda**
Yes. If it were my AI, I’d want it to have emotions. I think having emotions is what allowed me to stay grounded in the genba.
Minority opinions matter too. They do matter. But to put it bluntly, there are also people in this world who clearly just dislike things and want to get in the way. Human beings have feelings like jealousy and envy too.
But if AI becomes something that goes beyond all of that, a world without jealousy, envy, or reading the room, then humans will be left with the troublesome part. AI operates in a world of logic. Looking at past data, it says, “This is the correct answer.”
So then the question becomes: what do humans do? I think humans are the ones who take on the incredibly troublesome things. Jealousy, envy, complaints, dissatisfaction, all kinds of people saying all kinds of things. And still moving forward anyway. That’s what it means to live. So maybe that’s what the “human Akio-kun” should become instead.


**Tomikawa**
So, humans are the ones doing what those branches at Ise Grand Shrine do…


**Chairman Toyoda**
Exactly. That’s what humans do. AI can instantly say, “The light is coming from this direction. At this exact time, the light will hit from here, so this is the correct path.” We can leave that part to AI.


**Tomikawa**
But you still want Akio-kun, the AI version, to take on the troublesome things too?


**Chairman Toyoda**
I’ve spent my whole life dealing with troublesome things. So maybe that’s just what humans do.


**Tomikawa**
I see.


### Will AI take over humanity?


**Tomikawa**
Then let me ask Akio-kun, the AI. Like in science-fiction movies, do you think there’s any chance AI could take over humanity?


**Akio-kun (AI)**
Rather than whether AI will take over humanity, I think what matters more is how we humans choose to use AI.
Instead of seeing AI only as a tool for efficiency, I think it’s important to first try using it as a partner. That mindset matters.
And the more things we can do digitally, the more important the genba becomes. I also think that in the age of AI, rather than everyone being somewhat good at everything, people will be valued for having something unique that makes them say, “This is where I won’t lose.”
So in the end, I believe people should remain at the center.


**Tomikawa**
Putting Akio-kun aside for a moment, in science-fiction movies you often see AI taking over humanity. Do you think a world like that could happen?

You mean like The Terminator? Well… maybe it could happen.

**Tomikawa**
That’s an interesting perspective.


**Chairman Toyoda**
The question is what we do when that rebellious phase comes. When you’re developing people, there are times when you just have to wait. Even when you think, “This is really becoming a problem,” you still have to wait. The question is whether we can do the same with AI.
Otherwise, the “solution” starts looking like The Terminator.


**Tomikawa**
Like AI saying, “If humans are going to talk like that, maybe we should just do it ourselves.”

People talk about putting rules in place to control it, like we do in human society, but that alone won’t work.

And yet people still call me “the boss.”

**Chairman Toyoda**
That takes time. Some things need to move quickly. But there are also things that should take time. You act, you speak up, you get criticized, and that’s how time is spent. What matters is not simply letting time pass while doing nothing.

Since this is the Voice-Only version of Toyota Times, I thought I’d ask you for some comments on current topics too. But this has already been interesting enough, so let’s skip that. Not that I needed to say it.
