# The Responsibility of Tech: Reflections from Grace Hopper Celebration

# The Responsibility of Tech: Reflections from Grace Hopper Celebration


[Nida Pervez](https://medium.com/@npervez?source=post_page---byline--bc2a4c84775f---------------------------------------)


6 min read


·


Nov 11, 2021


--


The end of September was marked by one of the most exciting events for women in technology: Grace Hopper Celebration of Women in Computing (GHC). For those who do not know, GHC is a yearly conference that highlights women’s contributions/involvement in different aspects of the tech industry. In a field that is overwhelmingly dominated by men, it’s important to acknowledge the feats of women which otherwise might be drowned out. The week-long conference is packed with speakers, panels, interactive sessions, networking events, and a career fair. In my college days most of my attention admittedly was on the career fair — but this year I had the opportunity to attend virtually through Groupon, a platinum sponsor of the conference. This year’s theme was #DareTo, and I saw that play out in many of the talks, but the sessions that impacted me the most dared me to take responsibility for tech I produced.


Three talks that especially left an impression on me were Dr.Timnit Gebru’s keynote, Integrating Ethics and Responsibility in the Product Development Cycle by Kathy Pham, and Disability is an Opportunity for Innovation by Haben Girma. Each speaker speaks on the effect of taking responsibility (or lack thereof) for one’s technological feats.


Dr.Gebru’s talk highlighted how large tech corporations can take advantage of freedom for the sake of technological “advancement”. In her talk, Gebru explained in detail about her dismissal from the co-lead position of Google’s ethical AI team. There were two main aspects to this song that I understood. One being the sexism she faced and the other was the blatant unethical behavior of Google. Gebru addresses the inequality between men and women in the workforce at Google and highlighted that she was fired for doing the right thing, whereas many of her male counterparts could do ethically wrong things and still get by. As a company that is an Emerald sponsor (highest degree of sponsorship) of GHC, she asked what kind of message does that send to young female engineers interested in working at Google? Even for us at Groupon, do we live up to the platinum degree of sponsorship? Or is having women in the tech force simply another metric to cross off? What does it actually mean to support women in tech? From my experience as a woman recruited by Groupon from GHC, I have had a great experience at Groupon. From the beginning of my time here, I’ve felt supported, heard, and valued. Though I can only speak from my own experiences, company culture is made from the individuals and the values a company holds, so knowing my team and the values we hold, I have confidence that other women will also find it inviting here.


The other aspect of Gebru’s talk was how poorly Google handled the whole situation. For some context, Gebru was fired for not rescinding her name from a paper that explained the dangers of large language models (similar to what Google uses). The irony is that an ethics researcher was dismissed for taking a stance on unethical technology. Not only were her sharings shocking, but they also provided a platform to reflect — and reflect deeply. Why did an AI ethics researcher get fired for pointing out unethical aspects of AI research? Was that not what she was hired to do? In this instance, Google is practicing convenient ethics, where they promote ethical research so long as it does not jeopardize their business. Large companies such as Google, Amazon, Facebook, etc. are leading innovators in the tech industry and have a responsibility to create ethical technology. However, factors such as money and prestige often overrule the ethical standing of technology. Although technological breakthroughs are exciting, are they creating a safer and more inclusive world for everyone? We have seen time and time again that we cannot trust large tech companies to have our best interests at heart. Instead, as Kathy Pham states in her GHC session, we must take that responsibility as individual contributors and hold ourselves accountable.


Kathy Pham talks about responsible product and tech and how we as individuals are the ones who can make a change. Pham emphasizes the importance of the many small decisions we make in a day. Ultimately the cause of unethical tech is the culmination of our thoughts as humans and the prejudices we hold within ourselves. Pham cites multiple examples in her talk of “technology gone awry” such as, Joy Buolamwini’s discovery that the facial recognition technology only recognized her when she was wearing a white mask, Amazon “coincidentally” did not provide same-day delivery to areas that were formally red-lined, and Google Photos labeled some black people as gorillas. (She gave many more examples). Each of these blunders happened because of human bias and these errors have repercussions attached to them. However, with humans as part of the development cycle, how can we remove our personal bias from the equation? Pham emphasizes that we must do better and critically think about our algorithms, data models, and everyday bias. Tech is constantly impacting society so we must consider who is and isn’t at the table. Are the teams producing the tech diverse in their makeup? As someone who works primarily on front-end applications, it may seem that unethical tech is something far away from me. I’m not dealing with data sets or training data models, but as Kathy Pham stated in her talk, we must hold ourselves accountable.


As I was pondering what areas I could show accountability in, I came across Haben Gima’s talk, Disability is an Opportunity for Innovation. One takeaway I took from Gima’s talk was that oftentimes the dominant narrative is that disabled people are burdens on society, which she defines as ableism, when in reality disability does not stop someone from doing something, but rather ableism does. Once again our bias as humans creates limiting factors on people we have no business limiting. Gima shares that if you do not see a diverse team or company (i.e. race, gender, ability), then that means there could be barriers in place to prevent them from having the opportunity to work in such an environment. To combat that we must constantly review our processes and workplace environments. Do we have barriers in our hiring process? Do we have barriers in our product? These questions made me think about myself, my past experiences, and my current team/product.


Although my team does not deal with the struggle of unintentionally inserting our bias in data models, we do have the responsibility of making our product accessible to everyone. I work on the Layout-Service team as a front-end developer at Groupon under the Consumer Web organization. My team owns the header and footer along with other services for the Groupon website. The header contains the search bar, sign-in, and categories, which are all highly interactive parts of our site. For this reason, it is even more important for use to think about our user demographic. As we are all able-bodied people on my team, it may be easy to overlook or de-emphasize tasks related to accessibility, but the onus drops down to us, individually. On my team, the culture was already set to make sure we emphasized accessibility related issues. We currently have accessibility scores of 100 and 94 for mobile and desktop respectively. For me, there wasn’t much of a culture shift needed, as we were already taking personal responsibility, but that still makes me think of other ways to improve. For example, I have little experience with screen readers, so it would be worthwhile to check our product against different types of screen readers. For me, it’s important to think of what I can do with my current skills to make our product even a little better. Even acts as simple as describing images in a presentation allow for non-hearing and non-seeing people to better feel included, but it’s up to us and our individual choices to make those decisions.


Gebru, Pham, and Gima’s desire to build better technology for everyone is not only admirable but concrete. Their experience and advice give us tools to do our part in helping make the world a better place. With conventions like GHC, we have the opportunity to learn from women around the country about ways we can help. I appreciate GHC’s promise to allow these women leaders to speak freely and uncensored. These instances of raw exposure to these women’s experiences are what allow us to learn and grow.
