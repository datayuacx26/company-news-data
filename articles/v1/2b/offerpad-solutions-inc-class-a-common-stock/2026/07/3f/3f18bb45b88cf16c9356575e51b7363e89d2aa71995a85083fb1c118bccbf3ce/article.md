# What to Do If a Home Appraises for Less than What You Offered

After weeks of searching, you found the home you want, put in an offer, and got it accepted. Then the appraisal came back below your offer price. It is a stressful moment, and a lot of buyers freeze up when it happens. The good news: a low appraisal does not have to end the deal, and you have more options than you might think.


Appraisals come in under the agreed price on a meaningful share of purchase loans. It usually comes down to one of two things: the market moved faster than recent comparable sales, or the appraiser worked from incomplete information. Here is what is going on, and what you can do about it.


## Why it happens: the market


Home values are anchored to recent comparable sales, so a fast-moving market can outrun the data an appraiser has to work with. When demand is high and inventory is tight, buyers sometimes offer more than the latest comps support, and the appraisal lags behind. The reverse is true in a lot of markets in 2026: homes are selling at or below list price and sitting longer, which can work in a buyer’s favor. Conditions vary a lot by metro, as you can see in our look at[where homes still sell above asking price](https://www.offerpad.com/articles/where-homes-sell-above-asking-price/) .


## Why it happens: the appraiser


Home valuations are handled by licensed, independent third-party appraisers. They estimate value from several factors, with recent comparable sales carrying the most weight. Appraisals involve judgment and can be based on incomplete or imperfect information. Appraisers are human, and mistakes happen.


## Your options


### Renegotiate


Start here. Bring the appraisal to the seller and ask them to meet you at, or closer to, the appraised value. In a competitive market a seller may not budge, especially with other buyers waiting. In a slower market, or when the seller believes you are the right buyer, there is real room to work something out. It never hurts to ask.


### Cover the gap


There is an old line in real estate: a home is worth what a buyer will pay, not what an appraiser says. If you are set on the home, you can pay the difference between the appraised value and your offer in cash, on top of your loan. Some buyers plan for this ahead of time with an appraisal gap coverage clause, which commits you to covering some or all of a shortfall up to a set amount. If the appraisal comes in at or above your offer, you owe nothing extra. Just be confident in the home before you agree to pay above its appraised value.


### Request a reconsideration of value


If you believe the appraisal is wrong, you can ask for a reconsideration of value, or ROV, through your lender. Most lenders now have a formal process for it. Your case is only as strong as your evidence, so come prepared with specific, recent comparable sales the appraiser may have missed, corrections to any factual errors (square footage, bed and bath count, condition, or recent upgrades), and anything else that supports a higher value.


### Try a different lender


A new lender means a new appraisal, and occasionally a different result. This is not a guaranteed fix, it takes time, and it makes the most sense if your credit and finances are solid. Treat it as a backup plan, not a first move.


### Walk away


If the numbers do not work, you may be able to walk. A financing or appraisal contingency in your contract usually ties your obligation to buy to the home appraising at the purchase price. Check your contract and talk to your agent before you decide.


## The bottom line


A low appraisal is nobody’s idea of fun, but it is not the end of the road. However you move forward, you now have a clearer, more objective read on what the home is worth, and that helps you make a more confident decision about whether it is the right one for you.
