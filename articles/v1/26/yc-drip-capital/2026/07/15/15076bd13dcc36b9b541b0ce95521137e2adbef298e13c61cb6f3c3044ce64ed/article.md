# How Customs Delays Affect Your Cash Flow (and How to Bridge the Gap)

## How Customs Delays Affect Your Cash Flow (and How to Bridge the Gap)


When a shipment gets held at US Customs, the financial impact starts immediately. You have already paid your supplier. Your inventory is sitting at the port. Your customers are waiting on delivery. And the cash you need to run your business is frozen inside a container you cannot touch.


This is one of the most underestimated cash flow problems facing US importers and small business buyers today. Most business owners plan for the cost of goods, the shipping, and the duties. Very few plan for the cost of a hold.


## What a Customs Hold Actually Looks Like


A customs hold is not always a dramatic event. In many cases, it starts with a routine flag in the CBP system - a missing document, an unfamiliar supplier, or a shipment selected for random examination. The importer gets a notice. The goods sit. And the clock starts running.


The hold can last anywhere from 48 hours to several months depending on the reason. During that entire window, your money is tied up and unavailable.


Here is what is happening to your cash during a typical 15-day hold:


- Day 1: Your goods arrive at port. CBP flags for examination.
- Day 2: You are notified. Your[customs broker](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/customs-broker-us) files for release.
- Day 5: Physical examination scheduled. Port storage fees begin.
- Day 10: Examination completed. Additional documentation requested.
- Day 14: Documentation accepted. Release processing begins.
- Day 15: Goods released. Demurrage and storage invoice arrives.


While all of this is happening, your supplier has already been paid (or is waiting on payment). Your customers have not received their goods. Your next purchase order is on hold because your cash is not available. And you are now paying port fees on top of everything else.


## The Most Common Reasons for Customs Delays


Understanding why holds happen is the first step to planning around them. CBP holds shipments for a variety of reasons, some predictable and some not.

Since 2025, tariff-related holds have increased significantly. CBP enforcement of Section 301 classifications,[USMCA](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/usmca-explained) rules of origin, and country-of-origin marking requirements has tightened considerably. Food and beverage buyers, agricultural businesses, and manufacturers sourcing from Asia are among the most affected.


## The Real Cost of a Customs Hold - Direct and Indirect


Most importers think about the direct costs of a customs hold. Few think through the indirect costs, which are often larger.


### Direct Costs


**Demurrage fees:** Charged by the shipping line when your container stays at port beyond the free time (typically 3 to 5 days). Fees range from $75 to $350 per container per day depending on the port and shipping line.


**Detention fees:** Charged when a container is removed from the port but not returned within the allowed window. Common when goods are moved to a customs examination facility.


**Storage at examination facilities:** When CBP moves goods to an examination facility (CES - Container Examination Station), the importer pays the facility's storage rate, often $100 to $300 per day per container.


**Customs broker fees:** Additional filings, document preparation, and coordination during a hold typically cost $200 to $1,500 depending on complexity.


**Re-inspection fees:** If CBP requires a second examination, the costs repeat.


### Indirect Costs


**Lost sales:** If customers have a hard delivery deadline and your goods arrive late, you may lose the sale entirely. This is especially common in retail and food service.


**Damaged customer relationships:** Even if you do not lose the sale, customers who experience repeated delays may begin sourcing from other suppliers.


**Disrupted order cycle:** If your cash is frozen in a held shipment, you may not be able to place your next purchase order on time. This creates a cascading delay across your entire supply chain.


**Supplier relationship strain:** If you are unable to pay the next invoice because cash is tied up, your supplier relationship suffers even though the delay is not your fault.


**Late delivery penalties:** Some customer contracts include late delivery penalties. A 15-day customs hold can trigger these clauses.


A 15-day hold on a $200,000 shipment at a major port can generate $5,000 to $15,000 in direct fees, plus uncountable indirect costs in lost sales and disrupted operations.


## How Different Types of Businesses Are Affected


### Food and Beverage Importers


Food importers face the highest frequency of customs holds due to FDA prior notice requirements, labeling compliance, and foreign supplier verification program (FSVP) requirements. A single failed prior notice can hold a perishable shipment long enough to render it unsellable. The cash flow impact is total - not just frozen but lost.


### Agricultural Buyers


Agricultural imports are subject to USDA inspection and APHIS permits in addition to CBP clearance. Multi-agency review adds time and unpredictability to the clearance process.


### Manufacturers Sourcing Components


Manufacturers importing production components face a different problem: a customs hold does not just freeze cash, it halts production. A two-week hold on critical components can shut down an assembly line, creating labor costs with no output.


### Seafood Businesses


Seafood imports require FDA registration, catch certificates, and country-of-origin documentation. They are also subject to anti-dumping duties on shrimp, catfish, and other species. Holds in this category can be extended by AD/CVD review processes.


## How to Prepare Your Cash Flow for Customs Risk


The businesses that handle customs delays best are not the ones who avoid delays - they are the ones who have structured their cash flow so that a delay does not threaten operations.


### Step 1: Know Your Exposure


Calculate how much of your operating cash is tied up in goods in transit at any given time. For most importers, this is the largest single use of[working capital](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/vendor-financing) . If a hold affects all of it simultaneously, what happens to payroll, rent, and the next order?


### Step 2: Separate Your Supplier Payment from Your Operating Cash


The most common mistake importers make is paying suppliers directly from operating cash. When a customs hold happens, both the payment and the goods are unavailable at the same time.


A[vendor financing](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/vendor-financing) arrangement changes this structure. The financing provider pays your supplier. Your operating cash stays intact. If a hold happens, your business continues to operate normally because your cash was never depleted.


### Step 3: Maintain a Working Capital Reserve or Line


Even with vendor financing, unexpected costs during a hold (demurrage, storage, re-inspection) come from operating cash. A revolving[line of credit](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/line-of-credit) or cash reserve of $25,000 to $50,000 specifically for supply chain disruptions is a practical safeguard.


### Step 4: Work With an Experienced Customs Broker


Most documentation-related holds are preventable. An experienced customs broker reviews your[commercial invoice](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/commercial-invoice) s, packing lists, and country-of-origin declarations before filing. The cost of a broker ($300 to $1,500 per entry) is almost always less than the cost of a hold.


### Step 5: Build Buffer Time Into Customer Commitments


If your average clearance time is 5 days and you promise customers delivery in 7 days, a single hold breaks your commitment. Building 5 to 10 days of buffer into your delivery promises protects customer relationships without requiring any cash outlay.

## Frequently Asked Questions


**How long can a customs delay last?** Most customs holds resolve within 3 to 10 business days. Compliance reviews, anti-dumping investigations, and tariff disputes can extend holds to 6 to 12 weeks. The key is having a cash flow contingency plan before a delay happens rather than after.


**Who pays for demurrage and storage during a customs hold?** The importer is responsible for all demurrage and storage costs even if the delay is caused by CBP. These fees accumulate daily and can add up to thousands of dollars on large shipments. They are not recoverable from CBP.


**Can vendor financing help during a customs delay?** Yes. If your supplier has already been paid through a vendor financing arrangement, a customs delay affects your inventory timeline but not your immediate cash obligations. Your working capital stays intact while CBP processes the hold.


**What documents are most commonly missing during[customs clearance](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/us-customs-clearance-process) ?** Commercial invoice discrepancies (incorrect value or description), missing[country of origin](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/country-of-origin) certificates, and incorrect[HTS code](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/hts-code) classifications are the three most common causes of preventable holds.


**How can small businesses protect their cash flow from customs delays?** The most effective approaches are: (1) using a financing structure where the supplier is paid by a third party rather than from operating cash; (2) maintaining a dedicated working capital line for supply chain disruptions; and (3) working with an experienced customs broker who catches documentation errors before filing.


**What is the difference between demurrage and detention?** Demurrage is charged by the shipping line when a container remains at the port terminal beyond the free time period. Detention is charged when the container is removed from the port (for example, taken to a warehouse) but not returned to the shipping line within the allowed time. Both accumulate daily and both are the importer's responsibility.
