# Customs Duty Payment: Should You Pay Upfront or Finance It?

## Customs Duty Payment: Should You Pay Upfront or Finance It?


Every time a shipment crosses into the United States, customs duties must be paid before CBP releases the goods. For most importers, this payment arrives at exactly the wrong moment - before the inventory has sold and before customers have paid their invoices.


The decision of whether to pay customs duties upfront from operating cash or to finance them is one of the most consequential[working capital](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/vendor-financing) decisions a US importer makes. Get it right and your cash stays liquid. Get it wrong and you are perpetually behind.


## Understanding How Customs Duties Work in the US


When goods arrive at a US port of entry, the importer or their[customs broker](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/customs-broker-us) files an entry with CBP. Duties are calculated based on several factors:


**The[HTS (Harmonized Tariff Schedule)](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/hts-code) classification:** Every product has a 10-digit HTS code that determines the base duty rate. Rates range from 0% on many industrial inputs to 20%+ on apparel, footwear, and certain consumer goods.


**The customs value:** Typically the transaction value - what you paid your supplier. CBP can challenge this if it appears inconsistent with market values.


**Country of origin:** Where the goods were manufactured, not where they shipped from. Goods from countries with free trade agreements (USMCA, KORUS, etc.) may qualify for preferential duty rates or duty-free treatment.


**Additional tariffs:**[Section 301](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/section-301-tariff) tariffs apply to most Chinese-origin goods at rates of 7.5% to 25%. Anti-dumping duties (ADD) and countervailing duties (CVD) apply to specific products from specific countries and can be extremely high - sometimes exceeding 100%.


Payment is typically required within 10 working days of entry filing. Many importers pay at time of filing through their customs broker.

The financial challenge is not just the amount of the duty - it is the timing. Here is what a typical import cash flow cycle looks like for a mid-sized US buyer:

## When Paying Customs Duties Upfront Makes Sense


There are situations where paying duties directly from operating cash is the right decision.


**Your duty rates are low:** If you import under[USMCA](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/usmca-explained) or another preferential trade program with zero or near-zero duty rates, the cash impact is minimal and financing adds unnecessary cost.


**Your cash conversion cycle is short:** If you sell goods within 10 to 15 days of import and customers pay quickly, the timing gap is manageable from operating cash.


**You have strong seasonal cash surpluses:** Some businesses accumulate cash during peak seasons that naturally align with heavy import periods. If your cash position is strong when duties are due, financing adds cost without adding value.


**Your duty liability is small relative to your cash position:** A general guideline is that if duty payments represent less than 5% of your monthly operating cash, financing is probably unnecessary.


## When Financing Customs Duty Payments Makes Sense


For most small and mid-sized importers, particularly those sourcing from China or other Section 301 countries, financing duty payments is not just convenient - it is a practical necessity.


**High tariff rates:** Section 301 tariffs at 7.5% to 25% on Chinese-origin goods dramatically increase the cash required at entry. Many businesses that previously paid duties comfortably from cash find that post-2018 tariff increases have made this approach unworkable.


**Long customer payment cycles:** If your customers pay on Net 45, Net 60, or longer terms, you may be paying duties 60 to 90 days before you collect from customers. Financing bridges this gap.


**Multiple shipments arriving simultaneously:** If you have 3 or 4 containers clearing customs in the same week, the duty obligation can spike to a level that cannot be absorbed from operating cash without disrupting other obligations.


**Rapid growth:** Growth is the most common cause of working capital stress. As your import volume increases, your duty obligations increase proportionally. Many businesses that financed duties comfortably at $50,000 per month find they need a different approach at $200,000 per month.


**Seasonal inventory builds:** Businesses that import heavily before peak seasons (Q4 retail, spring agricultural season, etc.) often face their largest duty obligations at the moment when their cash is most constrained.


## Your Options for Managing Customs Duty Payments


### Option 1: Pay From Operating Cash


**How it works:** Duty payment comes directly from your business bank account, typically via your customs broker's ACH or wire.


**Advantages:** No financing cost, simplest administratively.


**Disadvantages:** Depletes operating cash at a moment when inventory is unavailable and customers have not paid. Creates cash flow stress that compounds with each shipment.


**Best for:** Low duty rates, short cash conversion cycles, strong cash reserves.


### Option 2:[Vendor Financing](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/vendor-financing) / Import Financing


**How it works:** A financing provider covers both your supplier payment and your broader import cash flow needs. You repay over 30 to 90 days once your customers pay.


**Advantages:** Your operating cash is never depleted. You can import at the volume your business requires without being constrained by your cash position. No collateral required with providers like Drip Capital.


**Disadvantages:** Financing cost (typically 1.5% to 4% per 30 days). Requires application and approval.


**Best for:** High tariff rates, long customer payment cycles, growing import volumes.


### Option 3: Working Capital[Line of Credit](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/line-of-credit)


**How it works:** You maintain a revolving credit line and draw on it specifically for duty payments and other import costs. Repay as customer payments come in.


**Advantages:** Flexibility to draw exactly what you need when you need it. Only pay for what you use.


**Disadvantages:** Requires creditworthiness review and ongoing compliance. May require collateral depending on the lender.


**Best for:** Predictable import schedules with variable volume. Good credit profile.


### Option 4: Customs Bond and Deferred Payment


**How it works:** A continuous customs bond allows importers to defer duty payment. Under periodic monthly statement (PMS), duties can be aggregated and paid monthly rather than per entry.


**Advantages:** Simplifies administration, provides up to 30 days of natural deferral.


**Disadvantages:** Only delays payment, does not finance it. The liability accumulates and must be settled monthly.


**Best for:** High-frequency importers looking for administrative simplification.


### Option 5: Bonded Warehouse Storage


**How it works:** Goods are stored in a CBP-approved[bonded warehouse](https://www.dripcapital.com/en-us/resources/blog/bonded-warehouse) . Duties are not owed until goods are withdrawn for domestic consumption.


**Advantages:** Can defer duty payment indefinitely if goods remain in the bonded warehouse. Useful for businesses that stage inventory releases to match demand.


**Disadvantages:** Warehouse costs, logistical complexity, and security requirements. Not practical for perishables.


**Best for:** Non-perishable goods businesses with sophisticated inventory management.


### Option 6: Foreign Trade Zone (FTZ)


**How it works:** Goods admitted to an FTZ are not subject to duties until they enter US commerce. Businesses operating in or near an FTZ can manufacture or process goods duty-free for export, or pay duties only when withdrawing for US consumption.


**Advantages:** Significant duty management advantages for high-volume importers.


**Disadvantages:** High setup cost and administrative requirements. Only practical for large-scale operations.


**Best for:** Large manufacturers with consistent import volumes above $5M annually.

## Frequently Asked Questions


**Can I finance my customs duty payment separately from my supplier payment?** Yes. Some lenders offer duty-specific import financing. However, combining your supplier payment and duty obligation into a single working capital facility is usually simpler and more cost-effective than managing two separate financing relationships.


**What happens if I cannot pay customs duties on time?** CBP charges interest on overdue duties. In serious cases, CBP can place a hold on future imports or initiate collection proceedings. If a continuous bond is in place, the surety may be liable for unpaid duties, which damages your bonding relationship. Having a financing plan in place before goods arrive prevents this situation.


**How do Section 301 tariffs affect the duty financing decision?** Section 301 tariffs at 7.5% to 25% on Chinese-origin goods substantially increase the cash required at entry. A $100,000 shipment from China may require $25,000 in additional duty payment on top of the goods cost. For businesses importing regularly from China, this level of duty obligation makes some form of financing a practical necessity rather than an option.


**Does financing customs duties affect my relationship with my customs broker?** No. Your customs broker files the entry, calculates duties, and processes payment regardless of how you finance the underlying obligation. The financing arrangement is entirely between you and your lender.

**Can I use vendor financing to pay duties on goods I have not received yet?** Drip Capital's financing covers the supplier payment (goods cost) rather than the duty payment specifically. However, by preserving your operating cash through vendor financing on the goods cost, you ensure that cash is available to cover duty obligations without disrupting your working capital position.
