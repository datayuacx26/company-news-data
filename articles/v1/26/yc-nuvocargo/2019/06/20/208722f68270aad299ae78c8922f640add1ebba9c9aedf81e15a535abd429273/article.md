I really like the new narrative that people like


[Justin Kan](https://medium.com/u/97ddc24c01db?source=post_page---user_mention--de201c80e08c---------------------------------------)


,[James Beshara](https://medium.com/u/4396b53c7d20?source=post_page---user_mention--de201c80e08c---------------------------------------)


,[Ryan Caldbeck](https://medium.com/u/15047b979080?source=post_page---user_mention--de201c80e08c---------------------------------------)


,[Sahil Lavingia](https://medium.com/u/8df60704dc5b?source=post_page---user_mention--de201c80e08c---------------------------------------)


, and many more are pushing about startup founders sharing struggles and trying to detach their happiness from external outcomes.


I have been guilty of this since I spent some time at Y Combinator and SF last year. Inside the Silicon Valley bubble, it’s really hard to get energized by anything other than raising money, getting press, or bragging about headcount unless you take a step back and remove yourself from the hype.


For me, the overarching message from this new mental health debate is that one *great* way to start enjoying the process of building your company is to get **energized** constantly by little daily things that are taking you one step closer to your goal.


Most importantly, I’m trying to enjoy these little things independently of whether or not they lead to a specific outcome for me or my company.


I felt like sharing some of these things that have been making my daily life very enjoyable and energetic for the past few months as a venture-backed startup founder. I hope this helps you notice and find energy in similar things in your life as a startup founder:


## 1. Uncensored founder stories


Today, we have podcasts, blogs, and books from startup founders that are raw, genuine, and uncensored. They have deliberately tried not to sugarcoat how hard being a startup founder is.


Now, when I’m struggling with something really hard, I feel like it’s more of a rite of passage that most of my role models & heroes went through rather than something I’m suffering due to my own incompetence.


Every time I have some free time on the subway or waiting somewhere, I’ll read/hear these stories and almost instantly feel relieved. Here are some of my favorites:


- [Feeling Good: Justin’s Program](https://justinkan.com/feeling-good-justins-program-b77c70437ee2) by[Justin Kan](https://medium.com/u/97ddc24c01db?source=post_page---user_mention--de201c80e08c---------------------------------------)


- [The Struggle](https://a16z.com/2012/06/15/the-struggle/) and[The Hard Thing About Hard Things](https://www.amazon.com/Hard-Thing-About-Things-Building/dp/0062273205/ref=sr_1_1?keywords=Hard+thing+about+hard+things&qid=1560974317&s=gateway&sr=8-1) by[Ben Horowitz](https://medium.com/u/be9da4c4955?source=post_page---user_mention--de201c80e08c---------------------------------------)

- [Shoe Dog](https://www.amazon.com/Shoe-Dog-Memoir-Creator-Nike/dp/1501135929/ref=sr_1_1?keywords=shoe+dog+phil+knight&qid=1560974203&s=gateway&sr=8-1) by Phil Knight

## 2. Stars align with your team


There are a few moments every week that me, my cofounder, and our team all get aligned on some critical decision for the company — we’re on an adventure together and everyone’s on the same page — that’s incredibly energizing to me!


## 3. Interviewing someone great


When someone really smart, well regarded, and energetic is **seriously** drawn to your startup and is seriously considering joining you despite all the risks — that energizes me in an incredible way.


It’s mind blowing for me that as an entrepreneur you can think up an idea from scratch, barely make it a reality, and then smart people with tons of career options actually consider spending years of their life working on it.


Even if they don’t ultimately join — their excitement about our future and potential in the interview process is energy I can feed off of for days.


## 4. New useful product features


For startups that build some kind of software — every time your product/engineering team rallies together to crank out a feature that is useful, or that makes our lives or our customers lives better in a small way, it’s pretty cool.


You can tangibly see the feature, and tangibly see its use cases, and it’s a living breathing example of the work that the team has put together for their common goal. Those moments make me proud and also energize me.


## 5. Receiving/giving mentorship


For multiple years, I’ve gotten some form of startup-related advice from over 50–100 people who are founders, investors, operators, etc.


Over time, I’ve carefully filtered that down to a few smart and successful people who I respect and really click with. One of these mentors of mine calls this group of people your “Brain Trust” as a founder.


Being able to share my struggles and learn from people who have been in my shoes recently is invaluable to me.


Conversely, I really enjoy sharing my learnings with other founders who are passing through something I went through 1, 2, or 3 years ago — this helps them and energizes me because it reminds me how far we’ve come — win-win.


## 6. 12–24 months of guaranteed adventure


Just a couple years ago as I fantasized quitting my job to pursue entrepreneurship, I vividly remember thinking to myself: “if only I had a salary to build my company and not burn my savings, I’d be the happiest guy alive.”


But the moment you raise money or start seeing some success, expectations grow, pressure mounts, and your goals have become even loftier. So it’s hard to remember how far you’ve come or the beauty of where you are now.


There’s a reason we chose this path and this journey. If you’re one of the lucky ones that succeeded in raising some money and have enough money in the bank to build your dream for 1–2 years with a salary and hire a few amazing people — I think that calls for some gratitude.


## 7. Serendipitous relationships


As a founder sometimes you’ll get sucked into these random calls, events, or situations which you will conclude are, for the most part, a waste of time. This is why over time most founders start saying no to most situations that allow for serendipity.


But 1/10 times when you do find yourself in these situations, it will lead to a lasting relationship with someone you admire and hope to work with one day. Some of my closest friends and mentors today came about this way.


—


The above list is only growing for me and that is exciting to me!


I think as the narrative of mental health or “enjoying the ride” of building startups permeates more and more, I hope it will make the journey more fun and less lonely for founders.


And to my fellow founders — keep fighting the good fight! If this sh**t was easy, everyone would do it!
