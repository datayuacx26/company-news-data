# Permitting Reform Is Now a Federal Priority. Implementation Is Where It Gets Real.

Getting a yes to build in America can be harder than the building itself. A single home can require approvals from a half-dozen departments that don't talk to each other, against requirements that live in an outdated PDF (or are unlisted altogether), on timelines nobody can see. Multiply that across tens of thousands of permitting authorities nationwide and you get a quiet tax on every home in this country.


Now Washington is moving to change that. And we're moving with it: PermitFlow is rolling out a national permitting visibility dashboard, a live picture of how permitting actually works in every community in America, paired with hands-on support for the municipalities doing the modernizing. This week, we're bringing it to the Permitting Innovators Expo in Washington, DC, hosted by the White House Council on Environmental Quality.


Here's why this moment matters, and why we're built for it.


‍


## Change in Washington


On March 13, 2026, the President signed Executive Order 14394, "Removing Regulatory Barriers to Affordable Home Construction." In May, the Department of Housing and Urban Development followed with its State and Local Best Practices for Home Construction: recommendations for states and municipalities covering streamlined permitting with a permit review fast lane, binding permit shot clocks, by-right approvals for single-family homes, regulatory transparency, and modernized, technology-enabled permitting offices. Today they are recommendations. But we believe that, in aggregate, these practices will make a massive impact on housing in America, and they are on a path to becoming the standard that shapes federal housing programs. The communities that adopt them early will build more, faster, and cheaper. We are eager to help make that happen.


It is a decisive federal push on a problem Washington has long left almost entirely to states and localities. And it goes after the exact problem PermitFlow has spent half a decade solving: removing permitting as a barrier to construction. This reform effort attacks that problem from a different angle, but it is the same problem, and many of the same solutions apply.


‍


## The gap between theory and practice


Best practices on paper do not shorten permit timelines - only adoption does. Adoption must happen jurisdiction by jurisdiction, across a permitting landscape that is profoundly fragmented: different forms, different fee schedules, different review sequences, and different portals (or no portal at all).


Builders, contractors, and municipal workers on the ground understand the difference between policy and practice. A jurisdiction can render a 30-day shot clock toothless by responding on day 29 with a single minor comment that resets the clock. Two identical applications can land with two reviewers and get two different answers. A published checklist can say one thing while unwritten requirements surface mid-review and send the applicant back to the start. None of this shows up in a policy scorecard, but it does show up in project timelines.


Today, no one can see or quantify this silent tax. An agency or state can fund housing programs, publish best practices, and encourage reform, but it can't see how these actions are operationalized on the ground: where projects stall, and whether reforms are working as written or quietly worked around. You cannot manage, or improve, what you cannot measure.


PermitFlow was built to make permitting seamless for the people doing the building, and we've learned that you cannot make a process seamless without first making it visible and accountable. That's how our platform works in the private sector today. Think about a high-rise apartment building: dozens of subcontractors, each responsible for their own sub-permits, across multiple departments and review cycles. On PermitFlow, all of it lives in one place: every permit, every status, every blocker, and exactly who holds the next action, so the developer can focus on building with confidence. The systems we built to do that for builders are the same systems this reform moment demands.

## Rolling out: a national permitting visibility dashboard


Our thesis is simple: the same engine that runs permitting for builders can give the federal government true visibility into how reform is landing on the ground, and give local governments the tools to act on it.


Start with visibility. Picture a live, national map of permitting in America at the level where it actually happens: the municipality. For every jurisdiction: what it requires, how its process really runs, which best practices it has adopted, and where reform is real versus still on paper. Communities report their own progress. Builders, contractors, and residents add what they experience on the ground, filling the gaps no survey captures. And for the first time, the agencies and states funding reform can see, across every community they touch, where adoption is working and where it's stuck.


But a dashboard alone doesn't modernize a permitting office, and this is the part of the rollout we care most about: hands-on support for the municipalities is the key to driving impact. Many communities want to adopt these best practices and simply lack the staff, budget, or playbook to do it. That's why our plan is to pair visibility with guidance, training, and resources for local permitting offices, so that a community that wants to adopt has a clear path to getting there.
