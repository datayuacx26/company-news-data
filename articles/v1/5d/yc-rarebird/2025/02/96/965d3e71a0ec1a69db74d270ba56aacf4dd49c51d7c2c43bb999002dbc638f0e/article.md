# Coffee and Cortisol: A One-Sided Relationship

## Caffeine’s Relationship to Cortisol and How You Can Reduce Cortisol Levels With Coffee


For many of us, coffee is more than just our favorite drink, it’s a ritual, a lifeline, and the non-negotiable start to our day. That first sip is often what pulls us out of bed and fuels our mornings.


But while coffee is celebrated for its ability to wake us up and sharpen our focus, it also has a hidden side effect: it spikes our adrenaline and cortisol levels, our body’s primary stress hormones. Over time, these repeated surges in adrenaline and cortisol can leave us feeling more stressed, fatigued, and burned out—ironically, the very things we’re trying to avoid.


In this post, we’ll explore the relationship between caffeine and cortisol, uncover how your morning coffee might be affecting your stress levels, and share practical tips to help you enjoy your coffee while keeping cortisol in check.


*And don’t worry, we’d never recommend you quit coffee. We’re here to help you enjoy coffee more, not give it up*


## What Is Cortisol and why do your cortisol levels matter?


Cortisol is most commonly recognized as the body’s “stress hormone,” but it helps orchestrate everything from your metabolism to your immune response. Produced by your adrenal glands, cortisol plays a key role in regulating your metabolism, immune system, and energy levels throughout the day. Think of it as your body’s built-in alarm system: it helps you wake up in the morning, powers you through stressful situations, and even gives you a boost to tackle midday dips in energy.


Cortisol follows a natural daily rhythm, known as the circadian rhythm. It typically peaks in the early morning (around 6–9 a.m.), helping you wake up and face the day, then gradually declines throughout the day, reaching its lowest point at night as your body and mind prepare for rest and recovery ([Fries, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18854200/) ).


However, when stress—whether physical, emotional, or environmental—triggers a cortisol release, it can throw this balance out of whack. While short-term cortisol spikes are an essential part of your body’s alarm system (i.e., helping you respond to emergencies), chronic high cortisol levels can lead to symptoms like weight gain (especially around the midsection, or ‘belly fat’), mood swings, and a weakened immune system.


In short, cortisol is a double-edged sword: it’s essential for survival in small doses, but can wear us down and make us feel fatigued and burned out when levels remain chronically elevated or when our cortisol levels are repeatedly spiking.


When cortisol remains elevated for too long, it can leave you feeling drained, foggy, and emotionally exhausted—common symptoms of burnout. ## **How coffee and caffeine can elevate cortisol**


Within the first 30-45 minutes after waking, your cortisol levels naturally increase sharply ([Clow, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20026350/) ), typically reaching their highest levels early in the morning — typically between 6 a.m. and 9 a.m. This surge, often called the “cortisol awakening response,” is designed to help you wake up and feel alert. Unfortunately, this happens to be the exact time most of us reach for that first (or second and third) cup of coffee.


Caffeine’s relationship with cortisol is complex—it’s not just about waking you up; it directly affects how your body responds to stress. Here’s how caffeine interacts with your stress hormones and how your coffee habit can be affecting your cortisol levels.


### 1. Caffeine Blocks Adenosine, Your Brain’s “Sleep Signal”


Caffeine’s primary job is to block adenosine, the brain chemical that promotes relaxation and drowsiness. By doing this, you feel more alert and focused. However, this same blockade signals your body to produce adrenaline and cortisol — both of which are tied to the body’s “fight or flight” response. In other words, caffeine isn’t just telling you to wake up; it can make your body scream, “danger!”.


Essentially, caffeine is hijacking a system evolved over thousands of years to help us survive life-or-death situations, like outrunning a predator on the savannah. But instead of fleeing from lions, we’re now sitting at desks, staring at laptops, and stressing over deadlines—proof that our biological wiring isn’t always the best fit for our modern lives.


### 2. Caffeine Can Spike Cortisol Levels


Numerous studies indicate that caffeine consumption can lead to a cortisol spike, even in daily coffee drinkers ([Cole, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39007443/) ;[Lovallo, 2005](https://journals.lww.com/bsam/abstract/2005/09000/caffeine_stimulation_of_cortisol_secretion_across.9.aspx) ;[Lovallo, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16631247/) ). While regular coffee drinkers may develop some tolerance over time, factors like stress, lack of sleep, and dehydration can amplify the effect.


For example, if you exceed your usual amount of caffeine on a particularly hectic day, you might experience an even bigger surge in cortisol—and the accompanying jitters, anxiety, and wired feeling. This is why that extra cup of coffee during a stressful workweek might leave you feeling more frazzled and less focused while a cup of coffee on a calm weekend morning has no negative effects.


### 3. The Domino Effect on Blood Sugar and Energy


Caffeine doesn’t just affect cortisol and perceived stress levels—it can also lead to a short-term spike in blood sugar by decreasing insulin sensitivity ([Shi, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5192567/) ;[van Dam, 2004](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/27/12/2990/26447/Effects-of-Coffee-Consumption-on-Fasting-Blood) ). In simpler terms, caffeine makes it harder for your body to process sugar efficiently, which can contribute to the energy crash you may feel later in the day.


If you’ve ever experienced a “coffee crash” a few hours after drinking coffee, this cortisol-blood sugar connection might be to blame. It’s a double whammy: caffeine gives you a quick boost, but the resulting cortisol spike and blood sugar imbalance can leave you feeling even more drained than before.


Now that we’ve explored how caffeine affects cortisol, let’s look at why timing matters—especially first thing in the morning—and how a few strategic tweaks to your routine can help you maintain steady energy levels all day long.


## **Signs of High Cortisol & Adrenaline From Too Much Caffeine**


Caffeine's dark side: spikes in stress hormones


Short-term spikes in adrenaline and cortisol, like the kind you may experience after drinking too much coffee, typically show up in a combination of physical and mental/emotional symptoms.


### **Common physical signs of high adrenaline and high cortisol levels**


- **Increased Heart Rate & Blood Pressure**


– The initial surge of adrenaline (with some contribution from cortisol) triggers your body’s fight-or-flight response, making your heart beat faster.


- **Sweating or Feeling Hot**


– Adrenaline revs up energy production, releasing energy quickly and causing mild sweating or flushing.


- **Jitters, Tremors, or Muscle Twitches**


– Elevated cortisol can exacerbate adrenaline’s effects on our nervous system leading to tremors or twitching.


- **Tension Headaches**


– Muscle tightness in the shoulders and neck can lead to headaches.


### **Common mental signs of high adrenaline and high cortisol levels**


- **Racing Thoughts / Overthinking**


– Heightened adrenaline and cortisol can make your mind feel like it’s on overdrive, leaving you feeling “wired.”


- **Irritability or Mood Swings**


– Sudden spikes in stress hormones may lead to feeling on edge and moodiness.


- **Anxiety or Feeling ‘On High Alert’**


– A surge of both cortisol and adrenaline can trigger physical and emotional stress responses that resemble an anxiety attack, even when there’s no actual threat.


- **Brain Fog or Difficulty Focusing –**


In small doses, adrenaline and cortisol can boost alertness. However, when these hormones remain high for too long (e.g., from excessive caffeine), you might experience scattered thoughts, difficulty concentrating, and a mental haze commonly referred to as brain fog.


Put simply, caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the brain, which normally help calm the nervous system. In response, your body steps on the gas — releasing adrenaline and cortisol. Adrenaline is released first and fastest, contributing to the immediate “jittery” sensations; cortisol increases contribute to longer-lasting feelings of stress or anxiety.


Thus, if you’ve ever described yourself as “over-caffeinated,” you were likely experiencing a spike in both adrenaline and cortisol. These effects are common, but they are also signs that your body is mounting a stress response.


## **How to Reduce Cortisol Levels Without Giving Up Coffee**


### **Px Coffee: A New Way to Help Manage Cortisol Spikes**


Px, or paraxanthine, is a metabolite of caffeine—meaning your body naturally converts some of the caffeine you consume into paraxanthine. Like caffeine, Px increases alertness, but early research points to Px having fewer stress-related side effects -- like sharp adrenaline spikes and increased cortisol levels. This makes it a gentler option for people who are looking for a morning (or mid-afternoon) coffee without caffeine's harsh effects on the nervous system.


Px coffee delivers energy and alertness, but without hijacking your body's stress response.
