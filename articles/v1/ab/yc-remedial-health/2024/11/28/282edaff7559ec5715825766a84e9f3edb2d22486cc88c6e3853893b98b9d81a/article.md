# The Impact of Supply Chain Disruptions on Healthcare Providers

### by Kolade Adebiyi


On my way to work this morning, I found myself eavesdropping on two mothers chatting in the back of the bus. Their voices carried the frustration so many people feel these days. One mother was lamenting how difficult it had become to find Silverbird oil (eucalyptus oil) for her baby, while the other added that even when she did manage to find it, the quality wasn’t as effective as it used to be. Listening to their conversation, I immediately empathized, both as a regular consumer and as a supply chain analyst. This wasn’t just an isolated incident. It’s a story I have heard repeatedly, a story that reflects a much bigger issue.


Eucalyptus oil, known as Silverbird oil in Nigeria, is a household remedy many of us grew up with. Whether it’s a cold, cough, or the flu, this oil has been a go-to for generations. It’s a staple for families trying to soothe their kids’ respiratory troubles or looking for quick relief from common ailments. But lately, it’s been disappearing from store shelves. Produced by Bell, Sons & Co. in the UK, this once abundant remedy has become increasingly scarce, leaving both healthcare providers and everyday people in a difficult position.


The scarcity stems from various disruptions in the global supply chain. In the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, industries worldwide experienced severe delays (Springer), and the production of essential items like Silverbird oil was hit hard. Factories were short on raw materials, and workers were either laid off or unable to return to their jobs, causing a ripple effect that still lingers today. As a result, fewer shipments of pharmaceutical products and other imported goods made their way to countries like Nigeria.


Even as manufacturers slowly ramped up production again, the challenges didn’t stop there. The cost of shipping skyrocketed, and Nigerian ports, notorious for their congestion, added further delays. Importers struggled to get goods cleared, meaning products like Silverbird oil and other pharmaceuticals spent more time stuck at the docks than in the hands of people who desperately needed them.


Compounding the issue is the complexity of Nigeria’s regulatory landscape. The National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) is responsible for ensuring that all imported products meet safety and quality standards, which is essential for public health. However, the sheer volume of imports and the sometimes cumbersome inspection process can mean that it takes much longer to get goods into the market. The intention is good for consumer protection but it sometimes inadvertently contributes to longer lead times for essential products.


Another factor hitting close to home is the economic situation in Nigeria. The devaluation of the naira has made imports far more expensive than they used to be (Punch NG). A product that once felt affordable is now out of reach for many. Pharmaceutical companies, dealing with rising costs, are importing fewer bottles of eucalyptus oil and other medications. Fewer bottles mean higher prices and higher prices mean families like those mothers on the bus are struggling to find or afford a remedy they have relied on for years.


The consequences of this scarcity are widespread. Healthcare providers, who have long recommended Silverbird oil for its natural healing properties, are now turning to alternatives. But these alternatives often come with their own set of challenges ranging from prices, unfamiliarity, or ineffectiveness. And then there’s the issue of counterfeits. Whenever there’s high demand and low supply, you can bet that counterfeiters will step in. Many people, desperate for relief, end up buying fake or substandard products from the open market, which could do more harm than good. This isn’t just an inconvenience, it’s a real threat to health and safety.


What’s the way out of this?


It’s not an easy fix, but some steps can help. One solution is encouraging local production. If Nigeria can start manufacturing its essential oils, it would reduce our reliance on imports, making products like eucalyptus oil more available and more affordable. At the same time, speeding up import processes and improving port efficiency could help reduce some of the current bottlenecks, getting products to people faster.


Another key step is building resilience in the[supply chain](https://medium.com/@remedialhealth/how-to-use-remedial-rx-to-fix-supply-chain-challenges-in-africa-752dab7b8057) . By sourcing products from multiple regions and working with diverse suppliers, companies can avoid being overly dependent on one country or one supply line. This way, even when disruptions happen, they’re better prepared to maintain a steady flow of goods into the country.


Listening to those mothers on the bus was a powerful reminder of how these larger global issues directly impact our everyday lives. The shortage of Silverbird oil is just one example of how fragile supply chains can disrupt access to basic healthcare products.
