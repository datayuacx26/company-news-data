# What’s Your Worst Makeshift Repair?

Makeshift repairs are intended to be temporary, and the worst examples often suggest that our efforts were in vain. The definition of the “Worst Makeshift Repair” could also be the opposite: something performed with the intention of being temporary can last far longer than expected. Perhaps something was overtorqued in haste, or the wrong adhesive was used in a pinch. Doing so ensures that performing the correct repair suddenly makes the process far more complicated. Either way, makeshift repairs are good at making themselves known in the future.


I have made several such repairs, and most were regrettable weeks (if not days) later. Even better, I am performing a resolution to a makeshift repair as I write this very story. You could say the content here at Hagerty Media is influenced by real-life events, but sometimes timing is everything.

Like every summer down here in Houston, Texas, driving a classic car on a cloud-free weekend can be concerning if you lack air conditioning. One such day arrived earlier this season, and I was in the mood to take my[1989 Lincoln Continental](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/the-essex-continental-is-a-boomerang-coming-back-home/) out for a drive to a local car meet. I turned on the HVAC control panel as I exited my driveway and was rewarded with hot air from the vents, which were stuck in the defrost mode.


I am no stranger to Fords of this era, and the HVAC system defaults to defrost when it either loses engine vacuum or a signal from the control panel. I drove back into the garage, opened the hood, removed the modesty panel affixed to the firewall, and found a broken plastic vacuum line that runs inside the cabin. “Diagnostic complete!” I yelled to myself. All it took was a hose clamp and a short length of vacuum hose to patch up the leak and get the A/C back into fighting form.


I am continually impressed with how cold the air conditioner is in this car, even below 1000 rpm and with the newer R134a refrigerant. But all the love was lost last weekend when the Continental started misbehaving again. So on Monday morning I took another quick look, finding that my hose clamp had snapped the plastic hose inside it. I spent a little bit longer searching for the correct vacuum line fitting, and it sealed tightly against both the T-fitting and the remainder of the hose that goes inside the cabin.


While I haven’t driven the Continental yet, a quick test in my garage suggests the problem has been resolved. First, I heard the HVAC box adjust itself to the presence of engine vacuum. After pressing the “MAX A/C” button, I was rewarded with cold air from the front vents.


Perhaps I am overly optimistic that this result is more permanent, but the question to you remains: **What’s Your Worst Makeshift Repair?**
