# A Primer on Car Batteries

One of the components in our cars that we take most for granted is the battery—at least, until that morning when you come to start your classic and find it has packed up, often with no prior warning.


But not all batteries are equal. Where, historically, older cars used simple electrical systems, modern classics are more sophisticated. Most will use battery power even when they’re not being driven, which can create what’s known as[a parasitic drain](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/articles/how-to-deal-with-a-flat-battery/) —a small amount of power required to keep items such as the immobilizer or the clock working. (That’s a good reason to look into using[a trickle charger](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/battery-chargers-and-maintainers-how-they-work/) or battery conditioner if your car isn’t in daily use.)


So, what are the different types of car battery, what do the numbers and letters on a battery mean, and how do you choose the best battery for your classic?


## How Do Car Batteries Work?


Optima


In simple terms, a car battery is no different from those you put in your TV remote control: They all work by[converting chemical energy into electricity](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/a-charged-debate-battery-maintenance-tech-explained/) . For the most part, car batteries are lead-acid units containing six cells, each housing lead and lead dioxide plates in an electrolyte solution made of sulfuric acid and deionized water. They generate electricity through an exchange of ions between the lead and the plates. Each battery cell produces approximately two volts of electricity, which is why most car batteries are rated as 12 volts.


Like all batteries, those in cars have a finite lifecycle—normally three-to-five years but dependent on usage, climate, and if the battery is ever left to fully discharge, which isn’t a great idea, as it will never fully recover.


You can check the age of your battery from either a label on the side of the unit that will show the month and year of its manufacture (0821, for example, means August 2021), or from a similar reference stamped into the battery casing.


## Types of Car Battery


Rob Siegel


Lead-acid batteries are the most common automotive batteries and come in two varieties: standard or calcium.


A standard lead-acid battery should last for around 20,000 starts, but they are susceptible to neglect and can suffer a build-up of sulphates that causes the internal plates to corrode. If you use your car regularly, though, a standard lead-acid battery will be perfectly fine and should last well.


Calcium lead-acid batteries contain a calcium alloy that reduces water loss and prevents corrosion. They’re usually around 25–30% more expensive to buy, but will last for about 30,000 starts and provide around 18% more starting power. They’re a better bet for bigger engines or cars with complex electrical systems.


You might also find an “AGM” battery in the right size for your car. AGM stands for “Absorbent Glass Mat” and uses fiberglass mats that absorb the electrolyte solution in each cell. They are faster to recharge and have a longer life but can often be twice the price of a lead-acid equivalent.


More recently, 12V lithium car batteries have become an alternative option to traditional lead-acid types, and while more suited to modern vehicles and motorsport (where their lighter weight makes them popular), they offer higher energy density and more consistent power delivery, while they also hold charge better. Lithium batteries also boast a longer lifespan, typically enduring more charge cycles before performance drops—just like your mobile phone, which uses the same technology. However, they can be a lot more expensive up front, and not all vehicles’ charging systems are compatible without modification.


## Which Car Battery Should You Buy?


This motorcycle battery provides enough power for a little pickup truck.


Paul Stenquist


The specific battery size for your car should be listed in the owner’s manual, but if you don’t have one then the best bet is to measure the battery tray, note which way around the terminals are and source one that will fit.


For example, a 1970s or ’80s Mini was originally designed to have an 38-type battery, but a larger 63 fits in the tray and will do just fine. A 14 will fit, too, but will sit slightly proud. Those numbers, incidentally, just define a battery type, and there’s no science behind them; it’s a common part number, or[Group Size](https://knowhow.napaonline.com/car-battery-sizes-does-it-matter/) , in battery jargon, that tells you the battery has a casing and terminal layout of set dimensions.


Before buying a new battery, check your owner’s manual and make sure it meets the needs of your make and model. Some cars will accept more than one group size; a Chevy Nova, for example, will take several sizes, with larger ones recommended for colder climates. A Jaguar XJ8 is similar—a 19 is the recommended fit, but a smaller 96 will do in warmer climes, and the battery tray has bracket mounts for both types. Be careful here, though, because if you have a diesel, then the likelihood is that a smaller battery is no good; they generally require more cranking power.


The next thing to check is the capacity in amp-hours, or Ah, which is displayed on the battery casing. This is the battery’s reserve capacity, or the total amount of power it will hold. A good-quality 19 battery (for large cars) will have around 100Ah capacity, whereas a budget one will have 80Ah.


For a 63 (as commonly found in smaller cars), high-quality batteries will have around 50Ah capacity, mid-range will have 40–45Ah, and budget ones will have 35Ah.


If you’re doing reasonable-length journeys, a lower-capacity battery will be fine, as the car’s alternator will replenish any capacity used by starting, but for frequent stopping and starting, or in modern cars with stop-start systems, a higher-capacity battery is more important.


Next, the battery casing will show its CCA, which stands for Cold Cranking Amps and is a measure of the battery’s ability to start the engine at 0 degrees Fahrenheit without dropping below a specified voltage; the higher the CCA rating, the better the battery will perform in low temperatures. This is particularly useful for vehicles in colder climates or those with larger engines that require more power to start.


Some batteries will also show the reserve capacity—this is the amount of time, in minutes, that a battery can deliver consistent voltage (around 25 amps) before it can no longer power your vehicle and needs to be recharged. A good 19 battery will run for 120 to 150 minutes in reserve, which is worth knowing in case your alternator packs up; if you’re only an hour from home, you might just make it.


## Batteries for Older Classics


If you have an older classic and value an original look, you can also search for a vintage-style 6-volt battery.


These are designed to replicate the look and functionality of the original units fitted to vehicles manufactured before the widespread adoption of 12V electrical systems and are typically pre-1960s.


Their signature black casing, often with hard rubber or Bakelite finish, makes them particularly sought-after for concours-standard restoration projects and enthusiasts who value period-correct details. You can often find them at farm supply stores, as well as battery specialists. They’re less powerful than modern batteries and generally more expensive but can add a nice period detail to a quality restoration.
