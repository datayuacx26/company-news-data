# The Last Piece of (and About) the Lotus’ Air Conditioning

It was nearly a year ago that I wrote[my first piece discussing the pros and cons of electric air conditioning compressors](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/why-retrofitting-electric-air-conditioning-is-scarcely-a-slam-dunk/) . In it, I said pretty stridently that a big con is that much of the PR for electric A/C is an actual “big con”—that is, anyone who claims that an electric compressor is more efficient than a belt-driven one, or that it doesn’t create a drag on the engine, is wrong. An electric compressor is spun by an electric motor, which is fed AC current by an inverter, which is fed DC current by an alternator, which is spun by a belt on the crankshaft pulley, and the laws of physics tell you that it cannot possibly be more efficient than a compressor that is directly coupled to the engine. If it has an advantage, it can’t possibly be “efficiency.”


However,[a month later](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/lotus-elan-2-air-conditioning-project-the-electric-compressor/) , I began installing one anyway in my 1969 Lotus Elan +2 because there appeared to be no way to mount a conventional compressor and run it off the engine without likely paying a thousand bucks for a machinist-fabricated bracket and a two-groove pulley. Well, that plus I became insatiably curious whether, efficiency or no, with all the other variables, an electric compressor could be part of a system that blasts enough cold air in your face to be useful (which is the ultimate test any A/C system has to pass). And I knew it would generate a lot of interesting content.


While the compressor is the beating heart of an air conditioning system, another biggie is the evaporator assembly—the component that lives inside the passenger compartment, lets the refrigerant expand and cool, and blows the resulting cold air at the occupants. I realized that the space constraints in the cabin were similar to those in the engine compartment. The only detailed post I could find on the Lotus Elan forum about an evaporator installation showed a Vintage Air Gen II Mini system shoehorned behind the dash. I could’ve done this when I[removed my dash to de-mouse the heater box](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/de-mousing-prince-harry/) 18 months ago, but c’est la vie.


I salute you and the path you blazed, sir, but I do not wish to follow in your footsteps. At least not today.


Crispy/lotuselan.net


Since, at the time, I didn’t even know whether the electrically driven approach would be successful, I went with the simplest, least-expensive evaporator approach possible: I bought a universal under-dash evaporator assembly on Temu for $48 and[installed it in the passenger-side footwell](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/lotus-elan-2-air-conditioning-project-the-lowdown-on-a-high-output-alternator/) . I knew full well that, down the road, there might be an issue with it not blowing enough cold air directly at me, but I figured that I’d not only cross that bridge when I came to it, I’d also be overjoyed if I reached the point in the journey when that was the only thing separating me from the land of air-conditioned driving bliss.


At long last, in early July, after addressing some other issues, I arrived at The Bridge of Direct Airflow. I drove the car in hot weather with the windows up and the A/C on, and determined unequivocally that while the evaporator was blowing nice cold air, there wasn’t enough of it reaching me for it to be useful. While this wasn’t surprising—I doubt that the A/C in *any* vintage car gets the cabin cold enough that you don’t need it continuously blasting directly on your bare skin—it meant that I finally had to do something about my cheap footwell-mounted evaporator. (Just in case it’s not obvious, air can’t blow sideways through louvered vents —adjusting them all the way to one side simply closes them).


Now, I was an engineer for 35 years, and I can’t help but make lists of options. From the least to the most invasive, this is what I came up with. The expensive, invasive, and time-consuming installation of a behind-the-dash evaporator, like the Vintage Air system shown above, and running hoses to either the existing in-dash vents or cutting new ones was the last option on the list. Fortunately, I had success before I got there.


**Option 1: Redirect the airflow with some sort of diverter placed in front of the evaporator vents.** There are oodles of redirection flaps for window and floor air conditioning available on Amazon, but none were suitable for my application. I saw a cardboard box I was about to recycle that was just slightly bigger than the front of the evaporator, and a light bulb went on. I cut off one end, unfolded the other flaps, jammed them above and to the right of the evaporator, and turned the blower on. I was absolutely astonished at how well it redirected the air at me. Unfortunately, it hit me in the chest, not the face, and to get it higher, I needed to pull up the end of the box (I used zip ties suspended from the rear-view mirror). I drove the car around the block in hot weather with the A/C on and the windows up, and for the very first time, was in cool air-conditioned Lotus-driving comfort and style.


Hey, you laugh, but…

A part of me felt that this approach was completely within my Hack Mechanic ethos and I could live happily with it for the duration of the summer, but this Elan +2 is a lovely little car, and it deserved better than a blatant kluge that I’d need to apologize for at every turn. I kept looking for a less hacky way to redirect the air, but the need for it to be canted upward seemed to require bendable or pre-molded ductwork, and I never found anything that fit the bill.


… it was utterly astonishing how well this worked.


Rob Siegel


**Option 2: Blow the cold air at me with another fan** . It was difficult to let go of the zero-cost cardboard box that worked so unbelievably well, but I tried the hit-the-cold-air-with-a-fan-from-the-side idea next. I didn’t really expect it to work, as it would be diluting the cold air with still-warm cabin air, but I had two leftover fans from when I[installed the condenser last fall](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/lotus-elan-2-air-conditioning-project-the-first-step/) , so why not try, right? Unfortunately, these are puller fans, and although you can reverse the polarity to make them push, they’re very inefficient that way. A big no.


Well, I tried.


Rob Siegel


**Option 3: Stick some combination of vents and hoses on the existing evaporator assembly.** It seemed like I should be able to mount *something* over the vents—either individually or together—to route the air in my direction. I looked in hardware stores and on Amazon at everything from shop vac attachments to rain gutter downspout extensions, and bought a 9-inch-wide brake duct that was big enough to cover both vents, and a furnace coupling that had two swiveling degrees of freedom. I held it in place and found that it was pretty effective at concentrating the air flow at me, but it was ungainly, stuck out way too far, and would’ve looked ridiculous unless I was consciously going for steampunk, which I certainly wasn’t.


Yeah, no.


Rob Siegel


(On the subject of vent extensions, since this is a family publication, I won’t include the link or the photo, but let’s just say that if you search Amazon for “Jewel cooler A/C vent extension,” you’ll get an eyeful. No, I didn’t buy one.)


**Option 4: Rotate the evaporator assembly sideways** . This would look stupid, but would almost certainly be effective. Whether it would be easy depended on how much slack in the refrigerant hoses there was. I wasn’t optimistic, but I disconnected the evaporator box from the three brackets that hang it from the underside of the dash and twisted it as far as I could. Unfortunately, that wasn’t remotely far enough to be able to blow air at me. I could lengthen the hoses, but that would mean having to evacuate the refrigerant. I tabled the idea. (Note that when I installed the evaporator, I tried leaving extra length in the hoses so it could be rotated if necessary, but refrigerant hoses are very stiff. You can’t just coil them up like extra wire.)


**Option 5: Replace it with a ducted system, mount it in the footwell, and aim the hoses at me without it looking like a scene from Terry Gilliam’s film *Brazil.*** Evaporator assemblies come in three basic form factors—1) finished-looking under-dash- units with controls and aimable vents on the front like the one I already had, 2) unfinished-looking units with the same form factor but with round ports to which hoses and/or nozzles can be affixed, and 3) units like the Vintage Air system shown above that are oddly shaped and are meant to be completely hidden behind the dash. Like with electric radiator fans, you might see a Cubic Feet per Minute (CFM) rating for the fan in the evaporator, but unless you’re buying something American-made from one of the vintage car A/C specialty shops (e.g., Vintage Air, Old Air Products, Restomod Air, etc) that costs five times what an unbranded Chinese-made evaporator assembly does, you really have no guarantee how hard it’s going to blow. Plus, I didn’t know exactly what I’d *do* with round ports. Clamp a hose to one of them, block the other ones off, suspend it from the mirror, and aim it at my face? It didn’t seem feasible to mount a box like this in the passenger footwell with the hoses facing the cabin and then try to route hoses behind the dash so they’d blow out through the original fresh air vents on the dashboard, and I certainly wasn’t going to cut any new holes in that beautiful wood.


Still, round ports seemed like they’d buy me options, even if I didn’t know what those were yet. I saw what were clearly versions of my evaporator box but with a front cover that had four round ports in it. Then, on the Qualy Air website (the company I bought the electric compressor from), I saw an evaporator with *both* front covers. I asked Qualy if they could sell me *only* the front cover with the four ports, but unfortunately they couldn’t. They also said that that particular evaporator was 24V, not 12V, but they could send me a 12V unit that had the four-port front cover.


Evidence that I was correct that both front covers could fit on my current evaporator box.


Qualy Air


While I was thinking about that, I began wondering whether I could modify my front cover to put circular vents on it. And then I remembered that I actually had another identical-looking unit that was configured as a heater box (a friend had bought it by mistake and had given it to me years ago). So I had a spare front cover I could try to modify without destroying the original one. I pulled it off and yanked the louvered vents out of it.


Operation modify-the-spare-front-cover begins.


Rob Siegel


Several trips to Home Depot later, I decided to cut a piece of plastic to cover up the rectangular openings, drill two holes in it, and screw in two slip-joint 1 ½-inch PVC trap adapters. The beauty of using trap adapters (the unscrewable piece you connect to a drain elbow so you can take the thing off if there’s a clog or a lost wedding ring) is twofold. First, they present a male flange on which the female end of an elbow pipe can simply be slid onto and twisted up and down to aim it, though it can’t move fore and aft. Second, unlike a cut-off piece of straight PVC pipe, you don’t need to glue the trap adapters in the holes. Instead, they can be mounted by passing the threaded part through the hole, spinning the big nut onto the back, and using a thick rubber O-ring to fill the gap.


Yeah, I didn’t know what a “trap adapter” was either.


Rob Siegel


As I began assembling things, I realized that the face of the front cover was curved, so the plastic plate I fabricated wouldn’t come close to sealing until I belt-sanded the cover flat.


Test-fitting the adapter plate.


Rob Siegel


With the replacement front cover fabricated, I swapped covers and bought 45-, 60-, and 90-degree elbows to find which ones maximized airflow at the driver.


Off with your face.


Rob Siegel


Sitting in the garage with the blower fan on, I found that using one 45-degree and one 60-degree elbow blew a lot of air at me, comparable with the cardboard box. However, I absolutely hated the look of the white PVC components, as there is nothing else on the dashboard that’s white. Reading online revealed that painting this kind of white PVC tubing black and having the paint stick is not easy. Still, I had nearly accepted a cardboard box as a workable solution, and this was worlds better.


Almost there.


Rob Siegel


When I was satisfied that this approach would work, I searched for black versions of the PVC components, and was delighted to find that they were available from homedepot.com with free shipping. When they arrived, I permanently epoxied the adapter plate onto the modified front cover, screwed in the black trap adapters, put the front cover back on the evaporator, reattached the unit to its brackets, mounted the black elbows, and on an 85-degree day, took the ’69 Lotus Elan +2 on its first actual pleasure drive with the windows up and the A/C on.


Not bad, right?


Rob Siegel


And?


She’s good! I had to switch to two 45-degree elbows (the amount of air on me is *very* sensitive to the angle), but with them installed, the system, even with the passenger-footwell-mounted evaporator, blows enough cold air directly at me to be useful. Booya! Yes, there are limits like there are on A/C in any old car—it *always* has to be on high and blowing straight at your face; it’s *never* going to get the interior cold, and I doubt I’ll drive it on 100-degree days. Plus, the high-output alternator stops charging below about 1200 rpm, so at stop lights, I do the left-foot shuffle, putting it on the brake so I can keep my right foot a bit in the gas (challenging with the Lotus’ pedals so close together). But for about $20 worth of black PVC, the Lotus’ A/C has officially been dragged over the line from functional to performing. Booya a second time!


Of course, there are a few more things to fiddle with. The first is that, when Maire Anne saw the two elbows, she chided, “So, all the cold air will blow on you, but none on me?” This was mostly a joke, as my darling and incredibly understanding wife doesn’t really share my love of smelly old vintage cars, and I’d need to have a really good reason to press her into a windows-up summer road trip in the Elan +2. She did, though, have a point, and if simply removing the right-hand elbow doesn’t direct enough air at her, I bought a pair of $11 circular louvered vents, one of which can be put in the right-side circular port to fine-tune the aim. (And yes, the irony that the original vents on the evaporator blew on the passenger just fine isn’t lost on me).


This option is likely to never be used, but it’s in the glove box just in case.


Rob Siegel


The second issue is that I really don’t like the Gilmer drive-toothed pulley set I spent a lot of time and money installing to address the issue of belt tension on the Elan engine’s fragile water pump. It putt-putts like a motorboat at low load, chatters when the electric A/C is on, and has an[alignment issue I still haven’t solved](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/a-surprising-diagnosis/) . If I could make the system work without it, I’d gladly get rid of it.


So, as we cross the finish line nearly a year after I began the Elan +2 A/C project, here’s my big wrap-up:


- Even though an electric A/C compressor cannot be as thermodynamically efficient as a belt-driven compressor, an A/C system with an electric compressor and inexpensive generic evaporator and condenser components *can* create and blow enough cold air directly at you to be usable in a vintage car.
- Don’t ever confuse “blowing cold air directly at you” with “cooling the cabin.” Vintage cars are different beasts than modern cars. Even if they have a tiny interior, the air leaks, lack of insulation, greenhouse of windows, and black materials make cooling the space a nearly insurmountable task.
- Even though I now have the system working, my advice remains what I said months ago: If you can easily mount a conventional compressor to the engine block and run it with a belt off the crankshaft, do it. The only reason I went with the electric compressor was that, on the Elan +2, there was no way I could see to do this without custom machine work. But I now have two grand into this project—more than twice what a conventional system would’ve cost. The compressor was inexpensive, but other things needed for the installation nickel-and-dimed me to death.


Stay cool, my friends.
