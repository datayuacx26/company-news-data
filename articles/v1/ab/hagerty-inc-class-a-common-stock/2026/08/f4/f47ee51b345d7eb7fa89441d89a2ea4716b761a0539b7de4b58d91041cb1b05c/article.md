# Biography of Porsche Co-Founder Adolf Rosenberger Sheds Essential New Light

In 2018, we published an article detailing the[history of Porsche’s unsung founding partner—Adolf Rosenberger](https://www.hagerty.com/media/people/the-story-of-adolf-rosenberger/) . Most people have never heard of Rosenberger, a German Jew, an accomplished racer, businessman, and early investor in Porsche’s nascent years. We outlined Rosenberger’s rise as a driver in the world of motorsport, role as managing director and partner of the Porsche design office in the early 1930s, later work representing the company’s foreign operations in Europe, internment in a concentration camp, and ultimate forced sale of his ownership stake in 1935 amid the rise of Nazism. He eventually emigrated to the United States, where he changed his name to Alan Robert. Settling in California, he repeatedly tried, and struggled, to achieve success until his death in 1967. The story’s writer, Don Sherman, expressed a desire for “Rosenberger’s life and times \[to\] finally get its due.”


Atelier Binder/Getty Images


That moment has come. Earlier this year, independent historian Dr. Joachim Scholtyseck published the first scholarly work on Adolf Rosenberger. *Driven Out—Adolf Rosenberger* is fundamentally a biography, covering Rosenberger’s life beginning with his upbringing and cultural milieu in Pforzheim, Germany. The work is underpinned by Scholtyseck’s complete access to Porsche’s archives, as well as access to Rosenberger’s previously untapped personal archives—the latter in the hands of Dr. Sandra Esslinger, his surviving relative.


Esslinger, Rosenberger’s cousin twice removed, helped found the Adolf Rosenberger gGmbH in 2020. Though Rosenberger had been touched on in prior historical research, the nonprofit determined more was warranted and approached Porsche in 2021. The two parties together agreed on Dr. Scholtyseck, of the University of Bonn, as the primary researcher and study author.


Prof. Dr. Joachim Scholtyseck, historian; Dr. Sandra Esslinger, of Adolf Rosenberger gGmbH; Achim Stejskal, Head of Porsche Heritage and Museum.


Courtesy Porsche


To learn more about the book, we sat down for a roundtable interview with Dr. Scholtyseck, Dr. Esslinger, Christoph Rueckel (lawyer for the Adolf Rosenberger nonprofit), and representatives from Porsche. We also received an advance copy to read.


“The independent study is an integral contribution to the culture of remembrance,” Achim Stejskal, Head of Porsche Heritage and Museum, said in a statement. **“** Porsche addresses its own past extensively and in depth, and faces up to its responsibility,” says Stejskal. “We are strongly opposed to exclusion, discrimination, and antisemitism.”


Porsche in part funded the study. Thus, going into the conversation, my biggest question was Porsche’s role—if any—in guiding the scholarly research, results, or subsequent analysis. According to Esslinger, as well as Porsche Head of Archives Frank Jung, the two parties agreed on 19 points of study for the research to examine. “\[Dr. Scholtyseck\] was free to write whatever,” explains Jung. “From the beginning, we accepted whatever would be the outcome… We were in contact with our head of the press department as well as informing members of the board and the advisory board. We had the full backup of the whole Porsche company.”


Prof. Dr. Joachim Scholtyseck


Courtesy Porsche


For Scholtyseck, the book was an opportunity to shed light on a fascinating figure. “Adolf Rosenberger was really unknown even in Germany. I think it’s very important that this co-founder of Porsche, who has been totally overlooked, is now being discussed,” says Scholtyseck. “He was really a person of the 20th century—a racer, a soldier, a member of a Jewish merchant family about which little was previously known. So it’s a book in many ways about modern Jewish life in the Weimar Republic and the horrible years of the Third Reich and during the Holocaust. What is important is to show him not only in the context of Porsche, but in the context of the 20th century.”


The book is extraordinarily detailed. From the first page, it paints a textured backdrop of Rosenberger’s family’s upper-middle-class merchant environment. (His family owned hardware stores and movie theaters.) Scholtyseck describes Rosenberger’s acclaim in the new sport of racing—both cars and motorcycles—and establishes the extent to which he was a known and beloved figure in 1920s Germany. It combines contextual information from the period, such as contemporary news articles, with deeply personal artifacts, such as military awards, photographs, and letters. As the story develops and Rosenberger’s technical acumen and partnership with Ferdinand Porsche and fellow co-founder Anton Piëch come into the picture, Scholtyseck weaves in company records as quotidian as chief designer Karl Rabe’s travel itinerary and as monumental as the original notarized contract that began the Porsche company in 1931.


Rosenberger seated at the wheel of a Mercedes, circa 1927.


Ullstein Bild/Getty Images


It is, naturally, a work of academia and as such it is a dense piece of writing. But it is far from stilted or anodyne; Scholtyseck’s voice is always present, supplying a human and at times sharply pointed perspective. Here’s how he characterizes Porsche’s archival records in the book:


> *“The holdings of the Porsche AG Corporate Archive provide* *a central basis for Adolf Rosenberger’s work in the Stuttgart design* *office. However, these files are extremely patchy. After the design office* *moved from Kronenstrasse to Spitalwaldstrasse in 1938, numerous* *files were stored in the attic. After the war began, they were moved* *as a precaution to the basement, which was considered fireproof, but* *they were destroyed there in 1944 during a bombing raid. Files were* *lost due to numerous relocations and probably also as a result of postwar* *attempts to sanitize the files.”*


That energy—not just about the subject but about the nuances of Scholtyseck’s research—enlivens what could otherwise be reduced to a stack of dusty records. Once you spend a bit of time with the book, it’s easy to sink into its rhythm and feel transported across the Atlantic, back in time.


Rosenberger (#2) participated in the inaugural race weekend on the Nürburgring, circa 1927.


/Picture Alliance/Getty Images


Esslinger noted the way the study respectfully handles her family’s legacy, which has its share of bitter memories. She holds a PhD in Art History with a focus on Nazi propaganda, so she is familiar with notions of erasure and remembrance in a historical context. “For me, he’s representative—his voice, his story—is representative of millions of lost voices. The cooperation between Porsche and the Adolf Rosenberger nonprofit came together and was exemplary of what can happen to raise a victim’s voice for recognition. The difference between approaches to German corporate history and American corporate history is very different because of World War II. There is a combination of approaches, some of which are commissioned and intended to clean up the history. This is not one of those.”


In her 20s, while in graduate school and working for *Motor Trend* , Esslinger became the custodian of her family’s records. All of it was in boxes, containing materials in German, French, and English. Some paperwork had Porsche letterhead, or “Ferry” Porsche’s signature.


Ferdinand Porsche (L) and Karl Rabe at the drawing board, circa 1938.


Porsche


“It wasn’t until I was approached by a journalist that I actually dug deeper into \[it all\]. And it was sort of a time when somebody was willing to listen. It was part of my bucket list, to tell the story. He made a huge impact on the automotive industry. This history is part of a public trust that had never been fully heard. It’s part of the lessons that we need to learn today. This story overall, and I just sort of need to say this as it comes out, is a story of antisemitism at its very, very heart. This is a person who was impacted by antisemitism on every level of his life.”


There’s so much more in the book itself—revelations about the grim financial picture that pushed Rosenberger to resign as managing director in 1933; context about his subsequent relocation to Paris to represent Porsche’s business (but also to get away from growing anti-Jewish sentiment in Germany); circumstances surrounding his committal into the Kislau concentration camp and his business partners’ efforts to intervene with Nazi authorities and secure his release; and Rosenberger’s failed postwar campaign to reestablish U.S.-based business relations with Porsche, which he persisted in even after the two parties reached a settlement agreement in reparations proceedings. (Scholtyseck writes that “the impression that had stuck in Zuffenhausen was that they were dealing with a man who would never be satisfied and who was better kept at arm’s length. It was more lucrative for Porsche to have the enterprising Max Hoffman as a \[U.S.\] partner.”)


The green light for the now world-famous Volkswagen “Beetle” was given in June 1934, when the “Reichsverband der Deutschen Automobilindustrie” (RDA) officially awarded Ferdinand Porsche the contract to design and build German “Volkswagen” prototypes.


Porsche


I highly recommend the book to anyone with an interest in history, particularly of Porsche, business, or 20th-century Europe. And for racing buffs, the author laments the way Rosenberger’s ostracization meant he was forgotten, unlike his heroic contemporaries: Rudolf Caracciola, Hans Stuck, and Bernd Rosemeyer. For Esslinger, of course, it’s a deeply personal matter.


“This is a story that I was raised with,” she says. “And the stories of Adolf Rosenberger, who we referred to as Uncle Al, were given to me and passed down to me. And Anna Junkert, who was Adolf Rosenberger’s wife, was more like a grandmother to me… he died two weeks after I was born. I think the torch was passed.”
