# Picture Car Confidential #45: Slate First Drive

A little over a month ago, I joined the assembled members of the Fourth Estate in Carson, California, at Slate Auto’s design headquarters. We were there for a “first ride” in the soon-to-be-launched Slate pickup. Like devotees of TV’s *The Bachelor* , *Love Island,* or *Love is Blind* (alternate titles: TV Hookup Shows are Stupid), the lot of us could be heard pontificating animatedly on the small electric truck’s prospects. Would America find true love for this perky, small, and decidedly spartan vehicle?


Slate’s truck has more in common with the bare-bones small pickups of the 1970s—think Toyota Hi-Lux, Datsun 620, Chevy Luv, Dodge Ram 50, Isuzu Pup, and Ford Courier—than the huge bruisers currently shuttling people to their offices and grocery stores. These 2WD small ones once sold in large quantities to millions of happy customers. What changed? Why’d they ever leave?


World-weary scribblers like yours truly and younger, generally more cheerful influencers and YouTube personalities in attendance wrestled with conflicting ideas about who would buy the truck—which seemed pretty good in theory to most of us—and whether or not there will be enough buyers ready to justify the Slate executive team’s enthusiasm. The automaker reports 180,000[deposits](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/slate-first-shift/) , with 10,000 purchase orders and counting. Still, it’s a big number in light of its Indiana factory’s capacity to build up to 150,000 units a year, with a break-even point of 75,000 units.


Jamie Kitman


Recall that Tesla once claimed to have a million deposits for its controversial and much more expensive Cybertruck, most of which failed to materialize as firm orders. The Slate pickup, with prices starting at $24,950 (plus a delivery charge of $1450), is the anti-Cybertruck in most every respect: size, style, price, and performance. For those of us who feel our trucks have gotten too big, too fast, too complicated and expensive, these are all potential points in the Slate’s favor. The simple little rig could also become the anti-Cybertruck in the most important way of all: sales success.


All queried at that first ride event agreed that the small truck market was indeed woefully underserved in the U.S., ripe for new entries, and that such models were better suited to electrification than large pickups. Supplies of the gasoline-powered Ford Maverick, its most natural small pickup competitor today, have been constrained, and Slate could benefit. Or maybe not. And, more pressing, the additional competition arriving from Ford in the form of a new downsized EV pickup (targeted to debut with a starting price tag in the vicinity of $30,000) could get in Slate’s way.


Jamie Kitman


Though it will be perfectly adequate for many use cases, Slate’s stated maximum range of 205 miles (recently revised upward to that mark) might comfort many buyers’ range anxiety were it greater. The sole battery offering is now a lithium-iron-phosphate pack delivering 65 kW.


While I counted myself among those on the optimistic side, I had to concede that day in Carson that the proof would be in the pudding. And that certain essential ingredients of that pudding remained to be seen. Build quality, for instance, and the viability of a promised, yet-to-be-detailed service network meant to boast more than 4000 points nationwide. Most important, what would it be like to drive?


I can answer the last one, now that I’ve driven the truck, spending almost an hour last week wheeling a preproduction example in and around that New England bastion of old money, Newport, Rhode Island. The test vehicle was built at Slate’s brownfield site in Warsaw, Indiana, a repurposed factory that used to print J.C. Penney catalogs.


In short, I was pleasantly surprised.


Slate Auto


Exterior fit of the standard grey vehicle appeared flawless. Preproduction interior plastics left something to be desired, though Slate PR boss, Jeff Jablansky, assured me that they will be significantly upgraded by the time production vehicles leave the factory in the fall. Climbing in, the interior was *molto* basic, grey and simple, a throwback to days when most car windows were operated like the Slate’s: by a hand crank. The “ignition” key slots into a dashboard receiver, like older Volvos before key slots went away. Stick the key in and you are ready to drive. Adjust your mirrors manually and select drive or reverse with a petite, plastic column shifter.


Exiting a parking garage, I was heartened to find that outward visibility, lacking in so many vehicles today, is superior. To be honest, this is not just good, it’s essential, because the reversing camera screen is easily the smallest one I’ve seen in years, now that dashboards with screens as large as sports-bar televisions are commonplace. The Slate’s dashboard display itself is a compact, strictly no-nonsense affair, positioned so only the driver can see it, with throwback graphics that remind one of the 4-bit screens from ‘80s computers and video games, minus the colors. They share essential information only: speed, range, percentage of battery charge. Standard air conditioning is sufficiently powerful. While an optional audio system has been slotted into the bare-bones dashboard in this prototype, along with an optional glovebox door, one can also make do with a Bluetooth speaker and the standard dashboard phone mount.


Slate Automotive


Despite being two feet shorter than Ford’s Maverick, the Slate pickup takes maximum advantage of having just two doors and no back seat, with a longer pickup bed (5 feet versus 4.5 feet in the Ford) with marginally greater cubic capacity. Against this, its towing capacity is not as great, though hardly useless at 2000 pounds (1884 pounds with the optional, $5000 kit that turns a Slate pickup into an SUV). Payload is 1550 pounds—acceptable for many uses currently handled by much larger and more expensive trucks.


More surprising, for me, was discovering that the truck rides and handles well, with a comparatively sophisticated de Dion rear axle marking a big step up from the low standard set by the smaller pickups of yore. Cornering is flat with none of the tendency of old mini-trucks to hop their way through bumpy corners. And with a tight (37-foot) turning circle and sharp steering with superior feedback, it is a positive pleasure to drive. With one caveat: The brakes, still being calibrated, are strong, but curious in the way of Mercedes’ early electric offerings, the pedal sinking eerily along the way to brakes biting. Though we were assured the stoppers will have a more familiar, linear feeling by first production. Regenerative braking is strong but not off-putting, with no provision for adjustment.


Slate Auto


The truck didn’t rattle or creak, another pleasant surprise. A brief highway run at speeds of 65-70 mph proved as unremarkable as one might hope. Acceleration is nippy around town and more than adequate for the open road. The car isn’t lightweight by historic standards, though a little over 4000 pounds is dainty for an EV. Yet it feels light on its feet, which is refreshing for anything nowadays.


My first drive affirmed the rightness of my belief that only time will tell. But the Slate’s pleasing price augurs well, as do first impressions of its driving characteristics. Ford’s upcoming EV with a promised $30,000 price tag will undoubtedly suit many buyers better, being more like what people are used to and what many will be happy to pay a bit more for. But I predict that high demand and expensive options will inevitably lead to dealer markups (they can’t help themselves) and many of the Fords will leave the showroom having cost $40,000 and $45,000. IRL, the price differential won’t be $5000.


Slate buyers will be going on a journey, no doubt. Execution will be critical, as will the adequacy and competence of the yet-to-be-named clip-on service network. But I like the philosophy behind the company, explained to me at the aforementioned first ride event by founders Jeff Wilke and William Barker. Wilke was CEO of Amazon World Consumer, which put him in charge of the company’s retail businesses Prime, the Amazon marketplace, Amazon stores, marketing, and Whole Foods. No slouch obviously, he persuaded his old boss Jeff Bezos to invest $100 million in Slate. The company’s CEO, Peter Faricy, is another Amazon veteran. This is not a wing-and-a-prayer operation. The company’s design, manufacturing and marketing teams are experienced pros, respected industry veterans who wanted to try something different.


Jamie Kitman


While I enjoyed driving the Slate, another thing I like about the truck is the idea behind it, which I’d learned about on the first ride. In 2020, along with investor and engineer Miles Arnone, Wilke founded Re:Build Manufacturing, Slate’s creator and parent, on the proposition that American manufacturing was dying and that offshoring, community disengagement and other favored strategies of private equity firms and activist shareholders were hollowing out American competitiveness along with American communities. (This is, of course, a bit rich coming from a former Amazon executive, given the mega-company’s profits from said offshoring.) William Barker, another Slate founder, was an early Amazon investor as a precocious teenager and at 37 continues to share this belief.


Describing America’s deindustrialization, Arnone has written, “there was a shift by corporations toward very short-term thinking, motivated mostly by private equity companies seeking a quick return on their investment. The second was investors’ decision to break the longstanding compact with workers that providers of capital could make substantial returns while providing solid job opportunities and, by extension, the opportunity for American workers to advance their careers and personal lives over decades of productive work.”

Slate says it means to address that, noting that 4000 employees had lost their jobs with the closing of the Warsaw catalog factory. Wilke recounted that history at the truck maker’s opening ceremony. “There were folks who had spent their whole careers at the plant, and they got really emotional, tearily recounting their experience and now joy at the fact that this place was going to go on.” The Slate plant, he said, will hire 2100 of them back.


Beyond its small electric truck—a market opening that remains, for the moment, at least, ripe for the taking—the investment philosophy behind Slate more closely tracks that of Bezos’ Amazon than that of recent automotive startups, targeting the deep pockets of investors both financially capable and realistic enough to expect—and stick around for—a long ride. This enduring trust, the sort of patient investor that Bezos and Elon Musk found at a larger scale while burning through substantial capital along the way to success, is what the Slate team looks forward to enjoying. One way to achieve this goal is by avoiding an early IPO, in favor of investors in it for the duration. Leaving the stock market out of it, for now, seems like a game-changer.


Slate Auto


Said Wilke, “When we raised the original capital for Re:Build, I just called people that I knew were patriotic Americans that were friends who could write significant checks. Will \[Barker\] was the first person that I called to invest in Re:Build, and we did the same thing for Slate. The first round were all just friends that I knew would immediately say ‘This is important for America, and I’m happy to write a check.’” The company has raised over $1.4 billion privately to date, most recently adding $650 million in a Series C round.


“You have to really believe in a 10-year journey,” Barker told me. “We’ve been really lucky and focused on making sure that everyone we brought into the company is very long-term orientated. Long-term greedy means a wake of value to the ecosystem. Short-term greedy usually is trading paper. Win-win versus win-lose.”


I like the sound of that. But true love? Perhaps. But I think I’ll need to see a few more episodes.
