# The Pony Cycle’s Short-Lived Success and Lasting Legacy

Terry Hawke isn’t a man stuck in the past, but at times he seems like a man stuck *with* the past. The collection of vintage scooters in his 1950s suburban Michigan garage is more than family history. It is also the history of an idea. Many people have ideas; few are prepared to actually run with them. In the 1950s, the Hawke family tried—and succeeded—to make its mark on the history of transportation.


## An Idea Worth Building


What kind and type of vehicle is considered viable for general transportation has changed dramatically over the decades since the automobile established itself as a way to travel quickly with minimal human effort. Sometimes, however, an idea comes back around. Similar to today’s e-bikes and battery-powered scooters, in the 1950s[a gas-powered scooter](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/vespa-lovers-passion-scooter-collection/) was something that those too young for a driver’s license would use to get around. Even those with a license might choose to enjoy the fresh air perched atop a pair of wheels powered by a simple drivetrain. The Hawke family saw a niche and decided to fill it in a very American, very 1950s way.

In 1950, Allen Hawke left his career at Ford Motor Company, purchased a factory from Ford on the way out the door, and set up shop in Clarkston, Michigan, about 40 miles northwest of Detroit. The *Pontiac Daily Press* shared the exciting news, announcing that Hawk Tool & Engineering (Allen chose to drop the “E” when naming the business) would be designing, engineering, and manufacturing a new scooter entirely of its own design. Three years later, the first Pony Cycles rolled out of the Clarkston factory.


The Pony Cycle came in two flavors, each delineated by the powerplant hiding under the long, flat seat: A 3-horsepower Continental four-stroke single-cylinder or a 6.5-hp single for those with a need for speed. The centrifugally shifted two-speed transmission was developed and built internally and enables the more powerful model to propel bike and rider to nearly 65 mph. A single drum brake fits inside the rear wheel and is actuated by a T-bar below the handlebars, allowing riders to easily brake with whichever foot feels most natural. The throttle is less conventional and is operated by a lever on the left handlebar.

Terry Hawke was only just born when his grandfather Allen created the prototype scooter that would become the Pony Cycle. Terry spent Saturdays at the Clarkston facility with his father, playing with the projects and various ephemera that occupy a machine shop. By the time he was eight years old, Terry was a regular part of the parade crew that rode a small fleet of Pony Cycles in various events. He was also a de facto durability tester on the grounds around the tool and die business that sat atop a small hill in downtown Clarkston, taking the Pony Cycles on such adventures as riding the railroad tracks to test the durability of the in-house stamped 4×12-inch steel rims and the compliance of the simple front suspension.


Terry also recalls the prototype cycles that his grandfather and father built and used around the property. One example featured a large front bin between twin front wheels. It was Allen Hawke’s favorite utility vehicle, and Terry remembers that the worst time to take it for a spin was after his grandfather had just hauled dirt with it. “No matter how well I swept it out, the dirt and dust would just fly right up into my eyes,” Terry says as he flips through a binder filled with photos of that era.

Hawk Tool & Engineering first sold Pony Cycles directly and through bicycle shops. As production smoothed and gained speed, it struck a deal with Montgomery Ward to make the scooter available through its catalog. This decision branded the scooters as Montgomery Ward models—Special, Deluxe, and Supreme—and started increasing both awareness and demand significantly. Unfortunately, that demand was a blessing and a curse for the Pony Cycle.


## The Good Ones Die Young


Despite the excitement of the catalog deal, Hawk Tool & Engineering couldn’t keep up with order demand. By 1957 Mongomery Ward had dropped the scooter from its catalog, and in 1958 the Pony Cycle essentially got mothballed. The business moved on to more profitable ventures, which might have been easier than it sounds. Terry recalls his grandfather’s insistence on building everything in-house, including the handlebars, which, due to that stubbornness, cost twice as much to build as they would have been to purchase. As the business moved on, more and more of the Pony Cycle items were moved into a second warehouse in Clarkston. In the late 1960s, that warehouse was struck by lightning and burned to the ground, destroying many parts and scooters along with early prototypes and tooling.

The company might be gone, but the Pony Cycles story now survives with Terry, who enjoys taking one of his small fleet of Pony Cycles to shows and events in the Detroit area, either in a large van or in the bed of the 1950s[Ford Ranchero](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/seven-generations-of-ford-ranchero/) he restored. He went on to his own career in tool- and die-making but couldn’t leave the Pony Cycle behind, even though he admits that it represents less of an exciting reminder of the days gone by and more of a “what could have been.” The Pony Cycles in his garage sit next to a few other Hawk Tool & Engineering projects, including a three-wheeled golf cart and sidecar “Pony Car” designed for military use. Of course, there are also a handful of[Harley-Davidson](https://www.hagerty.com/media/tags/harley-davidson/) motorcycles and a few other projects for good measure. How could someone who grew up in a tool and die operation have it any other way?
