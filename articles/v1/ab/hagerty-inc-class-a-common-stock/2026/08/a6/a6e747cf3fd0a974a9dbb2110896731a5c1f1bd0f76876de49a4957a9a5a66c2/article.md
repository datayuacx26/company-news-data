# After All These Years, the Chrysler Minivan Still Makes Sense. Is That Enough?

Both the best and worst thing about being a mother these days, just about any mom will tell you, is they’re no longer expected to be “just” mothers. On the plus side, that means having children no longer requires foreclosing or forestalling other dreams or identities, as it once did. Yet modern moms often find themselves effectively working two full-time jobs—the 9-to-5 plus the bulk of childrearing responsibilities. I offer all this partially as a nod to my own wife, for whom joining me on a recent family road trip test of the vehicle pictured here required her to take off a week from her job (of course bringing the work laptop anyway) and do most of the feeding, watering, entertaining, and disciplining of our children along the way. It’s also, though, good context for the challenge facing said vehicle, the 2027 Chrysler Pacifica.


Since its inception in 1983 with the[Dodge Caravan and Plymouth Voyager](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/hagerty-magazine/the-van-that-saved-chrysler-and-changed-the-way-america-hauled/) , no vehicle has been more closely associated with motherhood than the humble minivan. In their heyday, minivans were everywhere, with Chrysler and myriad competitors fighting to offer the most sliding doors, the easiest folding seats, the most commodious rear holds. Yet the very fact that they are so suited for mothers eventually became a liability. Owning a minivan meant carrying the stigma of a “minivan mom.” By the early 2000s, the segment was hemorrhaging market share to SUVs and large crossovers, which do everything minivans do but slightly worse while looking as if they’d rather be somewhere else (call them the dads of modern vehicles). Today, there are just four traditional minivans on the market, most of them long in the tooth.


The Pacifica will never be a performance vehicle, but it is surprisingly easy to maneuver for a large utility vehicle. We managed here to squeeze into the last spot on a ferry crossing from Vermont into New York.


[David Zenlea](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/david-zenlea/)


Chrysler, however, remains committed to the segment it invented, for two clear reasons. First, it remains the sales leader. Second, the brand actually offers no other vehicles. New vehicles are on the way, we’re promised, ones that will elevate Chrysler into a premium brand, no less. In the meantime, it has given the Pacifica, introduced a decade ago, some zhuzhing intended to drive home the idea that the consummate mom-mobile can be something more.


“No one ever grew up with a poster of a minivan on their wall,” acknowledges Matt McAlear, who heads up both the Chrysler and Dodge brands. The hope is clearly that premium touches convince those who buy a minivan for all the sensible, left-brained reasons to spend just a bit extra, padding the brand’s operating margins and positioning it for the upscale move to come.


Although it will still offer a base trim starting at just over $40,000, Chrysler has focused its improvements on higher trim levels, which makes sense given the brand’s desired positioning and where the car market as a whole is moving—the average new vehicle transaction price in June 2026 was $49,758, according to *Kelley Blue Book* . The biggest change is a new front fascia, offered on all but the base trim. It’s more angular than before and incorporates *au courant* full-width LED headlights. Also rendered in LEDs is Chrysler’s updated badge, as previewed on its recent concept cars. Refreshes like this often do more harm than good, looking like two vehicles smushed together. This one works well. Higher-trim models also benefit from new, 20-inch wheels, which give the van more presence. Perhaps the biggest difference maker, though? The color. You could have ordered a Pacifica in Hydro Blue last year, but few people did and, in all likelihood, few people will this year either. A shame. Just as my wife occasionally reminds me that being a father doesn’t *require* me to wear pants with a stretchy waistband, a minivan doesn’t have to come in an unattractive, always-dirty-looking shade of white, black, or silver. All in all, the Pacifica’s basic design has aged well. To our eyes, it’s the most elegant take on the traditional minivan design. (If you want something more adventurous, check out the[Kia Carnival.](https://www.hagerty.com/media/new-car-reviews/2025-kia-carnival-hev-sx-prestige-efficient-but-underbaked/) )


The updated nose previews Chrysler’s new badging. It doesn’t look half bad, but the main appeal of our van was its bright color.

Interior tweaks are restricted to the top trim (appropriately called Pinnacle), which gets new Blue Agave leather upholstery with contrasting piping. Nice but pricey, with an MSRP around $60,000 if you opt for all-wheel drive. Our test car, a Limited trim with all-wheel drive and an optional sport appearance package, seems more palatable, coming with a black leather interior and similar 20-inch rims for just under $55K.


The sport package, alas, doesn’t actually bring any extra sport. Suspension tuning is identical to other Pacificas and certainly leans toward comfort, as you’d expect. Steering is light, but accurate. This is an old-fashioned American tourer. That said, and despite its not-so-mini 4885-pound curb weight, the van was perversely fun to drive hard. Chrysler had us pick up the Pacifica in the tony ski-resort town of Stowe, Vermont—a not-so-subtle hint that family-friendly and upscale are not mutually exclusive. We headed out via a notoriously narrow, winding pass called Smuggler’s Notch. I found myself having fun slinging the van past giant fallen boulders, my children oblivious in back as they’d already figured out how to plug their tablets into the rear-facing USB ports.


The only significant mechanical update this year is in regard to the plug-in hybrid—it’s no longer offered. McAlear notes that the loss of federal incentives made them hard to sell. Companies like Toyota, with their deep coffers, can continue producing a full range of powertrains in expectation that the winds of regulation and consumer preferences may once again shift; Stellantis, which posted a $26 billion loss last year, clearly doesn’t have that luxury. For those who prioritize fuel economy, though, that makes for grim math. The Pacifica’s 3.6-liter V-6, paired with optional all-wheel drive as was our test car, returns an EPA-rated 20 mpg combined. The equivalent Toyota Sienna, now offered only as a hybrid, returns 35 mpg. There’s nothing wrong with the six’s performance, at least. Tied to a smooth-shifting nine-speed automatic, it made quick work of the winding inclines and had us chasing down smaller vehicles.


Stellantis CEO Antonio Filosa has promised a $25 billion investment in Chrysler, Dodge, Ram, and Jeep by 2030. Here’s hoping some of that goes toward the Pacifica, particularly its powertrain. A pricey plug-in hybrid might not have been the answer, but neither is a 15-mpg fuel-economy gap between it and its closest competitor.

Of course, the main appeal of the Pacifica isn’t performance or even efficiency. It’s the fact that it’s a minivan. Doors that slide; seats that fold flat into the floor (Chrysler remains justifiably proud of its “Stow-N-Go” second row seats, which neatly disappear into the floor); cubbies within cubbies; even a vacuum cleaner. I actually spend a good deal of time in minivans on assignments for *Hagerty Drivers Club* magazine—they are often posted up just outside the frame, holding all the photography gear and everyone’s luggage. Driving one for several long days with kids, however, is a proof of concept on a different level. A recent *Wall Street Journal* article profiled wealthy New York parents who pay helpers to clip their kids’ nails, read the same book over and over, and participate in “repetitive play.” Well, for one glorious week, the Pacifica spared me from repetitive reminders to my kids not to fling open their doors into other parked cars (and from repetitive yelling when they choose not to listen). It helped me clean up their messes and provided ample space to keep their crap away from mine. For one stretch, when both they and we needed space, we sent them packing to the third row, the folded second-row seats serving as a kind of DMZ. (We do love our children, I promise.) If you define luxury as convenience or, more precisely, the elimination of *inconvenience* , then a Chrysler minivan for parents may be more luxurious than a Bentley.


Chrysler introduced flat-folding second row seats in 2005. It’s still an asset, allowing you to re-configure the interior in minutes.

If you define luxury as craftsmanship, then, well, it’s decidedly not. Yet the disparity isn’t quite so bad as you might imagine. The Pacifica and all its modern competitors have come a long way from twenty years ago, when minivan interiors had an almost condescending cheapness to them. “You’re gonna ruin it eventually anyway,” they seemed to say. The Pacifica has none of that middle school cafeteria vibe. The leather feels like leather, plastics are well put together and nicely grained, and the touch screen, albeit not the biggest by contemporary standards, is responsive and easy enough to learn. One gripe: The glass over the instrument panel is curved such that in certain lights the analog gauges are almost impossible to read.

Leather seating with contrasting piping lives up to the $50k price tag. Of course, the real appeal of the minivan’s interior still comes down to space. The Pacifica swallowed three suitcases behind the third row and had plenty of room to spare.


Alas, what really sells many luxury goods, cars included, is aspiration. The promise to make you appear richer, more successful, better looking. That’s why Prada shoes tend to cost more than those from New Balance. Rolling through east-coast vacation hot spots such as Lake Placid, New York, I spotted more Porsche Cayennes, BMW X7s and even Tesla CyberTrucks than I did minivans. It was only as we hit the Pennsylvania turnpike en route home to Michigan that minivans, most of them older, became commonplace. Like it or not, image matters. There’s not much Chrysler or anyone else can do about that.


The minivan may yet have another moment, though. The segment’s sales grew 21 percent last year. Perhaps it’s nostalgia—today’s parents are millennials and Gen-Zers who may have grown up playing in the back of the vans of the ’90s and early aughts. Maybe it’s a product of the pandemic—the *New York Times* recently reported that thanks largely to remote work, parents are becoming more open and less apologetic about their childcare responsibilities. Or, perhaps, it’s sheer exhaustion. Make what you will of trends like “tradwife,” but they reflect a certain unwillingness on the part of parents, mainly mothers, to continue “leaning in” to everything all at once. My wife seems to concur. No, she’s not about to give up her career, but after a week in the Pacifica, she started wondering aloud why she’s been so resistant to a vehicle that works so hard to make her life easier—just like moms do for all of us.


## 2027 Chrysler Pacifica Limited S AWD


**Highs:** Spacious, versatile interior. Comfortable ride and surprisingly capable handling. A vacuum cleaner.


**Lows:** Less efficient than some competitors.


**Takeaway:** Still the right answer for most busy families.
