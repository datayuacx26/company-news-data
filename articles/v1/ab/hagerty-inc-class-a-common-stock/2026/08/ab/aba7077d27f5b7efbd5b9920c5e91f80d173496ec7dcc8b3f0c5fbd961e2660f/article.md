# The TVR Cerbera Did Everything the Hard Way, Which Makes It Worth Importing

If you were to ever fully understand the world of TVR, Britain’s much-missed sports car underdog, you had to visit Blackpool. The seaside town on Lancashire’s west coast has long been down-at-heel, a poster child for deprivation that feeds off kiss-me-quick tourism like the gulls tussle over fish-and-chip scraps.


Employment has been hard to come by. Social surveys would regularly highlight how Blackpool held the dubious honor of having more than half of the U.K.’s poorest areas within its footprint. Yet against the odds, the local people were full of fight, their spirit undimmed like the famous (146-years-and-counting) Blackpool Illuminations lights festival.


Against this backdrop, TVR was that rare thing: An employer offering the prospect of learning skills, taking home a wage and maybe even having a holiday on a beach somewhere other than Blackpool. Needless to say, jobs at TVR were prized. During the 1990s and 2000s, many of those jobs were in building one of the most interesting (and now importable to the U.S.) performance cars of the era—the Cerbera, named for the three-headed dog in Greek mythology that guards the gates to the underworld.


flickr/Alden Jewell


The workforce itself was, to put it mildly, charismatic. During one tour while waiting to collect a press car that had been delayed (or was being rebuilt, uncharitable types might say) Ben Samuelson, TVR’s marketing and PR man, told me a tale that illustrated the competitive banter the teams on the two production lines indulged in.


Behind the seats of untrimmed fiberglass bodies they would scrawl messages of the sort frequently found inside the lids of old-fashioned school desks. One TVR owner had had all manner of electrical problems with his new pride and joy, and it had been back to the supplying dealer numerous times for repair, only for it to be collected and still have faults. Exasperated, the chap in question insisted that before reassembling the car he come down to the dealer and they show him that everything is in working order. At which point he saw the offensive messages written across the rear bulkhead and, perhaps understandably, suffered a major failure in his sense of humor, which was directed toward company owner Peter Wheeler. But this put Wheeler in something of a predicament, as the workforce weren’t keen to toe the line and end their fun. Instead, they reluctantly agreed to tone down the language and, ahem, illustrations used…


The point is, TVR was unlike any other carmaker. Neither traditional like Morgan, nor corporate like Porsche, it was a wild child that did things its own way and didn’t care what anyone said about it, though Wheeler did listen to what dealers and customers said, especially when it came to placing orders. The company couldn’t afford to indulge in costly market research, so through a process of trial and error it would launch new models, first showing them as a concept at a motor show and then weighing up how many deposits came in and whether production was viable or a rethink was needed.


TVR Griffith


Dean Smith


The 1990 concept for the[Griffith](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/valuation/tool/results/?y=1991&mk=TVR&md=Griffith&sm=400&cc=3938&b=Convertible) is a case in point. At the 1990 British Motor Show, TVR showed two cars: the Speed Eight, a 2+2 roadster intended to replace the wedge-era TVRs like the[280i](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=tvr+280i) and 400SE; and another concept that brought back the Griffith badge. After the show, Wheeler counted up the deposits. There were 32 for the Speed Eight, and more than 300 for the Griffith.


At this point, the company was faithfully following the simple formula that its founder, Trevor Wilkinson, had adopted when he established the firm in 1947: be pragmatic and buy-in all the complicated stuff. Engines came from Rover, Ford, and Triumph, while gearboxes and differentials were off-the-shelf parts. The magic was in how TVR combined those parts with lightweight construction and arresting styling.


By the late 1980s, under the ownership of Wheeler, a chemical engineer who struck liquid gold supplying specialist parts to the North Sea oil industry, TVR had found a kind of commercial sweet spot. Cars like the the Griffith made TVR famous for offering near-supercar performance at sports car money. But Wheeler was not content with being seen as a talented assembler of other people’s mechanicals. He wanted TVR to be taken more seriously as a manufacturer. The Cerbera was the manifestation of that ambition.


In 1995, Wheeler told *Autocar* magazine, “You couldn’t have a Ferrari or a Maserati with someone else’s engine in it, could you?” He’d wanted TVR to build its own engines, to both stand apart from peers like Ginetta, Marcos, and Morgan, and to bring more power to the party. The Cerbera would be the first production car to bring the new engines to the road.


TVR Chimaera


TVR


Damian McTaggart takes up the story. The 58-year-old McTaggart was responsible for the exterior design of TVRs, starting with the company in 1989 after one of the strangest job interviews of his career: “Wheeler was in his office with his dog \[Ned, a German Pointer\] and his dog was sniffing my bollocks while I was doing an interview with him!”


The idea for a 2+2 coupe came from Wheeler’s right-hand woman, Dorothy Cureton. As company secretary, she was the trusted filter between TVR dealers and Wheeler, and was said to be at the heart of Wheeler’s closest circle. McTaggart describes Cureton as a powerful figure. “She really did steer the business, I think.”


Dealers were telling Cureton they needed another car in the TVR portfolio, something with more than two seats that owners could grow into without losing the brand’s unmistakable character, and Cureton duly convinced Wheeler it was the way to go. McTaggart was tasked with coming up with a concept that could be shown at the 1993 London Motor Show at Earls Court.


He remembers it being a frenetic part of his career. “I was so preoccupied with what was going on at Bristol Avenue that I didn’t even bother wearing a watch. I’d go in to work and come home when the task was finished—and if that was the day after, it was the day after,” he laughs. “It’s nuts, but it wasn’t until I left TVR in 2003 that I bought myself a watch. I didn’t need one!”


To get started, McTaggart and fellow designer Nick Coughlan scanned a brochure photograph of a[Chimaera](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/valuation/tool/?y=1993&mk=TVR&md=Chimaera) (a two-seat roadster built by TVR from 1992 to 2003), blew up the side profile and started sketching. The first proposal for the new Cerbera was too short. “I think it was six inches shorter than the production version. You can see that in the first sketch I did.”


TVR


A full-size prototype was created, which allowed the small team to chop and change to their heart’s content. “I remember taking 20 mm out of the roof \[height\] one weekend because we just thought the roof looked a bit high. I even remember remodeling the floorpan as well, so if I lowered the roof I could just take a bit more out of the floor and drop the back seats a bit. On the show car, they were separate parts, which lost us a load of space, so when it came to the production version we made the seat back and squab part of the floorpan, with foam straight onto the floor.”


McTaggart says working at TVR was both a blessing and a curse. It was a creative, fast-paced environment where decisions were made on the spot. Unfortunately, Wheeler “kept changing his mind” so there was no such thing as a design freeze, from which point a project could be progressed toward production safe in the knowledge there shouldn’t be any fundamental changes.


Regardless, the reception from public, press, and dealers alike was overwhelmingly positive: Wheeler reckoned he’d need to gear up the workforce for building 500 Cerberas a year. Then, in 1994, as the concept progressed toward production, a spanner was thrown in the works: BMW bought Rover, and the future looked shaky for the Rover V-8 engine TVR had relied on for so long and was then using in the Griffith and Chimaera. Wheeler decided now was the time to fulfill the long-held ambition and have TVR build its own engines for the Cerbera.


By the time the Cerbera went into production for 1996, the doors were 280 mm longer than a Chimaera’s, which helped with access to the back seats, and the wheelbase had grown by six inches. “It also meant we didn’t need a rear quarter light, which means less parts and cost. And it made the car look really fast…” And boy was it fast. With help from the engineers Al Melling and John Ravenscroft, Wheeler created the potent AJP8 (V-8) and, later, AJP6 (straight-six) engines. They were masterpieces of internal-combustion power… when they worked.


The all-aluminum AJP8 was said to be as light as contemporary Formula 1 V-8s, helped by a narrow 75-degree angle between cylinder banks (at least 90 degrees is much more common), a flat-plane crank, and a dry sump, as well as a simple two-valve know-how to keep the heads compact and give good torque low in the rev range. There was 320 Ib-ft at 4500 rpm and 350 horsepower at 6500 rpm. It could rev on to 7000 rpm, giving the 2425-pound Cerbera impressive power for the weight.


TVR


I vividly remember collecting an early Cerbera 4.2 from Blackpool in period for a test drive, and those first few miles on the road. The AJP8 sounded unlike any TVR motor before it, like a superbike on acid, the bassy rumble gone and in its place something altogether race car. It was a loud, purposeful, sharp-edged instrument that soared up and down the rev range with no discernible flywheel effect.


The long-travel, floor-hinged throttle had the most wonderful response, and speed would pile on with a voracity that was unlike anything I’d driven before. A Porsche 911 Turbo, Lamborghini Diablo, or Dodge Viper just didn’t feel as fast. *Autocar* magazine recorded 0–60 mph in 4.0 seconds, 0–100 mph in 9.1 seconds, and a standing quarter-mile in 12.4 seconds. All this from a car that was, at £37,000 (about $57,000), a staggering £20,000 ($30K) cheaper than a Porsche 993 Carrera 2.


And it was by far the best-handling TVR to date, too. The longer wheelbase made it more stable and predictable at the limit, the damping was composed, and an integrated roll cage (hidden from sight behind the trim) prevented unruly body flex. It meant you could draw on more of that explosive performance more of the time, especially on Britain’s bumpy back roads, as the Cerbera signaled its intentions and the loaded-up chassis transitioned from grip to slip (more like wild, smoky oversteer) more progressively than any TVR before it, which meant you were better able to put your trust in it.


After conducting its first road test of the Cerbera, *Autocar* concluded the driving experience “gives TVR great cause to be proud” but acknowledged there were still worrying issues around the dependability of the car.


I had first-hand experience of those teething problems. When I came to extract a set of performance figures from the car, for *Auto Express* , a driveshaft broke at the first attempt, despite dropping the clutch at a tentative 2000 rpm in the knowledge that the engine could light up the rear tires without much provocation. “Those driveshafts are the same ones fitted to our Tuscan race cars,” cried the PR man, suggesting I must have been abusing it. Alas, no. Later, its electronically operated door mechanism would lock us out, for no apparent reason, requiring a roadside rescue company to break into the car. A colleague from *Top Gear* magazine fared worse still, after their test Cerbera locked them *inside* the car in a hotel car park, at a time when mobile phones were still a rarity.


The Cerbera also eschewed modern safety devices such as antilock brakes, traction or stability control, and airbags. Wheeler’s philosophy was that they were an unproven technology, conveniently sidestepping the thorny issue that a little company like TVR couldn’t afford the R&D costs. Wheeler once told *PistonHeads* : “If someone crashes one of my cars and it’s their fault then I can live with myself. If we were to put an airbag in one of our cars and it ended up killing someone, I couldn’t live with that ”.

Yet ensconced in the car’s cockpit, you could almost forgive it its foibles. The Cerbera’s dash layout was the most playful yet from the company, and it represented a dramatic change from the conventional, with buttons for the windscreen wipers, headlights, and horn on the steering wheel, long before Ferrari thought of the idea. The speedo and rev counters sat in a traditional binnacle together with a dial for the water and oil temperature, while below the wheel there was a clock, air vent, fuel gauge and two buttons: a black one to start the engine, and a red one to switch it off.


By late 2003, says McTaggart, those early highs of launching the Cerbera were becoming a distant memory. The latest six-cylinder AJP6, aka the “Speed Six,” had been added to the Cerbera range, the[Tuscan](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/valuation/tool/results/?y=2003&mk=TVR&md=Tuscan+Speed+Six&sm=Base&cc=3996&b=Targa) targa model had arrived, and customer complaints were piling up. “We were having all sorts of problems with the straight-six engines, and it just drained the lifeblood out of the company,” recalls McTaggart.


Flickr/The Car Spy


“There was never very much profit in any of the cars, and I remember walking across to the service area one day, and there was something like 20 to 30 engines sat there, with their cars all out in the car park with their noses pointing to the sky. That’s not sustainable. They’d come back for one or maybe even two engine rebuilds, and there’s the profit from that car just gone…”


McTaggart thinks the idea to have home-grown engines was the wrong strategy. “I think that’s where he \[Peter Wheeler\] started to go wrong, because he started to rule the company with his heart rather than his head. I mean, a[McLaren F1’s](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/mclaren/f1/1993/1993-mclaren-f1-base?id=aCn1I000000D4G1SAK) got a BMW engine in it and nobody questions it—it’s seen as a good thing. He could have done something similar.”


If there was a moment in time when Wheeler’s commitment to the company began to waiver, it was likely in 2000, shortly after deliveries of the new Tuscan began. “We had been having all sorts of engine trouble,” recalls McTaggart, “and the press had been given a Tuscan to road test and the roof blew off, and I remember walking the production line with Peter and he said, ‘I can’t do this anymore’. I kept my mouth shut and didn’t say anything to anybody but started thinking about what I should do next.”


Wheeler sold the rights to TVR to Nikolai Smolenski, the son of a Russian oligarch, in 2004, for a reported £15M. By late 2006, the company had gone into receivership.


[Adam Wilcox](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/adam-wilcox/)


Reflecting on the rise of the Cerbera, in 2004 Wheeler admitted to *Management Today* magazine that it quickly went from being a high point to a low point in the company’s history. “It was a complicated car to make: the first with our own engine, and the trimming was very labour intensive to fit. I decided to man the company up to deal with a rate of 10 orders a week, just to make sure that customers wouldn’t have to wait longer than eighteen months. Because every job is hands on, it takes quite a lot of time to train workers, so it was 100 people—a big expansion of the workforce at that point. But the orders never came in at that rate.”


Today, the Cerbera occupies a fascinating place in TVR’s story. It remains astonishingly fast, charismatic, and unapologetically unlike anything else. It was the car that proved TVR could design its own engines, build a genuinely complex modern GT, and compete at the very highest level on its own terms. It was also the car that exposed the limits of that ambition, as complexity, reliability issues, and financial pressures began to mount.


TVR never made an attempt to sell the Cerbera, nor any of its cars of the 1990s and 2000s, in the United States. Now comfortably past the 25-year rule for importing foreign-market vehicles, though, it is possible for American enthusiasts to take the plunge and own one of these fascinating cars. Should you dare to import a Cerbera? If you crave one of the most exciting performance cars of the ’90s, don’t hesitate.


There are always examples for sale, some good, some bad, and prices range between £20,000 and £40,000 (about $27K to $54K at current exchange rates) in the U.K., depending on whether they’ve been rebuilt or not. A good place to start is Str8six, which often has excellent examples for sale. Values also depend on engine, as the car was available with the Speed Six and the V-8 that came in 4.2-, 4.5-, and 4.7-liter sizes. Prices for the[Cerbera](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/valuation/tool/?mk=TVR&md=Cerbera) are updated in the U.K. version of the Hagerty Price Guide. There are even specialists on hand in the U.S. who import and help maintain modern TVRs, notably[TVR Imports](https://tvrimports.com/) and[TVR Garage](https://tvrgarage.com/) , both in Arizona. On the U.S. side, barely more than a handful of Cerberas have sold publicly, and they’ve brought prices in the $40,000–$50,000 range. A lot of car for the money, but there are some caveats, of course.


Needless to say, a Cerbera is a purchase you make with your eyes wide open, ideally with the reassurance of an inspection by a trusted specialist who knows these cars inside out. Once you have a Cerbera, parts won’t be easy to source, and problems won’t be easy to diagnose. But it will look, sound, and drive unlike anything else on an American road.


For an all-too short and thrilling time, Wheeler and TVR made Blackpool as famous for its cars as anything else. And of the lot, the Cerbera really did shine brightest.
