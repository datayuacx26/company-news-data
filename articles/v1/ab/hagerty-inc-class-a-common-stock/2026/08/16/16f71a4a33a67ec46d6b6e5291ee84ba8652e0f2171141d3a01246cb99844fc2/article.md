# Against All Oddities: Tractor Tales

The crew and I at our ex-foundry-now-car-commune have amassed a sizable collection of not-so-sizable tractors and mowers. There’s always work to be done around the shop, farm, and garden, and somewhere among the fleet is a tractor ready to work if you look hard enough. (Except last week, when circumstances demanded I buy an electric mower.) To my great fortune, I have been viewed as a custodian of junk, so people just give me these things for free. No cost up front, but a lot down the line.


Still, a free tractor is a free tractor. So what projects and associated headaches have we managed to collect so far?


[Matthew Anderson](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/manderson/)


The first is a 1968 Bolens 1225 that my wife inherited from her late grandfather. It was maintained meticulously, even down to the switchgear descriptions printed on a label maker. From the afterlife, I know he must have cringed when I fabbed up a ridiculously loud stack for it, but he likely smiled when I used it to drive through our neighborhood in Charleston to go pick up tacos. Rumor has it that there is even security footage of my wife sitting on my lap as we brap-brap-brapped down Poplar Street at 2 a.m. on New Year’s Day.


After we moved to Germany, the Bolens floated from a friend’s shop in Savannah, to a shop in Durham, and finally to a spot underneath my buddy’s porch. (The same friend who had a certain[Century](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/against-all-oddities/against-all-oddities-the-deal-of-the-century/) sitting back there.) A couple years back, the Bolens returned home and has been sitting, photogenically, in the shop. Doing nothing and asking for nothing. I believe it was called upon once to clear ice from the driveway, but its turf tires and unweighted rear wheels just bunny hopped. It was so embarrassing that I just parked it. Wheel weights and real tractor tires would be nice!


The first Wheel Horse tractor that I ever got was a 20-hp Onan V-twin-powered 520-H model that was donated by Shade Tree Garage around the same time I started[relocating cars](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/against-all-oddities/against-all-oddities-germinate-my-lovelies/) out to my home in Statesville. I had thrown it in the back of my father’s truck along with a vintage armoire and was towing a Holden Commodore behind it. It was quite the hillbilly setup, which is kind of our style, if you’re new around here. That mower started on the key right off the rip and served beautifully, with little more attention than biweekly air in the right rear tire.


Studebakers and Red Tractors.


[Matthew Anderson](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/manderson/)


Until recently, that is. For some reason, multiple issues have befallen the old red steed. It took quite a bit of diagnosing in the back barn at the house, but I ultimately identified an intermittently faulty starter relay, a burned-up battery cable, a questionable battery, and the real heart of the issue: a blown fuse… probably caused by the untrustworthy battery cable. As with most electrical troubleshooting, I eventually found three unrelated problems and one actual problem. This forensic work happened at night, in fits and starts throughout the course of a week.


All the while, I was trying to keep up with summer grass growth using the gifted Honda push mower that I had previously killed (bent crank) hitting half-buried slag chunks at the foundry. I dubbed it Olde Wobbly. After several nights of working outside, supervised by a family of skunks and actively interrogated by raccoons, none of whom offered a useful diagnosis, I was finally able to permanently retire Olde Wobbly back to the foundry interior.


This was not expected.


[Matthew Anderson](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/manderson/)


Work at my house continued. My friend Johna-fun volunteered to help us rip out our old overgrown fence line and go on an invasive plant massacre with his three-cylinder Kubota. Pretty amazing how much work you can get done with a tractor when it actually starts every time. Between Johna-fun and his son (our foundry intern), they’ve done an immense amount of ground clearing at the foundry and at home. Inspiration!


Longtime readers likely remember the International 504 on which I had bushhogged as a teen. Again, Shadetree Garage (and dairy farm owner) Truett entrusted me with the tractor. Nowadays, I regularly commute between home and shop on this old beast. Eventually, the road miles took their toll, bringing a large power steering leak and a generator so noisy that it was starting to drown out the clattery diesel engine. Deciding I had enough vehicles to cross-reference parts to and from, I caved and just brought the generator by Phil’s Starter & Alternator along with the smoked alternator from the Hobby 600. While that was down for rebuild, I was able to locate the leaking power steering line, and all it took was a light cinch to get it back in working order. Now, with a functioning charging system and steering that goes where I point it without a steady stream of oil accompanying the request—a true luxury, in this economy—it was time for some real work.


Some months ago, I had found a really nice boom pole that I painted in the proper International red. Time to put it to the test. The old smelter unit from the foundry was buried deep in the brush, and we were gonna pull it out. With questionable rigging, head foundry gremlin Thomas was able to yank the thing out of the blackberry and maypop vines so it could be moved up to the driveway for use as a planter and part of a drainage system. Somewhere in all this, one of the calcium carbonate-filled rear tires suffered a puncture, and now I’m looking at a pair of new rear tires. Honestly, the 504 deserves some pampering. *Cha-ching!*


Thomas preparing to hurt himself.

The fourth tractor I’m about to mention was my *actual* first: the 1971 single-cylinder Wheel Horse pictured below. I first took the wheel of this thing when I was around 12 years old. At that point, it had been fitted with agricultural tires, wheel weights, and the mower deck was removed. To 12-year-old me, this was less of a tractor and more of a pickup truck saddled with dramatically lower expectations. I had a trailer made from a Whippet axle and some barn wood bonking around behind me at all times, usually equipped with some tools, PB Blaster, and starter fluid to kill the wasps. I was many times entrusted to drive down Carpenter Pond Road to go retrieve things from Truett’s son’s house, a feat one would not dare do today, mainly because there are a lot more people around to whine about a kid towing a trailer full of chemicals. Nearly every day I would drive deep down into the woods in search of Studebaker parts or just to be among the old Packards, Citroens, and a myriad of other weird vintage European cars.


Back to modern times. When Truett came by the foundry to pick up his son’s 1941 Studebaker we had been working on, he brought with him my old tractor, strapped down on the inbound trailer, as payment. I couldn’t believe my eyes. What a treat to have my first real taste of freedom back at my fingertips. The same old agricultural tires from the 1990s are still on it, and they’re entirely in need of replacement.


I dropped everything to focus on this valuable piece of junk. I have thrown a new battery in it and verified that it gets fuel, just no spark. We suspect that it won’t take much more than filing the points to get it manually shifting back through the gears again. I can feel a spendy repair coming, and I don’t care.


Wheel Horse in optimal configuration.

I should mention that my old rig wasn’t alone on Truett’s Trailer of Tetanusy Gifts. There was *another* larger two-cylinder Wheel Horse included, the one that I used to mow the lawn with back in the day, again out at the garage/farm. This sort of utility is becoming increasingly necessary as the foundry has developed an appetite for push mowers that borders on irresponsible. (First Olde Wobbly, then another one I had stashed away in storage.) The bent blades, broken cranks, carbs full of schmoo, and hours-long mow times were adding up.


My zero-down plus battery, fuel pump, battery-cable installment plan for the old mower was crucial. Now it runs beautifully, perhaps even on a fraction of the gas I last used to cut the grass 25 years ago. Foundry security footage now certainly exists of me smiling like an idiot while doing lawn maintenance.


Well, my wife’s Bolens continues to look pretty in the shop, we have two 520-H “Red Square” Wheel Horses cutting grass, three running push mowers, a charging-and-not leaking 504 with a tire that holds air (for now), and I haven’t even been sprayed by a skunk. Let’s ride this momentum, people!


Gotta start somewhere.
