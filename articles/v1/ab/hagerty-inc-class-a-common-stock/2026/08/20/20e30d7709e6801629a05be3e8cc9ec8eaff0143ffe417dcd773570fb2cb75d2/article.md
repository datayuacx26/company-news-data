# Which Nissan Z-Car Is Right for You?

For over 50 years now, Nissan’s (and Datsun’s) Z-cars have offered good looks and smooth six-cylinder performance, usually at an attractive price point and always with big aftermarket support. They’ve also taken many forms over the years, and as vintage collector cars they come at different price points.


Z-cars have been around for a long time, then. So have a lot of sports cars. But unlike, say, the Miata, Corvette, or 911, there isn’t the same consistent through-line in design philosophy, styling, or even engine layout. That’s why it’s worth examining each generation, their characteristics, pros, cons, and pricing before setting a budget and going off on your search. For example, someone seeking a proper, pure classic-car driving experience might prize an early 240Z and pay up for it, while a strapped-for-cash kid looking for a project might pick up a clapped-out 300ZX to fix on the weekends.


Let’s look at each Z-car, from 240Z up through 350Z (the newest Z-car we have in the Hagerty Price Guide) to see which one might suit your tastes and budget.


## 1970–78 240Z/260Z/280Z: The Original(s)


James Lipman


It’s hard to overstate[the original Z-car’s impact on the perception of Japanese cars](https://youtu.be/imsbLkTT9Ac?si=noayCGE8oesm1250) , and on the performance-car world in general. Enthusiasts and collectors have long since started to appreciate that impact, making early 240Zs rather collectible and in some cases surprisingly expensive.


Prior to the 1970s, Japan’s presence in the U.S. automotive landscape was tiny. In the sports-car realm, there was the[Toyota 2000 GT](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/toyota/2000gt/1970/1970-toyota-2000gt-base?id=aCn1I000000CubmSAC) and the Datsun (the brand name Nissan used for export products)[Roadster](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/datsun/2000/1967/1967-datsun-2000-base?id=aCn1I000000DDDYSA4) , but the Toyota only came to North America by the handful, and the Datsun Roadster, while a solid performer, was never a huge seller.


With its first Z-car, the 240Z, Nissan took a more thoughtful approach, aiming it directly at the untapped American market, studying what the drivers here wanted and ensuring a corn-fed six-footer could fit comfortably inside. Testing the new coupe, *Car and Driver* concluded that the “difference between the 240Z and your everyday 3,500-dollar sports car is that about twice as much thinking went into the Datsun…” When *Road & Track* got its hands on one, it concluded that the Z was “the most exciting GT car in a decade.”


Broad Arrow


Broad Arrow


Broad Arrow


The Z’s biggest selling point was value for money compared to Western sports cars. It essentially delivered Porsche-level performance for the price of an MG. It was more comfortable, more practical, more reliable, and quicker than many European sports cars, and also cheaper to buy. It offered good looks, too, and while its 2.4-liter, 150-hp overhead-cam straight-six, independent rear suspension, front disc brakes, and four-speed manual weren’t exactly cutting edge, they were contemporary. And it was all a winning recipe. Over the course of the 240Z’s 1970–73 run, Datsun sold 135,000 units in the United States.


But Datsun also faced the same 1970s challenges as everybody else trying to sell cars here. Emissions regulations were starting to pull some pep out of the Datsun six, and to claw some of it back, the Japanese made plans to replace the SU-type carburetors with fuel injection. Before a new fuel-injected Z was ready, though, Datsun sold an interim model called the 260Z for the 1974 model year. Still carbureted, it differed very little from the 240, but had its engine stroked to 2.6 liters and rated at 140hp. With the 260Z, Datsun also introduced a 2+2 model with a taller, slightly awkward roofline to accommodate a pair of small rear passengers.


Courtesy Tomas Gonzalez Jr.


By 1975, the fuel-injected Z was ready, now with 2.8-liter displacement, 149hp, and a 280Z badge. There were bigger bumpers and a heavier curb weight to deal with, but styling was nearly identical to previous Z cars, and the interior was largely similar, too. A five-speed joined the options list starting in 1977, and a 2+2 model stayed in the lineup.


By the end of the decade, the automotive landscape had changed drastically, and Datsun was just one among many carmakers prioritizing style and comfort rather than maximum speed. The Z’s successor marked the company’s shift to a bigger, softer performance car. The original Z, especially the 240Z, is the one favored by purists, and the model’s role in turning the sports car world on its ear in 1970 makes it one of the most important cars of the decade.


Mecum


**Things to consider**


The Z-car proved more practical and reliable than its European sports car contemporaries, but in the longer term the Datsun coupes proved to be similarly rust-prone. Areas to check include under the battery tray, around the windshield, the wheel arches and fender behind the front wheels, the rocker panels, the floorboards, and around the rear hatch.


Otherwise, 240/260/280s are mechanically sound and enjoy decent parts support, but there is always the usual old-car stuff to consider, like worn rubber components, weather stripping, brittle trim pieces, etc. Components from later Z-cars like four-wheel disc brakes, larger-displacement engines, or five-speed gearboxes from later cars are also fairly common upgrades on the early ones, and generally improve the driving experience even if they aren’t technically correct.


Broad Arrow


**What to pay**


In American eyes anyway, the original 240Z is sort of the granddaddy of all Japanese performance and tuner cars, the one that started it all. But there was a time when they were fairly cheap. Years ago, we even put them in the[Affordable Car Index](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-indexes-trend-upwards-despite-a-picky-market/) of the Hagerty Price Guide, because it was easy to find a pretty good one priced in the teens. Now, not so much.


Condition #2 (excellent) values for[240Zs](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/datsun/240z/1970/1970-datsun-240z-base?id=aCn1I000000DIiFSAW) peaked in 2022 at $78,500. They’ve corrected since then but still sit at $60,300. Partly because they were modestly valued for a very long time, there aren’t a ton of exceptional examples out there and few owners have bothered to lavish their 240Z with a high-dollar restoration like they would a[911](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/porsche/911/1970/1970-porsche-911-t?id=aCn1I000000DWjtSAG) or an[E-Type](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/jaguar/e~type/1968/1968-jaguar-e~type-sii?id=aCn1I000000DL2tSAG) . So, top-notch examples have sold for staggering prices in recent years, and condition #1 (concours, or best in the world) values are currently at an all-time high of $151K. Meanwhile, closer to earth, driver-quality examples in #3 (good) condition are a much more reasonable $23,900.


The Enthusiast Network via Getty


The one-year-only[260Z](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=datsun+260z) is a rarer find, but a much more reasonable buy than its predecessor with a $42,400 #2 value and a $19,700 #3 value. Snagging a more practical, but less sexy 2+2 version drops those numbers to $27,500 in #2 condition and $12,700 in #3 condition.


The fuel-injected[280Z](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=datsun+280z) commands similar prices to the 260. Cars with an automatic generally come at a 15% discount, and any 280Z with the rare ZZZAP Edition graphics package—sold to promote a[period racing arcade game](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/zzzap-in-1976-datsun-made-the-first-car-themed-video-game/) —can command a 40% premium. The long-term relevance of the Z-car in the market is also shown in buyer interest data, which Hagerty measures through insurance quotes. About 41% of average buyer interest for the 240/260/280Z comes from millennials or younger, so interest in these cars skews younger than the collector car market as a whole and younger than most enthusiast vehicles of this vintage.


## 1978–83 280ZX: The Sequel


Hagerty Marketplace/GlenShelly


The first Z-cars left big shoes to fill, so the 280ZX that followed instead walked its own walk. In keeping with the Malaise Era times, it was more of a grand tourer, with cushier suspension and interior, bigger dimensions, beefier weight, and a quieter time behind the wheel. It was the same basic shape as the Z of old but a bit more angular, and more aerodynamic thanks to wind tunnel testing. There were also big, big bumpers—a U.S.-market 280ZX was 10 inches longer than its European market counterpart.


The first 280ZX came with just 135hp from its 2.8-liter six, and with extra heft to haul around, it was actually slower than the 280Z it replaced; *Car and Driver* clocked it at 9.1 seconds to 60. But Datsun leaned into the car’s softer nature, with one ad asking: “Remember yesterday’s image of a sports car? It had to be as noisy as a foundry. Ride like a rock. And be as spartan as a monk’s cell. Well, the Datsun 280-ZX has turned it all around… Every luxury has been well attended…”


Two-tone paints, T-tops (called the “skyroof” in sales brochures), velour seats, and fancy stereos brought Datsun into the disco era. But the company didn’t give up on performance completely, introducing a turbocharged model in 1981 that delivered 180hp. Brakes and suspension also improved for 1982, which also saw a facelift for the model. Transmission choices were a five-speed manual or three-speed auto. On track, 280ZX also found success in both SCCA and IMSA, and Paul Newman raced one.


Datsun followed a similar philosophy with the 280ZX’s successor, the 300ZX, but there were also radical changes to the styling, under the hood, and the badge on top of it. The 280ZX, then, while a far cry from the original 240 of 1970, is sort of an end-of-an-era model, essentially the last of the classic Datsun Zs.


**Things to consider**


The 280ZX has the same considerations as the Z for the most part. Rust is still something to look for, but a well-kept example should be a reliable weekend driver. It has good ergonomics for a foreign performance car from this era, but it is worth noting that this is a softer, less overtly sporting car than its predecessor, and non-turbo versions may leave you disappointed in the performance department. A 1978 *Car and Driver* test noted that the ZX “feels rubbery at all times,” but that for “sedate touring… the ZX is easily more comfortable than its Porsche, Mazda, and Triumph competitors.”

**What to pay**


In part thanks to general growth across the collector car market but also thanks to skyrocketing prices for the older Zs, values for the next closest thing—the[280ZX](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=datsun+280zx) —started shooting up in 2021. The median condition #2 value has more than tripled since 2019, and nice examples with good specs can get downright pricey. At the top is a Turbo coupe, which currently carries a #2 value of $38,000 and a #3 value of $13,900. A Turbo 2+2 is a few grand cheaper, and a non-turbo 2+2 sits at the bottom with a #2 value of $27,500 and a #3 value of $9900. Like the earlier cars, automatics typically come at a 15% discount.


Curiously, even though the 280ZX is a younger car than its 240/260/280Z forefathers, it’s less popular with millennials, who make up just 14% of buyer interest for the model. Then again, Gen Zers seem to love it, as 21% of buyer interest comes from them, even though Gen Z makes up just 10% of the market as a whole and were born years after the Malaise Era ended.


## 1984–89 300ZX (Z31): Z-Car Grows up and Gets a Name Change


Mecum


For 1984, Datsun introduced the 300ZX, also known by its chassis code “Z31.” Wider and squarer than its predecessor and now sporting pop-up headlights, it was nevertheless still recognizable as a Z-car, and its chassis was still based on that of the 280ZX. Under the hood, however, was Nissan’s first V-6 engine, a 3.0-liter single-overhead-cam unit rated at 160 hp. There was also a turbocharged/intercooled version rated at 200 hp, and a 50th Anniversary Edition Turbo package for 1984 brought all the ’80s bling with flashy Light Pewter/Thunder Black paint, electronically adjustable shocks, speakers in the seats, cruise control and stereo controls on the steering wheel, mirrored T-tops, embroidered floor mats, 16-inch aluminum wheels, and a body kit.


Not long after it debuted, the 300ZX swapped badges. Nissan gradually phased out the Datsun brand in the 1980s, instead using the Nissan name worldwide, and the ZX started selling as a Nissan in 1985. The 300ZX’s full-power equipment and digital dash were all impressively modern, and the 2+2 model’s roof was better integrated and less awkward than in older Zs. T-tops were a popular option.


Although the 300ZX was a major update for Nissan’s flagship performer in America, it wasn’t groundbreaking. A 1986 *Car and Driver* test of the then-new Turbo model ran the headline “Better but Far from Best,” and noted that the “300ZX Turbo should not taunt[Corvettes](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/corvette/1986/1986-chevrolet-corvette-base?id=aCn1I000000DPRbSAO) or[944 Turbos](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/porsche/944/1986/1986-porsche-944-turbo?id=aCn1I000000Cv43SAC) , lest it find itself sliced, diced, and iced: the Chevrolet and the Porsche are furlongs faster.”


Nissan


**Things to consider**


The Z31 is one of those automotive middle children. Earlier Zs have cleaner and more classic looks as well as more cultural significance going for them, while the Z32 version of the 300ZX that came after is more handsome, more sophisticated, and significantly faster. The good news is that Z31s are generally quite affordable relative to other Zs and indeed relative to many other RADwood-era performance cars. But many have led hard lives. Areas to look for rust include under the battery tray, the wheel wells, and around the spare tire, while any car with T-tops may have had some leaks. And while the partly digital dash is oh-so-eighties, reviewers have noted the speedo can be hard to read.


It’s also worth noting that the Z31 is more GT than sports car, and at anywhere from 2900 to 3300 pounds, it’s a bit porky. All-out performance and hard cornering aren’t this car’s strong suits, but it should be great for sunny-day cruising with the T-tops out and a tape in the deck.


Hagerty Marketplace user Swiftmotoring


**What to pay**


Like the 280ZX, the Z31 saw prices shoot up in 2021. They peaked in 2024 and have come down a bit. At the very top of the Z31 ladder is the flashy[50th Anniversary Turbo](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/datsun/300zx/1984/1984-datsun-300zx-50th_anniversary_turbo?id=aCn1I000000DMgiSAG) , which carries a #2 value of $39,200 and a #3 value of $17,200. They get much cheaper from there, however. A normal[Turbo](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1986/1986-nissan-300zx-turbo?id=aCn1I000000CqA0SAK) coupe ranges from the high-20s to low-30s in #2 condition depending on year and trim, and about $12K in #3 condition. Non-turbo[2+2](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1987/1987-nissan-300zx-base?id=aCn1I000000CqZZSA0) models are downright cheap with #2 values in the mid-teens and #3 values of about $7K, and regular non-turbo[coupes](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1986/1986-nissan-300zx-base?id=aCn1I000000DDogSAG) aren’t much pricier.


Whether it’s their generally affordable price or the ’80s kitsch in the cockpit, Z31s are rather popular with younger buyers. Fully 50% of all interest in them comes from millennial or Gen Z enthusiasts.


## 1990–96 300ZX (Z32): Peak Z


Cameron Neveu


The 1990s were the golden age of Japanese performance cars, when Honda, Toyota, Mitsubishi, Mazda, and Nissan all brought out the big guns with high-priced, technologically advanced, world-class performance cars and plopped them into U.S. showrooms. The second-generation 300ZX, aka the “Z32,” built on its predecessor with the same basic ingredients but with more technical sophistication and plenty more performance.


Among the first cars designed with computer software, it brought a rounder and sleeker shape, while the cockpit returned to a conventional analog-gauge layout. While its 3.0-liter V-6 shared the same basic architecture as the one in the Z31, Nissan revised it with dual overhead cams and variable valve timing for 222 hp. A Turbo model got not one but two Garrett turbochargers and intercoolers for a full 300 hp (detuned to 280 hp when paired with an automatic rather than the standard five-speed). That was the exact same power figure as a contemporary Corvette, though the 300ZX was still a portly thing at up to 3400 pounds.


Turbo models also got a computer-aided hydraulic four-wheel steering system called Super HICAS (High Capacity Actively Controlled Steering), first introduced on the Skyline. Most U.S. cars got T-tops, and in 1993 a convertible Z joined the portfolio. Produced by ASC of Michigan, the convertible bodies were only available in naturally aspirated form, but offered Z-car buyers the full drop-top experience for the very first time.


*Car and Driver* called the Z32 “one of the most alluring cars to appear on the United States market in years”, and when testing a Turbo model called it “\[p\]oised, dependable, undramatic, balanced” while also calling out the “spectacular steering: heavy, but direct and accurate.”


Nissan


By 1996, the yen-to-dollar ratio had made it effectively impossible to sell cars like the 300ZX, Toyota Supra Turbo and Mazda RX-7 in America. A 300ZX Turbo that year *started* at over 44 grand, which is nearly $100K in 2026 dollars. For ’97, Nissan yanked the 300ZX from the U.S. market, but kept on selling it in Japan until 2000. As a sendoff, 300 U.S. market ’96 models came with the “Commemorative Edition” package. With no direct replacement, American dealers would have to do without a Z-car in the lineup for another seven years.


**Things to consider**


The Z32 isn’t nearly as rust-prone as the Z-cars of old, but hatch seals can leak, and by now it’s reasonable to expect failing clearcoat on cars that have seen some sunshine and haven’t been repainted. Since even the newest U.S. market Z32 is a 30-year-old car at this point, any worn rubber can be replaced, while interior plastics and leather can crack and/or fade. T-tops can leak, and fixed-roof Z32s are hard to find.


Though the Z32 is generally reliable if well-maintained, the engine bay in Turbo models is a bit cramped, and enthusiasts note that oil can leak from the cam end seals. This is also the most mechanically complex of the vintage Z-cars, and since it was popular with the tuner crowd, heavy amateur mods and hard driving are common features. The 1996 model is a rare one, but it’s also the only Z32 with OBD II diagnostics.

**What to pay**


Z32s aren’t exactly cheap, but in Turbo trim they aren’t a bad value relative to other performance cars of the era. The[1996 Commemorative Edition](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1996/1996-nissan-300zx-turbo_commemorative_edition?id=aCn1I000000DLQgSAO) is far from entry level with a #2 value of $56,300 and a #3 value of $34,700, while a normal[Turbo T-roof](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1994/1994-nissan-300zx-turbo?id=aCn1I000000DWNWSA4) is slightly more affordable at $51,800 in #2 condition and $27,500 in #3 condition.


A non-turbo[coupe](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1994/1994-nissan-300zx-base?id=aCn1I000000DPtrSAG) comes in at $25,200 in #2 condition and $15K in #3 condition, while a non-turbo[2+2](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1994/1994-nissan-300zx-base?id=aCn1I000000CzJvSAK) is the cheapest proposition at $19,600 in #2 condition and $13,100 in #3 condition.[Convertible](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/nissan/300zx/1993/1993-nissan-300zx-base?id=aCn1I000000DUPcSAO) models can be difficult to find, but if you must have a drop-top Z, it will cost about $21K for one in #2 condition and $12K for one in #3 condition. Once again, factor in a 15% discount for an automatic transmission. Nearly half of all buyer interest for the Z32 comes from millennials or Gen Zers.


## 2003–08 350Z: Modern Collectible or Used Sports Car?


Nissan


After a long hiatus, the Z returned for the 2003 model year as the 350Z. As was the case for many other Japanese performance cars as the 1990s turned into the 2000s, the newer car was altogether less ambitious, but still appealing. For its latest Z, Nissan went back to basics, drawing on the original 240 for inspiration and marketing.


Nissan boasted it was “50,000 performance for under $30,000” at the 2002 North American International Auto Show in Detroit, where the model debuted. In fact, it launched at a little under $27K, slightly more expensive than a Miata and MR2 but with twice the horsepower (287 hp in total), though it still had a Corvette-like curb weight.


Nissan


The new Z came standard with a six-speed manual and multi-link suspension, and a viscous limited-slip was optional. The engine was again a V-6, this time a 3.5-liter VQ-series unit. *Car and Driver* called it “a very satisfying drive with terrific control feel,” while *Evo* called it “hairy-chested, vaguely uncouth yet hugely desirable.”


There was no turbocharged model, and no 2+2, just a two-seater coupe and, starting in 2004, a convertible model called the Roadster. Trims included Base, Enthusiast, Performance, Touring, and Track. Touring models got power heated leather seats and xenon headlights, while the Track model got Brembo brakes and standard limited-slip. A 2005 35th Anniversary model got special badging, cast aluminum wheels, and either Ultra Yellow or Pearl Blue paint, and the whole range got a mild facelift in 2006. In 2007, power rose to 306hp, and the Track trim was replaced by the NISMO model, which got a body kit, diffuser, specially tuned suspension, Brembo brakes, forged alloy wheels, and a special exhaust. For 2009, Nissan debuted a new model, the 370Z, that was largely an update of the 350.


Nissan


**Things to consider**


There aren’t any huge gotchas or ticking time bombs with a 350Z. The engine has a reputation for oil consumption issues but is generally solid. Nissan has also used the VQ-series V-6 for many years and in all sorts of different vehicles. Support for it is huge, and so is the aftermarket.


Unlike the earlier ZX models, there isn’t the huge gulf in performance between turbo and non-turbo versions, because there was no 350Z Turbo. The available trims don’t differ drastically from each other, though the higher-spec versions will offer a nice bonus in terms of comfort or handling improvements. The interior is plastic-heavy, even by early 2000s Nissan standards. It’s also a tighter fit than you might expect, and there are some odd, cheap-feeling cubby spaces trying in vain to make up for the lack of a real glove box, thin door pockets, and minimal trunk.

**What to pay**


Values for the 350Z have been largely flat for the past few years, and prices between trim levels don’t differ too greatly. The one exception is the NISMO, which carries a $41,700 #2 value and a $26,200 #3 value. The rest of the 350Z lineup generally runs from the low- to mid-teens in #3 condition and the low- to mid-20s in #2 condition. Roadster models are worth a little more than coupes, but not much, and automatics carry a 20% discount. Nearly half of all buyer interest for 350Zs comes from millennials or Gen Zers, which makes sense for a car of this vintage, at this price point.


The 350Z offers a good value for some seeking a fun hobby car on a budget. It’s also unlikely that values will shoot out of reach anytime soon, as the similar, but newer and faster 370Z is still in used-car territory and not much pricier. NISMO versions are currently selling in the $30K to $40K range, while it’s easy to find a base model in the 20s.
