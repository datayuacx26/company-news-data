# Route 66 in a New Yorker: Nine Days, Eight States, and Two Transmissions

When we learned about the trip that Markku Jaakkola and Jacob Gilbert had planned, we were envious. The two friends devised to[set out from Detroit in a 1973 Chrysler New Yorker to drive the length of Route 66](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/two-friends-a-big-block-mopar-and-the-100-year-old-mother-road/) , or as much of it as still exists. The big Mopar had been owned by Hans Granberg, Jaakkola’s late friend who had planned to drive the luxury cruiser across the United States. The two invited Hagerty along for the ride; we’d help document their journey in honor of Hans.


Their timeline, planned for August between[Bonneville Speed Week](https://www.hagerty.com/media/tags/bonneville-speed-week/) and[Monterey Car Week](https://www.hagerty.com/media/tags/monterey-car-week/) , made things a bit tricky. We met them before they set off to get some shots of the 1973 New Yorker they’d be piloting across the country, but most of their trip we knew would have to happen without us. Instead, we planned to meet them in Albuquerque and catch the final, westernmost third of Route 66 through the high desert.


## Albuquerque, New Mexico


We met Markku and Jacob just in time to get dinner and make a parts run. Albuquerque has more businesses trading on the city’s position on Route 66 than it does its affiliation with *Breaking Bad* , although it’s close. The New Yorker’s two stewards filled us in on the trip thus far, including one major setback: at one point, the 727 transmission lost its sprag. They found a replacement transmission for sale, swapped the tailhousings, and installed it in the seller’s driveway. The New Yorker was on its best behavior on the last few days of the trip after that.

## Grants, New Mexico


[Brandan Gillogly](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/brandan-gillogly/)


Plenty of the best-known spots on Route 66 are studded with kitschy shops selling T-shirts, stickers, name tags, postcards, and plaques emblazoned with the Route 66 highway sign and chrome-laden 1950s cars. There are also plenty of Route 66 landmarks in a depressing state of disrepair. Many of the gorgeous, Art Deco gas stations that offered a fill-up, an oil top-off, and maybe even a mechanic on duty to help fix a nagging carburetor issue or timing trouble are now nothing more than cordoned-off pigeon rookeries. Twenty years ago, they might have been salvageable. In 20 more years, they may no longer be standing.

Even with relatively tall 2.76:1 gears in the rear axle, the 440 never had any trouble keeping up with traffic on the Interstate, where 75-mph speed limits mean cruising at 80 mph.


[Brandan Gillogly](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/brandan-gillogly/)


[Brandan Gillogly](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/brandan-gillogly/)


Markku and Jacob averaged about 12 miles per gallon in the two-ton Mopar C-body, so even with a 24-gallon tank, gas stops were frequent.


The more modern air conditioner—installed to replace the original equipment that had been removed ages ago—offered a respite from the punishing desert sun, although its draw took its toll on the cooling system. Example: In thin air, at more than 6000 feet of elevation and temps in the 110-degree range, turning on the air conditioner caused the coolant temps to rise. August is not the ideal month for traversing the country on Route 66… through the high desert… in a classic car… and we spotted only a few like-minded classic-car drivers. The vast majority of tourists on the trip seemed to be piloting rental cars. Indeed, we came across families of tourists from Japan, Italy, France, and Spain who made the pilgrimage. Everyone we met was enamored with the Chrysler.

## Kingman, Arizona


[Brandan Gillogly](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/brandan-gillogly/)


Stopping for lunch in Kingman, Arizona, we were joined by author, podcaster, and historian Jim Hinckley. Jim is a[treasure trove of Route 66 knowledge](https://www.hagerty.com/media/entertainment/11-cultural-hits-of-route-66-at-100/) that spans the length of the Mother Road and its history, even before its construction. He lives in Kingman and is well-versed in the local history there, as well.


Hinckley paints a promising picture of Kingman. The city is refurbishing historic buildings. He walks us along one of Kingman’s main streets, pointing out the new businesses that have opened up, including an ice cream shop out of which wafts the smell of waffle cones. We stopped at the sleek new café next door for iced tea.


Back on the street, Jim points out many of the historic buildings that have been refurbished. Some of the oldest buildings, built in the late 1800s, feature Art Deco upgrades to the facade. “Beale Street has been transformed,” said Hinkley, crediting the business owners who have taken charge of this rebirth. “The first thing you’ve gotta do is build a sense of community… you make it a place people want to visit, and make it a place they want to live, retire, raise families, and open businesses.”


Kingman is doing much better than many of Route 66’s smaller towns, which don’t have the benefit of a modern Interstate connecting them to the rest of the country. Though there are several cities on the western side we visited that show promise.

Starting at Cold Springs, where it was 112 degrees during our stop, the eight-mile section of Route 66 heading west is dubbed the Arizona Sidewinder. It touts 191 turns and no guardrails. Don’t be deterred, though there are some hairpins and some rather steep drop-offs. At the posted 20 mph, or 15 mph where there are hairpins, it’s much more scenic than it is scary. The rocky terrain is dotted with scrub brush, cholla cactus, and various yucca.


## Santa Monica, California

Unfortunately, in a feat of impeccable timing, the Santa Monica Pier is closed for construction during this summer centennial for Route 66.
