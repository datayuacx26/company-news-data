# Two Speed Shops Live and Breathe American Muscle… with an English Twist

If you’re after a lot of horsepower from a classic Chevy, Ford, or Chrysler race engine, you might think the only place to go is the United States. After all, these engines have been extensively developed over the years for all sorts of racing in the U.S., from sports and formula cars to dragsters and sedans, and everything in between.


However, deep in the British countryside, we visited two companies making American horsepower for drag and circuit racing: Mathwall and I.C.E. Automotive Engines. It turns out that racers in the U.K. and Europe often take advantage of the expertise in building these engines in England, of all places. That’s for two main reasons. One: because U.K.-based companies can provide on-site service. And two: because there are very specific classes and rules for drag and circuit racing in Europe. For example, the governing body of world motorsport, the FIA, has strict rules for pre-1966 race cars like Shelby Cobras and Ford GT40s.


All of this is detailed in Appendix K, which is the FIA’s rulebook for historic road-course and rally racing. In the U.S., you can run roller rockers and all sorts of tweaks that would get you thrown out of a sports car or sedan race featuring Cobras and Mustangs in Europe. Of course, everyone who goes racing wants to get as much power and torque from a full-race V-8 as they can, but tailoring these engines to Appendix K rules is a specialized art.

It’s the same with dragsters. Positioned below what people regard as the pinnacle of drag racing—Top Fuel dragsters and Funny Cars—there are classes specific to European drag racing. And as with all forms of vintage motorsports, the Brits have become very adept at building engines for so-called nostalgia drags.


## Mathwall


Mathwall’s Ford 289 engine is basically all-new, save for the timing cover.


Mark Gillies


Based in rural Surrey, south of London, Mathwall began business in 1967. It was formed by Stuart Mathieson and Peter Wallace, who had worked at Alan Mann Racing on their successful European Ford racing programs, with cars like the GT40 and Mustang. Their first job was to prepare a customer Ford Falcon for sedan racing and, while they branched out with automakers such as Mazda and BMW, they continually built Ford and Chevrolet engines for road and race applications.


Mathwall and Purple Sector owner Mark Mathieson stands by a photo that shows his Ford Mustang on the front row of the Ken Miles Trophy at the 2024 Goodwood Members’ Meeting.


Mark Gillies


The company has expanded somewhat since Stuart Mathieson’s son, Mark, took over the business in 2007. As well as building engines, Mathwall Technologies developed into an engineering services business that has been rebranded as Purple Sector, an engineering-led and data-driven consultancy. This builds on the work that Mark Mathieson did as an engineer on F1 powerplants at Ilmor/Mercedes-Benz and during the COVID pandemic, when he spearheaded rush production of ICU ventilators for the National Health Service.


While it’s a smaller part of the business these days, Mathwall still produces Ford and Chevy V-8 engines for historic racing, used in cars like Ford GT40s, Ford Mustangs, Shelby Cobras, and Bizzarrini 5300GTs. Specifically, these engines are built to the FIA’s Appendix K rules, and they are bespoke units.


Development engineer Liam Draper studies computer drawings of a small-block Chevy crankshaft that sits on the bench in front of him.


Mark Gillies


“We’re the only ones, to my knowledge, that design our own cam profiles, design our own valvetrain, cranks, rods, pistons,” Mark Mathieson tells us. “Obviously, we put all the internals together with a replacement block and cylinder heads. It’s a new engine, and we are in control of the parts that make the engine breathe and give it the torque and power characteristics that we want to have.


Piston and connecting rod for a Ford 289 engine. Made by U.K. manufacturers to Mathwall’s specifications.


Mark Gillies


“Every engine gets built with known parts from approved suppliers, inspected to a process. Blueprinted build, all recorded. Goes on the dyno, goes through a very arduous and thorough break-in and test cycle. When it’s been through that, it gets its final inspection, then it comes off, gets a leak-down check, borescope, oil samples taken. Once it has gone through all of that, if it has a problem when it first goes in the car, it’s likely to be an installation issue, because we know the engine’s good to go at that point.”


Ford 289 engine in build, showing fitted pistons, crank, and rods.

Mathwall builds for the premium end of the market, Mathieson points out: “It’s a more discerning customer who appreciates some of what I just described and will pay for it. Because of the way the business is set up, I don’t need to be chasing 50 to 100 engines every year. I’m quite happy if we do a number of 20, maybe 30 a year.”


These are essentially new engines; Mathieson feels that’s the only way you can guarantee reliability. Development engineer Liam Draper expands on this as he shows me around the spotless engine build facility and the engine dynamometer. The company uses modern tools such as a FaroArm, CAD software, and 3D printing to rapid prototype parts like cylinder head ports and test them on a flow bench. In the past, they would have had to work away on a grinding bench for hours to achieve the same result.


Among the tools employees like Draper use is this FaroArm: a portable co-ordinate measuring machine that can be used to reverse-engineer components using CAD modeling.


Mark Gillies


In period, he says a Ford 289 race engine made around 370 horsepower. These days, Draper says a Mathwall 289 for a GT40 or Cobra makes in the 440-450-hp region, but that drivability is more important than outright power. “We know we can get the horsepower out of these engines,” he says. “But then you end up with an engine that a pro might be able to drive around a track like Goodwood very quickly, but an owner might struggle with it. So actually, getting the torque curve right is way more important. Also, we go to a whole range of tracks that are very different. Getting a nice drivable package for everyone, everywhere is key, really.


“Mark \[Matheison\] spent a lot of time in Formula 1 at Ilmor/Mercedes, and his big thing was valves, valve springs, camshafts, controlling that environment, so he’s very clued up and knows all about camshaft design. There are natural inefficiencies with a Ford 289 engine, but we’re running more valve lift for longer thanks to the cam design.”


When it comes to Appendix K regulations, Draper outlines key differences between them and the freedom an engine builder would have in American vintage racing. “The main difference that you get from one of our engines and an American equivalent is normally around the valvetrain. We have to run solid tappets here, non-roller rockers, and a cast-iron camshaft. In America, you find that they’re running steel camshafts, roller lifters, and roller rockers. It just means you can put in a lot more lift and take the stress off the camshaft. We are governed by a 7000-rpm limit, as well.


Ford 289 engine uses a new block: Mathwall maintains that the only way to guarantee reliability is to build engines from new components.


Mark Gillies


“We’re allowed to run an MSD ignition controller, which is great because it keeps the playing field level: they are scrutineered for each race and for rev limit. The quality of points ignition just isn’t there anymore. You know with the MSD, you can sit at 7000 all day and the ignition isn’t going to start floating.”


Under Appendix K, porting is free and the valve size is set, but they also measure the ports to ensure no one is raising them. Draper points out that every time Mathwall builds an engine, it gets Motorsport U.K.—which governs racing in Britain—to seal the engines on site and check the capacity, valve sizes, and bore and stroke.


FIA-spec Ford 289 sedan engine on Mathwall’s in-house engine dyno: This allows the company to tune the engine with representative exhaust systems.


Mark Gillies


The dyno has been designed and built in-house. It’s rated up to 628 hp, but Draper says that what sets it apart is its ability to go up to 10,000 rpm and around 1325 pound-feet of torque. With 200 channels of data logging, Mathwall has the ability to do all its own development.


Mathwall also offers valuable customer service. The dyno, for instance, allows the company to run different exhaust systems—be they Cobra, Mustang, or GT40, for example—which enables engineers to get the fuelling right for the engine before it goes into the car. And then, they always try to look at the actual car and make sure the install is done properly so there are no issues with how the engine runs.


The dyno was designed and built in-house and allows Mathwall to log 200 channels of data if needed. It can handle more than 670 horsepower and 1300 pound-feet of torque.

Mathieson says that the company actually has a pool of engines ready so that it can simply adapt an engine to the specific vehicle application and not have to scramble with rebuilding a customer’s engine, which has often returned because it has reached its designated number of running hours. “You just need to be ahead of the game,” he says. “The buffer stop takes the stress out of the business.”

## I.C.E. Automotive Engines


Nick Davies is one of the two owners of I.C.E. Automotive Racing Engines, along with Rob Loaring.


Mark Gillies


Like Mathwall, I.C.E. Automotive Engines has been around a while. Rob Loaring founded the company in 1988 in Southend, east of London. In 1998, he was joined by Neil Davies, a one-time bond trader who “decided it would be a good idea to give up work” and go into partnership with the man who built the engines for his cars.


Initially, they worked out of Loaring’s Southend premises, before moving to the Silverstone Technology Park in the English Midlands in around 2005. Today, though, Loaring operates in a shop near Silverstone and Davies operates near Banbury, around 30 miles away. “Rob has got the big machines,” says Davies: “the dyno and the flow bench, the big honing machine.”


The “big machines” live at the shop of Davies’ business partner, Rob Loaring. Davies says they both need to do a lot of machine work, hence this milling machine.


Mark Gillies


Davies used to race this Austin Bantam AA/FA dragster called “Havoc:” It has run 5.98 seconds at 237 mph and won its class at the California Hot Rod Reunion in 2017.


Mark Gillies


For a while, I.C.E. built engines for circuit racing: “predominantly historic American stuff,” says Davies. They have dialed back on the circuit racing engines and, Davies says, “These days, it’s predominantly drags. We’re building more custom stuff, application-specific for specific class racing. And it’s still across the board, but not so many small-blocks: The drags tend to use bigger capacity engines because there’s no penalty for using them. So, we’re back on big Chevrolets, big Chryslers. We also have the odd Ford.”


When I visited Davies’ shop in the summer, he was working on a number of engines but stressed that the off-season is when they do most of their builds. “Most of the engines that we build will need servicing every, let’s say, couple of years. A lot of them use aluminum rods, a roller valvetrain, fairly high spring pressures. So, things need looking at and servicing on a fairly regular basis.”


When we visited, Davies was in the process of finishing a 600-cubic-inch big-block Chevy in a 2004 Grand Am for his daughter to race.


Mark Gillies


In the shop, he showed me several engines. First up was a 600-cubic-inch naturally aspirated Chevy, fitted into a 2004 Pontiac Grand Am that runs in a class called SuperGas. This one makes 920 horsepower and will be driven by Davies’ 16-year-old daughter. “Quite terrifying, really,” he says. Then there was a big-block twin-turbo Ford engine with a broken rod that was in for a rebuild; a 468-cubic-inch big-block Chevy for a street rod; and two Chrysler blocks, one for a 450-cubic-inch supercharged nitro Nostalgia Funny Car and the other for a twin-turbo alcohol Pro-Modified car.


Davies estimates that the blown nitro Hemi in “Havoc” makes “3200 hp based on its weight and how fast it has gone.”

The engine in Davies’ own outrageous Fiat Topolino AA/Fuel Altered is a 417 cubic-inch Hemi, with an original type of magnesium supercharger and intake manifold, running on nitro-methane. Davies reckons it makes around 2000 hp, but he also has an Austin Bantam that won the California Hotrod Reunion in 2017. That one makes around 3000 hp, Davies says, based on the car’s weight and a 5.98 quarter-mile at 240 mph.

Why the estimates? “We can’t actually measure the power,” Davies says. “Our dyno is pegged at 1000 pound-feet of torque. We use the dyno to break rings in and make sure they don’t leak, but can’t really tune the engine because of the limitations of the dyno. However, we run data loggers and record everything on them from boost to clutch speed. I can’t do anything without a logger.”


I.C.E builds engines for all forms of drag racing, save for the “Top Fuel Funny Car stuff,” he says. “The ‘[nostalgia scene](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/dragstalgia-a-bit-of-america-in-middle-england/) ‘ seems to be growing fairly well over here, and within that there are several different classes that we get involved with. Over here, Street Eliminator for streetable cars running on radial tires is in a growth phase. Other than no wheelie bars, a governed tire, and running on some kind of pump gas, it’s kind of free. We build for two or three of the fastest guys that run big-inch turbos.


Piston on a big-block 468-cubic-inch Chevy street rod engine.


Mark Gillies


“We’ve got a customer who’s won five or six championships with, would you believe, a 1956 Chevy with a 660-cubic-inch twin-turbo engine on E85. It has run 215, and to make that kind of speed, it must produce around 2000 hp. On a radial tire, and he can drive it around the streets. Part of the qualifying at every meeting at which these cars run is they have to go out on the public roads and do a 30-mile cruise. It’s mandatory to have MoTs.” (An MoT is the U.K.’s mandatory test of vehicle roadworthiness.)


Turbos are now competing with classical supercharging on all but the top classes, Davies says. “There’s a class that runs here and, in the U.S., called Pro Modified. In Europe, you can only run three types of engines: huge-inch nitrous engines, which are up to now 960 cubic inches; the second option is supercharged; and the third option is turbocharged. And for the last two or three years, the turbochargers, much as they’ve been handcuffed, have come out on top in the FIA championship.


“We chose the turbo route. The turbos are more complicated, but you absolutely can make more horsepower with them. The other nice thing is the operating RPM is significantly lower than the supercharger combination. We decided at our age, we didn’t want to do the work in the pits. You’ll see people with supercharged cars breaking stuff on a very regular basis. Well, we’d rather be lighting a barbecue while they’re fixing their engine.


Davies and Loaring are both members of the British Drag Racing Hall of Fame. As well as racing “Havoc,” Davies has campaigned this twin-turbo Pro Modified Firebird.


Mark Gillies


“I guess that the popular or common understanding is that the supercharged engines at the drags go fastest, because all the nitro classes are supercharged. Below that, I think the clever people would say it’s easier to make a huge amount of horsepower reliably from turbochargers than it is from superchargers. The parasitic loss from a supercharger is extraordinary. With the turbochargers, it’s not free horsepower because there’s a restriction on the exhaust side of things, but it’s nothing like as significant as the supercharger. We’ve built a lot of turbo stuff, and we’re quite recognized as we can tune them, as well, on the various engine management programs. I’m not afraid of a laptop.”


Davies says that the raw components come from the U.S., but that I.C.E. “can’t get exactly what we want. We can’t get heads with the right size intake runners, for instance. So, we get into a fair amount of machining. We don’t just assemble components. We’ll look into the corners and try to optimize the components we put together.”


Davies shares that, since COVID and the continuing consolidation of the American speed industry, lead times have become stretched, bigger companies are less interested in servicing small clients, and some of the institutional knowledge has gone.


Close-up of welded aluminum intake for the twin-turbo big-block Ford engine.


Mark Gillies


U.K. customers could go to a U.S. engine specialist and ship an engine over, but Davies says there are some advantages with going to I.C.E. “I guess the first one is that people rely on you for support on the ground. How good an experience do you get from someone who is remote and 5000 miles away? It is very easy *not* to prioritize someone who isn’t standing in front of you.


“Here you get honest horsepower. We have a notoriously stingy dyno. A lot of our engines win races. We don’t try to sell on dyno numbers. Rob and I decided years ago we didn’t want to give any happy numbers just to please our customers. We have had customers bring engines to us that show that the horsepower numbers that some people advertise just aren’t there.


Davies at work on the big-block Chevy engine. Most of the parts come from the U.S., but Davies says he and Loaring do “a fair amount of machining” to optimize components.


Mark Gillies


“We might be a bit more expensive than a U.S. engine builder who builds a comparable engine—maybe because they have better buying power than us—but I would argue you don’t get exactly the same engine, and you absolutely don’t get the same support. We might not be able to track every run an engine makes, but once we deliver, we will go to the drag strip for the first or second time a customer runs it just to make sure everything is OK. We are not just engine builders; we’re in the service industry.”
