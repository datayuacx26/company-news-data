# Cars Are Meant to Be Driven, but Collectors Pay a Premium for Ones That Aren’t

“It smells like the mid-2000s in this thing.” Dylan Mashburn was describing, in rapturous tones, the rush of finding a 2003 Ford Mustang Mach 1 with 62 miles. “To sit in that car and turn it on and see the odometer. To see the underneath with all the chalk markings. No oxidization, no rust. They’re only new and original once.”


Original, yes, but a 2003 Mustang is not new—it’s 23 years old. To believe otherwise is to trick yourself. But it’s a self-deception that low-mileage enthusiasts now seem happy to accept—and pay big money for.


I spent much of my adult life—24 years—living in New York City, a place not nurturing of the classic car hobby. When I moved to New England six years ago and renewed my childhood interest in all things automotive, I felt a little like Rip Van Winkle waking after a decades-long slumber. Everyone and their brother now wanted a Porsche, for one thing. And I noticed something else, too: a curious mystique among enthusiasts for low-mileage cars.


I’m not talking about Ferraris and Lamborghinis, either. Nor do I refer to the silver-anniversary Corvettes or first-gen Dodge Vipers that collectors mothballed as a future investment. Rather, much of the excitement these days is around low-mileage commodity cars, like Hondas and Toyotas, OBS trucks from the 1990s, and early-2000s SUVs. Go on YouTube and you will find scores of videos with headlines like “First Wash of an F250 with 139 miles” or “Low Mileage 1984 Buick Grand National press car.” It’s a world where a 2000 Geo Metro with 400 miles (as sold on[Bring a Trailer](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/2000-chevrolet-metro/) for $19,110) draws as many oohs and aahs (maybe more) as a Gullwing Mercedes.

Classic car dealers, not surprisingly, have jumped on the trend. Dylan Mashburn and his brother Noah, who own a Michigan dealership, run a Facebook group called[Low Miles No Miles](https://www.facebook.com/groups/2066782386864241/) , where proud owners share photos and odometer readings of their undriven cars. It has 442,000 members. There’s a New Jersey dealer who calls himself “The Low Mile King” on TikTok. Another dealer, also in New Jersey, features an “Ultra Low Mileage” section on its website, while a Florida dealer specializes in “time capsule” Chevy trucks. Frequently, these cars are pitched breathlessly as “the lowest-mile example in existence” or “one of the cleanest \[insert make and model\] anywhere in the world.”


As someone who has racked up their share of miles—I’ve never owned a car that didn’t exceed 100,000—I find the low-mileage fixation slightly baffling. Let’s not forget that a car, by design, is to be used. “You don’t necessarily want ‘original’ on wear parts,” noted Dave Kinney, vehicle appraiser and publisher of the Hagerty Price Guide. Yet Kinney readily admits undriven cars seem to bring the most excitement—and money. “I can’t think of a car that isn’t worth more as a zero-mile or 100-miles example,” he said, adding, “An appraiser sometimes has to suspend reality and pretend that a car that has been ignored for 10 years is better than one that has been cared for for 10 years.”


Howard Swig, head of auctions at Bring a Trailer, concurred. “I have an amazing sense of what cars will bring—except for the super-low-mileage ones that you’ve never seen before and may not see again.” Indeed, how could anyone predict that a[2003 Ford Excursion](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/past-sales/2003-ford-excursion/vin/466f4306-eefe-438e-bd9e-96c9a738591a#_) would go for[$100,000 on BAT](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/2003-ford-excursion-18/) ?


Bring a Trailer/ron67


What all this seems to be saying is that an otherwise unremarkable car can become an object of desire and value merely because it went undriven. This struck me as unique in the world of collecting. Is a Rolex more coveted because it was never on someone’s wrist, an Eames chair more valuable because it was never sat in? Then there’s the simple, unavoidable fact that every mile you put on a low-mileage car—i.e., every minute of driving pleasure—necessarily lessens the specialness and perceived value. I was curious what, exactly, enthusiasts are chasing. And why they’re willing to pay such a premium.


## New as a Restoration. Original as a Barn Find.


“For me, and for a lot of people, old cars are time machines,” Donald Osborne, the automotive historian and appraiser, told me. Osborne, it should be noted, is not a low-mileage guy. “To own a car and not to drive it is bizarre to me and ultimately unsatisfying,” he said. But he understands the basic appeal. “You want to experience a car as close to the original condition and aura that you possibly can.” This may seem pretty obvious to anyone who loves old cars, yet it’s actually a bit unusual. You wouldn’t necessarily assume someone who buys a Monet is seeking to recapture the feeling of late 19th-century France. But you can typically bet someone who drives a 1966 Pontiac GTO is nostalgic for the 1960s.


For many decades, the ultimate expression of car collectors’ desire to turn back the clock was a full restoration. Yet in recent years, there’s been a surge in popularity for “survivors,” regardless of their condition, in deference to their “originality.” Witness the popularity of Hagerty’s[Barn Find Hunter](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLHKCmmH-x9mLN0PNeFBtxyZ7olwptkO5q) videos or Broad Arrow’s recent Amelia Island auction, where a dusty, long-parked 1968 Shelby GT500 KR Fastback—a car valued at $225,000 in #1 concours condition—[brought $263,200](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/am26_r0033/1968-shelby-gt500-kr-fastback) .


Broad Arrow


There are also practical limitations to what even a talented shop can replicate. In particular, the plastics and cheap surfaces on 1980s, 1990s, and 2000s cars that are newly becoming collectible can make them harder to restore than the sheetmetal and chrome of old.


A low-mileage car is thus a way to have your cake and eat it, too—the authenticity of a survivor with crisp trim, firm seats, and an unworn steering wheel and shifter. Indeed, the Mashburns told me their customers prefer an untouched example over a professional restoration. “You restore one—oh, that’s beautiful,” said Dylan. “But it’s not original at all.”


For relatively pedestrian cars, there’s one more appeal: They’re relatively inexpensive. I liken it to artisanal chocolate: For the somewhat modest price of $10 or $15—bottom-shelf money in the wine world—you can buy one of the finest chocolate bars in the world. Likewise, $15,783, a sum insufficient even for a basket-case 911, could have made you the proud owner of a museum-quality 1995 Oldsmobile Aurora[sold recently on Hagerty Marketplace](https://www.hagerty.com/marketplace/auction/1995-oldsmobile-aurora/60jsvlvelrNZ6Zdc2tuI4w) .

David Marx, the author of S *tatus and Culture: How Our Desire for Social Rank Creates Taste, Identity, Art, Fashion, and Constant Change* , compared the pursuit of low-mileage cars to a strain of vintage fashion collecting. “Deadstock has been a virtue in the clothing world for a long time,” he said, referring to unworn garments. “It makes sense that the principle has now extended to cars. These cars seem like ‘mint’ collectibles.”


The car hobby is powered by dreams, and more than anything, the low-mileage car nurtures a fantasy—that you can go back in time to a mythical dealership of yesteryear and drive home a car that, for one reason or another, you were unable to purchase new. The purest expression I have seen of this futile attempt to recapture an imaginary past is a YouTube video posted by Dennis Collins, a Texas classic car dealer. Collins, who is 61, bought his first Ford Mustang when he was 15, has owned dozens of them over the years, and, judging by his YouTube channel,[Coffee Walk](https://www.youtube.com/@DennisCollinsCarWorld) , has an insatiable appetite for low-mileage survivors.


In the video, Collins travels to Eaton, Ohio, summoned by the owners of a 1968 Mustang with 1031 miles. The car was owned by an unmarried woman who lived with her mother and stopped driving soon after purchase because she preferred taking the bus; after she died, it was henceforth preserved by the woman’s family. Collins rhapsodizes over the original paint, intact interior, and a service sticker from Peffley Ford, showing one oil change at 456 miles, still affixed to the doorjamb. He rifles through the dealer paperwork still in its plastic, finds a period bottle of Highland Green touch-up paint in the trunk. “It’s like being in the showroom in ’68,” he says, awestruck, climbing behind the wheel and firing up the engine. “Unbelievable… it’s just like brand-new.”


Finding such a rare artifact would seem to be a professional and personal pinnacle. But backing out of the garage, Collins remarks to his videographer, “This is officially the crispest set of gauges we’ve had so far,” and it is in that so far that the madness reveals itself. You can almost see his mind turning, wondering if somewhere there exists a lower-mile, fresher Mustang that will bring him even closer to that imagined 1960s Ford showroom.


## Mileage Anxiety


A few months ago, I came across a listing for a 1991 Mercedes-Benz 560SEL for sale near my home in Connecticut. The car had been driven a claimed 9500 miles, or an average of 280 miles per year.


Because I am pitifully susceptible to marketing and images, I was getting sucked into the low-mileage cult at this point. My own classic Mercedes, a 1983 380SL that was my dream car when I bought it back in 2004, suddenly looked less dreamy, since the odometer reads 142,000 miles and counting. What would it be like to own the lowest-mileage Mercedes-Benz W126 S-Class in existence?


Hagerty Marketplace


When I called the seller, Roger Marcon, he told me he bought the 560SEL the year before from a widow whose husband had bought it for her new. The plastic wrap was still on the passenger-side floor mats. “It only had 9000 miles,” Marcon said. “That was the biggest curiosity for me. These things are tanks, so people rack up the miles.” Very quickly, however, what had drawn Marcon to the Mercedes as a buyer became a trap as its owner.


“I couldn’t drive this car. Every mile I put on it hurt,” he said. “I would have loved to take it out to get a coffee or to the grocery store just to use it. But I was very cognizant, ‘I’m not going over 10,000 miles.’ That was a mental barrier.”


Hagerty Marketplace


Marcon experienced what might be called mileage anxiety, a common condition for owners of low-mileage cars. If the main reason you bought a car (and paid a premium) was an untwirled odometer, well, you’re left doing a queasy rolling calculus in your head with each joyride. Ferrari owners are familiar with this catch-22, but rarely has it been faced by the drivers of Chevy Vegas and 2000s Mustangs.


Indeed, even the Mashburn brothers are not immune to mileage anxiety. “I have a 2004 Mustang Cobra, and I got the car with 24,000 miles,” said Noah. “Every thousand miles, I think, ‘Jeez, it feels less special.’” How much less special is a 12,000-mile car compared to a 1200-mile car? A 40,000-mile one compared to 30,000? Hard to say precisely. But mileage anxiety increases as the odometer reading increases. In extreme cases, you end up with a car you can’t drive.


Vern Kreckel owns a Ford Ranger that he bought new in 1992 and specced himself, checking the box for power windows, power doors, factory-installed fog lamps, and four-wheel drive. The truck was driven sparingly during his first decade of ownership, and sometime in the early 2000s, Kreckel—who owns a machine shop in St. Marys, Pennsylvania—realized he owned a Ming vase. “I’m very particular about it. I don’t want anybody touching it, I don’t want it bumped, I don’t take it anywhere it has to be parked,” Kreckel said.


By 2007, the Ranger was approaching 9000 miles. By 2008, it was sitting at 9800. Like Marcon and his Mercedes sedan, for Kreckel, going from four- to five-digit mileage was crossing a Rubicon. “I held it here, held it there,” he said. “Five miles a year. And in 2023, it was at 9995.” Kreckel finally bit the bullet (“The truck is getting older, it has to run”) and today, the Ranger sits at 10,050 miles.


Neil Jamieson


For his part, Marcon sold the 560SEL (not to me), vowing to someday own another one “with higher mileage so I don’t feel bad about it,” he said.


Nevertheless, the experience got him started on a low-mileage kick.


To complement his 2004 Toyota Land Cruiser with 380,000 miles, Marcon found a 2000 Land Cruiser with 20,000. He bought it sight-unseen from a family in Washington state and was awaiting its cross-country delivery when we spoke. Justifying its purchase, he sounded a familiar argument—and illusion. “There’s something about being in a very pristine car versus a worn car where you have squeaks and leaks,” Marcon told me. “You feel like you’re buying a new car.”
