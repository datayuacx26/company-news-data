# How Darryl Cuttell’s Would-Be IHRA Empire Came Crashing Down

If we define “failure” for a long-struggling drag racing series as passing ownership of said series along to somebody else because you no longer want to deal with it, here’s the shorthand for the 56-year history of the International Hot Rod Association: Fail. Fail. Fail. Fail. Fail. Fail. Fail. Fail. Fail. **Fail.**


We boldfaced that last fail because that’s the one going on now, and it dwarfs the rest. Ten owners, 10 attempts to elevate the second-tier IHRA to racing relevancy that might possibly rival the dominant, 75-year-old National Hot Rod Association (NHRA): The current unpleasantness is the most sport-shattering misfire of them all, as the IHRA’s current owner is blaming, by name, the richest man in the world.


Really? Elon Musk is responsible for the collapse of IHRA drag racing?


So says Darryl Cuttell, 61, who bought the IHRA in December of 2024.


IHRA owner Darryl Cuttell (R) with recently appointed (May 2026) president Dustin Farthing.


Facebook/IHRA


Cuttell, himself a master electrician, is the owner of 30-year-old Darana Hybrid, an Ohio company that describes itself as, “a Native American owned and operated international electro-mechanical contractor,” an acknowledgement of Cuttell’s membership in the Tuscarora Nation. In 2024, Darana Hybrid was the lead contractor on Musk’s new data center in Memphis, Tennessee, which Musk calls his “Colossus.”


Musk wanted contractors who would complete Colossus *now* —he needed massive computing capacity to train the company’s Grok large language models. Most contractors said Musk’s timeline was impossible, but Cuttell and Darana Hybrid signed on. Elon Musk and his company, xAI, say that Colossus was completed in just 122 days. Cuttell says 123. It is not the only thing Musk and Cuttell disagree on.


The $1.25-trillion merger earlier this year between Musk’s Space X and xAI, Cuttell said, was when the problems began. xAI promptly paid the invoices Darana Hybrid sent in. Space xAI, now the curator of Colossus and its data-center siblings, hasn’t.


Musk’s properties, including Tesla and The Boring Company, have earned a slow-pay reputation that may or may not be warranted. “It’s what Elon does. He goes out and takes on the small guys and just buries them,” Cuttell told WMC, the Memphis NBC affiliate. “I’ve never been treated so unfair for doing such a good job. That’s the worst part. I overextended myself, gave him everything he wanted, and now I’m getting trashed.”


So Cuttell filed liens with the county for unpaid work for Musk on two data centers totaling $136,892,782.78, and he plans to file liens for $400 million more. Cuttell told a Memphis newspaper that Darana Hybrid has done $1.5 billion worth of work on Musk data centers, and Darana is still owed a substantial percentage of that.


Typically, Musk and his companies ignore critics. Not this time. Last Thursday, Space xAI filed a federal suit against Darana Hybrid, and the 40-page complaint is just brutal. And if you were wondering what drag racing and the International Hot Rod Association had to do with all this, here it is: Along with Cuttell himself, the IHRA is actually named in the suit.


That suit alleges that Darana and Cuttell overbilled Space xAI “by hundreds of millions of dollars while selling out their own suppliers and subcontractors along the way,” and “diverted money to Cuttell’s personal side projects,” which would be the IHRA, the dozen or so racetracks he bought both before and after that acquisition, and a peculiar list of other competitive sports leagues he purchased, including a snowmobile racing series, 10 classes of jetski racing, at least five truck and tractor pulling leagues, 32 classes of Inshore and Offshore boat racing, drag boating, Formula 1 boat racing, and finally, “rodeo and bull riding.” Almost overnight, the IHRA went from being almost out of business to sanctioning 137 different classes of competition. (There are dozens of IHRA “member” tracks, as well as dozens of NHRA member tracks. The tracks run IHRA or NHRA rules, and victories at the little tracks help decide regional championships.)


Jared C. Tilton/Getty Images/NASCAR


Among those sanctioning classes was an oval track stock car series which only lasted for a handful of races before the IHRA pulled the plug. That parallels one of Cuttell’s most puzzling purchases: Rockingham Speedway, the (almost) one-mile oval track in Rockingham, North Carolina, in the middle of NASCAR country. The 61-year-old track hosted NASCAR races up until 2004, and the track was closed for long periods until 2025, when NASCAR returned with its Craftsman Truck series and the O’Reilly Auto Parts series, and the track was angling to get a NASCAR Cup race. Given the turbulence around Cuttell and the IHRA, some industry insiders wonder if Rockingham will be hosting any NASCAR racing at all in 2027.


For IHRA racers, Darryl Cuttell and his seemingly bottomless checking account was an answered prayer. In 2024, Cuttell came riding up on a white stallion to save the series—or more accurately, in a black, 1970 split-grille Chevrolet Camaro Pro Mod—which he had purchased from Larry Jeffers Race Cars, a dragster builder in House Springs, Missouri. It so happened that Jeffers was the latest owner of the IHRA, and it also happened that Cuttell had come into some money.


Instagram/ihradragracing


How much money? That’s unclear. But last week I spoke to Richard Freeman, owner of Elite Motorsports, which qualified seven cars into the Top Fuel and Pro Stock classes at the most recent NHRA race, the Northwest Nationals in Seattle. He also has several businesses back home in Oklahoma, not all of them racing-related. Freeman recently paid a visit to the secretive Cuttell at Darana Hybrid in Hamilton, Ohio. Cuttell wanted Freeman to buy the IHRA and become owner number eleven. The price: $500 million.


According to Freeman, Cuttell told him, “Man, I’m in bad shape,” and more than once Cuttell complained that “Elon Musk owes me $600 million.”


As mentioned, Cuttell said in an interview last Friday with a Memphis newspaper that Musk owes Darana Hybrid 40 percent of the $1.5 billion in work that he says he did for Musk on his data centers—which comes out to exactly $600 million.


So assuming that Cuttell has already been paid said 60 percent of $1.5 billion, that would suggest that Cuttell has collected $900,000,000. Out of that, Cuttell had to pay salaries, expenses, and subcontractors, but apparently he had enough left over to turn the drag racing world on its head.


Now, though, it sounds like there’s very little left of the money Cuttell was paid. He told the *[Daily Memphian](https://dailymemphian.com/section/business/article/65243/spacexai-contractor-memphis-data-center-owes-money-elon-musk)* that he owes $200 million to vendors and isn’t sure how he will pay them. Presumably that’s in addition to what he owes drag racers and support staff, as well as purses for the other non-drag racing series he bought, plus the notes and taxes due on his racetracks.


As for Richard Freeman, he offered Cuttell $7 million for the IHRA’s rolling stock, which included multiple 18-wheelers and some tractors. Cuttell turned it down. “And that was that,” Freeman said. “No way was I interested in buying the series and those racetracks.” By most accounts, Cuttell did very little negotiating for the tracks he bought, often paying well over market value. Freeman said Cuttell bought the long-dormant Heartland Motorsports Park in Topeka, Kansas, for $40 million. “Two years ago,” Freeman said, “I could have bought that place for $6 million.”


Facebook/IHRA


Aside from possibly overpaying for the tracks and series he bought, Cuttell offered race purses that were far richer than the IHRA could ever pay, in any of its nine previous incarnations. In April, I wrote about this particular IHRA’s last gasp,[the IHRA Triple Crown](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/nascar-at-coronado-and-ihra-triple-crown-leg-1-the-very-weird-weekend-that-was/) . Which turned out to be the Just-One Crown, as the last two legs were cancelled, and racers haven’t been paid for the first leg, with some owed six figures. The IHRA was offering comparatively enormous purses for the three Triple Crown races, all scheduled for off weekends for the rival NHRA, thinking some of the big names from that series would be attracted by the money; for Top Fuel and Funny Car, the three races would pay $150,000 for a win at each of the three tracks, more than double what the NHRA pays. And if a racer won all three Triple Crown races, they’d be paid an additional $1 million.


The Triple Crown was to be held at three separate tracks—first at Darana Maple Grove Raceway in Pennsylvania, then Darana National Trail Raceway in Ohio, with the finale at Darana Memphis Motorsports Park. (Cuttell names all the tracks he buys “Darana,” which is rumored to be a portmanteau of Darryl’s first name and the first name of his sister, which may or may not be “Ana.”)


Facebook/IHRA


Darana Maple Grove is one of the IHRA’s best tracks; Cuttell bought it while the Performance Racing Industry show in Indianapolis was underway last December. The purchase was a stunner, and if it was meant to embarrass the NHRA, it did, because they had a big race scheduled at Maple Grove later this year. The following week, the NHRA yanked the race from Maple Grove and rescheduled it at US 131 Motorsports Park in Martin, Michigan, which has never hosted a major NHRA race.

The leadoff Triple Crown race at Maple Grove was rained out; it was rescheduled for Darana National Trail Raceway in Ohio. But none of the marquee NHRA racers showed up, just the IHRA regulars. The IHRA promptly cancelled the two remaining Triple Crown races, then cancelled almost everything else, except for some tractor pull events. For some reason, they found traction.


Facebook/IHRA


Up until then, the money Cuttell was spending was nothing short of astounding, much to the delight of the long-suffering, IHRA-faithful racers. Besides the big purses, he provided multiple perks for all of the competitors, their families and crew members, including free dinners and free deliveries of ice in the morning.


Cuttell typically arrived in style, aboard a newly acquired helicopter or in the Darana Hybrid private jet, a Bombardier Challenger 300 with VIP seating. Once, he sent his jet to pick up drag racing pioneers[“Big Daddy” Don Garlits](https://www.hagerty.com/media/people/don-garlits-father-of-swamp-rat-ufo-enthusiast-and-unequivocally-the-greatest-drag-racer-ever/) , 94, and his longtime nemesis, Shirley Muldowney, 86, for a personal appearance at an IHRA track. Pro racers were impressed. Sportsman racers—those who do it primarily for love, not money—felt like they’d died and gone to heaven. By mid-season, they changed their tune and figured they’d only got it half right.


Garlits says he is still owed the $5000 appearance fee he was promised.


During this most recent decline, and amid the long list of warning signs, IHRA devotees were nevertheless stalwart in their love for Cuttell, roundly criticizing news outlets who reported the facts; bad news was the fault of those who printed it, and all. A much smaller contingent, though, saw black helicopters with every development, viewed every shred of information as an opportunity for a new and unsubstantiated theory of malfeasance. Leading that conjecture was an assertion that Cuttell was buying tracks to get the land, which he would eventually flip to Musk, who would replace the racing with data centers. That wasn’t remotely the truth.


But nobody knew for sure, because Cuttell wasn’t talking, and he did not return phone calls for this story. I also reached out to NHRA director of PR and communications Josh Hachat, for his company’s thoughts on Cuttell and the IHRA, but he replied, “We’ll probably steer clear on commenting. We prefer focusing on our 75th anniversary.”


## Failed Promises


Richard Freeman at left.


Will Lester/MediaNews Group/Inland Valley Daily Bulletin/Getty Images


Arguably, the biggest mistake Cuttell made with the IHRA was hiring, then promptly firing, some highly qualified motorsports executives. “You could see the writing on the wall, because anyone he hired, who had any sense at all, stayed there a very short time. And that’s because they weren’t ‘yes’ people. I don’t think his intentions were bad,” Freeman said, “but they were completely handled the wrong way.”


Early on, I was sufficiently interested in Cuttell and his IHRA to write a[story for Hagerty in April 2025](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/does-the-refreshed-ihra-mean-a-new-era-in-drag-racing-well-know-soon-enough/) titled, “Does the Refreshed IHRA Mean a New Era in Drag Racing? We’ll Know Soon Enough.” It was based largely on a phone interview with the IHRA’s Alan Reinhart, who had spent three decades as an announcer with the powerhouse NHRA. I spoke to him on Monday, April 21, and the story ran the next day. In it, Reinhart spoke passionately about the IHRA in general, owner Darryl Cuttell in particular: “You look at his business background, and his philosophy is that failure is not an option. He’s very driven, he’s very dedicated to it, and we have the resources to make it happen. And that’s exactly what we’re going to do.”


Six days later, I got an email from Reinhart. “Hey Steven, thanks for the nice story about the IHRA. If you haven’t heard, Rich Schaefer and I are no longer with the company. Not sure what their plan is now.” Schaefer, previously an NHRA division director, was named president of the IHRA less than three months before. He replaced Kenny Nowling, who lasted two weeks working for Cuttell.


Also gone: Scott “Woody” Woodruff, the chief operating officer who is very well regarded in the industry, and who had become the de facto face of the IHRA, since Cuttell is profoundly camera-shy. Woodruff said he pointed out internally that the IHRA’s online registration portal was so difficult to use that some racers just give up halfway through. It was not well received.


After nine months on the job, he was terminated with a one-word text from Cuttell: “Fired.” Woodruff’s response: “Thank you.”


Seemingly, there was no one Cuttell trusted enough to take their advice. He bought Atlanta Dragway, which had been owned by the NHRA until it decided it didn’t want the track any longer, and the grandstands were subsequently removed and the tower razed. What Cuttell bought was basically Georgia prairie, interrupted by two remaining strips of pavement. Cuttell said he’d rebuild it, and there would be racing there by the end of the year. There will not be.


Memphis Motorsports Park, renamed Darana Motorsports Park Millington.

He also bought Memphis Motorsports Park, which, like Heartland Park in Topeka, has a drag strip, oval track and a road course. It has been abandoned for years, and much of what is left has been vandalized. Cuttell pledged $60 million for track improvements, and there would be racing there by the end of the year, the local chamber of commerce was told. There will not.


Same thing with Heartland Park, though it is in far better shape than Memphis or Atlanta. The track was energized, and work was being done to bring the track back. There was even a manager overseeing it all, Emily Mawhinney, but she said on Facebook that she was fired by the IHRA, via email, in July. Amid the uncertainty, work has ceased, and several non-spectator events scheduled there have been cancelled.


No one knows what will happen to the tracks Cuttell bought: Some were reportedly owner-financed and would likely go back to the previous owner. Cuttell indicated to the Memphis TV station that he would likely have to sell some properties to help pay his bills, but potential buyers may be wary.

That visit to Darana Hybrid to look over inventory was not Richard Freeman’s first interaction with Cuttell. Two years ago, at a drag race, he listened in as Cuttell explained his business plan to other racers. Freeman was puzzled. “Nobody”, Freeman said last week, “who is a business guy, does something like that, because it makes zero sense. At any level, from any angle, zero sense.”


As for Elite Top Fuel driver and three-time NASCAR champion Tony Stewart—who drives for Freeman but owns Tony Stewart Racing, which fields an NHRA Top Fuel car for his wife Leah Pruett and a Funny Car for Matt Hagan—it caps a year of frustration. He was at least interested in what Cuttell planned for the IHRA and wanted to hear him out, but Stewart said “I couldn’t get him on the phone. Not once. Not all year.”

## The Leah Martin reign


Leah Martin


Facebook/IHRA


Between the date of Cuttell’s IHRA purchase and now, the organization had at least five presidents, or six, if you count a president who now insists that no, he was never actually president.


One of Cuttell’s more interesting hires for president was Leah Martin, who ran a prestigious boat race at Lake of the Ozarks in Missouri. Cuttell likes fast boats, and he had acquired *Spirit of Qatar* , a Mystic catamaran powered by a pair of T55 turbine engines that had reportedly been boosted to a total of 7500 horsepower. Sheikh Hassan bin Jabor Al-Thani, along with throttleman Steve Curtis, had made a 244-mph run, a world record, at the Ozarks event in 2014.


*Spirit of Qatar* was tuned up and rebranded the *Darana Hybrid* in the now-familiar chartreuse green that Cuttell favors on almost everything, be it his plant in Ohio, or his websites, or the buildings on his racetracks. At the 2024 Ozarks event, Cuttell and carryover throttleman Curtis made a 242-mph pass on a circuit that was a quarter-mile shorter than the one for the Sheikh’s 244-mph run.


Though Martin had no drag racing experience, she apparently impressed Cuttell, and she was introduced as the IHRA president at the December 2025 PRI trade show, the first female president of any major motorsports sanctioning body.


Five months later, Martin was overseeing the major Thunder on the Beach event in Cocoa Beach, Florida, part of the IHRA Offshore Series, one of the puzzling non-drag-racing series that Cuttell had either bought or begun. Cuttell had announced he would be pumping an incomprehensible $7.2 million into the Powerboat series, including a purse of $2 million.

In the middle of that Cocoa Beach event, with no discussion, Martin learned she was “let go in a very public way without an explanation I still haven’t received,” she wrote on LinkedIn. In a separate text, she wrote, “I’m at a loss for words, I have poured my heart and soul into this sport at the cost of time with my family.”


IHRA


According to the website[speedonthewater.com](https://speedonthewater.com/ihra-and-leah-martin-part-ways/) , which covered the Cocoa race: “Martin’s husband, Justin, was the IHRA safety director. When he found out Leah had been fired right before the start of the Super V and Extreme race, he reportedly had the pace boat head back in to the pits and he disembarked. Because it’s about a 15-minute ride from the pits to the racecourse, that created a major delay to the start of the race.” One of the competitors inconvenienced “was IHRA president Darryl Cuttle (sic) who was racing unopposed” in a 52-foot MTI catamaran.


That story also said that Utah insurance executive Tommy Thomassie, an experienced marine competition pro, had replaced Leah Martin, effective immediately. His presidential reign was brief. “Darryl asked me to take the position, I accepted it, and he personally told several people, introducing me as the president of the IHRA,” Thomassie told *Hagerty.* “The following day \[May 18\], I received a call from Dustin Farthing, who patched in Darryl, and Darryl then informed me that he had made a mistake; he intended for me to be president of Powerboating, and Dustin Farthing was going to the president of the IHRA. And all of this was weeks after I had resigned my position as executive vice-president and director of Powerboating.”


Indeed, Cuttell had reached into his IHRA Professional Watercraft Series for the latest and reportedly still-current overall president, Dustin Farthing, a watercraft racer. In fact, according to his[own website,](https://farthingracing.com/dustin-farthing) Farthing “is thought by many to be the greatest Jet Ski racer of all time.”


From outward appearances, it looks as though Farthing’s main responsibility has been to provide corporate quotes for press releases announcing the postponement and reorganization of the IHRA’s various series, with an exception being tractor pulling, which seems to have survived and is all that remains of the IHRA.com’s reporting on current events. Eleven of the last 12 stories in the “Latest News” section are about tractor pulls.

## The Cuttell-Musk Connection


As for the previously mentioned dealings between Darryl Cuttell and the world’s richest man, given the amount of time and energy and money Cuttell has spent on the IHRA these past two years, it seems possible that his dedication to the sport came at the expense of his core business, Darana Hybrid.


Which, the company says, has “over 800 fully completed construction contracts valued from $10,000 to $40,000,000,” as a subcontractor for companies including Amazon, UPS, FedEx, Starbucks, Coca-Cola, Target, Pepsi, Kraft Heinz, Cargill, Frito-Lay and Jennie-O. Darana Hybrid is an acknowledged expert in building conveyor-type systems used by companies like Amazon to pull products from stock and send them down the line from one step to the next, and the next and the next, and eventually out the door to the customers.


Brandon Dill/The Washington Post/Getty Images


In Darana Hybrid, Musk found a lean, fast-moving contractor to handle construction of his latest projects in Memphis and its Southaven suburb. And Cuttell responded in kind, dropping $10.5 million on a 169,000-square-foot industrial building located near Musk’s pet projects, to house Darana Hybrid equipment needed to work for Musk.


That first project, Colossus, went online in Memphis in mid-2024, not long before Cuttell became deeply interested in the IHRA. Called “the world’s largest supercomputer,” Colossus is essentially a giant data center, but not one built out in the open, which typically draws community criticism and often downright outrage because of energy, noise, and environmental concerns. Colossus was constructed inside a massive, long-shuttered factory once used by Electrolux to make vacuum cleaners and Frigidaire appliances.


Brilliant, really. Plus, Darana Hybrid knows its way around Memphis, as it was based there before its move to Ohio, and Cuttell himself used to be a resident. Regarded as a “midsized” contractor, there was an element of surprise within the data center world when Darana began applying for big work permits on the project. “A data center industry veteran who reviewed the permit applications said it’s unusual for a smaller company like Darana Hybrid to handle a project of Colossus’ scale,”[reported one data-savvy website](https://b17news.com/elon-musks-xai-is-spending-at-least-400-million-building-its-supercomputer-in-memphis-its-short-on-electricity/) a year ago.


In January, when he appeared in Memphis to announce his purchase of the moribund Memphis Motorsports Park, Cuttell visited St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital and gave the administrators a check for $10 million. He said there would be more.


Facebook/IHRA


Brandon Dill/The Washington Post/Getty Images


Although there is no question that plenty of proposed data farms are getting immense pushback from both politicians and residents, that’s not Musk’s style. In Memphis, he found a city eager to attract high-tech investors. Then he found that 750,000-square-foot vacuum cleaner factory that had been sitting empty since 2022, even though the LEED-certified building had opened just 12 years earlier.


Who else would need 750,000 square feet? It was perfect for an unobtrusive, nicely concealed data center. And Musk looks like a local hero for repurposing a legitimately unsellable building and populating it with new employees.


And to complete that project in 122 days, or 123, by Cuttell’s reckoning, is remarkable by any measure. Darana Hybrid’s work for Elon Musk and Space xAI should have been enough to ensure a busy, high-profile future for Darana Hybrid, a company that Cuttell started himself, working alongside his seven employees. According to the website[Datanyze](https://www.datanyze.com/companies/darana-hybrid/56480094) , Darana Hybrid now employs 379.


But as Cuttell told the *Daily Memphian* last week, had he known this crisis was coming, “I would have demanded more money up front. But we were in such a big rush to get this job done,” he said. “I never got a thank you or nothing for pulling one off in six weeks. I mean, that’s super dirty.”


Brandon Dill/The Washington Post/Getty Images


## Where’s the Money?


Even if Darryl Cuttell does get the $600 million he claims that Elon Musk owes him, why did he spend so heavily on the International Hot Rod Association? And all the tracks and series he acquired, or tried to? He had to know the odds of making a dime were slim.


I asked Cuttell that question at the 2025 PRI trade show, and he said it was because he wanted to make it “fair for the racer, for the little guy. He isn’t getting that now.” Without naming the NHRA, he was effectively naming the NHRA. That was all he’d say; it was Leah Martin or longtime broadcaster Ralph Sheheen who did the talking at press conferences that weekend. Never Cuttell.


Facebook/IHRA


Fast-forward to last spring. The money ran out, and apparently there was no one to tell Cuttell, “ *Enough,* already.” Indeed, the IHRA had been ripe for a revival. Had Cuttell invested in the series conservatively, and concentrated only on drag racing, that money might have lasted a lot longer, and perhaps could have provided a solid foundation. But as one drag racing insider, who had a very close look inside Cuttell’s IHRA, guessed at what happened: “Darryll wasn’t just spending money he had made from Elon Musk, he spent money he *thought* he would be making from Elon Musk.”


So the IHRA is effectively done. Again. There’s nothing left. So many people and companies have not been paid, including the racers who won in that first and only Triple Crown event. Tracks have not been paid. Emergency workers and concessionaires, most all of which fronted the money it took to get to the races and stay for the duration, are out that investment. At last word, the production company that provided the free, high-quality live digital feed of the major races hasn’t been made whole and is owed well into six figures. Even some of the companies that rented Cuttell the mandatory Ferris wheels that spun lazily over every major IHRA event are left standing in line.


Will everybody get paid? Richard Freeman said that based on his conversations with Cuttell, the odds seem long. Cuttell might be lucky, Freeman said, to just get out of this mess and remain a free man.


Facebook/IHRA


Many of the IHRA racers who planned their season by what they expected to earn by competing in the IHRA, spent money on their cars, shops, and equipment, based on what they were told by the sanctioning body. Either because they are limited geographically, or because the IHRA’s rules and competition classes differ from the NHRA’s, a substantial number of racers now have no place to race. There’s already a plethora of drag cars being offered for sale online and elsewhere, and seldom at a profit.


Alan Reinhart, the 30-year NHRA employee who jumped to the IHRA and then was gone a week after I interviewed him, finally made his true opinion of Cuttell and the IHRA known in an online post. “I do believe that Darryl went into this with the intention of it being Great! Then instead of listening to experts that he hired, listened to morons who knew nothing about how to make this work. Congrats to those guys, it was you, not us, that cost Darryl a few hundred million dollars and left this mess for the racers who believed.”


But none of that answers that question for Cuttell—what did he see in the IHRA that led him to spend that “few hundred million dollars” on the series? Leah Martin had been named president just five weeks earlier when she gave an interview to speedonthewater.com, and journalist[Matt Trulio asked that question.](https://speedonthewater.com/straight-talk-with-ihra-president-leah-martin-interview-no-2/) Here’s what Martin said: “For his legacy. He’s never been married. He’s never had children. So he feels like this is his legacy. He loves racing. He loves speed. Darryl sees this as his legacy.”


Facebook/IHRA


Well, not *this* perhaps. *This* will take a long time to reconcile. Is this the last chapter in the IHRA’s 56-year history? I asked Grok, Elon Musk’s online bot that researches questions and answers them, “What will happen to the IHRA?”


Grok said, “The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA) remains stable and operational.”


Perhaps I should be more specific. “What will happen to the IHRA racing series?”


Grok thought for another 20 seconds, possibly with the help of Colossus in Memphis that Musk and Cuttell built, and came back with this: “The International Hot Rod Association (IHRA) is in significant turmoil in mid-2026 under owner/CEO Darryl Cuttell (who acquired it in late 2024). Professional/national series have been largely canceled or heavily cut for the rest of 2026 amid budget shortfalls, facility issues, staff cuts/layoffs, and reported payment problems, with a stated shift toward grassroots programs and a planned ‘reset’ for 2027.
