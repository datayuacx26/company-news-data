# Celebrating Route 66: Photo Tips for Capturing “The Mother Road” on Its Centennial Anniversary

Say the name “Route 66” and it conjures up dreams of following legendary authors, songwriters, and filmmakers down a road famously called “America’s Main Street.” I did just that, in a Volkswagen van filled with camping gear and film cameras, multiple times in the 1990s. Revisiting those photographs had me wanting to[do it all over again](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automobilia/route-66-in-a-new-yorker-nine-days-eight-states-and-two-transmissions/) , and not just because the road’s 100th anniversary is being celebrated this year.


I remember thinking about why this cultural archetype has inspired so many similar road trips as I traversed the roughly 2500-mile section that makes up legendary U.S. Route 66. As John Steinbeck famously wrote, “We do not take a trip, a trip takes us.” My takeaway was that everyone loves photographing on a road trip. Great destinations lure you back for another look because you can’t forget your first one.


Randy Wells


I’m not alone in thinking it’s cathartic to find yourself without a locked-in agenda, following your own path using just a map as a guide. Route 66 is off-the-grid and has no GPS available at times. It’s the perfect opportunity to disconnect and escape from modern life with a car and a camera. Perhaps that’s part of the reason it remains this nation’s iconic symbol of automotive culture.


The idea was[born in 1926](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automobilia/the-quail-honors-route-66s-centennial/) just as the U.S. was launching its first federal highway system. A route connecting local, state, and municipal byways across eight states was soon created, but it took until 1938 before the first continuously paved road from Chicago to Los Angeles was completed.

Running across parts of Illinois, Missouri, Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, and California, Route 66 followed a path that had been used by settlers and those who migrated west from the Missouri and Oklahoma Territories. Soon service stations, diners, and motels had sprung up all along the route for eager tourists and motorists. Portable Kodak cameras made it easy to document the journey, and its storied history was ensured.

Eventually, a more easily navigated[interstate freeway system](https://www.hagerty.com/media/great-reads/70-years-of-the-interstate-how-the-u-s-highway-system-built-the-america-we-know/) was authorized, which would include a new connection between Chicago and Santa Monica. The thoroughfares constructed ran roughly parallel to “The Mother Road,” and today we know them as I-55, I-44, I-40, I-15, and I-10, traveling east to west. U.S. Route 66 became a “blue highway,” a secondary scenic road named because it was now outlined in that color on some maps.


Randy Wells


By the early ’60s, the motels and cafes along that road were mostly abandoned, with the slower route periodically merging with the major interstates. Even though it was no longer one continuous road, Route 66 developed a resurgence at the end of the 20th century, because travelers wanted to experience local nostalgia off the beaten path.


Randy Wells


I firmly believe that serendipity and following your intuition on a road trip like this can open new possibilities for creating some of your best photographs. So, if you decide to retrace this famous byway in a Steinbeck, Kerouac, or *Easy Rider* style, there are lots of interesting choices of charming, meandering back roads along the way.


Randy Wells


If you’ve seen Disney’s animated movie *Cars* , you’ll probably recognize some of the scenery and the portrayal of a bypassed town that was based on the western part of Route 66. This compelling imagery exists in real life as well. Capturing it in photographs can hopefully be made easier with some of the tips found here.


Pulling over when something catches your eye through the windshield is not a bad place to start. However, having some kind of plan for what you intend to photograph is helpful to making sure you don’t come home wondering, “Where are all the images I *really* wanted.” Know before you go, and check for activities and events along your path, so you don’t miss them on your journey.


Randy Wells


Aside from the Instagram images we’ve all seen of famous spots like the Blue Swallow Motel in Tucumcari, New Mexico, the oft-photographed Wigwam Motel teepees, and standing on the corner in Winslow, Arizona, there are many other less familiar but potentially more rewarding scenes along Route 66 to make pictures of.


I will skip over equipment and shutter speed/aperture settings in this article, because a cellphone camera is probably all you will need, unless your intent is to print large pictures. A tripod and polarizing filter are also not usually necessary, although they can be handy for certain images.


Composition and lighting are much more important to the success of a photograph, as are your emotion and connection with the subject. Periodically ask yourself why you are photographing, along with “what am I trying to say?”


Randy Wells


Arriving at optimal times for the light to enhance a scene can be tricky on such a sprawling road trip, so try to make room in your schedule to get to your chosen prime locations during sunrise or sunset. Avoid harsh midday sun, unless it accentuates texture, such as with adobe or peeling paint.

Some of the best shots of man-made structures on Route 66 are linked to graphic colors, nostalgia and details. Look for symbols of Americana, like abandoned gas stations and faded rusty cars, to evoke a sense of place. Glowing neon signs at dusk are always a hit, as are compositions that evoke a romantic, cinematic feel. Try to avoid clutter in your images, like telephone wires and poles.

Lighting and composition are even more important in photographing landscapes, as it takes low directional light to accentuate the flat land of the Midwest and majestic buttes of the Southwest. Emotional impact is your goal. Check the corners of your composition and remember that the viewer’s eye always goes to the brightest area of the frame, so crop or reposition yourself as necessary. Remember that tightly cropped “macro” shots can be just as informational as sweeping vistas.

A picture of a person says a thousand words, as they say, especially if it captures their authentic likeness. Soft light or open shade is usually ideal lighting for portraits to avoid deep shadows, but backlighting or side light can work on a sunny day. Try overexposing for a dreamy look. Look for a moment that rings true. Candid gestures can breathe life into a travel series.

Try finding a different angle of your vehicle and other cool cars on the road. Look for leading lines and winding byways from an elevated view at sunrise or sunset, if possible. You can also place your lens close to the asphalt and angle it up in harsh light, being careful to avoid flare and your shadow. Be safe, shoot for a lyrical feeling, and always be ready for the unexpected moment.


Randy Wells


Overall, I would recommend that you aim to go beyond a documentary photograph. See if you can make your photos more impressionistic than journalistic. Beyond that, the main thing is to keep it fun. The whole purpose of photography should be joy. Then you can be inspired to spend a little extra time *making* a photograph that will have you coming back for more.
