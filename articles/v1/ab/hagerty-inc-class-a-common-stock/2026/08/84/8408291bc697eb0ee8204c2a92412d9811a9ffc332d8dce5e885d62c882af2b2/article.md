# 5 Things That Turn Any Car Into a Maintenance Nightmare

The cool air of a summer night wafts in through the open garage door just as the jack settles your project car onto the jack stands while the radio starts the perfect song to kick off a night of wrenching. Unfortunately, there is always the chance that evening turns from wrenching night to wrenching nightmare. Even on cars we love, some jobs or situations sneak up on us and conspire to drain the fun out of the project like it was 0w-20 oil pouring from a large drain plug.


Not every job is a nightmare, nor does every car truly reach nightmare status; however, it’s things like these five items that pop up and can turn an already annoying project into a downright pain. Sometimes a little preparation goes a long way, and other times there is little to do in the war with motor nature. Don’t believe me? Here are five examples of small things that can remove a lot of the fun from wrenching on your project.


## Mixed language


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Some of us are the types of people who have had wrenches in hand often enough to eyeball a piece of hardware and grab the correct wrench out of the toolbox. Sometimes that’s based in experience with a specific project and having been there before, while other times it is just a finely tuned eyecrometer getting a perfect read on the size[at a glance](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/heres-everything-the-head-of-a-bolt-can-tell-you/) . If you work on mixed projects or even just cars of a certain era, you are likely not only figuring out the size but also what system that size might be measured in.


Luckily, there are a lot of crossover or near-crossover sizes that allow us to still look smart when our friends are over and we’ve already played off going back to the toolbox for a different-sized wrench or socket once and still didn’t get it right. A 13mm is very close to 1/2″ and 8mm can pass for a 5/16″ if you need to make things happen.


## Bad packaging


[Brandan Gillogly](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/brandan-gillogly/)


No brand is safe, but we all know a few of the greatest hits when it comes to bad packaging. Things like the “ring of fire” Subaru oil filter or the starter hiding under the intake manifold on a Cadillac Northstar. It’s something that we DIY home wrenchers often love to hate on. I’m sure there are great reasons for every one of those infuriating design flaws that lead to a 4-inch-long bolt constrained in a 3-inch-wide area. Surely there is a reason. We just haven’t heard a good one yet.


## Corrosion


The factory black paint is flaking off and making way for rust on this iron block.


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Oh, how nature is unrelenting. For most vintage cars, the lack of protection against corrosion meant steel began the transition into iron oxide hours or days after the mess of parts bolted together could be called a car. The battle between those who want to preserve a car and corrosion is long and well-documented. In fact, we’d wager the fear of finding or having to deal with rust has kept more than a few from plunking down cash on what[could have been](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/mechanical-sympathy/the-automotive-rorschach-test/) a dream car. Of course, there are no shortages of stories to the opposite.


Regardless of how or when it appears, rust is something that can take any simple job and quickly turn it into a three-evening nightmare. Threads disappear, and bolt heads somehow become convinced to shed their corners as if the car must fully molt during a restoration. Corrosion literally ruins all the fun.


## Previously deferred maintenance


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


A few years ago, a 1999 Chevrolet Silverado 1500 became the nicest truck I had ever owned. Unfortunately, I soon found out my rating system that revolved heavily around mileage and rust failed to account for the owner who will tolerate things that just stop working one day. Passenger window won’t roll down? Oh well. Tailgate rattles and won’t close consistently? Don’t open it unless you have to. Oh, and don’t forget if you want FM radio you need to be touching the shifter.


I love a good beater-with-a-heater that gets me around through winter with the compromise that it is also literally falling apart around me. This truck wasn’t falling apart. It just needed a full front-end rebuild, new rear leaf springs, two window motors, a handful of sensors on the engine, and a pair of O2 sensors. Most of those things had been problems for a long time and had stacked up since the previous owner seemingly never did any upkeep. It made working on the truck a pain because there was always one more thing broken and none of it ever seemed to be traceable to something I did. It got sold when I was tired of dealing with its endless problems and six-foot box. My friend who bought it enjoyed it for nearly three years relatively trouble-free. I guess that truck just liked him more. Or maybe I caught it up on maintenance and should have held onto it longer. I’ll never truly know.


## Bad parts availability


This is a Corvair fan that lost balance and broke apart while the engine was running.


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Here is a good cooling fan for a Chevrolet Corvair.


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


All of these items had to come off in order to change out the broken fan.


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


There is little more frustrating in automotive repair and restoration than knowing what is wrong with a system, and even having the exact flawed part in mind, but being unable to do anything about it due to parts scarcity. Whether it’s one-year-only models or parts that just haven’t been made since the Nixon administration, as I discovered after the magnesium fan shattered atop the 140-hp engine in my 1965 Chevrolet Corvair. While a few modern fans exist, the stockpile of good fans is only getting smaller as the last ones were likely produced in the late 1960s. A lot of cars have similar issues. In some modern cars, it might be one small chip or sensor, while in older cars it can be a carb float or brake lining rivet. Regardless, challenges like this are unavoidable when diving into the old car hobby.
