# American All-Stars: 4 World’s Fair Moonshots to Reunite at 2026 Pebble Beach Concours

The Chicago World’s Fair opened in 1933, during the centennial of the city’s founding. But planning for the event began in 1928, well before the October 1929 stock market crash that catalyzed the Great Depression. This “Century of Progress International Exposition” was initially intended to memorialize 100 years of scientific achievement. The nation’s financial implosion altered the Fair’s focus.


“The emphasis shifted,” says Bob Joynt, an automotive and Chicago historian. “Rather than explaining how wonderful everything is or has been, it focused on the idea that the future is going to be better.”


Skyline view of Grant Park and the 1933 Chicago World’s Fair, A Century of Progress International Exposition.


Chicago Sun-Times/Chicago History Museum/Getty Images


Luxury carmakers did not necessarily share this optimism. While mass-market General Motors and Chrysler profited and expanded share during the downturn, by 1933, many stellar marques, including DuPont, Marmon, and Peerless, had already shuttered. Still, four pinnacle brands, all dangling in varying degrees of precarity—Cadillac, Duesenberg, Packard, and Pierce-Arrow—committed to unveiling show cars in Chicago. They were the Duesenberg SJ Rollston Arlington Torpedo Sedan, Packard Dietrich Sport Sedan, Pierce-Arrow Silver Arrow, and Cadillac V-16 Fleetwood Aero-Dynamic Coupe.


“Each of these cars, individually, was representative of the very finest that these companies could manufacture,” says Joynt. Unfortunately, their target consumers had largely evaporated or renounced conspicuous consumption. “So, they were trying to compete for a market that had disappeared.”


Courtesy Lehrman Collection


Believing in the captivating power of aspiration, all four automakers aimed for maximum ostentation and experimentation. For two of the brands, the show cars turned out to be compelling but failed Hail Marys. Another left nothing on the table, but economic realities ultimately forced a retreat from grandiosity. The most pioneering of the four became an avatar of inventiveness, helping to transform the company and the industry at large.


***


At this year’s Pebble Beach Concours, these four moonshot cars will comprise a special class, gathering for the first time since[the 1933 Fair](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/the-ambitious-automakers-of-the-1933-chicago-worlds-fair/) . Three of the original cars still exist. Ironically, the most novel of the quartet didn’t survive, so a period-correct production version will serve as a substitute. Three will compete for Best of Show; one already garnered that honor at a past Pebble Beach Concours.


“I think the mere presence of them is more important than the trophy outcome,” says Pebble Beach chief judge Chris Bock. “When we present the awards for that class, we will bring all four up and over the ramp, so we can memorialize the moment.”


## Duesenberg SJ Rollston Arlington Torpedo Sedan


Courtesy Nethercutt Collection


Courtesy Nethercutt Collection


Courtesy Nethercutt Collection


Duesenberg SJs were pinnacle vehicles. By bolting a centrifugal supercharger to its Model J’s innovative 6.9-liter, four-valve-per-cylinder, double-overhead-camshaft straight-eight, the Indiana factory increased output from an already outrageous 265 horsepower to a stratospheric 320, creating the era’s most potent road car. Only 36 were produced.


An SJ chassis cost nearly $10,000; a coachbuilt body added 50% or more. But sales tanked after the Depression, and high prices didn’t yield fiscal success. “Duesenberg was always in an amazing niche at the top of the market. But I’m not sure it ever made any money,” says Bock, the chief judge. By the early ’30s, Joynt says, the company was “basically using up parts and engines they’d built in the late 20s, just trying to raise cash.”


Courtesy Nethercutt Collection


Duesenberg’s Fair entrant, an argent Rollston-bodied sedan, may have exemplified this practice but was no less magnificent. Its long, canted hood, chopped roof, side-mount spares, and abundance of chrome weren’t exactly radical. But its perfect Gordon Buehrig–penned lines (Cord 810, Auburn Boattail Speedster) were striking. As was its $20,000 price tag, which provided its nickname: “The Twenty-Grand.”


“The Twenty-Grand is one of our flagship cars,” says Cameron Richards, curator of California’s Nethercutt Collection, which his great-grandfather, J.B. Nethercutt, founded. “It’s a car J.B. knew of from reading about the Century of Progress fair.” A consummate collector, J.B. found and purchased it in the late 1970s.


Courtesy Nethercutt Collection


He subjected the car to an enduring restoration, even enlisting Buehrig’s assistance to reverse some unfortunate updates. “Buehrig said, ‘I not only have the original build sheets. I still have the paint,” Richards says. He was flown to Los Angeles to assist. The car won Best of Show at Pebble Beach in 1980. Its appearance this year is “at least its ninth time” at the Concours, Richards says.


Richards, 28, has strong lifelong memories of the car. “I recognized how people reacted to it when I was riding in it as a child,” he says. “Nobody cared about who was in the car. All they were looking at is the car. It really evokes emotion in people.”

The Twenty-Grand sold following the Fair, but it failed to save Duesenberg, which, despite its era-defining designs and engineering, lacked funding and personnel for ongoing innovation. It shuttered in 1937.


Despite pervasive reportage on the car’s presence in Chicago, there are no known photos of the SJ onsite. “We still ask the internet, ‘If you had a great grandpa there, and they had a camera, please let us know,’” Richards says.


## Packard Dietrich Sport Sedan


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


In the early 20th century, Packard was the dominant American luxury carmaker, having pioneered[the smooth, powerful, V-12 “Twin Six” engine](https://alloymag.com/packard-twin-six/) in 1915. The brand remained financially stable in 1933, according to Bock, but it was suffering. “Their production numbers just plummeted” during the Depression, he says.


Attempting to maintain dominance, but unable to afford or risk major invention, Packard, like Duesenberg, brought an upscale iteration of an existing product to Chicago. It was a custom Dietrich Sport Sedan in a rich mocha. Subdued and elegant, it featured a refined exterior nearly devoid of chrome, an extended hood, and a rear-mounted spare that visually lengthened the fenders and engine compartment.


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Inside, it was all flash. “Everything on the interior in terms of brightwork was gold-plated,” says Jeff Orwig, curator of Maine’s Bahre Collection, the deceased founder of which, entrepreneur Bob Bahre, purchased the car from former *Los Angeles Times* publisher Otis Chandler in 1993, before restoring it. This gilding includes the door handles, cigar lighters, steering column, gas pedal, and lighting, even the needles in the gauges. Burled elm rear cabinetry incorporates a bar, writing table, smoking set, makeup mirror, and magazine rack.


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


“The Packard was awarded the prize at the World’s Fair, by a jury of artists,” Orwig says. It was pulled from the brand’s display and placed in the Travel and Transport building’s rotunda. From there it derived its nickname, “The Car of the Dome.” After the Fair, it sold for around $12,000, a record for a factory Packard, according to Orwig.


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


Courtesy Bahre Collection/Packard


It is one of 13 stellar Packards in the 65-vehicle Bahre horde, including examples of all six 1932–34 12-cylinder V-windshield Dietrich body types. “Bob kept an open dialog with everybody he knew that had great cars,” Orwig says, a tactic to pursue and purchase notable classics. “It was one of his masterpiece moves.” This is the fourth time that the Bahre family has shown this Packard at Pebble, and Orwig’s 17th Monterey trip representing the collection’s cars.


Courtesy Bahre Collection


Packard’s battle for the shrinking pool of wealthy buyers proved futile. In response, in 1935, it released a lower-priced car line. “This broadened their appeal, but it softened the distinction of being a standalone holdout,” Orwig says. The pivot saved the firm financially but diminished its status. Packard lasted until 1958, never regaining its cachet.


Orwig is thrilled to participate in the class: “It’s a true honor to be part of this little subset of iconic vehicles that changed the automotive world.”


## Pierce-Arrow Silver Arrow


Courtesy Chris George/Barrett-Jackson


Buffalo-based Pierce-Arrow was known for producing stately vehicles for bankers and executives. “If you look at their cars from the late 1920s and early ’30s, they were elegant and refined, but sort of stodgy,” says Bock.


The car that Pierce unveiled in Chicago was a major deviation from the norm. From its recumbent V-shaped grille to its tapered and nearly windscreen-less fastback rear, the Silver Arrow showcased the emergent science of aerodynamics. Its headlights were deeply faired into the fenders, which also housed smooth enclosed compartments for the spare tires. It featured recessed door handles and lacked running boards. The Silver Arrow looked as if someone had taken a boxy 1930s sedan and placed it in a gale-force wind while it was still wet.


Courtesy Chris George/Pierce-Arrow


Courtesy Chris George/Pierce-Arrow


“I’m really attracted to a period of time in America when we used to make things that were permanent, and were creative about how we built things,” says northern California collector and mortgage industry titan, Chris George, who purchased the car in 2022. (It was previously in the[famed Harrah Collection](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/harrahs-oral-history-classic-car-sale-part-i/) and retains its 1960s Harrah restoration.) “This is a vehicle that is coming up on being a hundred years old, something that was hand-built in the early ’30s, and it’s something you can drive on the freeway at 75 mph.”


He is also drawn to its legacy. “Rob Williams, whose father Don Williams helped build the Blackhawk Auto Museum, said this is one of the most influential and forward-looking American vehicles, and probably one of the 10 most significant cars ever built in America,” George says. “They called it the car of the 1940s in 1933, because it was so advanced.”


Courtesy Chris George


Twenty-seven-year-old wunderkind Phil Wright developed the design when he worked at GM. “They turned him down, and Pierce-Arrow was really struggling in the Depression, so this was their kind of last-ditch effort to save the company,” says Brandon Baskett, George’s mechanic.


To complement its outré appearance, the car sported advanced engineering. It was powered by Pierce’s fluid 7.6-liter L-head V-12, and it employed a Stewart-Warner system that used the car’s rotational force to provide power braking.


Courtesy Chris George/Whitney Graham Co.


Courtesy Chris George/Whitney Graham Co.


Courtesy Chris George


Courtesy Chris George/Whitney Graham Co.


The Silver Arrow was priced at $10,000. “In 1933, the average yearly income was $800,” George says. “It’s insane to think that you built something that costs 15 times what a person could make in a year while the country’s in a state of depression, economically and emotionally.”


Courtesy Chris George/Barrett-Jackson


Courtesy Chris George/Barrett-Jackson


Pierce built and sold five copies of the car—three still exist—as well as several dozen similarly named, but far more traditionally styled, coupes. Sadly, this wasn’t enough to transform the company. Pierce-Arrow went bankrupt in 1938.


## Cadillac V-16 Fleetwood Aero-Dynamic Coupe


All images shown here of the V-16 Fleetwood show it as it appeared in February 1989. The restored car will make its official debut at the 2026 Pebble Beach Concours d’Elegance.


Courtesy Lehrman Collection


Cadillac entered the 1930s in a deficit and exited triumphant. “In the beginning of the decade, Cadillac was being outsold by Packard at least two-to-one,” says Joynt. “That net ratio had reversed by the end.”


The car it unveiled in Chicago, and its extended family of apex, “cylinder war”-dominating V-16 vehicles, contributed to that momentous turnaround.


“I’m not sure that the 16-cylinder Cadillacs ever made any money,” says Bock, noting that the division was barely viable during the Depression and was regularly considered for elimination. “But it certainly established their position and helped them to displace Packard as the leading luxury producer in the country.”


Courtesy Lehrman Collection/GM


The Aero-Dynamic Coupe shown in Chicago even further expanded the borders of Cadillac’s supremacy. One of GM’s first “Concept” cars, it was built on a production V-16 chassis but featured radical new design and engineering. This included a deeply canted windshield, an all-steel roof, a streamlined teardrop body, draft-less quarter windows, aluminum running boards, aircraft-inspired moldings and trim, and a built-in trunk that could house a spare tire, liberating the fenders (and tail) to flaunt their art deco swoopiness.


That prototype has been lost to history, sadly, perhaps a victim of WWII-era scrap-metal drives, according to Bock. Eventually, Cadillac built 20 production copies for discerning clients. Only eight were constructed on the V-16 platform from 1934 to 1937; just five still exist.


Courtesy Lehrman Collection


Courtesy Lehrman Collection


Courtesy Lehrman Collection


When Florida real estate magnate Sam Lehrman first heard that one of this literal handful of 1936 V-16 Aero-Dynamic Coupes was for sale, he performed meticulous due diligence. He researched the car, explored its significance, and visited Rhode Island to inspect, drive, and absorb it.


A highly disciplined collector who has shown at Pebble nearly a dozen times, he owns 14 cars—top-tier Packards, Rolls-Royces, and Duesenbergs among them—and only adds one if he deaccessions another.


Courtesy Lehrman Collection/GM


“The fact that it was available, and the fact that it was so rare and so iconic, I made a very straightforward decision,” he says. “I decided it was something I wanted to embrace.”


He has enlisted trusted specialist Steve Babinsky to perform a frame-off restoration, utilizing the original build sheet and employing period-correct colors and materials. The restoration began in 2024 and was not yet complete at the time of reporting. It will thus be fresh when it arrives at the Concours, something the judges there tend to privilege.


This suits Lehrman. “I do not go to Pebble Beach for my health,” he says. “I go to compete.”


Reuniting these cars is an ideal means to celebrate the diamond anniversary, the 75th iteration, of the Pebble Beach Concours, as it’s a jewel-box showcase of the zenith of the so-called Classic era. These vehicles, without a doubt, represent the absolute pinnacle of that period. “I think they are the ultimate,” says Boyd. But more than this, these cars represent an industry on the cusp of a key transition. “In 1930, cars that were being sold were essentially refined horseless carriages. By 1940, they were essentially modern cars,” says Joynt. “All of these four cars—and certainly the Pierce-Arrow and Cadillac—predicted this future.”
