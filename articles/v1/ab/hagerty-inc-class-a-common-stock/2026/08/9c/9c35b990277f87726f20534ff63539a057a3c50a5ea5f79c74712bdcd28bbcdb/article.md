# A Brief Guide to Weather Stripping

It’s hard to think of a car part as deeply unsexy as weather stripping—these are the rubber (or synthetics, like ethylene propylene diene monomer, or EPDM) strips that seal doors, trunks, and windows. Yet they serve as a vital barrier between sensitive parts of your vehicle and the elements. And if you’ve ever had to replace a cracked or torn one for your classic, you know they’re awfully hard—and often inexplicably expensive—to source. Like all plastic and rubber, this material degrades over time and loses its flexibility, so it doesn’t last forever, even in low-mile cars. We talked to John Kryta, a high-end muscle car restorer and owner of Inline Tube, and Richard Vaughan, a designer at Lucid, to learn why that is.


## There’s Nothing Like the Original


A lot of thought goes into these little pieces. A manufacturer usually specifies precise shape, thickness, and material corresponding to the duty it needs to perform. The problem with aftermarket (made by a third party and not the original manufacturer) weather stripping is that they often tend to differ from these original specifications—even if the shape of the piece pretty much looks the same.


“You don’t really know what the seal is chemically composed of,” says Vaughan.


Many aftermarket seals use a different material than the factory does—often a harder synthetic rubber—which can cause problems with fitment.


All of which is to say, if you can find OEM weather stripping, then you should pay for it. Often, that’s not entirely possible, in which case you should expect to do some fiddling to get things fitting just right. That being said, some aftermarket companies are better than others. Kryta recommends Metro Rubber, SoffSeal, and Steele Rubber Products.

## Profiles: Extruded vs. Molded


Many older cars (pre-1980) use simple extruded rubber—think Play-Doh fun factory—for their weather stripping. The catch is, manufacturers used different profiles for their weather stripping, so if possible, it’s best to buy weather stripping for your specific model.


Modern cars use more complex molded weather stripping, which is harder to manufacture and can’t be swapped for generic replacements. There’s hope for the future of keeping your newer classic weather-tight, though: Vaughan notes that prototype vehicles use 3D-printed thermoplastic polyurethane. Someday, DIYers may be able to print an entire set of seals. Til then, hunting for OEM and quality aftermarket weather stripping requires some diligence.
