# Automotive Design Takes a Commercial Break

Car designers used to be household names. New vehicle launches often provided a chance for motoring writers to meet[the design team](https://www.hagerty.com/media/design/the-myth-of-the-car-design-lone-genius/) and learn about their work, but you’ll be forgiven for struggling to name anyone associated with recent metal. Some even suggest that design is dead, but these accusations are nothing new.


Interestingly, Ferrari wheeled out renowned industrial designer Jony Ive to start promoting the work which has gone into drawing its bold new electric car,[the Luce](https://www.hagerty.com/media/design/ferrari-luce-design-conventional-or-intentional/) . Ive is one of very few modern designers that people actually know, and it’s no coincidence that his presence is felt more from the Apple products in every pocket or handbag, rather than transport.


Ferrari


Flickr/Marcin Wichary


Jony Ive’s first design for Ferrari, the Luce, and one of his first for Apple, the iMac.


Ive is a rare example of an industrial designer turning his attention to a car. Good design should have a positive impact on our lives, and cars are a rolling statement of our interests, beliefs, and values. They are a terrific canvas for anyone who is handy with a pencil. But if you really want to have your design noticed, you need to draw a bus.


Famous for his work on some of the prettiest, most successful sports cars of the mid-20th century, Giovanni Michelotti oversaw countless beautiful Ferraris, Maseratis, and Lancias, before spending time working for Standard Triumph and, eventually, the British Leyland organization.


Flickr/p&p photo


Wikimedia Commons/Riley


The Car Spy CC BY 2.0


Left to right: The Triumph Dolomite, Herald, and Stag were all the work of Michelotti.


While Triumph models, such as the Herald and[Dolomite](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/articles/buying-guide-triumph-dolomite-sprint/) , democratized the master’s work into a suburban setting, by the early 1970s, Michelotti’s style could be enjoyed for a few pence and a bus ticket. The successful Leyland National bus was arguably the designer’s most impactful work, being a common sight on British roads (and rails) for several decades—and serving millions of passengers in the process.


Wikimedia/Chris Sampson


Wikimedia Commons/Arriva436


Take a closer look at the elegant single-deck National, and you don’t even have to squint that hard to see certain similarities to Michelotti’s more famous automotive work, particularly given his involvement with almost every post-1960 Triumph built.


These buses were everywhere—on every street of every city in the U.K. well into the early 2000s. Ignore the exotica of the decades before, because this is the best way for any automotive designer to really contribute to the street scene.


Broad Arrow Auctions


Horacio Pagani’s[trajectory to greatness](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/kinardi-line/kinardi-line-4-every-pagani-driven-and-ranked/) was quite the opposite of that of Michelotti. Before a position sweeping the floors in Lamborghini’s Sant’Agata factory eventually guided him to become an engineering executive, the young Pagani cut his teeth using new composite materials to construct commercial and leisure vehicles.


For a few years in his early 20s, and before setting out to fulfill his Italian destiny, Pagani’s factory formed the shells and structural panels of Argentina’s finest campers, pickups, and even mobile studios from GRP and other novel materials. At that point in his life, the young Argentine would make whatever he was asked to by his clients.


Understandably unwilling to consolidate his position in the Argentine leisure vehicle market, the flexibility, innovation, and attention to detail that brought success in that field was soon applied to Pagani’s first racing car, a Formula 2 single-seater clad in panels of his own manufacture. It was his first step in the direction of hypercar greatness and some of the most beautiful wheeled creations ever seen.


Pagani


Pagani


A Pagani-designed camper from 1978, used as a mobile radio studio, and Horatio Pagani with an F2 car in 1979.


It’s not known if any of his contemporary leisure vehicles survived the political upheaval seen in the South American country, or the ravages of time, heat, and light on experimental materials used outdoors. If a ’70s chassis cab with a high-top camper framework does one day turn up bearing his name, it will certainly be interesting to see what Bonhams or Broad Arrow make of its prospects for resale.


Commercial vehicles live hard and often short lives, but that doesn’t mean they should be excused from being given character and identity. The Ford Cargo was a stalwart of the medium-to-heavyweight transport business for a couple of decades beginning in the 1980s, but you’ve might not have noticed the close resemblance it bears to two enormously popular small European cars of the late 20th century.


Wikimedia Commons/Rudolf Stricker


Flickr/Crash71100


Have a look at the Ford Fiesta Mk1 and the first-generation Renault Twingo above. You’ll see one very obvious similarity in the front end of these city cars: Both have their headlights stacked above a horizontally positioned indicator lens. Now, turn your attention to the bluff front end of the cabover Ford Cargo, and you’ll see that theme inverted, with a more obvious similarity to the Fiesta, naturally.


Wikimedia Commons/Mr.Choppers


French designer[Patrick Le Quément](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Le_Qu%C3%A9ment) was responsible for all three designs, as well as the earlier version of the Ford Sierra, which also bore his front-end design signature. Le Quément eventually became design director for Renault and was at the helm when many of the brand’s most distinctive creations, including the Series II Mégane,[Avantime](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/sorry-not-sorry-the-renault-avantime-does-froggy-style-like-nothing-else/) , and Vel Satis were launched, with mixed success.


Although the fortunes of his passenger-car designs ebbed and flowed, the Cargo truck sold strongly in Europe and the Americas from 1981 through 1997, although Le Quément’s original design was later watered down to align it with contemporary corporate design language.


Wikimedia Commons/Vauxford


Those distinctive early Cargos did maintain a consistent presence making deliveries, moving furniture, and handling any other task required of them, demonstrating the worth and penetration of good commercial vehicle design as an alternative to the more glamorous world of the car showroom.


If you want to take in the Anglo-French designer’s headlamp and indicator lens greatness today, you’ll probably have an easier time spotting an old Ford Cargo truck than you would an early Fiesta, and certainly a Twingo. Good automotive design always finds its way, and not always where expected.
