# 5 Collector Trucks You Can Drive Daily for $15K

Two years ago, a 1997 Toyota Land Cruiser[sold on Bring a Trailer](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/1997-toyota-land-cruiser-fzj80-140/) for $177,500. That is Ferrari money for what was, until recently, an ideal first car for a high schooler. The sale was far above[#1 condition (concours) value](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/toyota/land_cruiser/1994/1994-toyota-land_cruiser-fj80?id=aCn1I000000CwZvSAK) for the model, and it was a clear indicator of where the collector-truck market has gone: straight up, and mostly out of reach for us normal folks.


One level down, however, at the #2 (excellent) level in the Hagerty Price Guide, something strange happens. While pristine, show-grade trucks have gone stratospheric, higher-mile examples worthy of daily driving have been sitting flat—or quietly getting cheaper. Collector mania has, perversely, made the usable versions more attainable, not less.


Attainability—that’s the sweet spot for this list. And it has a name: The Fuel-Injection Era. It runs roughly from the mid-1980s through the mid-’90s and marks the period when trucks finally got electronic fuel injection, front disc brakes, and enough power to merge onto a freeway without drawing the ire of fellow commuters. They’re old enough to have souls, but new enough to start on the first cold crank on a daily basis, without drama.


I set three rules for this list: Each rig must run and drive like a modern vehicle; it has to be genuinely useful (a truck still has to truck); and it has to land around $15,000. Ideally, it will fall into solid #3 (good) territory, which we’ll use as a benchmark for an honest driver. The values here are current Hagerty Price Guide figures as of the latest update, so no letters from 2028, please.


To my chagrin, only five trucks (of the 12 or so I’d originally envisioned) came in below the $15,000 bar. Here they are.


## Toyota Pickup (1984–88, SR5 4×4, 22RE)


Mecum


Nothing on this list carries a reputation like the fourth-generation Toyota Pickup. And its fuel-injected 22RE four-banger is why. It’s understressed and functionally immortal, and it’s the engine that taught a generation that “old” and “reliable” aren’t opposites. Fuel injection enables it to fire instantly, even when cold. Beyond the powerplant, the Toyota’s front discs stop it in a reasonable distance, and its five-speed allows it to keep pace with traffic, albeit just barely. This is the paradigm ideal of a truck that you can trust on a Monday morning.


Here’s the wrinkle that makes it a “buy” right now: the market has split by condition. A concours SR5 4×4 is[around $44K](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/toyota/pickup/1988/1988-toyota-pickup-sr5?id=aCn1I000000CzaRSAS) and climbing (+7% on the year); an excellent one is $29,700 and rising. But a #3 driver has actually fallen 5.2%, to $12,700. Collectors are paying up for perfection while the trucks people actually drive get cheaper. Buy the honest, higher-mileage one and let the show-queen crowd overpay. The catch is rust: These dissolve from the rockers and bed up, so a clean Southwestern truck is worth traveling for.


## Jeep Comanche (1986–92, Base 4×4, 4.0)


Jeep


Everybody knows the Jeep 4.0-liter straight-six. Torquey and baritone, it’s the engine that gave the XJ Cherokee a cult following. Few people remember, however, that Jeep bolted a bed to that same platform. The Comanche is essentially an XJ with a pickup box. It boasts the same fuel-injected six, front disc brakes, and unibody road manners. You can commute in it exactly the way people commute in Cherokees.


And it’s the best bargain on this list, too. While XJ Cherokees, SJ Gladiators, and Grand Wagoneers all caught fire, the Comanche never did.[A #3 driver](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/jeep/comanche/1988/1988-jeep-comanche-base?id=aCn1I000000DT8qSAG) is just $11,000 and still drifting down (−9.8%). You’re buying the exact drivetrain everyone’s chasing, only in a useful little truck near the bottom of its market.


Like the SJ Gladiator before it, the Comanche is prone to rust between the cab and the bed. Scarcity is another issue, too: Jeep built a fraction as many Comanches as Cherokees. So, finding a clean one takes patience. With low production comes a dearth of spares, too. The parts shared with Cherokee are no problem; you can practically find those at Kroger. The Comanche-specific bits, however… those are near impossible to find.


Finally, and this isn’t a big deal, Comanches are tiny inside. Individuals over six-feet tall likely won’t fit without a femur reduction. Mark this as another small win for the short kings.


## Ford F-150 (1992–96 Fleetside, 2WD, 300 I-6)


Ford


If the Toyota is the import reliability benchmark, the ninth-generation F-150 with the 300-cubic-inch inline-six is its domestic equivalent. The low-revving, fuel-injected six is nearly as unkillable as the 22RE, but it moves a full-size truck without great strain. The 1992–96 body, now called “OBS” (for Old Body Style) or “Aeronose,” has aged better than any Ford truck before it. It has classic looks, potent power, and a cab modern enough to road-trip in without wishing you were dead.


The condition split here for the Aeronose is the most dramatic on this list. The value of an excellent one has[jumped nearly 20%](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/ford/f~150/1992/1992-ford-f~150-1!2_ton?id=aCn1I0000008Vo5SAE) in a year, to $29,400. At the same time, a concours truck is up to $44,500, while a #3 example sits essentially flat at around $11,000. That’s an $18,400 gap between “excellent” and “good” for paint that will get scuffed in a parking lot within a month.


Buy the good one, drive it, and let someone else fund the concours restoration. The catch, like with any reliable truck, is that a lot of these lived hard lives doing real work. Mileage and maintenance history will matter more than cosmetics.


## Chevrolet C1500 (1988–98 Fleetside, 2WD, 305 V-8)


Hagerty Marketplace/ThomasArcher


The GMT400 (the “OBS” Chevrolet/GMC 1500) continues to be driven daily by millions of people well into this century, and it shows. These trucks boast a fuel-injected V-8, ABS, a comfortable cab, and the deepest, cheapest parts supply of anything on this list. You can rebuild this entire truck from Rock Auto for $719. The base 305 V-8 won’t set your loins alight, but it’s smooth, simple, and starts every time.


The C1500 is also the calm center of this list. Where the Toyota and Comanche are falling and the Ford’s top end is rocketing, the C1500 just sits there, steady as she goes. The Fleetside shortbed in #3 condition is[priced between $13,500 and $14,500](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/c1500/1990/1990-chevrolet-c1500-1!2_ton?id=aCn1I000000DMbqSAG) , and values are flat across every condition. There’s no mania to this market, nor a falling knife. The C1500 is just a good truck at a stable, fair price. That’s a bonus: You’re not timing a market—you’re buying a tool that happens to be cool, too.


If you want more shove than the 305, the 5.7 (350) is a modest step up in price and the engine enthusiasts prefer. The 305 does the job, though. Don’t even think about the 454 SS, which lives in a different, pricier universe.


## Toyota Land Cruiser FJ80 (1991–92, 3F-E)


Toyota


Yes, the rig that opened this story for Ferrari money is on the list. But that six-figure example was a flawless, low-mile FZJ80—the later 212-horsepower version everyone covets. If you want to get into the 80 Series for $15,000, look to the early FJ80. It’s powered by the older, 155-horse 3F-E six. It is genuinely slow, especially on bigger tires with stock gearing. And the 3F-E does chug fuel like it’s a contest. But here’s what nobody tells you about that crude, agricultural engine: It is the 80 Series powerplant that will clear 300,000 miles while you neglect it (ask me how I know).


The FJ80 moved Land Cruisers onto coil springs and four-wheel disc brakes, making it the most road-friendly version to that point.[A #3 driver is $15,600](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/toyota/land_cruiser/1991/1991-toyota-land_cruiser-fj80?id=aCn1I000000Cu94SAC) —only slightly above the ceiling of our budget—but it’s also down 7.7% this year, a trend that is walking it into the $15K range. Mind the cliff, though. Below “good,” the values fall off steeply, and a #4 example is a mere $4200. The 80 Series market pays hard for a solid one and abandons the beaters, in other words. But if the pristine have become untouchable, the honest ones are quietly getting cheaper. Go figure.


## Final Thoughts


Notice the pattern running through all five rigs on this list? At the top, every one of these trucks is on fire. At driver grade, however, four of the five are flat or falling. That isn’t a coincidence—it’s an opportunity. The collector market is obsessed with perfection and has quietly stranded the usable versions. You know, the ones with 150,000 miles and a driver’s seat with some honest wear.


Nevertheless, every truck on this list will fire right up; merge onto a freeway (eventually); haul gear, dogs, and junk with aplomb; and cost less than a mediocre used late-model sedan. Buy a good example, skip the concours tax, and drive the thing. That’s the entire point of a truck, right? And somehow, it’s become a contrarian move.
