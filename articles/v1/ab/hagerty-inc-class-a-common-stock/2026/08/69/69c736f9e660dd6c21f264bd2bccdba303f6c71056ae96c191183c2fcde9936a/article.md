# What’s Your Craziest Auto Parts Recycling Story?

Auto parts aren’t only for the vehicles they were initially designed for. They can be so much more. Just ask owners of VW-sourced Bentleys, or any automaker that has multiple brands at various price points: a part made for one vehicle can very likely do the job on another, too. A cheap used Bentley can remain so (relatively speaking) with visits to VW and Audi parts bins, and Audis look pretty cool with Lamborghini Gallardo wheels. But we can do even more with car parts, as they can be recycled in very creative ways.


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


There will always be cars with a focus on art over operation, and many are festooned with objects that you might see on another vehicle. But this isn’t the only type of recycling out there. Car parts can be used in very creative ways. Take the 15+ year old electric string trimmer that I use to keep my lawn looking sharp.


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


I bought this thing from Harbor Freight, hoping I could get a few years out of it before needing to spend real money for a better-built unit from a more reputable brand. It exceeded my expectations, as the battery is still strong and the cuts are still precise. But after about a decade, an ancillary component failure (rollers used to edge a lawn) led to finding a nearly-new example on Facebook Marketplace for a mere $40. Now I had two of these things, and one turned into a “parts trimmer” that lived in my attic.


I consider myself fully vested in making one trimmer run from the two I have. So when both trimmers experienced eyelet failure at the trim line feed hole—sorry, I don’t know the correct terminology—I had to get a proper replacement made out of solid brass. I assumed I’d modify the plastic cup that holds the eyelet to make it work, and that was validated when a bag of new brass eyelets ($7) arrived in my mailbox.


Out came my cut-off wheel, a section of the plastic housing was removed, and in went the new brass eyelet. By design, the cut-off wheel made two long voids, which I planned on filling with epoxy once I verified the trimmer was working correctly. I was leery that the new eyelet would throw the trimmer out of balance, like mud in the wheel or a loss of wheel weights on a car.


A test of the new eyelet (with painter’s tape in place of glue) worked great, staying in balance and not chewing up the trimmer line. I was thrilled! A little too excited, because I kept on using the trimmer, and eventually the eyelet and unglued plastic housing launched itself free, flying deep into my lawn. I was furious with myself, but then I remembered I had a savior in my garage: spare plastic interior trim from a junked 1988 Ford Thunderbird!


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


My hero! After about a minute or so with the cutoff wheel, I made a new eyelet housing out of blue Ford interior plastic. Since I needed to finish my lawn to avoid the furor of my neighbors, I used quick-set super glue covered up in painter’s tape to ensure the tool would stay together long enough to tidy my front yard. It did, but peeling back the painter’s tape revealed the super glue’s stress marks. It was none too happy with operating in such a harsh condition. I took it all apart, cleaned up the mating surfaces, made a little batch of JB weld, and *voilà* : my beat-up trimmer works like new again!


It’s been two weeks since I took automotive interior plastics to a place it never belonged. And yes, this was a long-winded way of asking my question. So here it is again: **What’s your craziest auto parts recycling story?**
