# 1939 Maybach SW38 Special Roadster: Klockau Faints

As some of you fine folks may recall, last year I shared a[spectacular 1936 Mercedes-Benz 540K](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/klockau-classics/1936-mercedes-benz-540k-special-roadster-swoon/) from the Geneva Concours d’Elegance, held each August in downtown Geneva, Illinois. It is a fine show which I highly recommend to anyone who loves cars. And at the time, I thought that 540K was not going to be topped. I was wrong.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


At the 2025 show, I spotted easily a dozen awe-inducing cars to write up. But the top one was a car I knew about, even had a Franklin Mint model of one, but had never, ever seen up close: A Maybach.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


And while I knew of the cars, and the very, very basic history, my combined knowledge at the time of the sighting could be politely termed “slight.” But I was so gobsmacked seeing this fantastic car up close, I had to find out more.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


So with a bit of research, I wound up at Source Book Store, a fantastic cavalcade of vintage books in downtown Davenport, Iowa—a convenient five-minute drive from my office—and found a vintage Automobile Quarterly with a detailed account of the Maybach story. And I did it all for you, dear readers—but also to satisfy my own curiosity!


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


The most famous Maybachs are the V-12 Zeppelins, which came in both 7-liter and 8-liter versions. They were fantastic, *heavy* , and scary to drive, by many accounts. In fact, owners had to obtain a truck-certified license to drive one, as they were so heavy that they were considered trucks just by their weight. Later Zeppelins could do 100 mph for those bold or crazy enough to give it a try, considering the limited tire technology at the time.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


And if you’ve never seen one, that is likely because, for the entire marque’s existence, only about 2300 Maybachs of all types were ever built. And while the V-12 Zeppelins were the *creme de la creme* , all Maybachs were very large, very exclusive, and extremely luxurious conveyances.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


In fact, when I saw today’s featured car, I was not sure what model it was. The info card stated “1939 Maybach Special Roadster,” and at the time I was so dizzy from seeing such a fantastic motor vehicle that I didn’t read all the text below it. I was too busy gawking at the car and trying to get clear pics of it, and many of the spectators were just as interested in it as your author.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


To put a finer point on this fine automobile, this Maybach is a SW38 Special Roadster. And according to the info on it, it is the only known car with a two-seater body. The SW series was the smallest, least expensive Maybach—but that’s all relative, of course. Per the Automobile Quarterly article, even “a standard Pullman limousine delivered for just under $10,000.” So it’s kind of like saying an ’80s Ferrari Mondial or 328GTS is less expensive than a Testarossa—there still weren’t people lined up around the block to buy them.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Most Maybachs were sold as just a rolling chassis, at least for the larger V-12 Zeppelins. And the go-to coachbuilder when it came to Maybach was Spohn, in Ravensburg. Today’s car is no exception.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


And while the SW series was technically the smallest Maybach, they were still huge. Wheelbases ranged from 122 1/2 inches to 145 inches. Of the 2300 Maybachs ever built, about 1100 of them were the SW Series.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


The initial SWs had a 3.4-liter inline six (3435 cc) with an eight-main-bearing crankshaft and overhead cams. The most prominent feature of these cars, though, was the swing-axle suspension. It included transverse leaf and upright coil springs.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


There were three models: The SW35, SW38, and SW42. Said designations were for the engines, which were 3435 cc, 3790 cc, and 4197 cc, respectively. And while all three were rated at 140 hp by the factory, torque got a bump in each successive size.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Today’s car was owned by Dana and Patti Mecum, and was one of the last Maybachs made before WWII began. And, as I’m sure I’ve already mentioned, it was just stunning and impressive in person. A grand vehicle.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


As for Maybach itself, it built its last car around 1941. Postwar, the company concentrated on making stationary engines and power plants, though in the early ’50s they briefly considered bringing back the car line, and two prototypes were built.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


But nothing came of it. Eventually Mercedes-Benz bought the concern, which is how we started getting new Maybachs around 2002, and why there are still Mercedes-Maybachs in Mercedes showrooms now.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


And they’re all quite grand, too. But not nearly as imposing as classic models such as these.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)
