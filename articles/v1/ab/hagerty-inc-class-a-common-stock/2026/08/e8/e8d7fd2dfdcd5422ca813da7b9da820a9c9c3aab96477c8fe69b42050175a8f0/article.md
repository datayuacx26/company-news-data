# Homegrown: ’33 Ford Hot Rod with Factory Five Racing Bones

Savvy craftsmen know there are many ways to achieve the car of one’s dreams. Martii Hannula, 74, explains why he selected a kit from Factory Five Racing to start his ’33 Ford hot rod project: “I liked the fact that so many high-quality components were included. I thought the chassis design was excellent and since I’d worked on fiberglass customizing motorcycles, finishing the bodywork wasn’t an issue.”

Hannula, who resides in Ypsilanti, Michigan, retired after 30 years at Ford, where he was a machinist. His two-car garage is suited to this project, and over the years he has accumulated a fine array of tools. The starting point for this Homegrown project came 15 years ago. “In the dead of winter, my wife Cheryl and I towed my brother’s 18-foot trailer to Factory Five’s factory in Wareham, Massachusetts. We headed home with that rig totally full—40 boxes of parts plus the ’33 Ford roadster’s space frame.”

Factory Five Racing (FFR), founded in 1995, is the world’s largest kit car manufacturer. Forty employees produce and sell 500 kits per year, operating out of a 62,000-square-foot factory. While FFR’s most popular offerings are replicas of Carroll Shelby’s 427 Cobra roadster, the full lineup includes ten distinct models.


Factory Five Racing


“Taking advantage of a ‘winter special’ promotion, I paid $19,995 for my kit, which included optional powder coating for the frame and a tilt steering column,” Hannula continues. “The finished cost for this project was around $38,000. That covered the entire powertrain, wheels, tires, painting, and a folding top I purchased from Rod Tops.

“For the engine, I bought a junk small-block Ford V-8 on Craigslist. It’s bored 0.030 inches over to 306 cubic inches and equipped with a QFT Holley 4180-style carburetor and headers purchased from Summit Racing. On a dyno, it produced 387 horsepower at 5500 rpm. I also bought a Tremec T5 transmission on Craigslist for $200 and totally rebuilt it. My short-throw Hurst shifter came from Liberty Gears. The rear axle from a ‘93 Mustang has an 8.8-inch ring gear with a 3.27:1 final-drive ratio.

“Ahead of the sheet aluminum firewall, the space frame consists of[MIG-welded](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/15-must-have-mig-welding-tools/) steel tubing. The rear portion is rock-solid square and rectangular material. A custom aluminum four-row radiator was included in my kit. The front suspension has unequal-length control arms fitted with Koni coil-over shocks.


“The rear end is located by two lower trailing and two upper diagonal links and is also suspended by Koni shocks wrapped with coil springs. The rear brakes are stock Mustang discs. Up front, I installed a high-performance Wilwood system with cross-drilled rotors gripped by six-piston calipers. An unassisted rack-and-pinion steering gear came with my FFR kit.

“I installed Hankook Ventas radials on chrome-plated five-spoke wheels. The front size is 245/45ZR-17 and the rears are 275/40ZR-20s.


“Inside, I used the instrument panel provided by FFR fitted with Auto Meter oil pressure, water temp, voltage, fuel level, and mph instruments. I mounted a Sunpro Super Tach atop the steering column for easy reading. The vinyl-upholstered, low-back bucket seats came with my kit. I added Simpson safety harnesses to comply with local drag racing rules.

“Very little attention was needed to prep the fiberglass body for painting. I altered the transmission tunnel to clear my Hurst shifter. The front panels adjacent to the engine fit poorly so I tweaked them for a better appearance. All four fenders were initially painted and installed, but I preferred the look without them. The lovely polished aluminum grille shell required only buffing.


“Gary Steihl, a local custom painter, sprayed my car with 2007 Corvette Atomic Orange Metallic paint, accented with white pearl and gold stripes.

“After 26 months of effort, I couldn’t wait to break in the engine in hot pursuit of a test-and-tune day at a nearby dragstrip. Lacking slicks, my roadster smoked the rear rubber like crazy! The best run to date has been a 12.50 second ET with a trap speed over 137 mph. My calculated top speed is around 187 mph with the current final-drive ratio but I have no place to explore such velocities.”


When I asked for a test drive, Hannula instinctively answered “NO!” However, persistence paid off, and after my third request he granted me a quick spin around the block. After squeezing in via the rear-hinged door, I buckled my Simpson belts and fired the engine. The cockpit is surprisingly roomy. Thanks to well-engineered pipes and mufflers, the din is nicely contained with a lovely rumble provided by an aggressive camshaft.


Don Sherman


During my short run, on the third punch of the throttle, the rear rubber instantly lit up; thankfully, this vintage Ford held its aim straight and true. (Hannula was stunned mum.) The view forward is expansive and the bucket seats are reasonably comfortable. I was surprised how smooth the ride was… until we encountered a standard Michigan pavement eruption resulting in some commotion, but nothing terribly disturbing. The large-diameter chrome Corvette steering wheel is warranted: there is no power assist and the wide opening provides a full view of the tach.


Don Sherman


It’s clear why Hannula enjoys entering local shows and taking frequent drives in his hot rod. I’d rate it easy to live with and genuinely fun to drive. Over time I’d get used to the too-closely-spaced throttle and brake pedals. On a nice day with the top stashed and the engine burbling in fifth gear, this ride definitely yields a hint of heaven.


Hannula agrees with that conclusion while adding one last point of view regarding his kit car experience: “Yeah, it’s great to drive, but building this street rod was the genuinely fun part.”
