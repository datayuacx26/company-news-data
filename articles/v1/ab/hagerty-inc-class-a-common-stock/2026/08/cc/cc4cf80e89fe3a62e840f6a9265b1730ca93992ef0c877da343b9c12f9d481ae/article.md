# The Lure of a Valkyrie

My wife and I really loved the 1972 Lotus Europa Twin-Cam we purchased a few years ago. We always looked forward to taking it for a drive. Unfortunately, the Lotus went up in flames on start-up one morning a couple summers ago for reasons still unknown. The fire department came, extinguished the near-inferno, and the next thing you know, the car was history.


But a promptly received check from Hagerty put us back in the classic-car buying picture. This time, we wanted something different. In short order, a wicked-looking 1972 Fiberfab Valkyrie caught our eyes.


John Orlich


Fiberfab was formed in the mid-1960s and produced fiberglass car bodies,[kit cars](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/classic-meyers-manx-dune-buggy-reborn-with-new-diy-kit/) , and even completed cars. The bodies were designed to be placed on a chassis of the builder’s discretion, or you could buy a complete chassis kit. The kit came with the chassis and certain mounting components. Engine, transmission, and suspension were up to you, but the kit included[a comprehensive list](https://fiberclassics.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Fiberfab-Valkyrie-Assembly-Manual.pdf) of suggested parts to finish the car and instructions for modification. Fiberfab changed ownership many times and went through numerous restructurings and name changes during its decades-long, somewhat spotty existence. It closed up for good in the early 2000s.


Fiberfab


Fiberfab


The Valkyrie was based on the Ford GT40 and was heralded as the company’s flagship model. In keeping with the true mid-engine concept of the GT40, the Valkyrie could play host to much more powerful engines; the company suggested the 327-cubic-inch, 350-horsepower Chevy small-block.


Prior to flying from western North Carolina to southeastern Michigan to look at the car, I had informative phone chats with the seller. Once there, I found the cockpit cramped, just as had been described in advance. I’m 5-foot 10-inches with a slight frame, and indeed it is a squeeze to get into; no one larger will fit comfortably. I was happy with the car’s performance, though. The engine purred nicely, made great power, and nothing was leaking underneath—or so I thought. There was way too much play in the steering wheel, but I knew that could be addressed. After nearly an hour behind the wheel, I felt comfortable enough to make[the purchase](https://www.hagerty.com/marketplace/auction/1967-fiberfab-valkyrie/a1585205-f01e-4b55-a563-912d2460106a) , then had it shipped home.


John Orlich


John Orlich


My Valkyrie’s Corvette 327 dates from 1968 and is linked to a four-speed manual. Suspension was sourced from a Corvair, which was standard on these cars. Beginning in 1972, Fiberfab introduced the “second generation” Valkyrie, which featured a one-piece body rather than two-piece in order to aid assembly. This increased the wheelbase by four inches, adding even more sleekness to the car’s profile and providing more interior room. But the one-piece body does restrict access to components. There is no trunk space, storage, or spare-tire arrangement, though access to the engine bay is fine, made possible by a removable louvered cover.


The car has gorgeous aluminum mag-style wheels and nice wide tires. The interior needs a little help, but I am working on that. The doors do not line up properly and will be complicated to fix. Same goes for the broken window regulators.


John Orlich


Once the Valkyrie arrived, I had my mechanic put it up on his lift to give it a good going-over, which revealed some issues. The car was leaking oil from many places, no doubt indicative of tired gaskets and seals. Though the suspension members showed surface rust, I was assured the components were still road-worthy, though many bushings are needed. The tubular Fiberfab factory-made steel chassis is impressive, quite solid, and showed only minor surface rust. There was some indication of brake line leak, and the parking brake was not attached to the cable. The tachometer and fuel gauge were not working, nor were the brights or front turn indicators. The fuel tank, too, had started leaking. On the other side of the coin, my mechanic confirmed the engine ran wonderfully.


This is what you get when you purchase a car that is over 50 years old. It this comes with the territory. Or so I tell myself. It needs some work, sure, but I purchased the Valkyrie to satisfy my great need to drive a sleek classic car while I continue pondering a rebuild of the Lotus. I am sure the Valkyrie will be a sporty experience on the road and will turn quite a few heads. Which, after all, is what owning a classic car is all about, right?
