# Engineer Takes ’50 GMC Pickup from Stovebolt to Stylin’

What if a successful General Motors engineer set out to re-engineer a GM product from the middle of the last century? And let’s say he had about 40 years to think the project through. What would the result look like?


Blake Nye’s gorgeous 1950 GMC 3100 is the answer to that question. Blake is a Michigan native who grew up in the car-crazy 1960s. Like a lot of the state’s best and brightest, he wanted to create great cars for one of the Big Three. But when he graduated from Michigan State in the ‘80s with an engineering degree, he found that the Detroit automakers were in what amounted to a hiring freeze, so he ended up taking a job engineering missiles for Uncle Sam at Hughes Aircraft Company in Arizona.


The dry desert air of Arizona is very kind to automobiles, so Nye listened carefully when a friend told him he had a 1950 GMC pickup truck to sell. Equipped with the 216-cubic-inch inline-six, a manual trans with a stump-pulling low gear, a torque tube final drive, and a classic, albeit rotting, wooden bed, the old Jimmy spoke to the young engineer.


Blake Nye is a retired GM engineer who long helped enthusiasts plan restorations. As a key member of Chevrolet Performance’s crate engine program, upgrading older vehicles with new powertrains and supporting hardware has been his vocation and his passion.

While the truck projects an original look and personality, fine details, like inlaid taillights, add beautiful touches.

Unlike today’s prettied-up trucks, old-school pickups had nothing in common with SUVs. Heck, there were no SUVs back then!


Paul Stenquist


While Nye wouldn’t be able to devote the time and resources needed to remake the truck to his liking for many years, he did take steps to make it drivable. The Stovebolt six gave way to a small-block 350 V-8 with a warmed-over cam and an overbore, and a modern drivetrain replaced the torque tube and rear axle. He put about 5000 miles on the odometer during those days in the desert, even though the truck offered a less-than-nice ride with its straight axle and bump steer. Yet a vision of what it could be began to take shape.


Yes, it’s a GMC 100, reimagined by retired GM engineer Blake Nye.


Paul Stenquist


As the ‘80s gave way to the ‘90s and Desert Shield gave way to Desert Storm, guys and gals who could engineer the tools needed for battle were in high demand, and Nye soon found himself at General Electric in Cincinnati working on jet engines for the F-16 and F-14. The Jimmy pickup and his growing family went with him.


By ’94, the auto industry had recovered from a recession earlier in the decade, and Detroit was hiring. Nye soon found himself at GM working on ignition and related electronics for several GM engine families. In 2006, he took a position in the Chevrolet Performance group where he would remain for the next 19 years. His main focus was performance crate engines, along with the modifications necessary to make them essentially plug-and-play in nearly any application. You might call his role re-engineering.


Meanwhile, the aging GMC pickup sat. Nye’s wife, who had been his partner since the days at Michigan State and was herself a car-company employee, took to calling it “the garage shelf” as stuff filled the bed and piled up in the interior. He had pulled the engine, trans, and gas tank at some point, but restoration remained a distant goal for many years.


Like the prewar vehicles that preceded it, the ’50 GMC is a symphony of curves that reflect light in every direction.


Paul Stenquist


But you know what they say about starting with baby steps, so in 2013, Nye bought some oak planking that could replace the bed’s rotting floor, and he started collecting parts as he ran across them. As a principal member of the Chevrolet Performance Group, he attended a lot of enthusiast events, including swap meets, and spoke to a lot of car owners, helping them choose a crate engine and powertrain for their projects. Gradually, he developed a detailed and exacting plan for what a modern version of that old Jimmy should be. That plan included a Roadster Shop SPEC chassis, Willwood brakes, a Ron Francis wiring harness, and of course a Chevrolet Performance crate engine. In keeping with Nye’s penchant for moderation, it’s just an LS3 Chevy Mill with LS9 CNC-ported heads. The finished product produces about 495 horsepower. By the way, those LS9 heads were originally developed for the Corvette C6 ZR1 engine, but some production leftovers went to the crate engine guys, minus the titanium valves. A few pairs were still available at press time, according to Nye.


The Roadster Shop SPEC chassis. This shot from the Roadster Shop website includes options, as does the chassis under Nye’s truck—his employs coil-over suspension.


Courtesy Roadster Shop


Nye was a key player in Chevy Performance’s crate engine program. For his truck, he chose an LS3 engine with LS9 CNC-ported cylinder heads, all from Chevrolet Performance. The engine control system is from Chevrolet Performance. “It’s plug-and-play,” says Nye.


Paul Stenquist


That stout engine delivers power to a Chevy 6L80 trans and Ford 9-inch rear axle unit with 3.3:1 gearing. Because Nye chose not to tub the truck and retained the pickup bed, he selected a gear ratio that would keep rear torque multiplication in the almost-manageable range. The chassis is equipped with Roadster Shop’s non-adjustable coil-over dampers that are calibrated for the weight of the vehicle. No messing around necessary.


The classically rendered interior offers modern conveniences, including full instrumentation, power windows and locks, AC, a tilt steering wheel, and backup camera with Bluetooth radio.


Paul Stenquist


Nye was manning a Chevrolet Performance display at Metro Detroit’s Woodward Dream Cruise a few years back when Jim Fraser, a retired dentist from Nye’s hometown of Port Huron, stopped by to ask about an engine for a project he was working on. They got to talking and realized they were neighbors who were in the process of restoring cars. ([Fraser’s Firebird has been featured here, too](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/from-commuter-to-restomod-this-71-firebird-has-the-right-formula/) .) One thing led to another, and they ended up collaborating on several projects. Fraser and his partner Frank Young, another retiree, restore cars as a hobby and what one might call a community service to the automotive enthusiasts of Michigan. Nye fit right in, and soon his Jimmy was at Fraser’s shop and headed toward completion. The finished product rolled out of the shop just weeks before we were able to photograph it.


In the rear, American Racing wheels mount 235/60R17 rubber. The Willwood disc brakes can be seen between the spokes.

An aluminum step with the old-school GMC logo is embedded in the running board.

The pickup bed floor is finished with oak boards separated by metal strips. The planks are bolted to cross sills attached to the frame. That’s much like the floor that came from the factory and a major reason why Nye didn’t want to tub the chassis for big rubber.


Paul Stenquist


A head-on view showcases the heavy bar grille and bold GMC logo.


Paul Stenquist


If pressed to describe Nye’s truck in just two words, I’d have to say “meticulously executed.” The drivetrain isn’t just a source of mind-bending power, but rather an assemblage of perfectly matched components that work flawlessly together. The body panels fit precisely; the deep blue finish glows. Nye says the truck is a joy to drive. It’s tight but rides comfortably, and there is almost no body roll. Decades of planning and careful execution have created something very special: an engineer’s vision of what a mid-century pickup can be.


For a lot of us oldsters, the look of the 1950 GMC 100 defines a pickup truck: narrow cab, bulbous fenders, split windshield and an attitude that says, “I’m ready to work.”
