# Project Sven #7: Exhaust, Wheels, and a Fresh Connection

While at a local car show with my 1966 Volvo 122S, Sven, I took the liberty of walking around to enjoy the scenery and grab a beer on what was a very hot day. When I returned to my car, a gentleman was there waiting for me—a fan, it turns out. “This is it! I’ve been looking for this Volvo around town; it’s great to see it in person,” he remarked, grinning. Of course, I was flattered. Sven? A celebrity? If fenders could blush, they would have. But soon it was my turn to blush when the friendly fellow asked, “So, when’s the next update?”


I’ve been remiss, dear readers. Fortunately, there is much to share in this latest Sven chapter: the resolution of the false-start exhaust replacement, a new (very different) set of wheels and tires, and a very important networking connection.


Away we go!


## Exhaust, Redux


Loyal consumers of this column will remember the last hurdle I faced: attempting to install a stainless steel sport exhaust, built by Simons in Sweden. Long story short: I’d ordered the wrong downpipe. Once I got the new one in the mail, it was go time.


Undoing the first of the exhaust manifold bolts.


[Eric Weiner](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eric-weiner/)


Fitting an exhaust is one of those projects that *seems* like it should be easy. In reality, if you’re not a pro, it ends up taking about four times as long as you budgeted. Things will go wrong. Dumb things. The urge to curse and throw wrenches will simmer under the surface of your patience, until it boils over. I am grateful for what I learned in the process, but not as grateful as I am that it’s done and—barring calamity or an extraordinary time horizon—I won’t ever have to do it again to this car.


The correct kit. Shiny!


[Eric Weiner](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eric-weiner/)


The parts are simple: dual-port downpipe, a straight center section, a curvy section that routes over the rear axle, and a muffler. A gasket sits between the exhaust manifold and the downpipe. There are a couple of rubber donuts to hang the center section on brackets mounted to the bottom of the car. Clamps secure each section to the next, and there’s a handful of small hardware bits to hang the muffler from the car’s rear.


Step one is removing the exhaust that’s already on there. Mine was a stock unit, almost definitely not the original from the ‘60s, but it was damn tired and there was a big leak in the rear section. Unfortunately for me, most of it was welded together. This was a stupendous excuse to buy my first-ever reciprocating saw—an off-brand Sawzall from my local hardware store, Ann Arbor’s legendary Stadium Hardware. Let the *bzzz bzz* -ing commence!

New saw, new blades, no problem. A couple buddies and I cut the old unit into sections and removed it all without issue. The exhaust manifold came off without a fight, the handful of bolts cracking free and unthreading willingly once we figured out the best angle of attack for each fastener. So far, so good.


The downpipe went on easily, as did the center section and its donuts. We loosely fitted those two pieces together, opting to do final clamping at the very end. Here it got tricky—the Simons kit came with no instructions, and it wasn’t immediately clear how to orient the curved rear piece. Just routing it over the axle was a pain, especially while laying on the ground, but eventually we got it into place. Or so we thought. I noticed that the pipe was resting uncomfortably close to a brake line, such that movement over a bump would likely cause the hot pipe to come into contact with the line. I didn’t like that. We pulled it off, reevaluated, and thought maybe we’d put it on backwards. More battling with it, more hitting our heads on the ground, more dirt and undercoating falling onto our faces. Fortunately, we were right—once the piece was installed the other way around, it was clear of any potential hazards.

Fitting the muffler came next. We waited to do final hanging until all of the other sections were clamped down, so in the meantime we secured the tailpipe from the rear bumper with some wire and a towel to prevent it from scratching. That worked great, and we spent the next hour at least shifting each section to the right depth so the whole unit looked good and the tailpipe was in the proper place. So began final clamping. Close to the finish line!


Well, no. Once everything was secure, I started the car, but it was obvious based on the sound that there were leaks. From each joint, actually, confirmed by spraying on soapy water and watching bubbles form. Tightening the clamps more did little to help.

I called some other friends. One asked if I had coated any of the sections in muffler paste. “Uh, no. Was I supposed to?” Unfortunately, dear readers, I have a bad habit of learning most valuable lessons the hard way. Weeks passed, enough for my pride to recover and for friends’ schedules to align. (As many of you know, I sort of hate doing car projects alone.) Of course, the first step was completely undoing and disassembling all the work completed thus far, which nearly broke my heart. Round 2 went much faster, however, and I slathered on probably way too much muffler paste to make sure there wouldn’t be a Round 3.


Hours later, I could take solace in the fact that it all worked and sounded great. But it did not look pretty—particularly the downpipe. There were some very tiny leaks, but I suspected the heat cycling and resultant movement of the metal and the paste would eventually resolve into a solid seal. In this case, as the soapy water test days later confirmed, I was actually right.


Good stuff, in the end.

The last minor hurdle was the muffler hanging hardware. After a few drives, the tiny, thick fabric strap keeping the muffler from falling too far down proved ill-equipped for the task. Maybe it was an issue with how I put it on, but the washer and bolt cinched the hole so deep in the material—the same stuff Volvo people use for the suspension limiting straps—that the whole thing ripped apart. eBay was my next stop. I found a universal exhaust hanger made from a much-stronger-but-still-flexible rubber-ish material, and it bolted up without much fuss. So far, it’s held.

The sound! I love it. Sporty and throaty but not crazy loud. It sounds like a vibrant old car, not like the raspy coughing of a machine just barely holding on. B-series engines are inherently agricultural and never going to sing like an Alfa, but this noise does the job and has no drone at higher speeds. A win, and hard-fought.


## Wheels and Tires


Volvo 122s all came with silver slotted steel wheels from the factory. The car I bought from that nice Danish man in Los Angeles wore steelies, yes, but they were off-the-shelf, workaday black ones, albeit sporting Volvo hub caps. Original tire spec was skinny by modern standards: 165-80R15 mounted on 4-inch-wide wheels. My car came to me with cheap, 185-section all-season tires on 5-inch-wide wheels.


A 123GT with stock slotted steelies.


[Nik Berg](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/nberg/)


Wheels and tires, as delivered when Sven came to me.


Eric Weiner


Wheels are one of those visual elements that, when done right, can dramatically enhance a car’s appearance. Given that my vision for this car was that of a ‘60s weekend-warrior road-course or rally racer, only one style would do: classic Minilites.


As Hagerty contributor[Brendan McAleer explains](https://www.hemmings.com/stories/the-vintage-wheels-that-mean-speed-minilites-panasports-watanabes-and-all-the-rest/) , this simple multi-spoke design is code for speed, if you’re in the know. The lightweight little eight-spoke “rose petals” featured on mid-’60s, Paddy Hopkirk-era Mini Coopers earned respect when those cars won rallies—the 1964 Monte Carlo Rally, most famously. An aftermarket version arrived around the same time, for the U.K. and specifically for Minis, measuring 10 inches and cast from magnesium. They became wildly popular in their home market and even got a foothold in U.S. Trans Am racing in the early 1970s. Later evolutions swapped expensive magnesium for aluminum alloy.


Dad’s Morgan, on Panasports.


[Eric Weiner](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eric-weiner/)


Even in America, if you like British cars, you know Minilites. I first became enamored with the wheels through my dad, who swapped the standard wire wheels on his 1963 Morgan 4/4 for Minilite-inspired wheels from Panasport. When he took me along on British car meetups or road rallies in the Mog, we’d spy Minilites or Panasports on Triumphs, MGs, Morgans, Lotuses, all of ‘em. Fitting my Volvo with the iconic design felt like a natural connection to my dad, his Morgan, and childhood adventures that got me obsessed with old cars.


Some forum scouring directed me toward a slightly wider wheel measurement—5.5 inches. I wanted the additional stability a wider wheel would provide to my tires, and I liked how they filled out the wheel well in pictures I saw. After making some calls and looking around online, I was having a hard time finding the size wheels I wanted, in the style I wanted, with a 5×114.3 bolt pattern. Neither VTO nor Panasport had just what I was looking for. There were some U.K.- and Netherlands-based retailers that had such wheels, but I wasn’t able to determine where they were actually made or by whom. And the question of what tariffs I might have to pay was a constant source of confusion.


I had been looking for off-brand, non-Minilites because I assumed the genuine article was too expensive. Exasperated, I finally just called Minilite in the U.K. directly. Yes, they had the wheels I wanted, in the size I wanted, in the bolt pattern I wanted. And they were the same price or cheaper than any of the other companies making similar wheels—about $1000, including shipping. Tariffs might apply, might not. UPS would contact me at some point. Uh, OK!

When the wheels arrived just five days later (!), I was thrilled. Never heard a peep about an additional tariff, which was also a relief. Next up, tires.


For rubber, I settled on the original 165-section 15-inchers, which is a size that many vintage tire producers keep in stock. Many friends and colleagues, including former editor-at-large Sam Smith, encouraged me to go that route to best reproduce the feel and handling behavior of a sporty ‘60s car.


In Sven’s trunk I found a long-ago-rotted spare, sized correctly and marked as a Michelin XZX. Michelin makes these tires new, and Coker Tire sells them with a 10-percent discount if you’re a Hagerty Drivers Club member (if you’re not in the club,[click here to join](https://www.hagerty.com/drivers-club/marketing) ).


A local tire shop mounted the tires onto the wheels for me, and bolted them up to the car. Damn. It looked *great* .

The car drove great, too. The lighter wheels and skinnier tire profile gave a much more lithe attribute to the front end, particularly when first steering into a bend. Less effort to turn the wheel at low speed, as well. Things were coming together; once I got all the suspension bushings replaced and a thicker front anti-roll bar fitted, this Amazon would handle like a dream.


## Fresh Connection


Feeling chuffed at my latest round of mods, I brought the Volvo to a local car show here in Ann Arbor. It was a lot of fun to watch people admire the car, point to it, share memories of theirs or their parents’ old Volvos.


One of them approached me. “Do you know that other guy at the other end of the show? The guy with the white 123GT?”


I did not, but I wanted to! After hoofing it over to the 123GT, I met its owner, Ben Ernst. He lived relatively close by, in a rural area, and in his barn he restored this car from nose to tail. He bought it from a longtime Volvo enthusiast who bought it new, taking delivery in Sweden as part of the brand’s European Delivery Program. When Ben bought the car, it was extremely rough and in need of full rescue. He and I blabbed for more than half an hour, both eager to have a fellow Volvo dork with whom to kibbitz.

His restoration work was quite nice, especially when he showed me the state of the car when he more or less pulled the car out of the weeds. But the interior was pristine. Professional-tier, to my eye. “I like meticulous work. Headliners are really my jam,” he explained. “I weave seat bottoms for old chairs, as a side hustle.”


I have endless respect for anyone who has the patience to do such work. He seemed to genuinely love it, though. “It’s my therapy,” he said.


“Anytime you want to swing by the barn and tinker on cars, get in touch.”


Making friends in your 30s isn’t always easy, especially when you have young kids. Having a common passion for the same nerdy, niche stuff helps a lot. And so began a new bond. Score another one for old cars—they really do make life richer. Not in dollars, of course, but you get it.
