# The Pioneering History of Briggs Cunningham’s Stanguellini Formula Junior

If your summer plans include a visit to Laguna Seca to catch the Monterey Motorsports Reunion, or Lime Rock for the Historic Festival, or Watkins Glen for the Vintage Grand Prix weekend, be on the lookout for the little blue Stanguellini. This unassuming front-engine open-wheeler has a history that belies its on-track stature, and it began nearly 70 years ago with American sportsman[Briggs Cunningham](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/the-founding-fathers-of-car-collecting-briggs-cunningham/) .


Cunningham is maybe best known for the[eponymously named race cars](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/hagerty-magazine/briggs-cunningham-battled-europe/) he valiantly campaigned in the 24 Hours of Le Mans from 1950 to 1955. Although his prodigious racing resume boasts two class wins at the French classic, along with three more at Sebring, one might argue that Cunningham’s most significant contribution to American motorsports was his introduction and promotion of an affordable, entry-level racing category that would lay the groundwork for all the American open-wheeled ladder series to follow.


## From Privateer to Constructor and Back


Cunningham’s early years of racing began as a privateer, in the 1930s, campaigning an MG J2 alongside his college friends the Collier brothers. But it didn’t take long before he began to seek out ever faster and more competitive machinery with which to enter the granddaddy of all sports car races, the[24 Hours of Le Mans](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/the-deadliest-decade-the-end-of-the-le-mans-start/) . Cunningham’s obsession with winning Le Mans would see him eventually turn to constructing his own cars, which made their Le Mans debut in 1950.

After five years of building and racing his own machinery, however, with a fourth place overall and first in class in a C-2R in 1952 as his best showing, Cunningham wrapped up his carmaking business—not due to lack of interest or success, but because the Internal Revenue Service only allowed a business to run at a loss for five consecutive years before declaring it no longer a viable concern. Although Cunningham was the consummate sportsman, he was also savvy enough to understand when the Cunningham Car Company had run its useful course.


By the late 1950s, Cunningham had returned to his early ways as a privateer, and he campaigned a host of competitive machinery in various American sports car categories. He fielded a series of Jaguar D-Types and Lister Jaguars during this period, but he had also begun dabbling with Italian machinery. Cunningham had run the first Ferrari in the United States, in 1949, and he followed that up with a steady trickle of Maseratis, OSCAs, and even a diminutive 750-cc Stanguellini H-Mod, which won its class at Watkins Glen in 1954, in the hands of Marshall Lewis. Little did anyone know then that this car would lead to much bigger things.


Since Cunningham and his lead driver, Walt Hansgen, had been successfully campaigning Lister Jaguars in the U.S., Hansgen was invited to drive one of the factory Lister Sport entries at Le Mans in 1959. Cunningham and mechanic Alfred Momo tagged along in support. Hansgen’s car expired after five hours with engine trouble, so Cunningham and Momo made a side trip down to Modena to check on the latest offerings from Ferrari, Maserati, and the oldest manufacturer in the city, the workshops of Vittorio Stanguellini.


## Automobili Stanguellini


Stanguellini.it


As early as 1900, Francesco Stanguellini began constructing his own racing trikes and automobiles within the walls of the timpani drum manufacturing business he had inherited from his father. It wasn’t long before drums were ditched and Stanguellini became the first Fiat agent in Modena. Francesco passed away in 1932, leaving the business to his son Vittorio. The younger Stanguellini shared his father’s passion for racing and, like Carlo Abarth and numerous other Italian automotive entrepreneurs of the postwar period, he built a reputation for himself by “hoppin’ up” Fiat engines and componentry for racing.


Vittorio started with Fiat sedans in 1935, but by 1946 he had constructed his own, purpose-built sports car using Fiat components. Three years later, Stanguellini produced its first single-seater race car, powered by a 1.1-liter engine. The car was tested by no less than Alberto Ascari before being raced to its first win in the 1100-cc category of the 1950 Circuito del Garda in the hands of Sergio Sighinolfi. In fact, not only did Sighinolfi handily win his class, he also finished an impressive fifth overall in the larger-displacement F2 category, which was won by Ascari in a Ferrari 166.


The 1950s would see Stanguellini go on to create a host of elegant and competitive small-displacement sports racers using modified variations of Fiat’s venerable 750-cc inline-four engine. One of those jewel-like machines even found success on the other side of the globe in the racing stable of Briggs Cunningham.


Then, in 1957, another wealthy sportsman, Count Giovanni “Johnny” Lurani, approached Stanguellini with a proposition. Lurani was stumping to create an affordable, entry-level category for Italian drivers to get started in open-wheel racing. His idea was to create a series of simple, ladder-frame race cars that could only use small-displacement production engines and componentry derived from inexpensive homologated touring cars. Since Stanguellini had already produced a successful 1100-cc formula car back in 1950, Vittorio enthusiastically embraced Lurani’s new initiative.


## Birth of the Junior


Stanguellini.it


The creation of Stanguellini’s prototype “Formula Junior,” as it would come to be known, was entrusted to engineer Alberto Massimino. Massimino had quite a pedigree, having worked under Vittorio Jano at Fiat and Alfa Romeo before moving on to stints with Ferrari and Maserati. By the late 1950s, Massimino was working as a freelance consultant specializing in chassis and suspension design. For Stanguellini, he had already consulted on the 1100 Sport before being given the commission to work on the new Formula Junior.


He laid out a relatively simple, easy-to-repair, ladder-frame chassis constructed of two 80-mm longitudinal steel side members, joined by six smaller-diameter crossmembers with a stressed skin floorpan for added rigidity. A small-tube substructure was then welded on to provide additional bracing and the ability to hang the car’s 16-gallon fuel tank and aluminum bodywork. For the suspension, Massimino used the independent front wishbone arrangement derived from the Fiat 1100/103, while at the rear he incorporated the same Fiat’s live rear axle with parallel trailing arms for location.


Stanguellini


Power was, of course, provided by Fiat’s 1100-cc, OHV inline-four. Capable of producing 78 horsepower at 6500 rpm, Stanguellini’s tuned little buzzbomb of an engine transmitted its power through a standard Fiat 1100 four-speed gearbox that had been modified to accept close-ratio gears and a central shifter. Although Massimino was limited in what he could do with this powertrain, he was clever in the way that he installed it in his chassis. By placing the engine and gearbox slightly askew in the chassis—at a 9-degree angle relative to the longitudinal axis—Massimino was able to run the driveline alongside the driver in the cockpit, thus freeing him to seat the driver much lower in the car. This lowered the car’s center of gravity and gave it improved handling.


The Stanguellini was then clothed in a beautiful aluminum body penned by Franco Reggiani and hammer-formed by the craftsmen at Carrozzeria Gransport. If the result looked like a scaled-down version of the famed Maserati 250F, that was no accident, as Massimino had been one of the key architects of that car as well. As such, it may not be surprising that when it came time to shake down the new Formula Junior, Massimino turned to a trusted friend from his days with Maserati—Juan Manuel Fangio. Fangio tested the Stanguellini prototype at the Modena Autodrome in 1957 and provided invaluable feedback. With the new car now Fangio-approved and fully sorted, it was ready to contend the first Italian National Formula Junior championship.


## A New Class of Racing


Stanguellini.it


The inaugural race of Lurani’s new Italian Formula Junior Championship took place at Monza on April 25, 1958, and was won by Roberto Lippi driving the Stanguellini. In fact, Lippi would go on to post wins at two subsequent Vallelunga events, a win at Modena and a second-place finish at the second Monza event to claim the first Formula Junior championship for himself and Stanguellini.


The following year would reaffirm Stanguellini’s dominance with another Italian National Championship, this time in the hands of Raffaele Cammarota. Driver Michael May claimed the first International Formula Junior Championship as well, with wins at Monaco, Solitude, and the Nürburgring. That 1959 International season was also notable for Stanguellini wins in the hands of two promising up-and-coming drivers: Lorenzo Bandini (at Innsbruck) and Wolfgang von Trips (at the Eifelrennen). With such dominant performances in such a short period of time, it is no wonder that in the summer of 1959, Cunningham and Momo paid their visit to Modena to see what all the buzz was about.

Briggs Cunningham was impressed enough with the new Stanguellini that he not only ordered one to be air-shipped to the States as soon as possible, but he also arranged to become the official U.S. importer, via Alfred Momo’s Momo Corporation.


That first U.S. car, chassis 00139, was built July 10, 1959, and made its debut a month later at the SCCA National event at Montgomery, New York, in the hands of team driver Walt Hansgen. Since 00139 was the only Formula Junior in the country at that time, it was allowed to run as an exhibition in the G- and H-Modified race, where Hansgen still managed to finish in second overall.


From there, Hansgen and 00139 went to Connecticut’s Thompson Raceway, where he finished third overall and second in the unrestricted class. At Road America on September 12, Hansgen claimed an overall victory in the Stanguellini, though he was awarded no points, because again there were no other Formula Juniors to make up a class. Finally, on September 26, at Watkins Glen, Hansgen drove the Stanguellini to overall victory in the Seneca Cup. There he not only beat his sports car nemesis, George Constantine, in an Aston Martin DBR2, but he beat an Elva Formula Junior and two Cooper F3 cars to also win the newly created Formula Junior category, thus marking 00139 as the first officially sanctioned “FJr.” winner in the United States.

With such momentum, Momo and Cunningham then embarked on a major effort to promote Formula Junior in the U.S., offering $5000 in sponsorship to the organizers of the 1959 Sebring U.S. Grand Prix that December to stage the first all-Formula Junior race, in support of the Grand Prix. To ensure a good entry for the Sebring race, Cunningham organized two test days in September at Bridgehampton Race Circuit, where prospective customers could test-drive chassis 00139. The session resulted in nine orders, which, one could argue, made 00139 instrumental in the promotion of Formula Junior in the U.S. This in turn laid the groundwork for every entry-level American open-wheeled series that followed. Even with this lofty legacy, chassis 00139 had one more record to break.


The field for the Formula Junior support race at the 1959 U.S. Grand Prix was dominated by Stanguellinis. Hansgen, in car #1, is in the middle of the first row of the grid.


The Henry Ford


At that December’s U.S. Grand Prix, Cunningham entered two Stanguellinis in the Formula Junior support race—chassis 00139 for Hansgen, and chassis 00179 for himself. Of the 22 cars entered for this inaugural Formula Junior field, 12 were Stanguellinis. With such a loaded field, it should come as no surprise that the top seven finishers were the mini-250Fs from Modena, with Hansgen handily taking the victory over the Stanguellinis of Fritz d’Orey, Ed Hugus, and Cunningham, in fourth place.


Stanguellini produced more than 100 of its front-engine single-seaters before 1963, though their initial successes were soon rivaled with the introduction of mid-engine entries from the likes of Cooper and Lotus.


## After…


00139 sold to England in 1980, and a subsequent restoration resulted in red paintwork. It is seen here with previous owner Gordon Wright at Oulton Park.


Courtesy Peter Fenichel


Following its Sebring success, Cunningham sold chassis 00139. The car moved through a succession of owners in the New York area, and at some point it is believed the Stanguellini was refitted with an Alfa Romeo engine and raced as a Formula Libre car.


By 1980, in a sad state, it sold to the U.K. before being restored over a three-year period by a fellow named Brian Horwood and finished in red. He campaigned 00139 for three years before selling it, and from 1986 to 2003, collector and amateur racer Martin Bunn piloted the car extensively at historic events in the U.K. and Europe. Under his care, the Stanguellini was invited to participate in the inaugural Goodwood Revival in 1998, and again in 2003. Fellow Brit Gordon Wright, who campaigned the Stanguellini successfully from 2005 to 2015, claimed the Front-Engine title for the Formula Junior Historic Racing Association in 2007, among other crowns.


A shunt at Silverstone in 2007 required a rebuild, however. Traces of the car’s original blue paint were discovered during that process, so the shell was stripped and repainted in its original livery.

The car’s current owner, Peter Fenichel, purchased it in 2015, raced it in Europe for a couple of seasons, including at the 2016 Grand Prix de Monaco Historique, then sold it in 2017, only to buy it once more in 2022, this time from Gooding Christie’s London Auction. In his hands it has since been crowned the 2023 and 2024 Formula Junior Class A Champion.


Fenichel began racing cars in the U.K. and Europe in the 1990s through the Vintage Sports Car Club, primarily piloting a Lagonda Rapier and MG K-Type Special. He first tried his hand in Formula Junior with a Cooper T56 in 2010 because, as he puts it, “something safer was the objective.” He spent more than 10 years with the Cooper and only sold it after reacquiring the Stanguellini.

“Driving rear-engine FJs, or for that matter any rear-engine race car, is quite different from the earlier tech of front-engine race cars,” he says. “My experience with FJs is that the later rear-engine cars pretty much go where you point them, are clearly quicker than earlier engine tech, and with developments in chassis design corner with greater speed and handling. The Stanguellini habitually understeers, and the obvious weight in the front makes cornering rather an acquired art. But, while I find the Stanguellini more of a challenge to drive, I prefer that to what a ‘boy racer’ might.”


For Fenichel, there is also the undeniable appeal of this particular Stanguellini. “I just get a kick out of doing the best I can driving, with that historic equipment.”


Fenichel’s summer plans include a grand homecoming for the Stanguellini. He and the car will be in the U.S. to run a series of premium Formula Junior events that includes the Monterey Rolex Reunion (and Pre-Reunion) and then the Lime Rock Historic Festival in September. But the story will come full circle later in the month when Fenichel brings the Stanguellini back to where it all began 67 years ago—Watkins Glen—for the Vintage Grand Prix. There he’ll be sharing the track with the ghosts of Briggs Cunningham and Walt Hansgen, piloting the car that changed the face of American open-wheel racing forever.
