# Why Make an Air-Cooled Chevy Small-Block? “Why Not?” Say the Hosts of Driveway Finds

If you haven’t seen the most recent episode of *Driveway Finds* yet, it’s worth pulling up in short order. Hosts Dustin Hallinan and John Brito attempted something rather unconventional by taking a[Chevy small-block V-8](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/chevy-small-block-the-little-engine-that-did/) , ripping out the water pump and radiator, and attempting to use air to keep temperatures under control and cool the motor.


You, like us, might first react to that notion with a head scratch and a simple, “why?” Air-cooled engines are, for the most part, a thing of the past. Sure, there’s some panache in the Porsche world, and everyone loves a classic Volkswagen Beetle, but engineers long ago figured out that pumping a mix of water and glycol through an engine block and cooling it in a radiator is a far more effective way to regulate temps.


So what gives?


Well, who better to ask than Brito and Hallinan themselves? The duo recently sat down with Hagerty for an interview to discuss this ambitious project and the way they approach engines and the hobby as a whole.


YouTube/Hagerty


First up, of course: Why do this?


As Hallinan explains, there’s an innate sense of curiosity that drives their work, and it’s been there for quite some time. “Think about every kid who grew up around a[junkyard](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/how-to-build-the-perfect-junkyard-tool-kit/) or something like that, messing around with your friends,” he says. “You ask questions like, ‘How long could \[this engine\] run without water in it?’ We wanted to try to just trade water for air, and see what happens. It’s junkyard science, basically.”


Brito put it a little more bluntly: “Why not? Like, why not have fun with it? I’m also a[shop teacher](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/plymouths-1964-satellite-ii-concept-survived-a-michigan-high-schools-shop-class/) , and this is the kind of thing that I do all the time with my class.”


YouTube/Hagerty


Things started optimistically enough. “When we were first planning this, I was going around talking to people, and a lot of them were like, ‘Oh, it’ll make it, it’ll be fine,'” recalled Brito. When it came time to flip the fans on and actually *run* a waterless V-8, both Brito and Hallinan were stunned that the idea got off the ground at all. “I kind of feel like this is probably how the Wright brothers must have felt,” Brito joked.


Like the *Kitty Hawk* , the team’s setup was rather spartan. “I used to be really into livestock, and there are these livestock blowers; they’re like hair dryers, but really intense, and I had a couple of those lying around,” explained Brito. The blowers were mounted to the top of the cab with crossbars and zip ties (of course) and hooked up to a generator which rode in the truck bed.


Without the need for water or the pumping and cooling of it, the duo yanked the water pump and radiator from the truck, draining most of the coolant along the way. With the water pump out, the two exposed holes in the block were then plugged with the hoses—rigged up with bent bolts and band clamps, naturally. “We still had power steering, we still had an alternator, because we had plans of like, this is gonna work, watch us just be driving around forever,” said Hallinan with a smile.


They also fitted an external oil cooler with a fan and a temperature sender to try to keep oil temps in check. “Straight didn’t work,” lamented Brito with a bit of a chuckle.


YouTube/Hagerty


To clear the water jackets in the block, Brito and Hallinan yanked the thermostat housing from the top of the engine, flipped the fans on, and watched a geyser of coolant come gushing out. Freed of water, that thermostat outlet would be the lone exhaust for the moving air from the fans.


Brito and Hallinan both admit that plenty of countermeasures for the heat were left on the table. “We didn’t want to cheat,” said Brito. “We could have just made it run on methanol, and it would have been a lot cooler. I kind of wanted to take a freeze plug out, because I think that would have helped get the heat out of the side of the block.” A dry ice canister was even considered to chill the inbound air, but the duo ultimately opted against.


Spoiler alert: The setup they *did* go for worked … for 25 minutes. “We were driving it around in the 300 \[degree\] range for probably, like, five minutes,” said Brito. As the engine began to heat soak, the fuel began detonating prematurely (“pinging,” as the team called it) and temps climbed rapidly, sapping the engine of any power. “Toward the end, I’m just fully floored, and it just died like that.”


Brito acknowledged that it did not end well. “But I will say, the only damage was to the heads.” The valve guides eventually seized from the extreme temperatures—the heads were 500 degrees when the engine finally gave up the ghost, according to their infrared thermometer. “The whole drive was just fear, really. It wasn’t very relaxing,” recalled Hallinan.


YouTube/Hagerty


While the prospect of blowing up an engine “for the views” might rankle your sensibilities a bit, the choice to use this engine specifically was intentional. The engine in the video was not original to the truck. “This engine was just a run-down Goodwrench 350 from the late 1980s or early ’90s, tired, burning oil, not really doing that great, so it was going to be pulled out anyway for another engine,” said Hallinan. “We \[decided\] to send it off, give it a good death.”


What’s more, the film’s victim isn’t even on a scrap pile—at least, not yet. “The block is sitting at school with oil in it,” said Brito. “What’ll happen is, if I get a decent enough group in my automotive class, it’ll either get torn down and thrown away, or, if it’s looking pretty good, it’ll hopefully \[live again\].”


“Either way, even if the students take it apart, it[still has a purpose](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/collective-wisdom-how-to-keep-our-hobby-strong/) ,” said Hallinan. “How cool is that? To be a high school kid and think, ‘whoa, we’re taking apart an engine?'”


Remember,[Chevy small-blocks](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/ls-lt-encyclopedia-modern-gm-small-block-v-8s-part-1/) aren’t exactly rare. If they were, the two wouldn’t have dreamed of subjecting one to a torture test like this. “We’re also numbers-matching geeks, so if this was, like, a rare 1970 Z28 engine, or a ’69 Z28 small-block, we \[wouldn’t\] want to do that,” Hallinan said. “Even if it was a 327, but a good 327, we would still care about that,” Brito added. “The last time I counted, I think I had like 26 small-blocks—all engines I bought or Dustin and I bought.”


YouTube/Hagerty


Hallinan and Brito also understand that the entire hobby doesn’t revolve around junkyards. “I think that there are two degrees of car culture, and we have a foot in both,” said Hallinan. “We have cars that are fully restored, super nice. I have a car that I spent four years restoring. We love the restored stuff, but there’s a lot more pressure to it, a lot more work, more stress.”


YouTube/Hagerty


“But then we also have this other side of doing stuff on the cheap, and it’s more fun,” he continued, emphasizing where the air-cooled V-8 project stands. “What it makes me think about is how these old cars used to be when they didn’t have as much value. They used to be a little more lighthearted, a little bit more fun. It’s kind of that old-school mentality of just having fun with it.”


People park cars for all sorts of reasons. Too often, what sidelines a vehicle isn’t a monumental failure but rather a menial task that our brains get stuck on for too long. Stuck in the molasses of overanalysis, we risk letting an otherwise great ride go to waste because the prospect of the fix seems too daunting.


YouTube/Hagerty


“Stuff just wears out, and you just have to replace it and move along,” said Hallinan. “The older stuff is so easy to work on; it’s so easy to swap engines into them. Like, just because the engine blows up, the rest of the car is fine. Just find an engine on Marketplace or Craigslist, pick it up for cheap, put it in, and keep driving.”


That’s exactly what Brito and Hallinan did with the truck—there’s already a new engine between the frame rails, and that patinaed beauty will soldier on.


Look, nobody is telling you that the only noble enjoyment of your classic car is to throw a rod out the side of the block and burn the midnight oil scrubbing your garage floor with the shirt on your back as you wrestle several hundred pounds of aluminum and steel out and back into the engine bay. But if there’s anything to learn from this duo’s adventures (or misadventures? your call), it’s that curiosity is foundational to the enjoyment of this hobby. Maybe your version involves 100% fewer livestock blowers, but don’t be afraid to get a little uncomfortable every once in a while.
