# My LS-Swapped Corvette Returns to Jackstands

My 1985 LS-swap Corvette project exists because I wanted a sporty two-door with 400 horsepower at the tire, a manual transmission, and no nannies. In short, I wanted a gen-two SRT-10 Viper coupe. (Yes, I’ve spent a lot of time thinking about this. No, I can’t afford one.) However, after 20 years of owning project cars, I’ve learned that simply listing the attributes or components of a car doesn’t guarantee that the end result lives up to your initial expectations. In short, the end result can be more important than the parts you put into it.


My project Corvette has been a lesson not just in building one or two good sub-assemblies—like the engine, transmission, or suspension—but in stepping back and looking at each sub-assembly’s place in the project as a whole, and letting that larger perspective inform the work I do and the parts I use. The phrase “a good combination” continues to pop up as I wade in and out of the engine-swap chats and online groups, and though it sounds mundane, it’s become a sort of guiding star for this project. There are a bunch of different parts from a bunch of different cars in this Corvette, and my challenge is making them into a cohesive whole.

Having put together a sturdy iron-block 6.0 LS for the car, one of the last sub-assemblies I needed to tackle was the differential. However, you can’t think about a differential without thinking about the transmission. I knew I wanted a manual, and as I went about selecting one, I knew the approximate powerband and characteristics of the engine I would be pairing it with. Knowing the factory-installed 3.07:1 Dana 44HD in the car was in fine condition, it was easy to find a couple transmission options that got overall cruise rpm and acceleration charts into the right ballpark, but the more I looked at the numbers, and the more I chatted with friends and bench-raced the whole thing, one ratio kept popping up: Four point one to one. That ratio was the easiest way to get the Corvette to accelerate harder, and I wanted the Corvette to not just be quick; I wanted it to scare my friends.

The rear differential has two jobs: to turn the power put out by the engine 90 degrees (to turn the tires), and to multiply torque (to help optimize the engine’s powerband). The factory rear gears translate roughly every three rotations of the driveshaft into one rotation of the rear tires. The new set of gears will increase that to four, meaning the engine has to turn more to accomplish the same amount of work. Gear swaps like this allow you to take an engine with mediocre horsepower and make it very entertaining to drive. Changing the gear ratio to this numerically higher number will make slow-speed driving more tolerable with the lumpy camshaft and also make[the torque plateau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/mechanical-sympathy/my-ls-swap-corvette-takes-a-road-trip-test/) that much more fun when on the move.


By the numbers, with the Tremec T56 transmission (which I went *very* out of my way to get, nearly two years ago) and these 4.10:1 gears, cruising rpm should be in the 1700 to 1800 range at 75 mph. A perfect cruise rpm for the V-8 heart thanks to the transmission’s double overdrive. The rear-end gears will improve acceleration basically everywhere in the powerband, though, allowing me to have my cake and eat it, too.


Re-gearing a differential is a project I’ve never tackled before. Replacing the ring and pinion gears is not some kind of black magic for which I need a large kettle and the eye of a newt, but it’s also not a walk in the park. Just getting the differential out from under the Corvette took some effort. In particular, I had to wrestle with the C-beam that supports the tailshaft of the transmission. From there it was only more finicky disassembly: I took off the “batwing,” which serves as both the mount to the chassis and the cover to keep the differential oil inside, then removed the snap rings to free the stub axles.


It’s a job that doesn’t actually have a lot of parts compared to many automotive projects.

Like most of the other parts of this project car thus far, though, I have seen a differential rebuilt. I’ve watched a few videos, talked to a bunch of people, and even read a manual, but I hadn’t actually taken a differential apart with the intention of re-gearing it. I just didn’t have projects that required that change. Simply getting everything apart has been a learning experience—compressing the clutch packs to remove the spider gears required me to get creative with a bolt and some washers—and I know that disassembly is not actually the hardest or most finicky part of this process.


The Dana 44HD of the Corvette is shared with the car I wish I had, the[Dodge Viper](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/mechanical-sympathy/i-struck-a-deal-to-meet-a-dodge-viper-the-car-of-my-dreams/) , though the two cars have nothing else in common. To set the bearing preloads and gear spacing requires using shims under the bearings, unlike other, more common differentials that put the shims under the bearing races. This means that if the pinion depth or gear backlash are incorrect on first assembly, I will have to pull the brand-new, press-fit bearings to change the shims, hopefully not damaging the bearings in the process. I try not to rely on luck when working on automotive projects, but sometimes I’m left with no choice.


Right now, I’m about to roll up my sleeves to start lubing and assembling the clutch packs and getting the bearing races installed in the case. They are the first steps of assembly for this project and potentially the last in terms of drivetrain work. There are plenty of suspension and steering components to rebuild, but these gears came with a 500-mile break-in suggestion, so that should keep the early miles with this overpowered, garage-engineered sports car from becoming its last. If I can learn how to build this diff, surely I can learn how to handle it. Right?
