# My Datsun’s Wiring Harness Crumbled to Dust, with No Factory Replacement Available

In his novel *The Sun Also Rises* , Ernest Hemingway describes the path to bankruptcy as unfolding “gradually, then suddenly.” That same timeline could easily be applied to the trajectory that saw me needing to replace the entire wiring harness in my 1978 Datsun 280Z this summer.


The nightmare began innocently enough, with the loss of turn signals two years ago. Since I’d gotten used to holding the stalk in place to keep the blinkers activated, once they went away completely—with the fuse for the lights seemingly in good shape—I decided it must be the clock spring in the steering wheel that had gone out. I made a mental note to one day, in the distant future, disassemble the column and take care of it.


[Benjamin Hunting](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bhunting/)


It’s safe to say that the following year, when I lost power to the heater’s blower motor, I didn’t make the connection to my signaling woes. Nor when the four-way flashers decided to pack it in at the end of last fall, or the decision by the Datsun’s wipers this spring to call it quits. I found it odd that, once again, the fuses and relays for each of these features seemed to be in good shape, but it’s not all that uncommon for old motors and blowers to give up the ghost after five decades of service.


Given that none of these features were vital for my Z’s primary profile of track driving, they also went onto the “someday” repairs list while I focused on more mission-critical upgrades, like[replacing its fuel tank](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/what-a-dutch-made-datsun-z-fuel-tank-says-about-the-state-of-classic-car-parts/) and[installing a roll bar](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/installing-a-roll-bar-in-my-death-trap-datsun-track-car/) . As any classic car owner knows, however, ignoring a problem (or in my case, a series of problems) won’t make them go away. Most of the time, it simply defers the consequences of decrepitude to some other unknown, but certainly inconvenient date.


My own personal bill came due this April while driving home from the shop with a freshly painted roll bar sitting behind me. After 45 minutes or so of steady-state highway driving, I pulled off onto the two-lane that leads to my rural home, only to have the Z’s straight-six begin to misfire and the voltmeter on the dash read a flat zero for the first time ever. A mile later, I’d lost power completely and coasted into an empty parking lot.


[Benjamin Hunting](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bhunting/)


A quick call back to Andrew Grubb, the owner of AGM Performance where I had just picked up the car, came up with a tentative diagnosis that the alternator had gone out, explaining why the battery could sustain highway driving but not the up-and-down revs that came after. Still, as I sat waiting for the flat bed, my brain began to put together the trail of electrical gremlins outlined above, and I decided to cart the car back to the city for a more in-depth investigation rather than take it home and swap in a new alt.


This is how I discovered I had fallen victim to the worst-case scenario when it comes to electrical faults. It seems that the problem wasn’t the alternator, but rather everything that it was connected to. The wiring harness in my Z was kaput, and I was on the hook for a complete replacement of every strand snaking through its chassis.


[Benjamin Hunting](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bhunting/)


## One Loom to Bind Them All


What makes wiring harness replacement the terrifying final boss of any auto’s electrical system failure? In a word: labor.


Go back far enough and the electrical systems on carbureted cars can be boiled down to simple circuits that powered things like lights, radios, heaters, and ignition systems, and if you’re patient you can generally knock out a full replacement of these basic wires relatively easily. Still, you’re digging through door panels, floor coverings, and firewalls to make sure everything is plumbed correctly, and it can be tedious and time-consuming work even at its most basic.


Throw in advances like electric fuel pumps; power windows and seats; any kind of convenience-related amenity; and, of course, the horrors of electronic fuel injection, and you’re looking at a much more complicated task, with harnesses blossoming from a handful of wires to a multi-colored loom that wouldn’t look out of place in an explosion at the combination Skittles/spaghetti factory that lives behind the scenes in many classic automobiles.


Andrew Grubb


My Datsun’s late-1970s vintage had it straddling the line between heartbreak and disaster in terms of the work required to diagnose and repair a wiring harness fault. Although its engine featured computer-controlled injection, it was absent most of the interior amenities like air conditioning or power accessories that would complicate the wiring.


Still, attempts to trace back from the alternator to find out why it was no longer receiving a signal telling it to energize and get to work revealed a cornucopia of crushing faults in the harness that seemed to multiply with each hour spent. Those turn signal fuses that looked perfectly serviceable in the kick panel’s box? They weren’t getting any power at all, which explains why they hadn’t blown. In fact, very little electricity was being distributed throughout the Z’s harness, period, and what did trickle out was dead-ending in bizarre custom relays and kludges that had been grafted on to the original harness by previous owners.

Andrew Grubb


It’s here that the project shifted from repair to replacement. It didn’t make sense to keep burning shop time chasing bad connections when we couldn’t even be sure how many of the original factory circuits were left. Unfortunately, sometimes in the world of old-school automotive electrical, all the choices you’re facing down are equally time-consuming, expensive, or both, and the only thing you can do is pick the one with the most upside for the future enjoyment of your vehicle.

## No Option to Keep It Original


It’s here that I ran into the same problem many other classic car caretakers encounter on a regular basis: It turns out there simply aren’t any replacement wiring harnesses out there, factory or aftermarket, for my generation of Z.


The scarcity of replacement parts is a major issue affecting collectors and enthusiasts across the board, and it typically drives owners toward one of three different options. First, you can attempt to find a similar, if not exact, component from a different vehicle that fits the application. If that fails, there’s always the option of a used part. Finally, you might be forced to fabricate what you need.


Andrew Grubb


When it comes to wiring harnesses, another model’s design wouldn’t do me much good, as there would be too much detective work required to modify each circuit to my needs. I considered a used loom, but given that the newest secondhand harness for my car was now 48 years old, it didn’t make sense to spend so much time installing wiring that could very well disintegrate in a similar way immediately after the job was done.


Harnesses typically go bad due to long-term exposure to heat (which dries out insulation and makes wiring brittle), internal corrosion (from exposure to moisture and salt), and the wear and tear associated with small, incremental movement over abrasive metal and plastic surfaces. The chances that a used harness had somehow managed to avoid all of the above seemed slim, at best.


Andrew Grubb


This lead me to an option that hovers somewhere between the aftermarket and custom wiring work. A company called Godzilla Raceworks[offers a pair of “universal” wiring harnesses for the S30-generation Datsun Z](https://www.godzillaraceworks.com/electrical/painless-performance-21-circuit-universal-harness-for-70-83-datsun-z-car) , one with 21 circuits and the other with 28.


Andrew Grubb


These are, in fact, produced by Painless, a major name in automotive wiring, but organized, labeled, and packaged by Godzilla to be as close to plug-and-play as you can get while still having to match the fresh setup to the original wiring diagram. They’re also reasonably affordable considering the fuse box and connectors that are provided.


Andrew Grubb


Both were enough to cover everything other than the fuel injection and engine harness in my Z (you’ll have to custom-fabricate that wiring should it go bad on you, as there are no kits out there that cover them), which suited my needs perfectly. I went with the 21-circuit harness, as I don’t have any additional electrical accessories to power and don’t plan on adding any in the future.


## Not All Documentation Is Good Documentation


I wish I could say that installing the Godzilla Raceworks harness was trouble-free, but fate is rarely that kind. In addition to pulling the old wiring and looping in the new, AGM Performance had to contend with the fact that my Datsun’s electrical system didn’t match the wiring diagrams for its model year.


[Benjamin Hunting](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bhunting/)


Wait what? As it turns out, Datsun produced several evolutions of the S30’s wiring in the final few years it was on the market. For 1978 models like mine that were built in the first few months of that year, they are actually clones of the 1977 edition’s electrical system. This was a confusing discovery that required a fair bit of research on ownership forums before we were able to lock down the correct documentation.


While the process has been time consuming, it hasn’t been all bad. I took the opportunity to swap out the factory wiper motor for an early-2000s unit built by Acura for the Integra, which is a perfect mounting match and much more reliable than the original. While sorting through the harness, AGM also installed a GM-built ignition that will make future parts sourcing and replacement much easier.

## Let Me Be Your Cautionary Tale


The biggest lesson here is one that I should have internalized long ago: Don’t let small problems stack up until they’ve amassed themselves into an army of frustration powerful enough to park your classic just as the summer driving season rolls around. If I was more on the ball about tracking down the source of my Datsun’s electrical issues as they cropped up, I could have dealt with the problem on my own schedule, rather than scramble at the last minute for shop time.

That being said, Painless has been treating hot rodders, engine swappers, and restoration shops well for many years, and it was a no-brainer to go with a new, if not factory-spec, harness versus a used harness of questionable quality. If your classic is more recent than mine, you may have better secondhand options, but if you’re stuck in a similar position of limited parts availability, it’s just not worth rolling the dice on a used loom.


[Benjamin Hunting](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bhunting/)


Depending on how complex your vehicle’s wiring is, you may be in for a substantial amount of research and consultation with other owners about specific details for your application, but in the end, you’ll be driving an old car with a brand-new wiring harness, rather than living in fear of age-related short-circuits.


More importantly? You’ll be driving. Building that kind of reliability into a major repair is worth the hassle, every single time, no matter how much your wallet might protest.
