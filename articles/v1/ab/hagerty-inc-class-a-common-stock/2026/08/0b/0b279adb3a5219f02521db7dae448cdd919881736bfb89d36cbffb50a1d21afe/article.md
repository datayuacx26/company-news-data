# $2.1 Million Gets You a Museum-Quality 2006 Ford GT Heritage

When an online auction generates over 1000 comments, it’s probably one worth paying attention to. This past week, one comment section swelled to a mind-numbing 1780 chime-ins. The commenters had time to chat, too, since the auction extended roughly two hours after its scheduled end time. Two bidders kept upping the ante in thousand-dollar increments, each time resetting the sale’s two-minute timer. The impact to all 2005–06 Ford GTs out there is being felt—two Ford GT owners in the comments committed to hugging their cars after this auction ended. These are likely normal, downright rational actions when history is made in such a radical fashion.


The[auction](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/2006-ford-gt-heritage-edition-48/) in question resulted in a $2,108,500 final price, including fees, for a 2006 Ford GT Heritage Edition. But just because one example skyrockets to the moon doesn’t mean they all do. And time has proven that prices for Ford GTs with the Gulf livery theme are the ones to get, especially when they have fewer than 10 miles on the odometer. This could be an expensive outlier, but it’s still part of a larger trend in appreciation of Ford’s two-year Supercar tribute in celebration of its 100th Anniversary.


We previously noted a[35% increase](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/3-winners-and-3-losers-from-the-classic-ford-market/#:~:text=Condition%20%232%20(excellent)%20increase%3A%2035.0%25) in 2005–06 Ford GT values over the last quarter, but we never expected a Heritage Edition with delivery miles would exceed our $1.3M median valuation (in #1, or concours condition) by nearly a million bucks.

The Gulf racing livery on the Heritage Edition package is universally admired, and it was a one-year-only option valued at $13,000. That was nearly 10% of the GT’s base price ($139,995) at the time, but it was a popular add-on, and a large portion of production (343 units) was dedicated to the Heritage.


Considering 2011 units were sold in 2006, the Heritage is almost a high-volume configuration. There were[more acute rarities](https://www.hagerty.com/media/buying-and-selling/your-handy-2005-06-ford-gt-buyers-guide/#:~:text=but%20there%20are,2006%20GT%20Heritage.) made, including a one-off with silver racing stripes. Production was also minuscule for examples with conventional paint jobs but without the central racing stripe or the side stripes.


So it’s fair to chalk up this valuation high-water mark to the long shadow cast by Gulf Oil. And the fact that almost nobody drove this Ford GT for distances measured in units larger than yards, as this example retains all its assembly line markings and was never “dealer prepped” for a retail sale. A “wrapper car,” indeed.

While the plastic square over the airbag’s emblem could be original, this unit also has no outstanding recalls according to NHTSA. That suggests the previous owner ensured Ford went out of their way to install a replacement airbag without putting any mileage on the odometer. (There are reports of Ford towing affected vehicles to dealerships, and hiring third-party contractors to perform airbag swaps at the owner’s residence.)


The Ford GT Community is tight, and low-mile Heritage GTs are well known. I personally saw a 2006 Ford GT Heritage owned by a local[dealer magnate and car collector](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/before-the-ford-probe-iv-was-on-facebook-marketplace-it-was-a-crown-jewel-of-computer-assisted-car-design/#:~:text=That%20time%20is,one%20such%20example.) , who stored his in museum condition and claimed it only had 2 miles on it. There was also a 2.4-mile Heritage GT[that sold for $533,000](https://rmsothebys.com/auctions/am20/lots/r0124-2006-ford-gt-heritage-edition/) for everyone to see back in 2020, suggesting these modest odometer figures are far from a one-off occurrence.

The 2005–06 Ford GT, famously dubbed the “Pace Car for an Entire Company,” came from a unique moment in time, when a bespoke platform could be made in a low-volume Ford factory dedicated to other prestigious automobiles. It had an engine with the best traits of the SVT Cobra and the Lincoln Navigator, and the parts-swapping inside and out was downright challenging to spot. Its styling and layout were a clear nod to the GT40 of the ’60s, but it was also a legit supercar in its own right and not just an empty retro tribute. It was also delightfully analog, something increasingly rare just a generation later.


A car with the Ford GT’s blue-ovaled pedigree can never exist again, because the company that made it looks completely different today. This sale further shows just how kindly history is looking upon this unique creation.


The two Heritage GT owners mentioned above, and possibly every Ford GT owner out there, surely have what transpired this past week on the brain. But one $2.1M sale does not necessarily make the market. Other low-mile GTs will come up for sale in the coming months, which is when we’ll really see how high these cars can go.
