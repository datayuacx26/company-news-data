# Dragstalgia: A Bit of America in Middle England

The home of drag racing is, and always has been, the United States. But there’s a big following in Europe, particularly in England, where the sport has thrived since 1966, when the first meeting was held at Santa Pod, near Wellingborough, in the English Midlands.


The site is actually an old World War II airbase called Podington, which was opened in 1942 to accommodate the U.S. Air Force, particularly its 92nd Bombardment Group. In 1947, the base was decommissioned and remained largely inactive until the 1960s.


Overshadowed by a wind turbine, Garry Hill’s Ford Popular “HoppinPoppin” races Ben Montgomery’s “The Green Pop.” Seems like a popular color!


Mark Gillies


In 1964 and ’65, U.S. drag racers competed at airfield circuits in the U.K., but the first permanent site opened on Easter Monday in 1966 at Podington, newly renamed as Santa Pod. The name was simple enough: “Santa” invoked the spirit of the cradle of drag racing, Southern California, and “Pod” is a contraction of Podington.


Just like they did in the day, the Gasser Circus cars popped wheelies on demand. Brian Gibson’s Dodge Dart “Identity Crisis” takes on Emily Latham-Taylor’s Nova SS “Nurse Stoner.”


Mark Gillies


As well as hosting European drag championships for the FIA and FIM, the governing bodies of international car and bike motorsport, the track hosts events as diverse as drifting, VW fan fests, and nostalgia drag racing. Its premier event for these classic dragsters is Dragstalgia, now in its 15th year. This is a celebration of what many see as the golden era of drag racing in the 1960s and ’70s and is run in association with the National Street Rod Association, the U.K.’s premier club for hot rods, street rods, and customs.


As well as 10 classes featuring 202 drag car and bike entries, the event also featured a beer festival, live music, a display of classic drag machines, a car show, and a chance for people to drive their cars in the “Run What You Brung” category.


Gavin Meanley adopts period race gear in a very historic dragster, Dean Butler’s Ford flathead-powered 1963 “Charles Bang Special.”


Mark Gillies


Funnily enough, when we attended this three-day festival at the end of June, the weather was more like Pomona than Podington: blue skies, with temperatures in the 80s and 90s. We could have been transported far away from Merry Olde England thanks to the heady smells of burning rubber and nitro fumes on the track and meat grilling in the campsites, along with the sounds of rockabilly and roaring V-8s.


The 10 classes covered the gamut, from Superstock to Top Fuel dragsters and Funny Cars. We trawled the pits, looking for dragsters you might not ordinarily see in the U.S. We stumbled across Fiat Topolino Altereds and a whole class for British Ford Anglias, Populars, and Fordsons from the 1940s and ’50s, mixed in with the kind of machinery you’d see, well, at Pomona. Here are five standouts.


## Austin Devon Pickup: “Glenn & Glenn” / Gasser Circus


The heart of the pickup is a 472-cubic-inch Pontiac that is supercharged and fed by twin 440 Holleys. Glenn says it makes 505 hp at the wheels.


Mark Gillies


Originally, the pickup was going to be green, but Glenn had second thoughts when he saw so many green nostalgia dragsters. The inspiration for the orange color scheme was a local tow truck, which he tracked down to get the paint code.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Who said old pickups are exclusively American? Simon Glenn’s Austin Devon runs in the Gasser Circus class, which invokes the heady days of the Gasser Wars of the 1960s, when blown short-wheelbase coupes and sedans fought for A, B, and C/GS honors. The Gasser Circus in the U.K. has now been going for 23 years, and the classic Tri-Chevys, Fords, and Willys from the[period Gasser Wars](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/southeast-gassers-light-up-historic-tennessee-drag-strip/) have been joined by English Fords, Morrises, and Austins.


Glenn came late to drag racing. The retired design and technology teacher first came to Santa Pod when he was 17. “I was into bikes then, racing motocross. I got into hot rods, but then I sold everything when I got married, and then had my kids. As they were getting older and didn’t need so much attention, I started building cars.”


Retired teacher Simon Glenn used to race motocross but has swapped bikes for this Austin Devon pickup.


Mark Gillies


His first street rod was a Fiat Topolino, but he didn’t drag race that, because it had fragile Alfa Romeo running gear. Instead, he built a 1918 Buick roadster “that was made out of a shell that was a classic barn find. I even had a picture with a chicken sitting on the cowl.”


The roadster was fitted with a 400-cubic-inch Pontiac V-8, so it was only natural that Glenn went with Pontiac power when he built his Gasser. The reason for going the Austin pickup route was simple: “I had a steel one, actually, many years ago,” he says. “It was mildly hot rodded, and I used it for going to work, taking stuff to the tip, just as an everyday runaround.


“The inspiration for this car was the C&O Austin of Gene Ciambella, and I had a picture of it from *Rod & Custom* magazine on my garage wall since they invented photocopies. I thought about using the car I had, but I was having too much fun with it, and you’d lose all the value, hence the fiberglass body on this one.”


Mark Gillies


The car has a self-built 2×4 chassis with a 9-inch Ford rear end, a solid front end with Willys springs, and a 472-cu-in Pontiac motor with a stroker crank and a 6V71C supercharger running around 9 pounds of boost. The engine makes 505 horsepower at the wheels and drives through a TH400 reverse transmission.


Glenn built the engine and fabricated a lot of pieces on the car, including gorgeous machined-from-solid linkages and shifters, as well as the wheelie bars. “You can buy wheelie bars,” he says, “but I made them. That was partly born of necessity because it’s cheaper, but also … because I can.”


Mark Gillies


The bright orange paint occurred by happenstance. “All along, it was going to be green,” he says. “And then I discovered, there’s a green Austin. There’s a green metallic Ford Pop. And a green Morris Z van. Coming here one day, a Ratcliffe tow truck went past me. It was a day like this, and there was a radioactive glow coming off this truck. I went to their yard, as they’re fairly local to where I live, and asked what the color was. I got the paint code, and I’m really chuffed with it. The lettering was done by Neil Melliard. He sponsors the class and has lettered quite a few of the Gasser Circus cars.”


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Glenn loves making parts for his cars: these are the manual shift levers for the TH400 transmission.


Mark Gillies


More Glenn artistry in metal: The beautifully machined and fabricated throttle linkage is all his own work.


Mark Gillies


Glenn’s had his share of mechanical issues, but the scraped paintwork on the fender was down to a family member…


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Glenn, who has run 129.8 mph at 10.145 seconds, had a trying weekend at Dragstalgia. A new radiator failed, and once that had been fixed, the car developed a fuel leak. Once he and his teenage crew had sorted that, he made the semis only to trip the timing beam. On his Facebook page, though, he’s as enthusiastic as ever: “40 days to the Mopars,” he writes.


## Volkswagen Beetle: “Nitro Bug” / Outlaw Nostalgia Funny Car


The fuel-injected and supercharged Hemi is a thirsty beast: Raw says the Beetle goes through around eight gallons of nitro per quarter-mile run.


Mark Gillies


The Beetle’s engine needs a lot of maintenance: Raw says they do two runs before having the heads off to circulate the pistons.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Andy Raw’s Beetle is a far cry from the vehicle that put large parts of the world on wheels after the end of WWII. And it’s a long way from the Beetle he started out drag racing 20 years ago, a 1200-cc Sportsman-class car. This Beetle is a fire-breathing Outlaw Nostalgia Funny Car that runs on nitro and makes more than 4000 horsepower from its 500-cubic-inch Brad Anderson Hemi.


At Dragstalgia, Raw ran in the Cannonball class, with the fastest dragsters, Funny Cars, Altereds, and rails at the event. The format is unusual: The first three qualifying run times are added together and on the fourth and final run, the first and second lowest times face off for the win, with third and fourth running for third place, and so on down the order.


Mark Gillies


The Beetle shape was a no-brainer for Raw. “One of my businesses is old VWs, air-cooled stuff. But we take the motors out, fit Subaru engines, we do our own gearboxes, ABS, traction control, electric power steering, all of that stuff. I love Beetles. I love the shape.


“I had another VW Funny Car before this. I managed to find the original mold from Brian Burrows’ Outrage dragster, which ran here. And then I went as far as I could with an alcohol blown motor. So, the decision was made to go nitro. That was my progression. I’ve never sat still.”


Andy Raw runs several businesses, but he’s a die-hard VW Beetle fan, which explains the body on his nostalgia funny car.


Mark Gillies


Raw’s best time with the Beetle is a 5.84 on the full quarter-mile, which the nostalgia class runs. “The car was built to run sixes,” he says. “I never thought I’d ever go that fast. My 1000-foot speed was 237 mph and I kind of bottled it. I love driving the car. People love the car. When I finish competition driving, I ain’t gonna sell it. It’ll just come out at these meetings. I love my lifestyle and my lifestyle events. We’ve got[Bug Jam](https://bugjam.co.uk/) coming next, which is the biggest VW event in Europe. Every year, we get asked to come and we just put on a show for the crowd.”


Running a Funny Car, even on the nostalgia circuit, is an expensive business. The Beetle goes through about 8 gallons a run, most of which is used doing warmups. It uses less fuel on the actual run because it’s making power inside the motor rather than dumping it out the headers.


The Beetle has something far more potent than a four-cylinder air-cooled motor: The Brad Anderson Hemi runs on nitro and makes more than 4000 hp.


Mark Gillies


Raw lists his sponsors, including American company Dunn & Gone Racing, Westwood Cylinder Liners, and main sponsor Cool Flo. “You do need sponsors. It helps massively. It really, really does. These cars are mental expensive to run. I mean, we go through a barrel of nitro, which is about 1500 quid \[about $2000\]. We go through a barrel of oil, which is another 1500 quid.”


Mark Gillies


Raw’s team doesn’t totally rebuild the engine after every run, but they do take the heads off and circulate the pistons after two runs. He says he gets 25 runs out of a crankshaft, whether the car runs the full quarter or goes 100 feet. “Everything is a consumable on these engines. One of the reasons we strip them is that if something like a bearing that costs 10 bucks fails, it can destroy a whole engine. So, you have to keep on top of it if you want to run fast and hard.”


Mark Gillies


It takes a lot of money to run these cars, so sponsors are important. Raw says: “If you want to run fast and hard, it costs.”


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Underneath the Beetle body, this is a classic funny car, down to monster rear Hoosier slicks.

Raw has been bitten so heavily by the drag-racing bug that he has raced some modern Funny Cars that will clear 300 mph. He says of drag racing: “It’s a life. It’s a family thing. My kids grew up on this track. I’ve been doing this for so long. And, you know, the guys who work on the car, they do it for free, they become lifelong friends. And I’ve been doing it that long now that I can’t see myself doing anything else. My next step would be to retire, but that ain’t happening anytime soon.”


## Ford Anglia: “Al’s Gasser” / Outlaw Anglia


The line is from Motorhead’s song, “Ace of Spades:” *You win some, lose some / It’s all the same to me.*


Mark Gillies


Under the hood, “Al’s Gasser” runs a naturally aspirated 362-cubic-inch Chevy making around 550 hp. He ran a 10.026-second pass at Dragstalgia at 129.78 mph for the quarter.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Al O’Connor has been racing his Ford Anglia for more than 50 years and is a member of the British Drag Racing Hall of Fame. His Anglia runs in the Outlaw Anglia class, which has been around for 34 years. These classic 1940s and ’50s Ford Popular and Anglia sedans, vans, and pickups are short and squat, but are also seriously fast; the record for this class is 6.77 seconds at 203.49 mph.


O’Connor was at Santa Pod from the get-go. “I lived around here and came up in 1966, when they first opened. I was here on the first day, and I was like, this is brilliant! The Americans had come over with all their big stuff and there was an Anglia with a small-block Chevy in it. I thought, I could do with one of them.”


Al O’Connor has been racing his Ford Anglia for more than 50 years and is a member of the British Drag Racing Hall of Fame.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


So he did. “I started drag racing ’cause, in its day, it was cheap motorsport and anyone could do it. You could go down to a junkyard and pick up everything you wanted. Weld it together, or pop rivet it together, or do whatever you want. Everything was built out of junkyards, but nowadays, obviously, you just buy everything from America. Nowadays, you need a bit of money.”


He first ran this Anglia in 1974 but crashed it and in 1978 replaced it with the car you see here. The chassis was built by Alan “Bootsie” Herridge, a legend of the U.K. drag racing scene, and O’Connor finished it off. Unlike most of the cars running at Dragstalgia, the Anglia has a steel body. “This has got a fiberglass front end,” says O’Connor, “but the rest of it is all proper Ford. I have Mustang A-arm front suspension with a 9-inch Ford rear end and a Powerglide. I’ve been through a lot of gearboxes. Started off with a Jag\[uar\], then went to a Muncie, then went to a Liberty. But I decided not to have a clutch and went to an automatic. It’s popular, because it’s consistent.”


O’Connor hasn’t just raced his car in the U.K.—he took it to the U.S. in the late 1980s and “nearly got a win in the semis in Georgia in 1988”.


Mark Gillies


The engine is a naturally aspirated 362-cubic-inch Chevy with a single four-barrel Holley, running on pump gas. “It used to have 600 hp,” says O’Connor, “but it’s now down to around 550. It has run 9.50 but these days it’s in the 9.9s. I can’t win outright in my Anglia anymore. The cars have got too fast.” On the Ford’s dash, there’s an NHRA sticker from the days when O’Connor ran SuperGas in the U.S. “I ran the semis in Georgia and nearly got a win. I didn’t know about timers at the time. Still don’t…“


Connor says he has always been a mechanic, “but computers came into the world, and I ended up working on old cars. People are now tuning with laptops, but I tune with a screwdriver and pliers. I just need pliers to open up the secondaries on the butterflies on the carb when I run 9.9s. There’s nothing trick on this car. I learned about wheelie bars back in the 1970s when I tried doing it without them. Stood it on its back end so I run them now.”


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


O’Connor crashed the car in 2026 and, according to fellow Outlaw Anglia competitor Phil Middleton: “Al said he should cut it up after the crash. We sat in the bar chatting and I said we could repair that. His car is what got me into this.” O’Connor takes up the story: “It was rebuilt with help from everyone in the paddock. Everyone chipped in. A wheel from here, a wheel from there. Didn’t really cost a lot and worked out all right.”


## Ford Anglia: “Acute Angle” / Supercharged Outlaws


After Dale’s recent accident, new funny car headers appeared on the Anglia. They’re attached to an injected and supercharged 355 cubic-inch small-block Chevy.


Mark Gillies


An AutoMeter Sport-Comp tach provides all-important information, including shift lights.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Among the Nitro Altereds, Ford coupes, and insane muscle cars in a class of serious supercharged horsepower and quarter-mile times below 9.90, Paul Dale’s Supercharged Outlaw Ford 105E Anglia stood out. Not just because I grew up loving the shape of these cars, with their reversed rear window, but because of its proportions. It’s got a short wheelbase and it’s narrow.


Dale says: “I used to go to the Chelsea Cruise years ago. There was always a dark metallic green Anglia there. And I just fell in love with it from that day and always wanted an Anglia. This body came up on eBay one day, so I thought, right, this is it. We’re doing it.


Builder Paul Dale was lucky to be at Dragstalgia: he hit the wall at his previous meeting and had to “do a hell of a lot of bodywork” to get the car ready in time


Mark Gillies


“Me and my dad built it about 11 years ago. We put a bog-standard Chevy engine in it and just came racing for a bit of fun. Back then, we were running 11s, and we got into the 10s. As soon as I got into the 10s, I wanted a nine. When we got into the nines, I wanted an eight. And then last year, we broke the world record for a Ford 105E Anglia, a 7.71 at 174 mph.


Mark Gillies


“A very good friend of mine, Andy Robinson, builds drag cars for a living, and he actually told me I was crazy. Even after driving the car for 10 years, I still get in it, and I’m shaking like a leaf. You’ve only got to lose traction slightly on one wheel and the car just steers itself.” He found that out weeks before Dragstalgia when the car hooked right into the wall at around 150 mph and they had to race to rebuild it in time for this event.


The Anglia has a steel body over a full tube CDS ([cold-drawn seamless](https://www.industrialtube.com/cold-drawn-seamless/) ) chassis. Dale says the wheelbase was stretched to 100 inches to give a bit more stability. It runs a 355 small-block Chevy with a Motown block and Dart Iron Eagle heads, an 871 blower, hat injection, and methanol. Dale says it makes around 1100 horsepower and that while running methanol was difficult at first, it has made a huge difference to a car that was always overheating.


Mark Gillies


He uses a two-speed Powerglide transmission. The back axle is—yep, you guessed it—a 9-inch Ford, with Capri struts up front and Mark 2 Escort brakes. ”Luckily,” he says, “we get a mile to stop. It’s not a very technical car, if I’m honest. It’s got an Enderle mechanical fuel pump on it, so, you know, there’s nothing high tech. It’s well old school.”


Dale got into drag racing through his father, who raced a Fiat Topolino Altered. “When I started getting the buzz for it, he said, ‘right, you can start driving.’ I carried on for about five or six years, I suppose, and then I got into girls and lost interest in drag racing.”


Mark Gillies


He’s back into it now, though. “The main reason for me coming back is I’m at an age where my mum and dad are still around and they adore drag racing. I’ve got a disabled daughter who’s 24, who absolutely loves drag racing. My wife Emma loves drag racing. So it’s really an excuse to get us all out and spend the weekend together. And honestly, having my dad here at 80, still crewing for me, and still coming down on that start line every run, it’s just amazing.


Yes, the toy dog atop the parachute goes all the way down the strip with the Anglia, even when Dale was running 157.96 mph and 8.617 seconds in qualifying.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


In his day job, Dale runs a building company. “I’m always stressed,” he says. “As soon as I get through the gates here, it’s like a release. Just switch off, concentrate on the car, and spend time with family and friends. That’s what I love about it. Yeah, it’s an amazing hobby.”


## Plymouth Superbird: “43” / Nostalgia Superstock


Hood-mounted gauges weren’t standard on a Superbird but are useful on a dragstrip when you’re going 130 mph in the quarter.


Mark Gillies


The famous Road Runner logo adorns the rear wing of this Superbird, which features a paint scheme that pays homage to “The King,” Richard Petty.


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


In among a sea of Superstock Chevy Novas and Camaros, Plymouth Barracudas and Dodge Challengers, I spotted Don Scott’s Plymouth Superbird. Its wild rear wing and prow of a nose make it look special enough, but it also has the distinctive color scheme of NASCAR great Richard “The King” Petty. “I’ve just been fascinated by Petty, always thought he was a great racer,” says Scott. “And I always loved the look of his car, and the fact the Superbird walked him back from Fords when he was racing Torinos in 1969.”


Don Scott is unusual in that he has not only drag raced his Superbird, but gone circuit racing with it as well.


Mark Gillies


Scott says he used to own a genuine Superbird. “Six-pack, four-speed, but it was too valuable to do anything with. I wanted to race one, so I bought this kit in South Carolina 20 years ago and converted it into this lookalike Superbird.” Under the wild bodywork, there’s a naturally aspirated 572-cubic-inch all-aluminum Keith Black Hemi with twin Holley 1050 Dominators.


The monster Keith Black Hemi underhood is naturally aspirated, runs twin Holley 1050 Dominators, and makes 860 hp on the dyno.


Mark Gillies


Scott says that it has been dyno’d at 860 horsepower and should go “about 9.4/9.5 at 150.” The engine drives through a race-built TorqueFlite transmission to a 9-inch Ford rear end that “is unbreakable and gave me options to run different ratios for the drag strip or the race track. If you run a Dana rear end, you have to strip it to change ratios.”


Scott says the rules for Superstock in the U.K. aren’t the same as they are in the U.S. “I used to be on the committee. A lot of them I invented myself, just things that are unique to appeal to the limited number of cars we’ve got in the country.”


Mark Gillies


“In the States, they’ve got thousands of them,” he continues. “The best we can put together, I think, is we’ve had 72 cars here. And that’s pretty much it. There’s not much else that’s race orientated among American muscle in this country. We’ve got to have stock suspension, which is totally different to the rules in the States. We have different tire regulations—we can run 10.5-inch-wide tires on a 10-inch rim. We haven’t got any limitations on back axles.”


Before he retired, Scott split his time between the U.K., Canada, and the U.S. “So I kind of grew up with drag racing. I’ve raced in other classes, like Pro ET, but I could be racing a Morris Minor as long as it went quicker than 11.99. But I like mixing it with muscle cars, and this is the class for the Superbird, really.”


Mark Gillies


Unusually for a drag car, Scott’s Superbird has been used on the circuits as well, running against old NASCAR stock cars. “I got into a proper scrap with an ex-Jeff Gordon #24 stock car at Brands Hatch once,” Scott says. “And I was actually quicker down the straights, but they’re like on rails in the corners and I was hanging on for dear life. I’d have them dead on the straights, and they’d have me on the corners, and it kind of panned out fairly equal in the end. And the crowd loved my sideways antics around corners, particularly around Druids.”


Scott won the Nostalgia Superstock title in 2022 after deciding to concentrate on the drag strips. “They both have their own appeals in different ways. In the U.K., we were limited to racing in[Bernie Codosh’s V-8 series](https://berniesv8s.com/) , but the thing against that was that you had to sign up and do a certain amount of the series.

Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


Mark Gillies


“The beauty in this sport—and it’s because we’ve got a big family as well—I can take it or leave it, I can come and go. If I’m in the championship, you know, we’ll try and see it through. But if I don’t, it’s not the end of the world. So, I just opted for the drag scene instead of circuits.”
