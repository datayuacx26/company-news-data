# Adventures in Induction Heater Land

There’s a lot of hype about induction heaters, the stuck-nut removers that look like a hair dryer and get nuts and bolts glowing cherry-red so you can presumably spin them off with a wrench held delicately by two fingers like you’re eating sugar cookies at an English country estate. Last winter I went down the induction heater rabbit hole.


They work by setting up a rapidly changing magnetic field that creates heat in ferrous metal. There seem to be two American companies in the game—Induction Innovations, which makes the Mini-Ductor, and Lace Technologies, which makes the Bolt Buster, both of which start at about $400. As near as I can tell, everything else is Chinese-made and costs half that or less, depending on the amount of power. The Amazon reviews of the inexpensive units are rife with complaints of heaters that worked twice and then died, and nonexistent customer support. As with many goods in this era of internet purchases, the label on these generally isn’t an actual manufacturer, but rather an importer and/or marketer. Right now, the brand “KKSevens” is popular on Amazon, has marketing videos on YouTube, and their website lists a contact address in Maine, but a web search shows that[it’s a trademark name registered by Shenzhen Shuangyue Pengxiang TechnologyCo.](https://trademarks.justia.com/904/21/kksevens-90421441.html) Not that there’s anything inherently right or wrong with that.


My takeaway was “Get something with as much power as $200 will buy on Amazon and use it a bunch of times in the first 30 days so you can get it replaced if it breaks.” But when I found that the 1500-watt “Mxmoonfree” (doesn’t exactly roll of the tongue, does it?) induction heater had fairly good reviews and was available for $150 on Temu, I took the risk. But then for a number of reasons, the thing just sat unused.


My $150 gamble on Temu. Like any of these induction heaters, it comes with a couple of sizes of coils, all of which attach to the heater via a pair of thumbscrews.


Rob Siegel


My first chance to use it was for something completely non-automotive. One of the burners in our 30-year-old gas stove would no longer light. When I pulled it apart to replace the igniter, I found that it was attached to an aluminum disc by a small steel Phillips-head screw, which was corroded and instantly began to strip. Okay, I thought, let’s test-drive this puppy. Unfortunately, the fact that the screw was recessed into the disc meant that there was no way to get the heater’s coil of wire directly positioned around it. A combination of old-school methods (slotting the head of the screw with a Dremel tool and using penetrating oil) got it out.


I admit that this was a poor application.


Rob Siegel


The next try was also a non-starter. About six weeks ago, I needed to pull the top off an old Kugelfischer injection pump to answer a question from a friend about something inside it. Unfortunately, the Allen-head bolts holding on the top tend to strip. I tried heating the bolt, but as was the case with the stove igniter, there was simply no way to get the heater’s wire coil around it.


Failure #2.


Rob Siegel


The third try occurred a few weeks ago. My daily-driver 2003 BMW E39 530i began making a metallic banging noise from the left front over bumps. Since[you never want to ignore the possibility of a bad ball joint](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/lower-control-arm-failure-could-be-ball-joint-failure/) , I put the car up on ramps to have a quick look. When I crawled under it, I was stunned to find that the bracket holding the top of the sway bar link to the strut had snapped.


This looks awful, but replacing the bracket is much easier than replacing a ball joint or a lower control arm.


Rob Siegel


My goal was to get the needed parts on order and, for the short term, stop the thing from banging, so I wanted to detach the sway bar link and the broken piece of bracket. However, since I’m still nursing my injured back, I didn’t want to aggravate it by manually putting torque on rusty nuts. I could’ve driven the car into the garage where I have the compressor and the air tools, but it was already up on ramps, so I broke out the induction heater for its first run at real-world automotive stuckness. I attached the coil that fit easily around the nut, and went to town on it. The threaded end of the bolt began to glow, and a fair amount of smoke came off the joint.


You can probably see what’s going on better than I did at the time.


Rob Siegel


I put a socket and ratchet on the nut and gave it a tug, and it didn’t budge. Not wanting to risk injury, I thought, “So much for this being a miracle tool, retreat,” and decided to at least move the broken end out of the way so it wouldn’t bang. When I did, it came off in my hand, leaving the smoking remains still attached to the sway bar.


Why does this remind me of a scene from *The Terminator?*


Rob Siegel


What had happened was that although the induction heater was unsuccessful at loosening the nut, it was spectacularly successful at heating the threaded bolt and the ball part of the ball-in-socket sway bar link enough that it melted the plastic socket, as well as the rubber boot. So if the sway bar link wasn’t bad before, it certainly was now (fortunately they’re cheap).


When the replacement bracket and sway bar link arrived, I gingerly went about my first real automotive repair in nearly three months. I put the E39 in the garage, jacked it up, set it on stands, fired up the compressor, broke out the impact wrench, and pulled off the left front wheel.


Let’s do this.


Rob Siegel


Just wanting to get it done without injury, I wasn’t even thinking about using the induction heater—the impact wrench should make short work of things. But to remove the broken piece of the bracket, I had to first pull the nut off the bolt holding the knuckle onto the strut, and despite using SiliKroil and repeated blasting with the impact wrench, it didn’t budge. It needed heat.


This was less trivial than I thought.


Rob Siegel


Okay, so round 4 with the induction heater. This time I checked to make sure there were no rubber or plastic components nearby that I might accidentally reduce to a smoldering mound. I again attached the coil of wire that fit easily around the nut, squeezed the trigger, waited, and again the results were seriously underwhelming. I began to think that I had blown $150 on my, uh, Mxmoonfree.


But then I thought to check if another coil fit around the nut better. I found that the coil one size down was very snug, but did fit. The results were startlingly better. Within seconds, the nut glowed cherry-red.


*THAT’s* what I’m talkin’ bout!


Rob Siegel


However, when I put the impact wrench on the nut, it still went *whacketa-whacketa-whacketa* without the *WHEEE* part of the nut spinning loose. But that happens when using heat from a torch as well. Sometimes you need to heat things multiple times. You don’t want to heat it once and then either keep wailing away with an impact wrench or apply monstrous amounts of torque with a breaker bar and a four-foot pipe, because odds are that, in doing so, you’ll either round the ends off the nut, or snap the bolt, in which case you cry because now you’re in The Land Of The Drill, which carries the same “Abandon All Hope Ye Who Enter Here” sign as Dante’s gates of hell. Three or four cycles of the induction heater later, and the impact wrench spun the nut off.


These both tag-teamed against the stuck nut.


Rob Siegel


I still needed to get the nut off the back of the exposed ball from which I’d melted its plastic socket two weeks prior. This time, using the small coil on the induction heater, it came off easily (unlike the nut on the broken bracket, it hadn’t been on there for 200,000 New England miles).


So, with this single successful episode under my belt, here’s my conclusion: An induction heater is *not* a magic wand that you simply wave and corroded fasteners simply spin off, fleeing in terror. It’s just another way to deliver heat. Is it a quicker, easier, safer way to deliver heat than an oxy-acetylene torch with its foot-long flame? Sure looks like it. I never look forward to dragging the torch out from the back of the garage and potentially melting a rubber brake line. But I was really surprised at how much the induction heater’s efficacy depended on the coil wrapping as tightly around the nut as possible, and at how much heat was transmitted to anything in direct contact with the nut and bolt. Also, the more powerful a unit you buy, the bigger it is, and the ability to, say, get at nuts on the exhaust manifold or headpipe might not be easy. The torch’s ability to supply heat at a distance likely outperforms it there. And it does require A/C power, so it’s not going to save you during a roadside repair. Then again, it’s not like I travel with a torch.


One can never have too many tools to throw at stuck nuts. I expect this to be a useful one.
