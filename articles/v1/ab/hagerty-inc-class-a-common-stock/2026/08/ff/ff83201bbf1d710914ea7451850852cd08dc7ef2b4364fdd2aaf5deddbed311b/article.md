# 10 Hero Cars of Group B Rallying

If you watch archive footage of Group B rallying today, it’s enough to make you wonder how on Earth it was ever allowed in the first place: screaming, turbocharged four-wheel-drive cars with a vague appearance of something in your local showroom, blasting through forest stages or around tight mountain passes, with spectators just inches away, often throwing themselves into ditches or flocking into fields if the drivers got it wrong.


Group B always seemed an accident waiting to happen, and unfortunately, that’s how it met a very abrupt end. Following a series of tragic accidents that highlighted the series’ dangers, the final straw came when Henri Toivonen and co-driver Sergio Cresto were killed on the Tour de Corse in a Lancia Delta S4.


Nobody knows whether it was mechanical failure, a misread pace note, or driver error, but the Lancia appeared to miss a turn completely and launch itself off a cliffside, ending up in a fireball on the side of a mountain. Harrowing scenes, and with increasing pressure from teams, drivers, and the public, the FIA made the decision to ban Group B cars from the World Rally Championship at the end of the 1986 season.


It was an awful end to an incredible era. Renowned for its daring drivers, ground-breaking technology, and extreme cars, Group B made its debut in the World Rally Championship in 1982, following the introduction of new regulations by the FIA. These rules were designed to encourage innovation, allowing manufacturers to create highly advanced vehicles with fewer restrictions on design and production numbers. The minimum production requirement was set at just 200 homologation units, enabling manufacturers to push the boundaries.


Cars featured lightweight composite materials, turbocharging, and advanced four-wheel-drive systems, which resulted in machines that were insanely quick and agile. These were 10 of the most memorable Group B cars, even if a couple never even made it to the start line…


## Audi Sport Quattro


Audi


The[Audi Sport Quattro](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/group-b-audis-and-lancias-are-hitting-full-throttle-once-again/) was a pioneer of Group B, introducing four-wheel drive and turbocharging to the sport. Its short wheelbase and powerful 2.1-liter five-cylinder engine made it agile and incredibly fast on all surfaces. Audi’s relentless development helped secure multiple wins, changing the face of rallying forever. The Sport Quattro’s influence can still be seen in modern rally cars, and its distinctive boxy design remains an icon.


## Lancia 037 Rallye


Lancia


The Lancia 037 Rallye was the last rear-wheel-drive car to win the World Rally Championship, in 1983. Powered by a supercharged four-cylinder engine and loosely based on the Beta, the 037 was lightweight, responsive, and was particularly rapid on tarmac stages. It also happens to be, in our eyes, one of the most[visually stunning](https://www.hagerty.com/media/entertainment/new-race-for-glory-movie-jumps-into-lancia-audi-battle-for-group-b/) cars of all time.


## Peugeot 205 T16


PSA


Almost from nowhere, the Peugeot 205 T16 transformed Group B rallying. It was the first to use a mid-engine layout, despite its visual similarities to a front-engine showroom hatchback. Turbocharged and four-wheel drive, it delivered more than 400 horsepower and dominated the championship from its first season, winning back-to-back titles in 1985 and 1986.


## MG Metro 6R4


MG


The[MG Metro 6R4](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/articles/the-mg-metro-6r4-a-lesson-in-punctuality/) was the smallest car of the Group B era and used a mid-mounted 3.0-liter V-6 engine that was naturally aspirated, unlike most of its rivals. It had a four-wheel-drive system mounted into a tubular spaceframe chassis and was recognizably a Metro, just like your British granny’s shopping car. While turbocharged rivals fared better on tarmac, the 6R4’s nimble dimensions, all-wheel grip, and smooth power delivery made it a forest favorite.


## Ford RS200


Ford


The[Ford RS200](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/articles/automotive-history/the-ford-rs200-at-40-a-sit-down-with-the-man-who-developed-them/) was designed specifically for Group B and didn’t resemble any factory Ford car, though the windshield and doors came from the Sierra. It used a mid-mounted turbocharged engine making 444 hp. Its lightweight composite construction, incredible chassis, and four-wheel-drive system allowed for exceptional handling and acceleration. It was ambitious, and prototype testing began during the 1986 season, where it was clearly competitive—but it never got a chance to shine, as the category was canned before its first season.


## Opel Manta B400


LAT Images


Although never a championship contender, the Opel Manta B400 must take the spoils for being one of the best-looking rally cars of all time. Unlike most Group B models, it also retained the bodyshell of the road car on which it was based. It used a 2.4-liter engine tuned by Cosworth to develop 255 hp, and while it lacked the outright power of its turbocharged rivals, the Manta’s rear-wheel drive and linear power delivery made it a hero on gravel stages—and always good for a photograph!


## Lancia Delta S4


National Motor Museum


The Lancia Delta S4 was a technological marvel, featuring both a supercharger and a turbocharger, which gave it explosive power delivery. Its advanced four-wheel-drive system and lightweight construction made it one of the fastest and most sophisticated cars in Group B, pushing the boundaries of rally engineering so far that it ended up being tragically associated with the series’ demise. It was arguably the ultimate Group B car—but was it a step too far?


## Porsche 959


Porsche


The[Porsche 959](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/articles/news-articles/without-this-porsche-959-there-would-be-no-nissan-gt-r/) was developed with Group B regulations in mind, blending cutting-edge technology with iconic Porsche styling. Its twin-turbocharged engine, all-wheel drive, and advanced electronics were all focused on the series, but just as it appeared, Group B ended. So Porsche reengineered the 959 for endurance racing instead, notably the Paris-Dakar, with the French team of René Metge and Dominique Lemoyne dominating the 1986 event.


## Ferrari 288 GTO Evo


Flickr/Luca Rocchi


The stunning[Ferrari 288 GTO Evo](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/articles/auctions/one-of-five-ferrari-288-gto-evoluziones-is-for-sale/) was the Prancing Horse’s ambitious attempt to enter Group B and was based on the 288 GTO road car. Equipped with a twin-turbocharged V-8 and lightweight fiberglass bodywork, which influenced the shift from steel bodies on its road cars, the GTO Evo showed huge promise. But the project was halted before it could compete, leaving the car as a tantalizing “what if” in rally history.


## Citroën BX TC Evolution


Citroën


The Citroën BX TC Evolution was the French carmaker’s entry into the Group B category, built on the BX platform, but with extensive modifications. Featuring a turbocharged engine and hydropneumatic suspension, the BX TC Evolution was a fascinating and intriguing car, but not a massively competitive one, and with no notable results. Looks wild though, doesn’t it?
