# Old Trucks Are Worse for Expeditions—That’s Why You Need One

Earlier this summer, I was in the southeast corner of Oregon exploring the Owyhee region with two of my closest friends, Coach and Winslow. Coach was in a 2024 GMC Canyon AT4X AEV Edition. Winslow was piloting his 100,000-mile 2017 Jeep Wrangler JKU Sport. And I led in my 2026 RAM 2500 Tradesman.


Often called the “Oregon Outback,” the region is considered the lost corner of the wild west. It’s an[International Dark Sky Place](https://darksky.org/places/oregon-outback-international-dark-sky-sanctuary-oregon/) , too. There’s essentially nothing out there aside from big horn sheep, wild horses, trout, and stars. There’s no cell service. And even if there were, there’s no one within 100 miles to call for help.


John Allen


John Allen


For most people, going out to a remote area like that would be nerve-racking; the fear of a potential truck breakdown, even in a relatively modern rig, conjures images of four-figure tow bills, search-and-rescue missions, or, worse, downright death. For me, imagining such scenes dumps dopamine into my system; a euphoric state washes over me.


Coach, Winslow, and I spent a flawless six days driving more than 400 miles off road, winding our way southeast to northwest across the Oregon Outback. Until the penultimate day of our trip, anyhow. Winslow’s voice crackled over the radio, “My brake pedal just went to the floor.” *Dopamine dump.* My elation was involuntary. We pulled to the side of the trail. A big grin spread across my face. We finally had some excitement.


Winslow was mortified, worried he had ruined our trip. No, we assured him, it was fine; Coach and I welcomed the challenge. I dropped to the dirt and slid beneath the Wrangler to investigate the cause of the brake failure, my face inches from the scalding exhaust.


Lying there, looking up at the 9-year-old 4×4, high on my own supply, I was both grateful and sad that I picked a brand-new truck for my expeditions. Old trucks are worse than new ones, especially if you’re venturing into the remote reaches of America. But that’s exactly why you need one. Let me explain.


## Concession Stand


Nick Jaynes


Let’s get this out of the way before we go any further: Modern trucks are objectively better than old ones. They’re safer, faster, more capable, more comfortable, and they don’t strand you. If you’re looking for a daily driver and adventure rig in one, you’d be hard pressed to beat the current mid-size GM truck or a[Jeep Wrangler](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/hitting-the-trail-in-jeeps-easter-jeep-safari-2026-concepts/) JLU.


They’re relatively fuel efficient, loaded with safety and creature-comfort tech, and backed by a deep, far-reaching network of dealers and parts suppliers, plus an extensive aftermarket that is engineering genuinely incredible expedition gear. If you only have the time, space, money, and wherewithal for one do-it-all rig, you’d be well served to buy something new.


In a new truck you’ll waft to work in luxury leather one day and take on a tough trail the next. But that 100,000-mile worry-free warranty comes with a big trade-off: boredom. You’ll be anonymous on the street and on the trail, and your adventures will lack any real depth.


## Breaking in Interesting Ways


Trevor Ryan


Old trucks break in interesting ways, and when they do, they make *you* more interesting in the process. Here’s an example:[My 1976 International Scout Traveler](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/the-suffering-is-the-point-on-the-trail-in-a-scout-with-needs/) has a transfer case linkage warped in such a way that it disassembles itself when I shift from 4-Hi to 4-Lo. In order to be able to get back into 2WD at the end of a trail, I have to preemptively take it apart at the start of the trail and keep the bits in the glovebox. This so they don’t rattle loose and disappear along the trail, stranding me in low range.


Sure, I could fix this by installing a new transfer case—one that’s not bent. But where would the fun be in that? A T-case that just … works? I might as well get a ’26 Bronco with an electronically controlled one. The eight-minute process of shifting into low range is bespoke to my truck. It makes the thing unique and interesting, and it strengthens the bond I have with the rig.


That bond is the thing modern machinery has quietly deleted. Forty years ago, even a brand-new car demanded constant fiddling. My dad still tells the story of the ’78 VW Super Beetle he owned in Colorado in the 1980s: To reach his favorite ski resort, he’d pull to the side of the highway and re-tune the carburetor half way up the mountain.


The low-horsepower engine gasped for fuel in the thin, oxygen-starved mountain air. A minute with a screwdriver and he was back on his merry way. A 30-year-old today might find that story nightmarish; my dad counts it among his favorite memories, one that outlasts the days actually spent on the slopes, and it bonded him to that Beetle in a way few modern cars can manage.


## The Freedom of Not Caring


Trevor Ryan


Having an old, already kinda-broken truck also frees you from greater worry. I’ve been on too many trips with guys, usually in Tacomas, who nearly start sobbing at the first pinstripe left in their truck’s paint.


Conversely, when I returned from a 400-mile overland trip in my Scout, my buddy pointed out that I’d caved in a portion of the right rear quarter panel on a boulder. “Huh, so I did,” I shrugged. Not sure I’d feel so nonchalant about seeing that on a factory-fresh Tundra. In fact, when I bent the D-pillar on my first overland expedition in my then-new 2022 Tundra, I was quite upset.


In 2026, it’s nearly impossible to be truly helpless, truly self-reliant. Old trucks offer a rare vacation from modernity and safety. Risk is the adventure. You’re not really driving 400 miles through remote Oregon just to see wild foals and stars. You leave the pavement to push your own boundaries, in the hope of feeling alive. A modern truck insulates you from the exact thing you drove out there to find. An old one heightens it.


## Self-Reliance and Lots of Tow Straps


Nick Jaynes


I don’t yearn for life- or limb-threatening breakdowns. But the ones that stop you in your tracks, focus your attention on the here and now, and activate your ingenuity and fortitude—those are the ones I get excited about. And that’s exactly what happened in Owyhee.


Winslow, Coach, and I determined it was the JKU’s brake master cylinder that conked out at 108,000 miles. We were hundreds of miles from the nearest parts store, so we improvised. We strapped the front of Winslow’s Wrangler to the rear of my Ram; I’d be his propulsion, and he’d scrub speed with the handbrake. Because a handbrake is a nerve-wracking way to slow two tons of Jeep, we ran a second strap from his rear bumper to Coach’s GMC, giving us a more trustworthy brake bringing up the rear.


Picture that sketchy little conga line of trucks inching across the high desert, me dragging Winslow, Coach ready to reel him back, none of us entirely sure the straps would hold.


The ridiculous chain worked well enough to get the Wrangler to the town of Burns, where AAA took over and hauled it to Redmond. I raced ahead and grabbed a master cylinder from O’Reilly. AAA dropped the Jeep at a Harbor Freight, and we replaced the brake master right there in the parking lot.


It wasn’t the most harrowing rescue, nor the most ingenious trailside repair I’ve ever pulled off. But it’s the only part of the weeklong trip through Oregon’s Outback that any of us keep talking about.


Nick Jaynes


Six days, 400 miles, and the only thing worth covering is the 20 minutes we spent strapping three trucks together like a bad idea in a parking lot. That’s not a coincidence. The trip was flawless right up until it wasn’t, and the moment it stopped being flawless is the moment it became an adventure instead of a drive.


A new truck will get you out there and get you back—reliably, comfortably, and without incident. An old one makes you earn it, and it gives you something to say afterward.
