# Woodward Dream Cruise: Celebrating What We Do in Detroit

Much has been written about Metro Detroit’s Dream Cruise, and a lot of it misses the forest for the trees. The oft-repeated origin story is heartwarming and true: a 1990s cruise on Woodward Avenue that started as a charity event meant to raise money for a soccer field. That cruise mimicked the auto enthusiast scene on an Oakland County stretch of Woodward in the ‘50s, ’60s, and early ‘70s. The event’s popularity snowballed, and soon began attracting tens of thousands of hot rods and classics and nearly a million spectators.


That feel-good coverage from outside the car world is now occasionally accompanied by complaints about the noise, the blocked streets, the inconvenience of it all. And, because most observers arrive in mid-afternoon, they are dismayed to see the predominance of SUVs and other ordinary stuff in the cruising lanes, each packed with gawkers who want to be part of the action and are content to admire the classics and rods, by then parked at the sides of the avenue, from afar.


That’s not the Dream Cruise. At least it’s not my version. My cruise is a giant week-long celebration of what we do in Metro Detroit. We celebrate the fact that we make cars and trucks, and lots of them. We design them, engineer them, assemble them, advertise them, repair them, and even write about them. If we don’t touch those Motown products directly, we might bake bread for the people who build them, teach their children, or care for them when they’re not well. Here in “the D,” we are all about cars. And every year for a week we celebrate that with a big party we call the Dream Cruise.

This doesn’t just take place on the street. It’s in backyards and bars on the banks of mighty Woodward and all over town. It’s anywhere car people gather to reminisce, show off their hardware, and tell lies. I show up at one of these parties every year. It’s an invitation-only event held in the backyard of a former Budweiser distributor named[Steve Guenther](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/steve-guenther-and-his-71-stingray-still-love-to-play-in-the-street/) . Steve, who once kept Detroiters in suds is now the pied piper of Detroit car folk. He knows everyone in the biz and can tell you what each of them drives.

I thought it would be nice this year to take Hagerty readers along to Steve’s annual cruise celebration. Any car guy or gal would enjoy the folks who gather at Steve’s place on Wednesday of cruise week. Some of the attendees made their living creating cars and earned a place in automotive history. Many are former or current GM, Ford, and Stellantis employees. Others just have a passion for great rolling stock and live it. Among them this year was Jerry Harkey, who was a powerplant engineer at Chrysler and helped develop the Gen III Hemi. Jerry arrived in his ‘55 Packard Patrician, a car he’s owned for more than half a century. Also joining the festivities were Tom and Carolyn Peters. Tom spearheaded Corvette design development for many years and is an automotive icon. Carolyn is a designer as well who created many automotive interiors and now heads her own design firm. Tom arrived in his 396-powered red-on-red ’65 Impala convertible. Carolyn rumbled in at the wheel of her extensively reworked 440 six-pack ’69 Dodge Super Bee. Then there were GM crate-engine guru Brad Nye in a hot Camaro, Peterbilt dealership owners Greg and Amy Reefer in their totally streetable 8-second, 140-mph 2003 Mustang Mach 1, and Steve’s neighbor (and brother from another mother) Brad Mali burning the tire off the rim of his Buell M2 Cyclone naked motorcycle. (Brad was fully dressed. Only the motorcycle was naked.)

The gathering begins on Steve’s long driveway and on the street in front of his house where folks move from car to car, admiring the hardware, and talking shop. Eventually, the group moves to the backyard for Coneys dressed in Steve’s own potent chili and assorted snacks. Passengers down some Buds, of course. After some more spirited conversation, the cars fire up, and all head east to Woodward for an hour or so of cruising. At 9 p.m. sharp, they pull into the parking lot of a shuttered restaurant. Steve, who can talk almost anyone into almost anything, has convinced the establishment to reopen for the gang and serve them a late meal. The laughter rings out until the night finally ends with a toast to Motor City and what we do here. We make cars.
