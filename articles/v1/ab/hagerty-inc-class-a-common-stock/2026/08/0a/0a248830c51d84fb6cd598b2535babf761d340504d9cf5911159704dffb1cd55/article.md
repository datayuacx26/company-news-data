# 3 Winners and 3 Losers from the Classic Chevy Market

A couple of weeks ago, we reported on the market winners and losers from Ford in the latest Hagerty Price Guide update. This week, we’re talking about cross-town rival Chevy. The gainers haven’t gained as much, and the losers haven’t lost as much, so there’s that. Notable absences from this list include first-gen C/K pickups, which are up nearly 4% since April, while second-gen Chevelles are up nearly 3%. Third-gen Camaros, too, are up by a couple of percentage points. The following six cars, however, represent the biggest increases and decreases among classic Chevys, averaged for all body styles and trims for a given model in #2 (excellent) condition.


## 1983–88 Monte Carlo SS: +15%


Mecum


Chevy’s Monte Carlo spanned six generations over more than three decades of production. Conceived on the A-body platform for 1970 as a personal luxury car to compete with the likes of the Thunderbird and Grand Prix, by 1978, the third-generation car was radically downsized in keeping with a philosophy of shrinkage across the divisions of General Motors. A sleeker fourth-gen Monte debuted in 1981, and though it was similar to the car it replaced, a[convoluted name change](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/the-80s-or-back-when-celebrities-were-not-cause-for-celebration/#:~:text=Timing%20is%20everything%2C%20but%20time%20wasn%E2%80%99t%20on%20Chevy%E2%80%99s%20side%3A%20In%20GM%E2%80%99s%20alphabet%20soup%20of%20naming%20designations%2C%20both%20FWD%20and%20RWD%20mid%2Dsizers%20were%20called%20%E2%80%9CA%2Dbodies.%E2%80%9D) meant its chassis was re-named into the now famous G-body moniker in 1982.


Following a 12-year hiatus,[the Super Sport moniker](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/1983-88-chevy-monte-carlo-ss-buyers-guide/) returned to the Monte Carlo for 1983. The cars were initially offered in white or dark blue, and the SS package included a rear spoiler and revised front fascia, Chevy’s F41 heavy-duty suspension, special wheels with Goodyear Eagle GT tires, and the 305-cubic-inch high-output L69 V-8 cribbed from the Camaro, which put 175 horsepower to the rear wheels through a Turbo 350 automatic. NASCAR success beginning in 1984 helped bolster sales, a better (TH-200 4R) automatic found its way in, and more colors joined the spec sheet, as did the Aerocoupe, starting in 1986.


Values for the Monte Carlo SS have been a bit up and down over the last five years, though an uptick in the latest Hagerty Price Guide update has #2-condition examples[up to roughly $31,000](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/monte_carlo/1986/1986-chevrolet-monte_carlo-ss?id=aCn1I000000DDMoSAO) , from their springtime pricing of $26,700. In the same period,[Aerocoupes have climbed](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/monte_carlo/1986/1986-chevrolet-monte_carlo-ss?id=aCn1I000000DFa7SAG) from $29K to $33,400.


## 1962–67 Chevy II Nova: +11%


GM


GM introduced the Chevy II in 1962 as a counter to Ford’s incredibly popular Falcon. Small in stature and powered by four- and six-cylinder engines, the compact car was as economical as Chevy machinery got in those days, with a top trim level called the Nova 400. Body styles included two-door sport coupes, four-door sedans, convertibles, and station wagons. By 1964, a 283 V-8 was available, and a 327 joined the lineup a year later, making the Nova SS a force to be reckoned with in straight-line performance. A redesign for 1966 sharpened the Nova’s soft contours, and new engines joined the fray, including a 350-hp L79 V-8.


The beauty of the Chevy II Nova was its versatility; from mild people movers to wild muscle machines, there really was (and is) a model for everyone. Naturally, the performance-oriented ’65 Super Sport sits at the top of the range, with #2 examples up from about $24K to nearly $27K in the latest HPG update, and the ’67 SS car with the L30 327 moving from $40,400 to nearly $45,000. A ’64 four-door sedan, meanwhile, gained about $1500 in value in the same period, up from $12,500 to nearly $14,000 today.


## 1946–48 Stylemaster: +4.4%


Mecum


As American industry recovered from the enormous contributions required for the war effort, Chevrolet returned to its prewar tooling to get “new” cars out the door. The Stylemaster series was the carmaker’s budget offering and was based on the Master Deluxe of the early 1940s. Compared with the “upscale” Fleetline and mid-tier Fleetmaster, the poverty-spec Stylemaster lacked chrome trim around the windows (the Fleetline also featured chrome strips on the lower half of the fenders and rear quarters. Inside, it featured a more spartan interior and lacked amenities such as a clock, ashtray, and armrests.


Power came from a 216.5-cubic-inch inline-six mated to a three-speed column shifter, and body styles included two-door business and club coupes, a two-door town sedan, and a four-door sport sedan. The sedans have been the movers in this group, with[values for four-doors](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/stylemaster/1947/1947-chevrolet-stylemaster-base?id=aCn1I000000DKZsSAO) climbing from $19,500 to $21,500 and[those for two-door sedans](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/stylemaster/1947/1947-chevrolet-stylemaster-base?id=aCn1I000000DPkwSAG) jumping from $23,000 to $25,300.


## 1973–91 Suburban: -9.0%


GM


Chevrolet introduced the Suburban in 1935, and no other nameplate has enjoyed that kind of production span—now standing at a staggering 91 years over 12 generations. And one of the longest-running generations was the seventh, which hit the streets in 1973. The truck was distinguished from its predecessor not just because of its sharper “square-body” lines, but because it was the first to feature four passenger doors instead of the sixth-gen’s three or the two doors of all previous models.


While still infused with the rugged underpinnings that were its bread and butter, the Suburban was also available with more luxury amenities than any before, including A/C, power seats and windows, and less bare-bones upholstery. Two- and four-wheel-drive models were offered (as C and K series, respectively) in both half-ton (C10/K10) and three-quarter ton (C20/K20) payloads. Trims included Custom Deluxe, Scottsdale, and Silverado, and power came from a range of small-block and big-block V-8s, as well as a straight-six for the model’s first six years of production.


These were always simple, hardy vehicles, and with so many built over so long a time span, many are still on the road happily chugging away. Since the spring, almost universally, all Suburbans of this generation in excellent shape are[down about 9%](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/suburban/1981) , so if you’re in the market, their dip in pricing should save you some money. An[’85 C10 Silverado](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/suburban/1985/1985-chevrolet-suburban-c10_silverado?id=aCn1I000000D9fJSAS) is now a $23K rig, down from $25,400, while[a K20 Silverado](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/suburban/1985/1985-chevrolet-suburban-k20_silverado?id=aCn1I000000D9fTSAS) from the same year is down from just over $31K to just over $28K. Find the trim, powertrain, and layout that works for you, and get after it.


## 1991–96 Caprice Classic: -5.0%


Chevrolet


Perhaps no car in Chevy’s catalog ever received a glow-up quite like the fourth-generation Caprice, which went from the exemplary definition of “three-box sedan” to “jellybean” in the blink of an eye. Underneath, however, the aerodynamic new Caprice relied heavily on its predecessor’s B-body platform and powertrains, which included a 4.3-liter V-6 and 5.0-liter V-8, as well as the 5.7-liter V-8 originally meant for 9C1 police versions of the Caprice and, for 1994–96, a detuned, 260-hp LT1 borrowed from the Corvette.


The Caprice came in both sedan and wagon form, and a[base ’91 Caprice four-door](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/caprice/1991/1991-chevrolet-caprice-base?id=aCn1I000000DPjqSAG) is down about $600 from the spring, to $12,400. A “hot”[Caprice Classic LS](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/caprice_classic/1994/1994-chevrolet-caprice_classic-ls?id=aCn1I000000kCs9SAE) with the LT1, meanwhile, is down from $23,700 to $22,500.


## 1975–76 Vega Cosworth: -4.8%


Chevrolet


It’s a shame the little Vega never made more of a splash than it did. In period, it was arguably a far more attractive alternative to its domestic and import competition. Even today—if you find one in the wild—the shape has aged well. That it was[rushed to market](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/the-infamous-vega-could-have-been-great-if-chevy-hadnt-rushed/) was the Vega’s undoing, however, and it was let down by a host of issues, including the reliability of its aluminum four-cylinder engines, despite hundreds of engineering corrections over the car’s production life. Vegas were offered as a hatchback, notchback coupe, two-door wagon, and sedan delivery, but the model people seem to remember most is the hotted-up Vega Cosworth of 1975–76.


Development had begun under the direction of John DeLorean, with British engine specialist Cosworth tasked with building a performance engine capable of slaying the little BMWs and Alfas of the day. In the end, the twin-cam all-aluminum 2-liter four was rated at 110 hp, and little more than 3500 were built, with an MSRP of more than $5900—twice the cost of a regular Vega.


Vega Cosworths enjoyed a long, steady climb in values beginning in 2016, which accelerated in the pandemic era, before falling off in the last couple of years. Since July 2023, they’re down almost $9000, and[the most recent decline](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/vega/1975/1975-chevrolet-vega-cosworth?id=aCn1I000000D2oASAS) has seen excellent ones drop from $22,900 to $21,800. Who can say where the floor is, but if you’ve been looking at these interesting compact performance cars, now might be the time.
