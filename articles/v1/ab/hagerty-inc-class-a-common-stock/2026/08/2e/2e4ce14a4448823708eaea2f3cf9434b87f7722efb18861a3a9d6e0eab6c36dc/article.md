# Go Slow and Say Hello: Celebrating 50 Years of the Baja 500 with Vintage Iron

We bounced along a thin ribbon of hardpack, a safe zone in the expanses of a salt flat known as Laguna del Diablo, or Lagoon of the Devil. Small tornadoes of silt caught in tailpipe vortices behind my Jeep spun through the cockpit like a sack of flour escaping from a blender. Beyond the hood, a procession of vintage four-wheel drives trudged along like a camel caravan en route to Timbuktu, their dusty wake pushed east by a fresh westerly. The two-way radio crackled to life. It was Sal Fish, considered the godfather of go-fast Baja racing, sharing another outlandish tale from the early days. Something about a biplane and the Red Baron. But there wouldn’t be a checkered flag, trophies, and Tecate girls at the end of the day. This was the Slow Baja Vintage Expedition, and it was more likely someone would be handing us shots of tequila and plates of street tacos.


Baja California, referred to as Mexico’s La Frontera, is not your average place. This wasn’t your average liver-soaking Cabo Wabo spring fling, and Fish is not your average guy. If we roll the clock back to 1973, he was overseeing *Hot Rod* , *Car Craft* , and a host of other automotive titles for Petersen Publishing. But his life took a drastic turn when Mickey Thompson talked him into swapping the perks of publishing for a clapped-out VW Thing, a sleeping bag, and coyotes for traveling companions. The duo launched the SCORE Baja 500, an updated version of the then-defunct NORRA 500, the following year. Fast-forward to fall 2025, when Slow Baja founder Michael Emery invited us south for a weeklong celebration of the event’s golden anniversary.


En route to Rancho La Concepción, Mark O’Connor pushes his 1966 Jeep J-2000 up a steep grade in Baja Norte’s Sierra de San Pedro Mártir, which defies the assumption that Baja is only desert.


Chris Collard


Five days earlier, we crossed the border at Tijuana and were following Mex 1, the Transpeninsular Highway, south toward Ensenada. In the pack was a rare[Bill Stroppe Baja Bronco](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BV1UZabkdwI) , a Jeep Forward Control, a Gladiator J-2000, an ’82 Datsun, and a contingent of old Land Cruisers and Land Rovers. Rounding out the pack was Kurht Gerhardt’s Peking-to-Paris Porsche 912, man-of-mystery Mack Johnson’s Rod Hall Dodge tribute truck, and my Expedicion de las Americas CJ-7. There was nary an OBD port, ABS, or LCD screen among us.


Although many in the group had competition credentials ranging from the Mint 400 and Vegas to Reno desert races, and our El Jefe Emery ran the Carrera Panamericana multiple times, there would be no road books, rally trackers, or pit crews. What we did have was a plethora of old-school distributors, carburetors, and drum brakes. A prerequisite was that your rig must be at least 30 years old, though there were a few OBD-II interlopers. We’d be tracing the original 500 route, but with the Slow Baja mantra of going slow and saying hello, taking time to smell the cacti, and seeking out the peninsula’s hidden playas, taco carts, and treasures. When the sun rested on the western horizon, we’d pitch the tents, pull out a round of cerveza fria, and enjoy liberal pours of Tequila Fortaleza around the campfire.


## Conquistadors and La Frontera


Chris Collard


To appreciate the Baja Peninsula and its residents, one must flip the calendar back to the 16th century, Spanish conquistadors, and the Holy Cross. Hernán Cortés, after conquering the Aztecs in central and southern Mexico, turned his attention to Honduras and years later north and west to Baja. It was here, the place thought to be Isla de Califia, a mythical island supposedly ruled by beautiful Amazon women, that he finally got his ass served to him on the pointy end of a cactus. In a competitive quest for power with his rival Nuño Beltrán de Guzmán, this charming guy with a bloody sword and deep pockets dispatched a small fleet of ships to set up a colony.


Legend has it that one was lost in the Colorado River tidal surge, another captured by Guzmán, and the crew of the third mutinied, killing the captain and landing near present-day La Paz. Some succumbed to starvation and dehydration, while others, after their brutal treatment of the indigenous people, were appropriately dispatched to the underworld. The few who survived returned to Mexico to embellish tales of pearls and riches, a likely attempt to save their hides. Cortés sent a second fleet, which also failed, but finally set foot on the peninsula in 1535. The indigenous people, now privy to the ruthless ways of the invaders, eventually sent Cortés and his scalawags packing.


The peninsula then sat relatively unmolested for more than 150 years before Spanish Jesuits weighed anchor near Loreto in 1697. Again, with the Bible as their doctrine, they laid the cornerstones for the first of 17 missions that would in time dot a mule trail known as El Camino Real, or Royal Road. This set in motion several centuries of arduously slow but persistent development. Environs of the central peninsula are harsh, summers are brain-boiling hot, water sources are infrequent, venomous snakes lurk in the shadows, and bumping into nearly any plant will produce a generous sample for your next phlebotomist visit. Considering the singular ribbon of asphalt that connects Tijuana to Cabo San Lucas, a span of 1063 miles, took nearly 300 years to complete, one would surmise that Mexico’s La Frontera is one tough hombre.

## Rod Knocks and Roast Pig


Chris Collard


I stabbed the brakes hard, swerving around the Porsche 912 to avoid turning it into a green accordion as it abruptly headed toward the guardrail. Gerhardt came on the radio: “I’ve got a problem.” No s***, I thought. One of our crew pulled him off the highway, where we opened the rear deck. Engine knock, possibly a rod—no bueno! Its slow trip through Baja might have come to a speedy end. We hooked it up to the business end of a tow strap and dragged it to the first night’s bivouac for further inspection.


Rancho la Bellota, 2800 acres of mountain cattle country, is a hidden gem deep within Valle de Guadalupe, Baja Norte’s developing wine region. Built from scratch by owners Raul and Caroline Aguiar among towering cottonwoods, it is a verdant oasis replete with stables, casitas, and a large European-style dining area.

A pig was slowly roasting over a bed of coals as we pulled in. The Porsche was pushed into the barn, and while some opted for glamping in a casita, most of us laid out our tents and bedrolls. This night would be a meet-and-greet for gearheads from all walks of life: a dentist, a firefighter, a lighting consultant, and a dirtbag Jeep guy like me. Also on hand were the guys from Binder Boneyard, the International Harvester outfit from Oregon; Baja Bound, the San Diego insurance agency that provides coverage in Mexico; and the owner of Baja Broncos. Mack Johnson is well-known in these circles for having collected and restored some of the best off-roaders and taking them to Baja. And then there was our tail gunner, Curt LeDuc. He and Fish are both inductees in the Off-Road Motorsports Hall of Fame. While Fish promoted off-road races, LeDuc, who began as a shade-tree fabricator from Massachusetts, spent 30 years knocking the competition off the podium. The common thread among the group was a love of old iron and a quest to explore the remote reaches of Baja. The first of many feasts ensued, followed by libations and a healthy round of yarn-spinning around the fire.


## Beer Bashes and Dumpster Mattresses


Chris Collard


Our fears of terminal cancer were confirmed. The 912 was DOA and would need to be retrieved on our return trip north. Now without wheels, Gerhardt grabbed his ruck-sack and bedroll and jumped in with me. From a rattly old Porsche to an even more rattly old Jeep. Though he was still grieving, I wanted to slap him, as I had so been looking forward to photographing and riding in his classic steed.


Although we were tracing an off-road racecourse, this day would be nearly all pavement. South through the congestion of Ensenada and passing the Jesuit mission ruins of Santo Tomas, we were in for another of Emery’s surprises. Everyone followed suit as he pulled into a Pemex station and headed over to the adjoining cafe. Walking out, our hands were stuffed with steaming hot burritos for the road. This would be a repeating theme, far from the nearest fancy coffee shop or prefab burger joint. Baja “fast food” is authentic, fresh, and oh so tasty.

We had hopefully rooted out our mechanical gremlins, and our departure from Mex 1 led us high into the Sierra de San Pedro Mártir. For those who envision Baja as a vast and desolate desert, which much of it is, there are pockets that defy the laws of logic. Picacho del Diablo rises to 10,157 feet and the adjoining area is blanketed with thick stands of pine and oak. Meandering streams are home to rainbow trout and California condors ride thermals above the occasional cluster of bighorn sheep.


Climbing from sea level to 5000 feet, the ’61 Forward Control, powered by a Super Hurricane six-banger, nearly flatlined as owner Mark Jukes pushed the redline up 18 percent grades. As we turned off the pavement, a 6-mile dirt road deposited us in yet another of Baja’s hidden gems. Located on the old route between Rancho Meling and Mike’s Sky Rancho, Rancho La Concepción holds a bit of its own history. At the turn of the 20th century, Arthur W. North passed through these parts during a multiyear mule trek retracing the El Camino Real. In his book, *Camp and Camino in Lower California* , he wrote of vaqueros, bandidos, Kiliwa Indians, and someone stealing his mules in the middle of the night. The bandidos are long gone, and owners Vitza and César have carved out what they call a regenerative and sustainable retreat. Around the fire that night, Emery recounted his first trip south and explained how he came up with the concept of Slow Baja.


The cacti forests of Baja’s central peninsula are surreal.


Chris Collard


During one of his college Baja bashes, a buddy suggested they drive south until their money ran out. “We salvaged a mattress topper from a dumpster to sleep on,” he said, “and shoved it in the back of my Toyota Corona.” Their combined $28 ran out in Santispac, where they scrounged beer bottles, returned them for the deposit, and collected enough pesos to get back to the border.


The Slow Baja idea developed years later during the Baja XL, a 3000-mile race from Los Angeles to Cabo and back. “I was in a bone-stock ’71 FJ40 Land Cruiser and had no intention of winning or even racing,” Emery recalled. “I just wanted to participate and not get run over. I didn’t know what Slow Baja was but had painted it on the side of the truck. After the event, I realized this is the job I want. I want a job driving around Baja.”


## Puppies and Oysters


Chris Collard


Mornings were becoming pretty lax, at this point. If El Jefe said, “Wheels up at 0800 tomorrow,” we were lucky to be rolling by 0900. One might suggest folks couldn’t get their s*** together or grasp the English language, but I could feel the group was slipping into mañana mode. Ah, mañana mode, you ask? In our world of rigid deadlines, time clocks, appointments, crappy co-workers, and even crappier bosses, the oft-heard phrase “mañana, mañana”—which roughly translates to “tomorrow, maybe the next day”—puts life in proper perspective. Toss your cellphone and wristwatch in the glovebox when you cross the border, and La Frontera becomes timeless. The BS from the north is relegated to an alcove of the brain where its radioactive half-life accelerates until it lacks any and all significance. Achieving mañana mode is the first midterm in Slow Baja 101. Not a bad thing; it is true bliss, in my opinion—but also might be why it took Los Californios three centuries to finish Mex 1.


We’d get our first taste of Baja’s endless sand playas this day, a 12-mile run between Punta San Telmo and Bahia Camalú. With the Pacific at our tailpipes, we blasted south, doing what we could to outrun the incoming tide. Climbing a dune to high ground, Emery guided us to Baja Animal Outreach, an adoption center for the area’s abundance of street dogs. Years ago, owners Rick and Maria bought a place on the beach so they could surf every day, but as they collected stray, sick, and disabled dogs, they realized their purpose in life was more than a big wave.


The Baja Peninsula, stretching 800 miles south from the U.S. border to form the Sea of Cortez, is peppered with ancient lakebeds called lagunas. Slow Baja founder Michael Emery leads the team across Laguna Chapala in his ’71 Toyota FJ40 Land Cruiser.


Chris Collard


Everyone had ponied up pesos for 500 pounds of dog food, and most of us added dinero to the cardboard donation box for doggy shots and spaying. A new litter of puppies was so damn cute, Jukes and his daughter Maysie kidnapped one, tossed it in the FC, and stole away.


Another beach run deposited us in a private camp in Reserva Natural Punta Mazo. Three squares a day were included with the price of admission, and being just a few meters from the crashing surf, the nightly feast would be a bounty from the sea. A constant stream of ostras, tacos de pescado, and burritos de langosta flowed from the open-air grill, topped off with pineapple upside-down cake for dessert.


## Mama Espinoza


Chris Collard


A typical coastal haze obscured the reserve in the pre-dawn light, slowly dispersing as the sun cleared the horizon. In my opinion, the best part of waking up is watching sunrise with a healthy dose of addiction in my cup. As I fired up a Jetboil on my hood, a small queue formed, and the aromatic mix of coffee and salty sea air drifted through camp.


A very important Baja morning ritual is to fire up the Jetboil for a hot cuppa joe.


Chris Collard


Jumping back on Mex 1, we made a quick stop at Mama Espinoza’s in El Rosario. Born Doña Anita Grosso in 1910, she was whisked north to the U.S. when Pancho Villa’s revolutionary forces began sacking Baja Norte. Her family returned after the war, and she opened a restaurant in 1930. With a command of the English language, she became the town’s postmaster, greeting agent, and unofficial translator.


In the ’50s and ’60s, explorers and authors such as Erle Stanley Gardner, Howard E. Gulick, and Harry Crosby frequented Mama’s to feast on her famous lobster burritos. Providing the only services before the long haul south to Guerrero Negro, she also hosted pit stops during the early Baja races. The beloved matriarch of Santa Rosalía passed away in 2015 at the ripe young age of 105, but her family still runs the restaurant with the same grace and hospitality.


## Coconuts, Motorheads, and Movie Night


Chris Collard


The gods must have been smiling when they fashioned the enchanted landscape of the central peninsula. Peeling off the tarmac near Cataviña, we followed a sandy track through massive granite boulder fields pierced by towering cardon and spindly boojum (aka Dr. Seuss trees). Settling in at a place known as Chimney Rock, the nightly social hour commenced with fresh-squeezed margaritas.


Alan and Jonny Feld, owners of Field Van and driving custom Ford E-350 palaces, were one of the OBD-II outlanders in the group. I’ve known them for years, great guys, and they scored a big hit when Jonny set up a movie screen, an audio system, and fired up the Starlink to stream *Dust to Glory* —a Dana Brown film about the 2005 Baja 1000 featuring Fish, Mario Andretti, and James Garner. Although most of us had boycotted technology, scarcely a word was spoken for the next 90 minutes; even the coyotes piped down and tuned in.

The next morning, we continued south 120 miles to Laguna Chapala and the ranch of Mama’s older brother, Arturo Grosso Peña. Back in 1955, Baja’s governor offered him $800 to build a road connecting Chapala to the fishing village of San Felipe. With picks, shovels, a few laborers, and a truckload of dynamite, they blasted a rough two-track, which remained the primary artery to the Sea of Cortez until the current highway (Mex 5) was completed around 2010.


Regardless of our quest to go slow in Baja, if you put two motorheads together on an anvil-flat dry lake, things are going to happen. The expanses of Laguna Chapala allowed everyone to release their inner 16-year-old self, mash the skinny pedal, and let ’er rip. Chuck Mills took the lead in his LS-powered 60 Series Land Cruiser, and the rest of us relegated ourselves to eating his dust.


A highlight of any trip to Baja is cruising its wealth of endless playas. Mark O’Connor and Andrew Norton make their way north along the Sea of Cortez to Lynn Chenowth’s Legacy Lodge.


Chris Collard


Following Peña’s general track, we traced the Sea of Cortez’s dreamy coastline to Bahia San Luis Gonzaga. My first visit here was in 1990 and its cobalt-blue water, sandy beaches, and abundant marine life have drawn me back many times. Back then, there were no services, and settlements of the modern era included Papa Fernandez (1950s), Alfonsina’s (1960s), and Punta Final, an expat outpost that in the 1800s served as a supply depot for the local mines. We ducked into Rancho Grande for fuel and chilled cocos—a coconut with the top cut off and a straw poking out. Making our way north around Punta Willard, we pulled into La Poma, where Emery had another seaside fiesta lined up. If gluttony is one of the seven deadly sins, our days were getting shorter.


## Horsepower and Crazy Gringos


The setting sun chased Ian Tydeman and his ’69 Series II Land Rover up a beach.


Chris Collard


While crossing Laguna del Diablo, Fish came on the radio with his barnstorming tale. “We were having a heck of a time finding a track over a mountain when a crop duster flew by, circled around, and landed. It had two wings, open cockpit, and the pilot jumped out wearing a leather jacket. I thought he was the Red Baron. Pepé talked him into giving me a ride, but the rear seat was full of chemicals so he motioned me to sit on his shoulders. There I was with my legs wrapped around this guy’s body, trying to take notes and give him directions. I’m sure he thought: ‘This gringo must be crazy.’”


After stopping for a photo, Gerhardt and I got separated from the group. No problem, just follow the dust trails or the old racecourse. That didn’t work out so well. They got on the highway heading to Horsepower Ranch, but Gerhardt and I had Mike’s Sky Rancho etched in our brains—a four-hour dirt detour to the middle of nowhere. Pulling up to an empty parking area in bewilderment, in unison, we said, “Horsepower.” Crap! So much for boycotting technology. I dug out my iPhone and sent a satellite text so they wouldn’t dispatch a posse to find us. Needless to say, we arrived late and missed the final night’s festivities and the “Is Baja Right for You” awards. What a couple of knuckleheads.


If you plan to drive vintage iron in Baja, it’s good to know how to spin a wrench.

At Campo La Poma, the group enjoyed evening libations from the bed of Joss Mikelonis’ ’82 Datsun.

Considering we had spun more than 700 miles on the tickers of a dozen vehicles with four-plus decades under their wheels, our contingent of old iron had held up damn good. Jukes and Gerhardt would collect the 912 in the morning, and we’d head for the border. If deadlines, time clocks, and crappy co-workers awaited up north, this was irrelevant. We were returning with an advanced degree from the Slow Baja Academy. Mañana, mañana.

## Sal Fish for President


Chris Collard


There is good reason why Sal Fish is referred to as the godfather of Baja racing. At 86 years old, he possesses thousands of far-fetched tales spanning 50 years and probably a million backcountry miles. Some are so wild, you just can’t make this stuff up: racing down the 405 in Los Angeles at 100 mph scraping door handles with Parnelli Jones and Walker Evans, or surviving Baja on a bag of crackers, a gallon of 7-Eleven water, and a footlong salami. He is as sharp as a tack, humble as pie, and has a smart sense of humor akin to a late-night comedy show host. Fish said, “Mickey saw something in me I didn’t know I had. Southern California was the hotbed of motorsports, and I was in the center of it. Really a dream job, and I felt very blessed to have the opportunity.”


## Meet Curt Leduc


Chris Collard


Considering Curt LeDuc is an internationally recognized champion and Hall of Fame inductee, he is extremely approachable. “I like to say I’m a blue-collar guy. I didn’t have a boat or a river house. I just wanted to be in my shop, creating the next project. I never had to force myself to get up and go to work. I’d go to races with roll cages and parts, set up a booth, and built my business one handshake at a time. I met Walker Evans, and he asked me to pit for him in the Baja 1000. I became enthralled with Mexico and had to figure out how to move out here and make a living. With no backup plan, I had to make it work.”


LeDuc divides his time between California and his home near Ensenada, where he supports orphanages, a school for the deaf, and has adopted a baseball team. His current fundraiser is for the foster care organization Casa de Esperanza.


One might say LeDuc is a romantic philosopher, as he was once quoted: “When I die, in my next life, I want to come back as a giant cardon cactus 100 miles north of La Paz. Even though I won’t see or hear, I’ll feel the ground shake. I’ll know things are OK in the world because fathers and sons and daughters are going to challenge themselves and come to Baja.”


## Lynn Chenowth’s Legacy Lodge


Chris Collard


Chris Collard


This place should be in Las Vegas rather than on an obscure coastline 140 miles south of the border. Chenowth, also a Hall of Famer, began welding sand buggy chassis in the early ’70s and became legendary in the off-road world. Opening the heavy barn doors of his museum reveals priceless examples of automotive history. Walking upstairs to the cantina, we found strangely erotic scenes carved into the structure’s massive wood beams—apparently the previous owner was a pot-growing sex fiend from the ’70s. The museum, pool, waterfront dining, and nightly accommodations are amazing, and the Legacy Lodge is not to be missed. Don’t worry about bringing your kids—the dirty stuff has been covered with race memorabilia.
