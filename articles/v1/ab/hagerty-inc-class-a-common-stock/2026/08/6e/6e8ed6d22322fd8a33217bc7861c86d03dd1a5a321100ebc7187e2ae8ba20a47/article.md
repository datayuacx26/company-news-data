# Piston Slap: Torque Converting Faults Become Engine Problems?

*Brian* writes:


Hi Sajeev, I enjoy your column so here’s one that is frustrating me. I recently purchased a ’72 Corvette that has a base 350 / TH400 drivetrain. The engine has been modified with an Edelbrock intake, Holley carb, and headers, and it runs fine. The previous owner also replaced the distributor with an older Chev HEI unit.


The issue I’m having is that when the car is warmed up, after 15 minutes or so of driving, it sometimes stalls when coming to a stop, but not always. I can feel the transmission changing down from 3rd to 2nd, but it seems to not shift down to 1st when it stalls the engine.


Brian the OP


I also had to replace the control module in the HEI distributor as it died recently. I’ve replaced the vacuum control module on the TH400 and the vacuum line to the manifold from it. Could it be the older HEI vacuum advance is not working properly, causing the issue?


If I drive with two feet and keep the revs up just above 1000 as I come to a stop, it doesn’t stall. I have the idle set now to 1000 but still need to two-foot, as sometimes it just feels like it’s going to stall, and I push the gas pedal just a bit to stop that. It’s very annoying when trying to drive in traffic in town with frequent stops needed.


*Sajeev* answers:


Brian, here’s the thing about me: As the *Piston Slap* series matures and my readership’s automobiles get older and older, I sometimes focus on obscure problems being the solution. This notion was magnified when my body threw every doctor a curveball by triggering the extremely rare, life-threatening autoimmune disease known as Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Don’t Google it (the photos are gross); just know that my penchant for obscure, rare diagnoses runs deep within my veins.


But I have ample reason to think the engine stalling has nothing to do with the engine, because the torque converter plays a key role in this problem. Or maybe the transmission fluid (which the converter relies on) is too low and also degraded to a serious extent with age and mileage.


Be it the torque converter or its fluid, this part is supposed to disengage the transmission from the engine as engine RPM nears idle. For those who are visual learners, this delightfully cheesy video from the “Pre-AI” era of YouTube does a good job showing off the torque converter’s bits.


If your Corvette’s transmission is original (i.e. never rebuilt), it’s high time to go through the whole thing: 54 years of service is long enough. But if it’s been rebuilt in the last 20 years or so, look at the fluid level and condition. If the fluid is up to par, consider replacing the torque converter, as I reckon it has a bad stator.


There’s also the remote chance that there’s a mismatch with aftermarket parts, as torque converter stall speeds and the specs of a hot camshaft allegedly cause this problem. But I am neither qualified to discuss this, nor do I consider this to be the problem in Brian’s Corvette.


But I am qualified to suggest an upgrade to a replacement torque converter with a 2500-ish stall speed, provided you still have a stock camshaft or a mild aftermarket replacement. The extra stall speed (stock is usually around 1800 rpm) will really perk up the Corvette’s acceleration from a standstill. I have installed such an aftermarket converter in big and small V-8–powered American cars alike, and the modest loss in fuel economy (lock-up torque converter or not) was never a concerning trade-off for me. It’s worth the penalty for jackrabbit launches and wide eyes from your passenger.
