# How to Organize a Driving Rally (And Why You Should)

We’re hoping[our third annual Road of the Year](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/hagerty-road-of-the-year-2026-pennsylvania-route-144/) article already has you thinking about gathering up a group of your car friends and driving somewhere outside the county line. Whether it’s a simple out-and-back day trip or a multi-night excursion, there’s no better way to get away from the daily grind—and to strengthen friendships, and build new ones—than a road trip. Someone’s gotta organize these excursions, and that someone should be you. Trust us, it will be worth your time.


If you’ve been a soccer coach and chaperoned kids to an out-of-town tournament; or if you’ve organized an NFL betting pool; or if you’ve tackled any sort of medium- or long-term car restoration project, you have the skills to pull together a group drive. Of course, one of the organizational skills you will exercise is to delegate, because a[road trip](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/two-friends-a-big-block-mopar-and-the-100-year-old-mother-road/) or rally must be fun for you, too.


A word of caution, though: The need for planning increases exponentially with every[temperamental car](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/when-broken-down-in-the-boonies-get-creative-and-rely-on-community/) and human you add to the convoy. Ask us how we know. Most of the road-trip stories you read from Hagerty Media involve several cars and people (even one-car features, like[this father-and-son road trip to Death Valley in a C2 Corvette](https://www.hagerty.com/media/great-reads/father-and-son-road-trip-americas-sports-car-to-death-valley/) , tend to require a photographer and assistant trailing in a separate vehicle). Deciding on a route and other activities, and figuring out where everyone will eat, drink, and pee, are skills that many of us here at Hagerty Media have perfected—often by learning from our mistakes.


Here are some nonnegotiables to internalize.


Cameron Neveu


## 1. Someone Has to Be in Charge


“That doesn’t mean you have to stand up in front of the group and deliver a lecture,” Hagerty’s director of driving events,[Dave Hord](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/driving-canadas-most-challenging-roads-in-a-100-year-old-ford/) , advises. You can lead more quietly. “It can be a cooperative exercise where you decide on a date and solicit a bunch of your friends to exchange Google map routes in a text trail,” explains Hord. Not every participant will agree with every aspect of your plans, which is fine. The most important act of leadership is simply to commit to going. “Tell people, ‘I’m doing this, at this place, at this time. Come or don’t come,’” says editor-at-large[Aaron Robinson](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/aaron-robinson/) . “They will line up.”


## 2. Take Advantage of Technology…


Both Hord and this Hagerty Media editor-in-chief,[Larry Webster](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/lwebsterhagerty-com/) , are fans of the[Rallista app](https://rallista.app/) , which allows you to preload routes that can be followed even if cellular coverage is spotty. “One of my pals might text: ‘I found a new route someone posted in Rallista,’” says Hord. “And the rest of us will be like, ‘Let’s go try it this weekend.’”


Cameron Neveu


## 3. …But Don’t Rely on It Exclusively


Road conditions change constantly. Sometimes, what looks like a great two-lane on a mapping app turns out to be washed out or closed for construction. If you can’t pre-run the whole thing in person, do your best to research road and weather conditions ahead of time. State authorities often publish scheduled road maintenance on their DOT websites. Don’t forget to look for clean restrooms.


## 4. Plan Around the Neediest Participant


Robinson, who lives in L.A., recently pulled together a Saturday morning drive for the Southern California chapter of the Horseless Carriage Club of America. He’s no stranger to organizing, having set up many a comparison test during his long stint at *Car and Driver* . But this time, he had to secure a parking lot big enough to accommodate trucks and trailers, since owners of Brass Era cars aren’t trundling down the 405 freeway in them.


“It was a steep learning curve for me,” Robinson admits. “When you invite Brass Era cars, it’s a whole different thing.” Sometimes, the limiting factor isn’t the car. “Someone will show up with their dog, or a friend who can’t do stairs and I’ve chosen a restaurant for dinner that’s reached via 26 stone steps,” Hord adds. Patience and empathy are your friends here.

## 5. Make Clear the Intended Style and Tone of the Drive


Is this a leisurely drive where everyone will stick together? Or a pedal-to-the-floor romp where participants will stretch the legs of their cars as well as the speed limits? Are coffee breaks optional or required? Know before you go.


## 6. Start Small


There’s nothing quite like a multiday road trip and the camaraderie that comes from sharing meals together. But make no mistake, these are big undertakings. “People who start planning multiday driving events understand why I do it as a profession, not as a hobby,” Hord quips. A half-day excursion is a good way to dip your toe into the water. Even these simple trips need planning—Hord recalls a recent two-hour drive to Vancouver Island Motorsport Circuit, with friends joining along the way. He and his pals made sure to outline a route and specify a gas stop. But if worse comes to worst, no one is far from home.

## 7. Don’t Fear Breakdowns


Someone will get stuck. With enough participants, it’s a matter of when, not if. But it’s all part of the fun, an opportunity for group problem solving and good stories at dinner. ## 8. Don’t Forget About Food


Even if it’s just a stop at a donut shop. Everyone is happier on a full stomach.
