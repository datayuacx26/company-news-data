# American-Made Tools Are Worth It Every Time

Joel Camassar lies awake nights wracked with anxiety about screwdriver handles. It’s an odd image for a night terror, sure, but consider their importance to Camassar’s livelihood. As an owner of Chapman Manufacturing, which has been making the same small precision screwdriver and ratchet bits in Durham, Connecticut, since 1936, Camassar has a tradition—and a business—to uphold. Every Chapman product is made in America. Still, the company isn’t large enough to vertically integrate its supply chain (à la Henry Ford’s attempts to establish rubber plantations), and some materials are difficult to come by domestically. “If you want it made in the U.S., the suppliers are much more limited,” Camassar says, adding that for plastic[screwdriver handles](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/mechanical-sympathy/phillips-screwdrivers-memories-and-trusting-the-process/) , “there’s one company to do that.” So he has fitful dreams about extrusion molds.


To manufacture in America is to face innumerable obstacles, as I learned when I wrote a book about a group of scrappy, determined American-made apparel entrepreneurs. Sometimes, like Camassar, it’s as if you’re trying to keep a restaurant going with a thin pantry of ingredients. Almost always you’re competing with cheap, foreign-made products and asking consumers to consider more than just their wallet. For these reasons and more, I buy made in the U.S. whenever I can.


Courtesy of Chapman Manufacturing


And I am heartened to report that the tool industry, unlike clothing, toys, appliances, and countless others, hasn’t been hollowed out by offshoring. Indeed, the mindful wrencher has enough options to fill out a tool bench.


True, there are some gaping holes. Generally speaking, you can’t buy power tools made in the U.S.; if it has a power source, in almost every case it’s going to have imported parts or be made overseas (beware of sneakily worded claims to be made in the U.S., “of imported materials,” an attempt to win points without doing the hard work).


There have also been high-profile industry missteps in recent years, like the $90 million automated factory that Stanley Black & Decker opened near Fort Worth, Texas, in 2022, to bring its Craftsman line back home. In the end, as The *Wall Street Journal* put it witheringly, the world’s largest toolmaker “couldn’t figure out how to make a wrench” in America.


Courtesy of Estwing


But plenty of other companies, including Klein (wire strippers), Channellock (pliers), Estwing (hammers), Wright (ratchets), Snap-on (wrenches), and Bondhus (hex keys), have solved the riddle. They produce hand tools forged with American steel and made by American workers—and have been doing it for decades. Although U.S.-made tools hardly monopolize the market, neither are they a dying niche. Industry-wide figures are hard to come by, but Snap-on alone operates 15 manufacturing facilities in the United States, and its tools group posted revenues of $2 billion in 2025. (The company also has global factories in South America, Europe, and Asia, which it says mostly serve local markets.)


“There are a few reasons for their staying power,” says Scott Paul, president of the Alliance for American Manufacturing. “Many of these American-made brands are synonymous with quality and customer service.”


Courtesy of Wright Tool


You won’t find these hammers, wrenches, and ratchets in Home Depot or your local hardware store, however. They’re often sold through select distributors or the brand’s own channels. Snap-on, the Wisconsin-based company whose tools have become a kind of status symbol in automotive shops, regularly calls on nearly a million technicians—automotive, heavy-duty, collision—and approximately 250,000 repair shops. About 35 percent of its tools are sold from its mobile vans, direct to technicians. “I met a guy with a Bentley. He said, ‘I feel like I have to throw myself in front of a Snap-on truck to get your tools,’” says Nick Pinchuk, the company’s chairman and CEO. “He was right.”


What might seem like bad business—why wouldn’t you sell to everyone?—is in fact a survival strategy. For one thing, the hand-tools market is a bit like an iceberg in that the biggest chunk isn’t visible above the surface. A 2023 study of the industry by the research and consulting firm Arizton found that “the industrial segment holds the most significant share” of the market. American-made tool companies have also learned that by making tools for specific, critical tasks where the penalty for failure is high—say, building a bridge or repairing a military vehicle in wartime—they can charge a premium. Camassar put it bluntly: “The guy using a screwdriver around the house to open paint cans is not what our tools are for.” Rather, Chapman’s precision-milled bits are used for work in robotics, stadium sound systems, and even pinball machines.


In automotive applications, American companies’ niche is tools engineered for very tight spots. Snap-on, for instance, recently came up with a custom-profile socket that allows mechanics to reach into the heart of Ford’s 6.7-liter engine. “Customize a tool for the task,” Pinchuk says. “We’re not trying to beat on scale. We’re trying to beat on application and experience.”


Courtesy of Snap-on


What you’re getting for the extra money is a tool guaranteed not to fail and customer service that’s a few states rather than an ocean away. “I answer the phone. I’m easy to do business with. Lifetime warranty,” says Tom Futey, the president and co-owner of Wright, which makes a 400-piece hand-tool set for military Humvees, among thousands of other items, at its Barberton, Ohio, plant. “Buying a cheap tool is not saving you money,” he adds. “It could be costing you money.”


Weekend mechanics, take heart—you can still get your hands on these American-made tools even if you’re not a NASA engineer or Ferrari restorer. Chapman sells direct through its website. Wright works with a network of distributors that includes Amazon sellers, while Snap-on sells through its website. Will you pay more than the Chinese-made, $20 socket set at a big-box store? Yes. Is it worth it in the long run to buy more durable tools and support American manufacturers? You already know the answer.
