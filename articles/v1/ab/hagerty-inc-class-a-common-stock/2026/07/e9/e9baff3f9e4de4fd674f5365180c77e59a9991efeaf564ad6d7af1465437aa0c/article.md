# Kings of the Road: 20th-Century American Enthusiasts Set the Stage for the Modern Collector’s World

When you stop to think about it, collecting automobiles requires a lot. Money, time, and good roads are the first few things that come to mind, and following the end of World War II, we in the United States happened to have just a bit more of each than almost any other Western nation.


Credit first and foremost our mighty manufacturing capability. Our ability to pivot in mere months from building automobiles, tractors, and refrigerators to war machinery was a primary reason we came out on top—and wound up winning the peace, as well. Since World War II was not fought on North American shores, those factories hadn’t been devastated as was the case in much of Europe and Japan. Moreover, because war-ravaged countries had to remove rubble and rebuild cities before retooling, modernizing, and starting over, our industrial base could immediately focus on transitioning back to civilian production. And because American citizens didn’t have to bear the enormous costs of said rebuilding (aside from some $13 billion in aid to Europe via the Marshall Plan), they were relatively well positioned to purchase whatever those resurgent factories could produce—and then some. In contrast, when manufacturers in countries like the United Kingdom got back on their feet, there was relatively little consumer economy to serve. Hence the U.K.’s famous postwar mantra, “Export or perish.” All to say, most of the best cars produced in the decades following World War II found their way to our shores.


Italy built some of the world’s most valuable classics, and Japanese cars are on the rise—but it’s actually American makes that dominate overall.

When it comes to time, there are subtler factors at work. The generation that fought in the war—and later gave birth to what became known as the baby boomers—had a large segment who grew up on farms but, following the war, moved to the rapidly expanding suburbs. About 23 percent of the United States population lived on farms in 1940. By 1955, the number was only about 14 percent. Why does that matter? In two very important ways. Kids who grew up on farms were much more likely to possess the skills needed to keep old equipment running (a skill set that is, sadly, increasingly lacking in more recent generations). The second difference is not as obvious, but owning a newer house on a quarter-acre lot requires quite less in terms of the chores involved than running a farm, which helped to increase leisure time.


The United States hardly had a lock on[great roads](https://www.hagerty.com/media/tags/roads/) , but we did have increasingly accessible wide-open spaces and an infrastructure that was, by the 1950s,[built around the automobile](https://www.hagerty.com/media/great-reads/70-years-of-the-interstate-how-the-u-s-highway-system-built-the-america-we-know/) . The concept of a road trip was, for a short period of time at least, a uniquely American phenomenon. The thought of taking a pleasure drive existed in many places, but extending that for hundreds or even thousands of miles would have been far-fetched for all but extremely wealthy European drivers. Our gasoline was cheaper and our cars were larger. We were willing and able to take the whole family, gear, and pets on a trip to the beach, grandma’s house, or Wally World.

Our American love of cars is hardly unique, but our combination of geography, economy, time, skills, and just plain luck all came into play to make us the Big Dog in global car collecting. Hagerty estimates there are presently more than 40 million enthusiast cars in the United States. For reference, that’s roughly equal to the total number of motor vehicles in places like France and the United Kingdom. And it’s worth noting that despite the rise of Japanese and European collector cars, most collectors continue to favor American cars—auction data show that domestics dominate both in terms of the number of vehicles sold and the total dollars they bring in.


Will we continue to reign supreme? The only constant is change. But for now, and in the near future, American car collectors remain the Kings of the Road.
