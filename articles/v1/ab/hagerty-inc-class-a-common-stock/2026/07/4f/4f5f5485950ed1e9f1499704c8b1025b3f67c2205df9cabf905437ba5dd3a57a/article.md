# The Lamborghini Portofino Concept Paved the Way for Chrysler’s Cab-Forward Aspirations

The Chrysler LH platform was a major turning point for the automaker, an early ’90s coming-out party that proved it could build more than just K-car–derived econoboxes and minivans. The LH underpinned swoopy sedans like the Dodge Intrepid, Eagle Vision, and the upmarket Chrysler LHS, and the curvy charms of these family cars not only unlocked major sales success for the Pentastar as it left the financially shaky 1980s behind, they also sat well in showrooms beside equally striking models like the Dodge Viper roadster and the Dodge Ram pickup.

The design language expressed by these LH cars happens to have an unexpectedly exotic origin story. In fact, while the much-touted “cab forward” look found in Chrysler’s family of four-doors was born in the company’s Pacifica Design Center in San Diego, it had to make a detour to visit an Italian cousin on its passage into production.


To untangle the web of the cab-forward design’s ancestry, we sat down with longtime Chrysler design chief Tom Gale, who played a pivotal role in shepherding the LH’s look from early-’80s clay model to global Lamborghini concept to ’90s mainstream sales success.


Tom Gale

## Out with the K, in with the Future


“I just wanted to prove we weren’t brain-dead as far as design goes,” says Gale, referring to his approach when he took over the role of head of design at Chrysler in April 1985.


At the time, the company had just emerged from fulfilling its loan repayment obligations, after the federal government had bailed it out of bankruptcy at the end of the previous decade—a financial reorganization that Chrysler had completed ahead of schedule largely thanks to the popularity of its cheap and cheerful K-platform commuter cars. Despite their popularity, it was clear the K-car party couldn’t go on forever, as there were definite limits to the looks and capabilities offered by these models.


President Ronald Reagan and Lee Iacocca discuss the K-car platform.


Bettmann Archive/Getty Images


“What we were looking at was, what is the future of sedans?” Gale says. “Because the shape \[of the K platform\] was all too familiar. It came from a philosophy that was more comfortable with vinyl roofs and wire caps and white walls than the stuff of the future. We were really trying to figure out what is the most remarkable and effective way to package \[a sedan\]. And \[we\] were really trying to push the envelope.”


Two designers—Kevin Verduyn and studio boss Tom Tremont—were spearheading the effort at Pacifica, where, Gale says, numerous design studies were underway to try to answer these questions. One pair stood out: the Mojave and the Navajo. The latter caught the eyes of Gale and Chrysler executive vice president Bob Lutz at a design review in 1986, and the clay model was brought to the company’s studios at Highland Park, where Verduyn and others continued to refine its look.


“Kevin was just phenomenal. A great designer, a futurist, and a wonderful guy,” says Gale. It’s the future-focused nature of the Navajo that intrigued the design chief and proved it had potential to break Chrysler into a post-K-car future.


## Highland Park, by Way of Italy


Lamborghini Portofino concept


Stellantis


It’s here that the cab-forward path skips across an ocean to engage in a little palace intrigue.


In 1987, Chrysler purchased Lamborghini, a company that had spent much of the previous 10 years[bouncing from one steward to another](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/lamborghinis-wild-ownership-history-is-almost-as-colorful-as-its-cars/) . It wasn’t clear exactly why the American automaker was so keen to add the beleaguered supercar builder to its portfolio, but the presence of Lamborghini under the same corporate banner offered an unexpected opportunity for Gale in terms of advancing the work that had been done so far on the Navajo. That chance came from a certain Chrysler executive vice president.


“We had just bought Lamborghini … and we needed something to stir stuff up. And, of course, Bob was always a champion of stirring stuff up,” says Gale. Seeing the Navajo’s potential, Lutz threw his support behind building a full concept version, now renamed the Portofino, by way of the company’s international operations—specifically, Lamborghini. This allowed Gale to avoid significant amounts of budgetary and planning red tape back home.


“Lamborghini really had nothing to do with the Portofino,” says Gale. “They were supplying the name and the conduit. It was a way of really attracting attention to something.” Lutz was able to ensure, too, that Chrysler’s design team retained control over the final version of the Portofino concept, something that was a sticking point with Lamborghini from day one.


Stellantis


“Lamborghini at the time, they were primarily using their designer there, the former Bertone guy, \[Marcello\][Gandini](https://www.hagerty.com/media/design/marcello-gandini-drove-a-renaissance-in-automotive-design/) . He called the Portofino a potato, you know, because basically, if it wasn’t done by him, he didn’t like it. He wasn’t very tactful at all, and he was hard to deal with.”


Interacting with Gandini’s team was so difficult that shortly after the Portofino concept was built, Chrysler’s management clashed with him over the design for the Countach replacement. Finding the Italian completely closed off to any suggestions from Chrysler, Gale ended up taking the Diablo back to Highland Park, completing the design, and then sending not just the completed blueprints but the full tooling model for the surface of the Diablo to Lamborghini.


“I know that Lamborghini, being tight with Gandini, they would change it \[otherwise\]. I was content with being silent about it … and that never came out until the last few years, because nobody wanted to hear about Chrysler designing the Lamborghini,” says Gale. “I knew this was always going to be an argument, so I saved a photo book of the proposals that came about that were done in Highland Park.”


## Portofino’s Success Births a Cab-Forward Future


AutoWeek/Crain Communications


The Lamborghini Portofino concept debuted at the Frankfurt Motor Show in 1987, and it made considerable waves. It was built on the same bones as the Jalpa sports coupe and featured a high-revving V-8 engine, but looking back on it today, its influence on LH styling is crystal clear. Swap out the scissor doors and move the engine from the middle to the front of the sedan, and you’ve got a ringer for the original Dodge Intrepid.


Dodge Intrepid model transparency


Stellantis


“Cab forward wasn’t just two words and a term. It was something you could see because it moved the A-pillar forward, it allowed the rear door to expand, and it allowed entry and exit to the car in a much different way,” says Gale. “From a packaging point of view, it became something real. It wasn’t just an advertising term.”


Gale points out that the Portofino was “cab-forward on steroids,” primarily because it was a mid-engine car with short overhang at each end. And yet, despite its dramatic layout, much of the concept transferred into production, with even the Portofino’s greenish gold color and wheel design finding its way onto the Intrepid’s order sheet.


Stellantis


Dodge Intrepid ES


Stellantis


The Portofino endured long past the time when most concepts are put out to pasture, thanks to policy changes Gale made where concepts returned to the mothership at the end of their show career (rather than being dispersed to dealerships, collectors, or scrap heaps). An unfortunate fire during transport in 1991 nearly destroyed it completely, but instead of walking away from this important piece of its history, Chrysler used its insurance settlement to have California’s Metalcrafters rebuild it to an even higher spec—using real glass rather than the original Plexiglas for the greenhouse, for example. The vehicle remains in the company’s possession today.


## A Lasting Influence


Stellantis


Stellantis


Lamborghini’s time under the Chrysler umbrella was brief, but there’s no question that using the supercar brand to birth the Portofino concept into the world paid massive dividends for the company’s more mainstream offerings, even if the bull badge was entirely superficial to the vehicle’s development.


That wasn’t the only long-lasting gift that the Lamborghini concept bequeathed to the Pentastar, however.


“The car was kind of a hit,” says Gale. “I really think it brought a lot of attention to Chrysler that was unexpected. And so (Chrysler CEO) \[Lee\] Iacocca asked the question, ‘If you can do concept cars like that, why can’t you do production cars?’ And I said, ‘Lee, we’ve been waiting for you to ask that question.’”

With Iacocca’s support, Gale was able to start a production design committee that tied in key executive management—manufacturing, planning, engineering, marketing—into the design process. Suddenly, the design department was on equal footing with the rest of the company’s approach to future planning, giving it a much stronger voice in how Chrysler brought cars to the market.


How much of an impact did this have on the automaker’s fortunes? Take a look at the Chrysler of the 1980s and then contrast that against the much rowdier, risk-taking, and eye-catching efforts that emerged in the 1990s. Yes, the Lamborghini Portofino had a direct hand in the success of the LH cars—but its prominence on the international circuit also brought changes that introduced dynamic shapes like the Dodge Neon, the previously mentioned Ram and Viper, the Jeep Grand Cherokee, and the Plymouth Prowler and PT Cruiser, making the decade that followed perhaps the strongest in the company’s history.
