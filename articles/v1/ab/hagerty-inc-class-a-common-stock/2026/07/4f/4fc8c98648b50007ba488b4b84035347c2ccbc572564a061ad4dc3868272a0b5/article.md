# 4 Things to Consider When Buying a Lift for Your Garage

If you’re constantly working on cars, or, like me, want to keep working on cars well into the future but don’t look forward to the cocktail of middle age and cold concrete, a lift can be a lifesaver. Knowing that wasn’t too far down the road, I sprung for a two-post lift this past fall, and now I don’t know how I managed without one. If you think a lift might be a good idea for you, here are a few things to consider.


## Two Posts or Four?


Say hello to Turbeau Sweetpea. She doesn’t need as much clear height as others in this barn.


[Eddy Eckart](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eeckarthagerty-com/)


The first thing to ask yourself is what your priorities are. Given that I do my own brake work, change out snow tires on at least three cars, futz with suspension bits reasonably often, and have two cars for which it makes sense to[drop the engine out the bottom](https://www.hagerty.com/media/our-garage/bringing-boxster-back-1-eyes-wide-open/) , a two-post lift made a lot of sense for me. A four-poster serves as a more stable storage platform, and while you can perform all sorts of maintenance on one (especially with jacks designed to lift the car while on the four-post), the design does limit things somewhat.


## Check Your Constraints


Before the install, the team took measurements of my largest vehicle to ensure the lift was appropriately placed in my workspace.


[Eddy Eckart](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eeckarthagerty-com/)


Particularly if you’re going with a two-post, you’ll need to have enough concrete to anchor into. When I had my barn floor poured in 2020, I specified an eight-inch depth in the area where the lift was to go. My 10,000-lb capacity Challenger Lift technically requires just four inches of concrete, but added depth means added peace of mind. Check the lift manufacturer’s requirements before proceeding. Also make sure the concrete at the desired location is in good condition and free from cracks.


Now, look up. More specifically, check your ceiling height. My rafters clock in at just over 12 feet, so the Challenger squeaked in with a couple of inches to spare. They do make lifts for buildings with shorter ceilings, but that may limit how high you can raise the vehicle and/or what vehicles you can put on the lift.


For particularly low vehicles, a set of ramps helps with clearance.


[Eddy Eckart](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eeckarthagerty-com/)


Speaking of which, will you be lifting a 5500-lb pickup or a[2300-lb Miata](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pdKLZtS2QVY) ? That’ll decide how heavy-duty your lift should be. Once you’ve got the lifting capacity sorted, you’ll also need to verify the height of the lift point. This is the lowest that the four arms can go to slide beneath a vehicle and pick it up. Your low-slung sports car may need ramps to help get the ride height up above the minimum lift point. Conversely, a higher-riding vehicle like the truck pictured above may need height extensions to hoist it safely.


Finally, most lifts need a 220v power source, and it’s smart to run it on its own breaker. My setup required a 30-amp breaker, and my electrician cousin and I ran the conduit and wires to about a couple of feet from where the lift would be located.


## New Lift or Used?


Lift install underway


[Eddy Eckart](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eeckarthagerty-com/)


Chances are you can find a good deal (anywhere from $1500–$5K) on a used lift near you with a quick search online. It’s not a bad way to score very capable machinery for a reasonable price.


That said, a new lift is a better investment. There’s no question about whether they’ve been properly maintained, and more importantly, you’re buying time. New lifts typically come with warranties and, beyond that, are likely to have better and longer-lasting parts availability.


Regardless of the route you choose, it’s good peace of mind to know that the machine holding a ton or two (or more) over your head is well-designed and soundly built. The Automotive Lift Institute (ALI) certifies lifts based on structural integrity, control function, lowering speeds, overload protection, and quality control.


[Eddy Eckart](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eeckarthagerty-com/)


I also wanted to have a pro perform the install. After a bit of searching, I found a company near me, Northcoast Equipment Specialists, who sells and installs lifts for residential and commercial use across Northeast Ohio. They’re happy to install used lifts provided they are ALI-certified, but cautioned that used lifts usually get taken down for a reason, and that it’s not guaranteed a used lift will function properly. The crew they sent out was friendly, knowledgeable, and gave me a great tutorial on how to operate my new lift.


## Planning for the Future vs. Value Today


Northcoast Equipment’s team provided a helpful tutorial once they’d completed the install.


[Eddy Eckart](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eeckarthagerty-com/)


My selection came down to the economy-grade Challenger VLE10, an imported unit with a 10,000-lb capacity for $5695 (plus a $1K install fee), or the commercial-grade, American-made CL10AV3 with the same capacity for $1500 more. That’s not a massive delta, and armed with the knowledge of a more robust parts warranty, better access to parts, and the fact that a local dealership had just installed a fleet of CL10s, I felt comfortable that my additional investment would be serviceable for years to come. I chose that peace of mind. There are several other quality brands out there—it’s more important to take your time to understand which lift will best suit your needs and to identify a quality company near you that can service this body-saving tool for years to come.
