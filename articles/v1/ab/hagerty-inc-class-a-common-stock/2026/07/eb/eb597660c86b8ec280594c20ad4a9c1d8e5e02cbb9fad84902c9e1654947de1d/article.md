# Project Cheap Camaro #8: Sneaking Up on Color

It’s only July, but it has already been a long summer of sanding on project Cheap Camaro. All that sanding is a journey toward a destination, however: The car has come a long way since the days of[fixing the roof](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/project-cheap-camaro-make-mine-a-coupe/)) with a block of wood and a ratchet strap. We’re getting closer to the finish line, at least in terms of an actual finish.


The car had been over at Alliance High School for most of the year, where the students were working on aligning panels and learning the process of block sanding. But the end of the school year saw me trucking the freshly polyester-coated Cheap Camaro back to my own garage. The window of weather for paint projects is a short one in the Pacific Northwest, so with the car home—and three or so months of good, predictable weather ahead of me—I got to work sanding out the rest of the body to get it ready for the next steps in the paint process.


## To Poly and Beyond


When I last wrote about[the paint process](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/project-cheap-camaro-6-high-build/) , we had just shot a coat of Eastwood Contour Polyester Primer-Surfacer across the[epoxy-coated body](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/project-cheap-camaro-3-back-to-school/) . The poly fills in and covers over small imperfections and doesn’t tend to shrink after it dries. It sands easily, too, so blocking the car flat with this stuff is a straightforward process that takes more time than it does muscle. It’s more about finesse. And dust.


The Cheap Camaro needed one final coat of polyester high-build primer due to a few thin spots that popped up when blocking, so I sprayed it in one piece as soon as it came home for the summer.


Jim Pickering


The car looks pretty good here, but poly requires a bunch of sanding to get things truly flat and even. It’s easy to work with, though, and the final result is worth the time it takes to sand the finish out.


Jim Pickering


The students did a great job at starting the polyester sanding process using guide coat and flat blocks, but a few visible thin spots meant I had more work to do. I ended up spraying another coat of poly on the body in an inflatable booth in my driveway in June. Same setup as before: 2.0 tip in the Eastwood P500 HVLP gun; 30 psi spraying pressure; 50% overlap on the wet edge.


An inflatable booth like the one I’ve been using requires one blower fan to keep it inflated, along with two fans for proper airflow. It’s simple enough, but that’s a bunch of electrical amperage turned into airflow, and it was apparently one amp too many for the circuit I was using in my garage. So when the fans suddenly went quiet and the booth started to droop mid-spray, I had to jump into action as the Usain Bolt of circuit-breaker fixers. Fortunately, the poly was already dry to the touch by that point, and the booth’s roof just barely brushed it before the fans forced it back up. Photo-finish lesson: Dedicated circuits are key.


The sanding process is a simple one: Apply dry guide coat, sand with 220 until the guide coat is gone and there’s a uniform, smooth finish left behind, with no visible highs, no obvious lows. Then, guide coat again while moving up to 320-grit paper, which leaves a smoother finish behind.


220-grit paper on a flat block is a good place to start on the second coat of high-build, again using guide coat to show where you’ve sanded, as well as revealing any major issues while you’re working. Note the tape, used to mark and avoid the hard body lines.


Jim Pickering


Flat blocks will get your sanding efforts most of the way there, but you’ll also need different block shapes to get into curves and tight spots. Dura-Block makes some good, rounded options, but sometimes something as simple as a paint stick is the best tool for the job. Cut them to fit, wrap them in paper, and have at it.


Jim Pickering


Block sanding is pretty easy when it comes to big, flat surfaces such as a roof. But cars have complex shapes and curves, so sanding becomes a game of ingenuity. Rounded blocks are good for curved areas, and smaller blocks are needed for tighter areas. I found myself using everything from the proper Eastwood Elite Dustless Sanding Blocks to rounded Durablocks to your basic paint sticks wrapped in sandpaper. I even cut one paint stick into a two-inch-long mini block for getting into really tight spaces on the tail panel and inside the rain gutters. What it comes down to is this: There’s no one right or wrong answer to what you can use as a sanding block as long as it’s consistent in whatever shape it has. Bigger is better when you can use something big. From there, blocks are about the result, not so much the process.


After a couple of days’ worth of blocking, first in 220-grit and then in 320-grit, the Camaro looked ready for the next paint application step—but looks can be deceiving…


Jim Pickering


After the sanding looked done, it was time to stand back and take a look—but to really see the surface, it needs to be glossy, which meant applying Panel Check fluid to make the car’s surface wet and reflective. Dents, dings, low spots—every imperfection will show up right away as soon as the car has a shine to it. And fixing those issues means either more sanding to level out the panel or adding Contour Premium Finishing Glaze, which is a body filler designed to solve smaller imperfections. It’s an “apply, sand, repeat” process, until the reflections look even.


Jim Pickering


Jim Pickering


Jim Pickering


Jim Pickering


Clockwise from top left: Eastwood makes a Panel Check fluid in a spray can, which offers up the telltale shine. The gloss on the surface acts like a temporary clear coat, and the reflections won’t lie about issues that are otherwise hard to see or feel. I found low spots around the roof; some will sand out, others will require a little filler work. Marking them with a graphite pencil keeps them visible as you’re working. Finishing Glaze is a finer version of body filler but it works exactly the same way. It lays down thin and fills slight imperfections, pinholes, etc. The glaze goes down the same way standard body filler does: Mix it up on a board, then spread it smooth over the trouble spot. Once dry, it sands smooth and can be primed over.


## 2K Urethane


Poly is not a sealer, and even when blocked down with 320-grit paper, it will still have sanding scratches that show up when dry guide coat is applied. So the next step is a good 2K urethane primer, which lays smoother than the thick poly and fills in the smaller 320-grit scratches that the final paint would otherwise show. Block that down to 600 grit, and you’re basically ready for a sealer and color.


Again, when it comes to paint, it’s best to stick to one system. I began the process with Eastwood, so I went with its 2K Urethane Primer-Surfacer in gray. This stuff is the final element to lay down before a topcoat—meaning the color—so it’s a key step to get right.


I started by blowing the car completely apart so I could paint it in sections and get down into the jambs, starting with the fenders, doors, trunk lid, etc. I picked a relatively cool morning to tackle this, as I learned the hard way when I was laying the last coat of polyester just how hot an inflatable booth will get in the sun. So, an overcast, 70-degree day was just the thing, and right before July 4, that’s what I got.


With all the panels blocked first with 220 and then again with 320, it was time to re-inflate the paint booth and prepare for a coat of urethane primer. I elected to start with the small parts.


Jim Pickering


A couple of things to keep in mind about inflatable booths, aside from the Usain Bolt Electrical Challenge: Paint overspray will dry in the air if the booth is warm, and it lands as dust on the floor. But beyond that, the booth’s inherent static electricity will draw that dust to the walls, so you need to be careful to avoid bumping them while spraying, otherwise that dust will find its way into the finish. I found that blowing everything out thoroughly with a leaf blower was the first step in my booth setup, before bringing any parts into the space. I was amazed at how much dust and grit was in there just from the primer work I’d done a month or so before.


Once the space was as clean as it could be, the next step was to bring in the parts, set them up on stands, and then clean them with Panel Prep (or grease and wax remover) and a lint-free rag. From there, I sealed up the booth, tack-clothed each panel to remove any remaining dust and dirt, and got ready to spray.


The 2K mixes at a 4:1 ratio with the activator, and another :1 if using reducer to thin it down. Adding that reducer is a good idea when you’ve already blocked the poly down flat, because this stuff sprays pretty thick at a 4:1 ratio.


Eastwood’s 2K Urethane Primer-Surfacer has high-build characteristics and will flow out over the sanding scratches left in the poly from the 320-grit paper. It mixes with an activator and optional reducer to create a thinner, easier-to-spray mix than the poly, and it can be used as a primer sealer just prior to paint.


Jim Pickering


The first step before spraying is to clean every panel with a wax and grease remover, in this case Pre Painting Prep in a spray can and a clean lint-free rag. This removes any remaining dust or fingerprints that may impact the primer’s adhesion.


Jim Pickering


The instructions suggested a 1.2- to 1.6-mm fluid tip in the P500 gun, again with 30 psi of pressure, and with a 10–15-minute flash time between coats. The compressor needs an air/water separator on the air line that feeds the gun, as your compressor will condense the water in the air and push it out the air lines, which means it’ll get in your finish.


As with the poly, gun setup for the urethane is critical, as is your distance from the panel while you’re spraying. The idea is to have a good, even fan pattern coming out of the gun, which is adjustable with the flow and pattern knobs on the back of the gun. While spraying, it’s important to keep the gun about six inches from the panel and parallel to it, keeping a 50% overlap on what you just painted with the wet edge. It sounds easy enough, but it takes practice to get it right. That said, primer is the perfect thing to start with; even if you end up with tiger stripes from some setting or technique that’s wonky, it’s all getting sanded (and probably reapplied) anyway.

The 2K is a two-part system: primer and activator. I mixed it out in a 4:1 ratio. Adding the activator starts the clock on your spray time, which will vary based on the ambient temperature of your workspace. Adding additional urethane reducer will change the characteristics of the primer, based on your goals. I’ve found it sprays more evenly with some reducer added. I worked with one cup mixed and had a second ready to go—minus activator—to speed the process of making more when we needed it. After using some tack cloth on the panels, it was time to prime. Note the 50% overlap, distance from the gun to the panel, and 30 psi pressure setting. There’s an art to how this goes; fortunately, primer is a lot more forgiving than base or clear coat, so this is the natural place to learn.


I was able to get two wet coats on all of Cheap Camaro’s panels using about a half-gallon of primer, and for the most part, it laid down nice and smooth. The only issues came up when I bumped the booth (where’d that dust come from?) or when I started to run low on paint in the gun’s cup. But as with the poly, painting with two sets of hands means you can have another batch mixed up and ready to go in just a quick minute. My dad proved an apt second set of hands.


I sprayed two solid coats on all the Camaro’s loose sheet metal, with a 15-minute flash time between coats. Once they’d sat for another hour or so, we moved them out of the booth and under a cover where they could degas and cure fully over a hot afternoon. From here, the sanding process starts again, first with 320 and moving up to 600, just prior to paint.


Jim Pickering


This stuff dries fast and is ready to sand within about four hours. I waited overnight just to be sure, and then started working it with 320-grit paper on a block with guide coat. The plan is to move up to 400-grit and then 600-grit before moving on to color.


That’s a lot of sanding to tackle in just a couple of months, but summer waits for nobody.
