# Wandering the Oregon Woods in a Tired, Good Truck

I seem to have a particularly drug-resistant strain of terrier-chihuahua, so as July 4th approached and I considered my options to remove little Bowie from the endless whiz-bang-booms that are his greatest terror, I broached to my kids the idea of a backpacking trip to a lake. That got me nothing but hems, haws, and exaggerated eye rolling. “What if we drove Sally to the lake instead?” I then suggested. “Do some car camping?” At the mention of[utilizing the Xterra](https://www.hagerty.com/media/great-reads/a-crusty-first-gen-nissan-xterra-takes-man-and-mans-best-friend-off-the-beaten-path/) they had inexplicably named several years ago, I got zero pushback. They were in. So began the search for a lake that required a truck to get there.


Badger Lake sits at the bottom of a glacial cirque in the middle of the 29,000-acre[Badger Creek Wilderness](https://oregonwild.org/saving-the-big-trees-of-badger-creek-as-wilderness-a-lucky-break/) , just east of Oregon’s Mt. Hood. It was designated as a wilderness area in 1984, and while such places are considered pristine and undeveloped and contain nothing manmade, the 40-acre lake was dammed in the 1950s, and a rugged two-track spur, Forest Road 140, wanders four miles down to it. As a result, the road and the dam exist as a sort of long, skinny thumb hitchhiking their way into the wilderness.


FR 140 curls down into the Badger Creek Wilderness to the lake.


Gaia GPS


Because the best laid plans of mice and men are almost always disrupted by a failure to get your work done early ahead of a holiday weekend so you can get your groceries, load the cooler, pack the rig, and get out of town with plenty of afternoon sunshine, it was 6:30 before we got on the road. Thankfully, here above the 45th parallel, that still gave us a good two-and-a-half hours of light for our three-hour drive. Mine is not the overlander du jour, replete with light bars and spotlights and ditch lights and probably some other lights I’m not aware of, so I didn’t necessarily *want* to navigate the woods in the dark, although secretly I knew it’d be fun and we’d be fine. More concerning was the campground at the lake on July 4 weekend: With just four sites, each on a first-come basis, after a long trek into the forest, I really wanted to make sure we got one.


Off the main highway, then the secondary highway, we followed Forest Road 4890 for a few miles. Just about every forest road in these parts is an old logging road dating back to the 1950s or so, and 4890 is a winding, climbing, paved single-lane I’ve driven several times over the years. And it always, *always* , leads me to imagine taking it on as a tarmac stage at 100 mph in a Martini-liveried Lancia—until my inevitable spectacular crash.


We arrived at an intersection, and there we took a sharp left onto the unpaved 4891, a road I’ve never driven, and were immediately confronted by two signs: One told us we had 11 miles to Badger Lake, while another, large and bright yellow read: TRAILERS AND BUSES PROHIBITED AT BADGER LAKE ROAD 48601140.


[Stefan Lombard](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/stefan-lombard/)


I aired down my tires to 17 psi, and so began the steady, slow-rolling, carom from rock to rock up the trail. North, roughly, with the sun on our left and still hot through the firs. It was about 8 p.m. and we were doing great; Google Maps told me we’d be at the lake in an hour…


And we would have, too, if it weren’t for that meddling F-350!


We were halfway down the spur road to the lake when we came upon it, just two miles to go and still in daylight. The Nissan’s high clearance and squishy, grippy Falken Wildpeaks had rendered 4WD unnecessary, and the constant bucking, the squeaks and groans, and the oh-well! hard knocks off the bump stops elicited nothing but whoops and giggles as we’d motored along. We’ve had some great adventures in this machine over the years, and all of the above, giggles included, are a familiar, welcome accompaniment.


But then there it was, all 21 feet and 7.3 diesel-y liters of an OBS crew cab long bed. The only thing that could have made it any bigger, in fact, would have been a dually rear end. Or a trailer.


Dead slow ahead, hard to port…


[Stefan Lombard](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/stefan-lombard/)


While it’s no Wrangler, a first-generation[Xterra is a nimble creature](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/nissan-dealers-previewed-the-new-xterra-and-initial-reports-are-promising/) , and its 35-foot turning radius is the kind of thing a fella can be proud of. By which I simply mean: It is easily maneuvered down tight, twisty, rutted, two-tracks that sometimes zig and zag sharply between the sawed ends of large fallen trees, and you’re rarely at risk of holding people up. Something four-thousandths of a mile long, with a 60-foot turning radius, on the other hand, is another creature entirely. This was very much a one-way road, with no place for them to pull off and no place for us to get around, so there we were. Resigned to idle along for the duration at a sub-2-mph pace, I put the truck into 4Lo, which is as close to Level 1 autonomy as Sally the Xterra gets.


Google Maps then got very confused, constantly trying to reconfigure our time to destination and effectively scratching its head while wondering how we possibly hadn’t arrived yet. Darkness fell in the forest. Real darkness, the nighttime kind. I’d had my headlights on the whole day but now switched them off so as not to annoy the people annoying me. And it was fine, of course—we would get there.


Then, in a literal flash, Johnny Brodude arrived on the scene, hot on my six in a TRD Tacoma bedazzled with all the lights I lacked, possibly all the lights in the known universe. Certainly enough lights shining with enough impatient intensity to elicit a firm middle finger from the guy commanding the carrier in front of me.


“Great,” said my oldest kid. “Just great.”


Finally, though, after being so agonizingly close for so long, we all arrived at the lake. The Ford and I pulled into a small gravel lot. The Tacoma raced around us and off down a little road that fronted the dam. We turned around in the lot and backtracked to another road I’d sort of made out on the way in, and there, before us in the dark, was an empty campsite.


Which was great, because while I’d been eyeing potential places we might retreat to in case the place was already full, I really didn’t want to drive back up that road to one. This was a lake trip, dammit.

We read, we napped, we looked at the lake but never ventured in.


We then proceeded to hang out in camp just about all weekend. Turns out none of us had a great urge to swim, and we had tiny Badger Creek flowing past our site, which was perfect for cooling off. Instead we read, played games, talked, napped, stuffed our faces. It was perfect.


On Saturday the 4th, my friend Collin joined us with his giant lumbering goldendoodle, Abby. His last two cars have been a leased Honda Accord and a leased Hyundai Ioniq 6, but late last year he got himself a Chevy Colorado ZR2.


“Are you leasing this thing, too?” I asked him.


“Bought it,” he said. “Because I knew I wanted to do this kind of stuff.”

For our purposes on the 4th, “the stuff” included all of us piling into the Chevy for a twilight drive up to a vantage point I’d been eyeballing on the map, with the goal of watching the sunset, making dinner, then looking for silent fireworks in the distance.


[The ride in the ZR2](https://www.hagerty.com/media/new-car-reviews/first-drive-350-off-road-miles-in-the-2023-chevrolet-colorado-zr2/) was noticeably less groany and squeaky, of course, the truck’s luxe Multimatic DSSV dampers essentially soaking up everything that would pitch the Xterra’s ancient leaf setup this way and that. I rode shotgun with equal parts envy and disgust at the ease of it all. On the way, the white Chevy got a liberal dose of squealing trail stripes down both sides, not exactly something you’d want to deal with on your lease return.


The bombs bursting in air… Or the sun setting behind Mt. Hood, anyway, which was glorious in its own way.

The evening proved everything we’d hoped it would be, though certainly the fireworks of Madras, Redmond, and Bend—50, 75, and 90 miles from us as the crow flies—were less spectacular than I imagined they might be. Far less spectacular than my Lancia shunt, that’s for sure. But the rest of it—clear views not just of Mt. Hood, but of Mounts Adams and Rainier to the north, and of Jefferson, Three-Fingered Jack, Three Sisters, Broken Top, and Bachelor stretching out far to the south—that was unforgettable. Dehydrated chicken and mashed potatoes? Also not bad.


In the morning we broke camp in no great hurry, and some part of me jumped for joy when the Xterra started right up. It’s never given me any reason to suspect it wouldn’t (except when the starter went out last year, which, OK, fine), but who among us doesn’t share that thought ( *C’mon, baby!),* particularly when you’re far from home?


Collin had come in from a different direction than we had, and once out of the wilderness area we followed him. This put us onto Forest Road 3550, which, unbeknownst to me, turned out to be Bennett Pass Road, a one-lane relic of 1930s CCC work that rides atop a ridge at 5000 feet and includes a section known locally as the “Terrible Traverse” for its wintertime susceptibility to avalanches.

When we reached the highway, we parted ways. On the drive home, I ran the Xterra’s A/C for the first time, and it even sort of worked.


My love for this truck comes and goes as my wandering eyes find cheap chunks of steel I might replace it with. I have no need to replace it, but there’s always that classifieds itch. Mostly, it sits in my driveway, where it gets stellar fuel economy, until it’s time for an adventure, when it gets terrible fuel economy. Still, I can’t help being grateful for it. Our holiday excursion was a wonderful reminder of what it does and where it can take us.
