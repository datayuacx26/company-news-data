# Return to Sender: The Grumman LLV Has Finally Reached the End of Its Mail Delivery Days

The Grumman LLV—for Long Life Vehicle—mail truck is proof that sometimes federal bureaucrats get it right. The aluminum-bodied lump just completed four decades as the principal mail carrier for the United States Postal Service.


The history of mail delivery is, of course, inextricably linked with the history of American transportation. Legal documents and love letters were once transported via Pony Express and horse-drawn carriages, but by 1906, the USPS began using gasoline-powered vehicles. With the advent of Parcel Post in 1913, the need for commodious, motor-driven mail vehicles was ever more acute, as Americans a century ago liked to ship and receive stuff just as much as we do today. Over the ensuing decades, the postal service fleet reflected the times, with the Ford Model T making way in the postwar period for surplus military Willys Jeeps. In the early 1960s, Studebaker collaborated with Pennsylvania manufacturer Met-Pro to develop a purpose-built mail wagon known as the Zip Van to promote the newly introduced ZIP codes.


The Zip Van was short-lived but pointed the way toward a solution to an ongoing problem: The postal service used too many different mail carrier vehicles, from too many manufacturers, and it was a huge logistical challenge to stock parts and train mechanics. By 1984, the USPS solicited bids for a purpose-built vehicle rather than continuing to adapt existing vehicles. After a series of brutal endurance tests, the LLV, the product of a joint venture between military contractor Grumman and General Motors, was the winner. Its garage-style rear door, sliding side door, and right-hand-drive setup seemingly were inspired by the Zip Van’s form factor from two decades earlier.
