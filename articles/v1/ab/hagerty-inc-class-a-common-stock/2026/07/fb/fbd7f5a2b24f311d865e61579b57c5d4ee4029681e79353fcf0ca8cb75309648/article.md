# Never Stop Driving #193: Two Decades of the Amazing Bugatti W-16

The crazy stopped in France this past week when the last Bugatti Mistral rolled off the Molsheim production line, marking the end of the brand’s wondrous W-16 engine. It’s hard to believe Bugatti unveiled the W-16 more than 20 years ago. Could the first 1000-hp production engine really have been around for that long? Indeed, it has been.


Bugatti


The engine started with a proverbial[back-of-an-envelope sketch](https://newsroom.bugatti.com/en/press-releases/15-years-of-bugatti-veyron-how-it-all-began) penned by Ferdinand Piëch in 1997. Piëch, the all-powerful chief of the Volkswagen Group, was traveling on a Japanese train when, in conversation with VW’s head of powertrain development, he imagined three VW VR6 engines on a common crankshaft, creating an 18-cylinder engine. In an example of the tail wagging the dog, the following year Piëch secured the rights to the Bugatti brand so he’d have a fitting home for his outlandish engine. Can you imagine how awesome it would be to dream up a crazy engine and then have the wherewithal to make it a reality? So cool.


Piëch, who died in 2019, was a special human, in both good and bad ways. In the automotive world, he was hugely impactful, spearheading the Le Mans-winning Porsche 917 and the[Audi Quattro all-wheel-drive system.](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/driving-the-group-b-stars-that-define-rallys-wildest-era/) Later, he brought a roster of historic luxury brands under the VW umbrella, including Bentley, Lamborghini, and, of course, Bugatti, which was founded by Ettore Bugatti in 1909 and became famous for building exquisite racing and luxury cars in the prewar era. By the time Piëch’s Bugatti Veyron 16.4 entered production in 2005, its engine was a W-16 configuration—basically two narrow-angle V-8s on a common crankshaft—which was marginally less crazy than an eighteen-cylinder engine but insane, nonetheless. With the help of four turbochargers, it produced a cool 1001 hp.


Bugatti


Horsepower, however, is easy. VW and Piëch’s real trick was how they made the W-16 so civilized. I drove Veyrons a few times and found them a little boring until there was room to uncork the W-16. Even then, the wooshing and whirring of the turbo wastegates behind my head didn’t equal the tingly feelings I’d experienced in, say, the V-12 Ferrari Enzo of the same era. That’s how sedate the German engineers had made the Veyron. The Ferrari, however, couldn’t touch the Veyron’s 253-mph top speed. The Bugatti had a great sense of theater, too: To increase the top speed from 230 mph to the full beans, you inserted a key into a hole on the floor near the driver’s seat, which prompted the car to lower itself and tuck away any exterior components that would create drag. (To be clear, the key insertion is done while the car is at rest, in anticipation of a top-speed run.) Csaba Csere, my old boss at *Car and Driver,*[reported](https://www.caranddriver.com/reviews/a18201251/2006-bugatti-veyron-164-road-test-review/) that the Veyron was so incredibly stable, he could have placed a cell-phone call at 185 mph.

We’re talking about a kiddie fantasy car for rich adults that Piëch singlehandedly willed to reality. A brilliant engineer, he was also known to be an intimidating dictator, particularly during his tenure as CEO from 1993 to 2002. Piëch was no longer the head of VW in 2015, when the company was caught cheating on emissions tests, but he had remained a powerful fixture as chairman of the supervisory board. I wondered if he had established a culture in which results mattered more than following the law, so I called Bob Lutz, the seasoned automotive executive, for his take. “It’s what I call a reign of terror,” he[wrote in a column](https://www.roadandtrack.com/car-culture/a27197/bob-lutz-vw-diesel-fiasco/) for *Road & Track,* which I was editing at the time, “and a culture where performance was driven by fear and intimidation.”


When VW engineers couldn’t find a way to make the company’s diesel engines pass U.S. emissions laws without an expensive urea-injection system or sacrificing performance, perhaps they were afraid to admit the technical dead end, Lutz theorized. So, they found a scheme to circumvent the tests, uncovered only after VW had been selling cheater cars for nearly a decade. It seems that Piëch didn’t follow what I think is the first rule of leadership: Don’t be an A-hole. By the way, I ran into Lutz a few months ago. “Authoritarians are attractive because they get things done,” he told me, ever the sage. “But they come with huge risks.”


In 2020, *Reuters* reported that the so-called Dieselgate scandal[cost VW some $30 billion](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/volkswagen-says-diesel-scandal-has-cost-it-313-billion-euros-idUSKBN2141J9/) . Six years later, the company is in crisis mode amid falling sales and high costs. CEO Oliver Blume says[it may have to cut 100,000 jobs.](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/volkswagen-may-cut-50-000-111909985.html?guccounter=1&guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuZ29vZ2xlLmNvbS8&guce_referrer_sig=AQAAAEFdBEtMeD-0WUWs_zS2cmsv_zWzJ7AIL9eruX0oT1QIXg6TQKoP1fjDVvbDiRutsOgEXLxLBvkg6qjqORmniR2j3QEpailV8MLA2agfFWlkVFi6atUsb18pj8Ga4un_lPxmNDr3WDvfwnTxoWR_lN9Loj2zhAP4_O8KrEH-EMpZ) I bet Blume wishes that $30 billion was spent on developing compelling new products rather than cleaning up a mess.


Those unpleasant realities aside, one can’t deny Piëch’s mechanical brains or the scale of what he achieved, especially for those of us who love interesting machinery. Piëch was such a monumental figure in the modern automotive firmament that my colleague Jason Cammisa[devoted an entire film to him.](https://youtu.be/1KKiqibOxoc?si=dXVzNsAcW91-jJ0r)


Bugatti


While the Veyron has never appealed to me—I thought it lacked personality and its baroque styling looked overcooked—it’s quite a final achievement for Piëch. Later Bugatti models with the W-16 engine[passed 300 mph](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/bugatti-hits-300-mph-in-modified-chiron/) and they’re real, drivable cars, not hopped-up maniac machines. That is incredible. VINwiki founder Ed Bolian, who calls himself “the poorest Veyron owner,”[has put some 15,000 miles on a Veyron](https://youtu.be/sJf7FsBC79M?si=VJOIqTqhEQP4h1Nl) he bought used. Piëch’s Bugattis are supremely well engineered and not designed to be garage queens.


Amazingly, Veyrons have never really depreciated like some other ultra-luxury hypercars that don’t wear a Ferrari badge. Even still, five years after the launch of the Veyron’s W-16 successor, the[Chiron](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/driving-the-bugatti-chiron/) , VW decided it didn’t need a halo car like it in the portfolio. In 2021, VW formed a joint venture that handed control of Bugatti to EV startup Rimac and its founder, Mate Rimac.


Alex Sobran


Rimac has equally ambitious powertrain plans for Bugatti. The next model, the Tourbillon, will hit the road with[a V-16 gas engine coupled to an electric hybrid system that](https://newsroom.bugatti.com/press-releases/a-spark-of-genius) promises more than 1500 combined horsepower. The new car will cost some $4.6 million, and production is limited to 250 units.


Piëch’s W-16 will survive for now in some future special Bugattis. Whatever that future is, the engine’s legacy is already established as the driving force behind Bugatti’s ascendancy to the peak of the supercar pyramid.
