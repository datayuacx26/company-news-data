# Lanchester: The Other British Luxury Car Drafted into WWI

As any student of history knows, part of the fun of flipping back through the record of how things actually happened is ruminating on how things might have happened differently. In another universe, Lee Harvey Oswald’s Carcano rifle jams. The *Titanic* avoids the iceberg. A British sniper sees a scrawny Austrian corporal poke his head above a trench and pulls the trigger. In these alternate, imaginary timelines that flowed from those moments is different—perhaps for the better, perhaps for the worse.


Open your history books to the spring of 1917 and the beginning of the Russian Revolution. Tsar Nikolai Alexandrovich Romanov has abdicated the throne, and his family is imprisoned under house arrest. In Moscow is a Royal Naval officer named Oliver Locker-Lampson, whose grandfather was captain and teacher to a young Horatio Nelson, who went on to become Britain’s greatest naval hero when he smashed Napoleon’s fleet during the Battle of the Nile in 1798. Locker-Lampson is in possession of a secret plan devised by British intelligence services to rescue the Romanovs, or at least the Tsar.


Why Locker-Lampson? Though he is a lieutenant commander in the Royal Navy, as commissioned by First Lord of the Admiralty Winston Churchill, the civilian head of the navy, Locker-Lampson has a fighting force on land. His armored-car divisions and machine-gun crews stand ready to perform a daring rescue.


Perhaps because of political pressure brought to bear by the royal family of the United Kingdom, the rescue never happens. A year later, in the summer of 1918, the Bolsheviks killed the Tsar and his family in a basement in Yekaterinburg.


Imperial War Museum


As for Locker-Lampson, he and his men spent the previous four years as a hugely mobile fighting unit[in World War I](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/discover-wwis-only-surviving-cadillac/) (1914–1918), traveling some 53,000 miles over mountains, deserts, and arctic tundra. In sharp contrast to the stagnation of trench warfare in the war’s west theater, these armored cars have journeyed farther than any other WWI force, fighting all the way from above the Arctic Circle into the heat of Turkey and northern Persia. They engaged in action everywhere from Romania to the shadow of Mount Ararat, rumored to be the resting place of Noah’s Ark.


The backbone of the Royal Navy’s British Armoured Car Expeditionary Force (ACEF) was the Lanchester armored car, a vehicle built in nearly the same quantities as the[much better-known armored Rolls-Royce Ghosts](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/irish-army-armored-rolls-royce/) .


## On Orders From Churchill


It’s hard to picture Winston Churchill as a young man. The picture of him in most people’s heads is that famous portrait where he glowers at the camera, the epitome of bulldog determination in the face of overwhelming odds. He seems to have been born old. In 1914, Churchill was just forty, a bumptious First Lord of the Admiralty, anxious that the Royal Navy should cover itself in glory in the war ahead.


Churchill, circa 1916


Public Domain


Picture his meeting with the slightly younger Oliver Stillingford Locker-Lampson in some wood-paneled room, a desk in the middle that weighs as much as a Ford F-150. A Conservative Party member of a respected family, Locker-Lampson had the aforementioned connection to Horatio Nelson and was a grandson of Lord Elgin (as in[The Elgin Marbles](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Elgin-Marbles) ).


Locker-Lampson’s post-WWI politics are something of a rollercoaster ride. A hatred of communism from his wartime service pushed him to approve of the rise of anti-communist fascism in Italy and Germany, at least at first. This sentiment was relatively short-lived, and by 1933, he was fighting to provide refuge and citizenship to Jewish refugees fleeing Nazi persecution. One notable effort of Locker-Lampson’s was securing safety for a refugee near his home in Norfolk, England, after the man was forced to leave Belgium after receiving death threats. That man’s name was Albert Einstein.


Back to Locker-Lampson and Churchill’s discreet meeting, and the understanding that was struck. Being extremely wealthy, the former had the wherewithal to pay for a fleet of armored cars out of his own personal pocket. For Churchill, that was good enough to earn an officer’s commission. The British Armoured Car Expeditionary Force (ACEF) was formed.


Albert Einstein with Commander Oliver Locker-Lempson MP, 1933.


Leslie Cardew/National Science and Media Museum


Here’s another little historical wrinkle that’ll widen the eyes of any readers with Irish roots. Locker-Lampson was a Conservative Unionist politician, and he had connections in Ireland with the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF), a paramilitary organization opposed to home rule in Ireland. A couple of months before WWI kicked off, the UVF had actually been successful in smuggling some 25,000 rifles into Ireland. The source was none other than Imperial Germany. Some of the money to buy these armored Lanchester cars came from UVF funds, and three of them were named after places in the north of Ireland: Londonderry, Mountjoy, and Ulster. Many of the unit’s recruits would also have been loyalist Ulstermen.


As a politician and former journalist, Locker-Lampson knew that it would be no good wading into the quagmire of the Western front and spending his days sitting around or being reassigned to trench warfare. He therefore pushed to be sent to the East, where he would support the Tsar’s Russian forces. These were having a terrible time of it, and their requests for assistance spurred Churchill into one of his largest blunders, the disastrous invasion at Gallipoli in February of 1915.


Imperial War Museum


In the east, however, Locker-Lampson could roam free, far enough from headquarters to be his own man. In December of 1915, his division of 39 armored Lanchesters and supporting crew set sail for Russia. Embarrassingly, the radiators all froze and exploded, and the cars and men had to be shipped back home again. However, by 1916, they were back, ready to go to war.


## Lanchester, an Equal to Rolls-Royce


At this point, it’s worth a brief look at Lanchester, a car company only a dedicated Anglophile may be aware of. The company can claim to have produced the first car fully engineered and built in Britain, in 1886. (Lanchester is still technically in existence as a subsidiary of Tata, although nothing is actually built under the name.)


This pioneering spirit set Lanchester up for further advances, including the development of the very first disc-type brakes in 1902. By 1913, the company offered a fairly conventional luxury touring car, fitted with a 5.5-liter inline-six good for just under 40 hp. (The Ford Model T, for context, had 22 hp.) This output made the Lanchester very competitive with the contemporary Silver Ghost from Rolls-Royce, and Lanchester’s build quality was praised at the time.


Imperial War Museum


In armored form, both the Ghost and the Lanchester look very similar, as[the turret](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/preston-tuckers-ingenious-5-ton-tiger-tank-was-too-fast/) was standardized between the two cars. It’s up front that the difference is noticeable, with the Lanchester having sloping front armor. This makes it look very much like Roberto, the knife-wielding robot from *Futurama* . The Lanchester was certainly a pretty dangerous and alarming foe if you were an enemy combatant with nothing more than a bolt-action rifle.


Imperial War Museum


Thanks to a top speed of as much as 50 mph, the Lanchester armored cars were fearsome strike weapons. When supporting infantry, they were essentially rolling bunkers. However, they weren’t particularly effective in helping Russian forces repel attacks from troops allied with the Imperial German forces, because Russian morale had begun to fray badly.


Still, the sight of a Lanchester roaring into battle, its Vickers .303 belching flame and lead, Cossack cavalry riding close behind, was certainly impressive. The tales of the squadron’s exploits were maximized by Locker-Lampson, who could spin a good yarn, and they provided a welcome distraction from the meat-grinder tales from the Western front. It helped when Germany’s Kaiser put a reported bounty of 20,000 Marks on Locker-Lampson’s head.


Imperial War Museum


Eventually, the Russian Revolution put an end to things. Despite negotiations with the new administration that provided resupply, the squadron often found itself covering the retreat of demoralized Russian troops. Only a third of the Lanchesters survived. As a final insult, revolutionary Russian forces eventually confiscated the last of the cars and equipment, and the British went back to England without their material. Locker-Lampson never forgave the betrayal.

Many of the men Locker-Lampson had led were snapped up by other armored regiments, eager to make the most of their experience. Many ended up in the amusingly named Dunsterforce, a small and secretive team in contemporary Persia that trained local irregular troops and fought the Ottoman Empire, much as Lawrence of Arabia had done.


Locker-Lampson ended his career in politics in 1945, having spent the 1930s as one of the few supporters of Churchill. He was active in opposing appeasement of German and Italian aggression leading up to the war, and actively campaigned against British fascists like Walter Mosley and managed to get raging antisemite Arnold Leese locked up in 1936.


Imperial War Museum


Reliable, fast, and in some aspects a better fighting platform than the Rolls-Royce, the armored Lanchester found itself part of a story that was popular with audiences at the time, but has long since faded. A second generation of the vehicles was built after WWI, but proved too bulky to be effective. Lanchester faded with the Great Depression.


Time has not entirely forgotten the tale of Locker-Lampson and his adventuring Lanchesters, but they are nowhere near as well-known as the exploits of the armored Rolls-Royce Ghosts. Perhaps, had history taken a different turn that saw him actually rescue the Romanovs, the names of both would be better known.
