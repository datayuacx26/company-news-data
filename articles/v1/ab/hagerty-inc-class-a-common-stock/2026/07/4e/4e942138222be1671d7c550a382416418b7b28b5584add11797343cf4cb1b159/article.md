# 1968–70 Plymouth Road Runners Aren’t Walking Away with Younger Buyers Just Yet

In the horsepower heyday of the late ’60s, there was a muscle car for every buyer. The archetypal Pontiac GTO could be had in several iterations from sinister to ostentatious. Chevrolet’s Chevelle came with brawny big-block power in an understated wrapping. Buick and Olds had GS and 4-4-2 offerings with their own flavor of V-8s. Charger offered unique, Coke-bottle bodywork to go with its all-big-block engine lineup. Rambler’s conservative lines could be transformed by patriotic red, white, and blue livery in the SC/Rambler. Ford’s sinewy Torino came with contrasting graphics and blacked-out hoods complete with gaping scoops. At Plymouth, meanwhile, buyers could opt for a more serious and luxurious GTX or the cheerful and chirpy Road Runner ( *Acceleratti Incredibilus)* .


Plymouth


Plymouth split its completely redesigned 1968 B-body (shared with the Dodge Coroner and Charger) lineup into five columns: GTX, Sport Satellite, Satellite, Road Runner, and Belvedere. While the GTX and Road Runner shared sheet metal—including the side-scoop hood—the Road Runner offered a lower cost of entry with a hardtop-style roof that gave the front door frameless glass but included a B-pillar and tilt-out quarter glass. A hardtop, identical to the GTX with roll-down quarter glass, was a later addition to the lineup. Both of the sporty Plymouth B-body muscle cars could be optioned with the mammoth 426 Hemi with twin four-barrel carburetors.


GTX came standard with a 375hp 440. The playful, more affordable Road Runner, marketed as “Private property of the young and aware,” came standard with a 335hp 383 big-block that it didn’t share with any other Mopar muscle car. Its 5-hp bump over the Super Commando 383 found in other Mopars was thanks to the camshaft from the high-performance 440. Speaking of the 440, it wasn’t initially available in the Road Runner.

Of course, several key components came with the Road Runner that just weren’t found in any other Mopar muscle car. Its horn, labeled with a decal noting “Voice of Road Runner,” was emblazoned with the Warner Brothers cartoon of the same name and its signature goofy grin. It gave the car its signature sound and was eventually finished in purple to match the cartoon bird’s wings and crest. There were also cartoon Road Runners on the doors, decklid, and instrument panel. Eventually, the speedy Southwesterner’s nemesis, Wile E. Coyote ( *Carnivorous Vulgaris* ), could also be found on one of our favorite air cleaner decals of all time.


The 1968 Road Runner used a flat black horn. The later models embraced the cartoon bird’s color palette and went with purple.

## 1969


1969 brought the Road Runner an updated look with freshened sheet metal. Up front, the basic quad-headlight design remained, but with new bezels and a similar grille shape, now divided into fewer rectangles. Along with the rest of its Mopar siblings, Road Runner got rectangular side marker lights, replacing the small, circular lights that Mopars wore in 1968. At the back of the car, square, concave taillights nestled into the quarter panels. It was also the first time that Road Runner was offered as a convertible. Partway through the model year, a new engine option was added: the 390hp, 3×2 version of the 440.

## 1970


More significant design changes came in 1970, with another set of new fenders and a racetrack grille up front that previewed the chrome bumper coming for the fuselage-body redesign of 1971 B-bodies. A new bumper carried round marker lights. The quarter panels got non-functional scoops, and the rear fascia got a completely new look with horizontal stripes for taillights.

The Road Runner soldiered on in various forms until 1980, when it was a package on the compact F-body Volaré platform. Today, the first generation 1968–70 Road Runners are established classic Mopar muscle cars with a solid fan base and support.


The most affordable iteration of the 1968–70 Plymouth Road Runner is the 383-powered 1968 coupe with a #2 (excellent) value of $55,300. The hardtop carries an 8.5% premium. Naturally, convertibles also demand a premium. No matter the body style, any 1968–70 Road Runner is worth at least double when equipped with a 426 Hemi when compared to the base 383 V-8. With fewer than 11,000 produced for general consumption and far fewer than that left, the street Hemi remains a big draw. Other value differentiators include transmissions (four-speeds can command a 10% premium) and paint. In the vintage Mopar world, colors can make an astonishing difference in both value and salability.


[Brandan Gillogly](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/brandan-gillogly/)


Hagerty Senior Auction Editor Andrew Newton took a look at the current state of the Plymouth Roadrunner market and pulled out some interesting numbers. “These were hit hard by the late 2000s muscle car bust,” says Newton. “From January 2007 to January 2010, average #2 value dropped 29%. Prices have rebounded quite a bit, as Road Runner’s average #2 value is up 27% over the last 10 years, including an 18% increase in just the last five years. Unfortunately, Newton also noted that inflation over the last five years is more than 20%, keeping those values gains in check. Certain variations of Road Runner also haven’t even recovered from their pre-recession highs, without accounting for inflation.


Looking at the collector market as a whole, baby boomers make up 34%, Gen X 31%, millennials 20%, and Gen Z 10%. We’re not surprised to find that insurance quotes for ’60s and ‘70s muscle cars favor older buyers, but a lot more younger buyers are picking up contemporary Chargers and Chevelles. Gen X interest in 1968–70 Chargers is 30% higher than Road Runner, and millennial interest in 1968–72 Chevelle is 50% higher. You can credit at least a bit of that to the pop culture staying power of both of those models for keeping them relevant, and pushing demand a bit higher. Meanwhile, Plymouth is a defunct brand and has been for 25 years, and the Road Runner’s fairly high price also puts it out of reach for many young enthusiasts.


We haven’t missed the irony that it’s the older buyers who remain more interested in a car marketed by a Saturday morning cartoon character. Still, it’s hard to bet against the 1968–70 Road Runner. There are enough subtle variations in each production year for a collector to find a favorite look, and the hourglass body lines built into each one give it classic Mopar muscle style. Standard big-block V-8 power under the hood is, naturally, another big draw. As with other niches in the collector market, like sports cars or vintage 4x4s, a slightly less popular model like the Road Runner could lag behind many of the more popular models in its category until enough buyers realize what they’ve been overlooking.
