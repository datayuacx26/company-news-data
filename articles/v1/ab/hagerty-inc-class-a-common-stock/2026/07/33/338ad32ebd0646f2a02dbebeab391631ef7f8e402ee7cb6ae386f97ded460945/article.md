# High Fidelity: The Story of House Yamaha

At 9000 rpm, the 1LR-GUE V-10 of the Lexus LFA produces a howl that vibrates your soul. Forget bone conduction; this feels like the plates of your skull achieving valve float, a blend of big displacement bass and golden-era F1 scream. If you had one jerry can of high-octane fuel left for the rest of your life, you could do worse than burn it here, in one of the best-sounding automotive altars of them all. But of course the LFA sounds like this. A musical instrument company taught it to sing.


Never a full-on production-car company, Yamaha has nonetheless had a hand in some of the greatest cars to ever come out of Japan (and a pretty interesting Detroit-built sedan, too). As the old internet meme goes, do you need a tuba, the greatest blue-chip Japanese collectible car, and also an outboard motor? Says Yamaha: Have we got good news for you! For a time, the company even had a swimming pool division.

You can still find Yamaha’s musical roots in the crossed trio of tuning forks on the badge of one of its motorcycles or Wolverine side-by-sides or Waverunner jetskis. Or by buying one of its pianos. It’s more than a little bonkers that you can either play “Chopsticks” on one example of a company’s products, or twist the throttle of the barely streetable road-going MotoGP-inspired YZF-R1M and hear its crossplane-crankshaft four-cylinder scream.

Or drive your Toyota MR2 or Supra on a sunny Sunday morning, and delight in the way those engines love to rev. There’s a Yamaha touch to both of them. And to find out how we got here, we must begin in the most unlikely of places, with a shabby samurai doggedly hauling a reed organ across a changing landscape, only to be told that his workmanship is useless.


## The Last Shōgun


Torakusu Yamaha, the company’s founder, was born in 1851, before Commodore Matthew Perry’s black warships arrived in Edo Harbor to force an end to Japan’s closed borders. While not high in rank, his father was a samurai with the unusual job of being an astronomer for the Kishu clan. While a fuller look at Japanese history is beyond the purview of the story of how cars like the LFA came to be, it’s worth a quick look at what it meant to be one of the last samurai at the dawn of the Meiji Restoration.


At the time when Yamaha was born, Japan was still a feudal society, with the emperor and nobility at the top and the samurai class pulling the strings. While there were warriors and military minds among the samurai, a long peace meant that the title was more akin to a noble rank in the U.K. today. Their swords were very beautifully crafted, traditions were adhered to with great reverence, but symbolism was far more common than some manga-imagined duel.


Wiki Commons


If the Western view of a samurai is broad-brushed as some prideful, easily offended caricature obsessed with honor, then Torakusu Yamaha was the exact opposite of this. Instead, he forged humility and curiosity into an alloy of success.


The science of astronomy progressed fairly rapidly in Japan during the 1800s, changing from a near-copy of Chinese learning to adopting Western technologies like telescopes. Growing up surrounded by knowledge of the stars gave Yamaha an interest in machines and technology. As the traditional feudal system pathways evaporated, and Japan’s last shōgun resigned in 1867, the young Yamaha began looking for a more modern profession. Traveling to Nagasaki, he began apprenticing with an English watchmaker.


Nagasaki, circa 1865


Public Domain/Antoon Bauduin


The move was not an overnight success. By his mid-thirties, Yamaha was living in Hamamatsu, the home city of Japan’s first shōgun, the one fictionally portrayed in FX’s television series. Not a big city by Japanese standards even now, Hamamatsu was small enough that Yamaha had to do a bit of everything to get by. He repaired medical equipment, serviced watches, and even pulled a rickshaw.


There’s something fitting about the broad scope of things his namesake company is involved in today and the variety of work its founder was involved in. And one day, at the local elementary school, an unbelievably expensive Western-made reed organ broke. Who could fix such a thing? Perhaps the local village handyman.


## Back to School


Here’s where Yamaha’s curiosity opened up a new path. Instead of simply repairing the reed organ—it was a case of two broken springs—he began sketching out a blueprint of the instrument’s entire workings, with a view to building his own. Reportedly, this took so long that the principal of the school ended up yelling at him about the delay. Unperturbed, Yamaha took his time.


Model 1 Reed Organ


Yamaha


With a little financial backing from friends, he was able to build his own version of a reed organ, the very first such instrument ever made in Japan. Encouraged, he set out for the long walk over the Hakone mountain range to Tokyo, a distance of 160 miles, carrying his heavy invention.


When he arrived, the president of Tokyo University’s music department granted the weary Yamaha an audience. But on hearing the instrument play, he winced.


Yamaha could actually play a musical instrument, the three-stringed shamisen, a Japanese lute, but none of that experience really translated into Western-style musical theory. The reed organ was wildly out of tune, a discordant cacophony that was as if it were coded in the wrong language. Which, in a way, it was. The kindly music director agreed to let Yamaha stay on at the university to learn a bit about Western music.


It’s important to pause and consider Yamaha’s resilience. Remember, he was basically a skilled handyman, dressed in work clothes, approaching his forties, sitting in a classroom with a bunch of young students just over half his age. Yet, he was there to learn, and learn he did. Fast.


Moving back to Hamamatsu, he founded a company to make reed organs, and within two years they’d built 250 of them. He selected a phoenix holding a tuning fork in its beak as the symbol of the brand. The company was called Nippon Gakki, and 100 years after that first reed organ was built, in 1987, it would take on the name of its founder.


## Okay, Enough With the Reed Organs. Let’s Ride.


Yamaha


Having grown to become Japan’s largest manufacturer of musical instruments, Yamaha found itself in the post-WWII period with excess capacity. Like almost every other Japanese automaker of the period, it started building simple motorcycles, in this case based on a German DKW 125 cc two-stroke bike. Called the YA-1 and nicknamed the *Aka Tombo,* or Red Dragonfly, it was a reliable, extremely pretty little bike.


1955 YA-1


Yamaha


In the postwar rebuilding period, small-displacement motorcycles and mopeds were how most of Japan got around, and Yamaha found an eager market for its products. Soon enough it was entering them in racing, both national and international, showing that it could go toe-to-toe with two-wheeled machines from Suzuki or Honda.


Motorcycles on a production line at the Yamaha Motor Company factory in Hamamatsu, Shizuoka Prefecture, Japan, circa 1965.


Keystone/Hulton Archive/Getty Images


The main driving force behind Yamaha’s rise in the 1950s to late 1970s was company president Genichi Kawakami. Four years old when Torakusu Yamaha died, Kawakami followed his father into the presidency of what was then Nippon Gakki and began broadening the company’s focus.


Kawakami was a work-hard, play-hard type of fellow and an enthusiastic boater. Under his direction, the company would begin producing the outboard motors it still builds today, and it also built fiberglass racing hulls. However, at least as important was his dedication to investing profits into research and development efforts, which led to the creation of a technical school and a materials and techniques laboratory in Hamamatsu.


Yamaha outboard engines advanced big time with electronic system upgrades in the mid-’80s.


Getty Images


Here is where Toyota comes into the picture. In the 1960s, Toyota already knew how to build cars, but it didn’t have the expertise to build something particularly special. It wanted a flagship, and Yamaha had the skills to do it.


Look over a Toyota 2000GT today, and you can see all parts of Yamaha’s diversified businesses on display. In the interior, the piano-making branch provided the expertise in the wood-trimmed cabin. For the bodywork, the ability to shape boat hulls paid dividends. Under the hood, a 2.0-liter inline-six with Toyota bones was enhanced with a double-overhead cam head using Yamaha’s understanding of airflow in motorcycle engines.


Toyota


The production 2000GT was built in Yamaha’s Iwata factory, which still builds most of the company’s motorcycles today. If you ride a modern or vintage Yamaha, there’s a strong chance it was made in the same facility as Japan’s premier collector car.


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


Arguably, the amount of Yamaha influence in the car and the fact that all were hand-made by the company makes the 2000GT not really a Toyota after all. Certainly, its great cost and rarity are not usual Toyota characteristics. However, when it came to greatness at a broader scale, Yamaha would influence much more accessible Toyota products.


Take the mid-engined MR2 of any generation, the scrappy rear-wheel-drive Corolla GT-S, or even the Celica All-Trac. All of them feature revvy four-cylinder power with headwork input from Yamaha. You might not be able to park a 2000GT in your garage, but the ripples of that 1960s partnership have spread out over decades of fun-to-drive Toyota products.


## For the Everyman, and for No One


Toyota is Yamaha’s most famous partner, but it is not the only one. Especially for the Ford fans in the audience, there’s one car that stands out as the company’s best work in turning an everyday commuter into something properly special.


Ford


That would be the Ford Taurus SHO. Featuring probably the best-looking intake manifold ever fitted to a mainstream car, the first-generation SHO wasn’t quite the equal of a contemporary BMW M5, but it offered way more bang for the buck than you’d expect. A four-door sedan that could give a V-8 Mustang trouble? People loved it.


You can[read about the development of the SHO’s 220-hp 3.0-liter V-6 here](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/ford-taurus-sho-history/) , and even see some rare period photos of the first prototype car with the two engineers who built it. It’s a great artifact of history, as Ford’s lawyers would have hit the roof if they’d known photographs of a secret prototype were sitting at home in somebody’s family picture album.


Another of Yamaha’s notable partnerships was with Volvo, on the B8444S, a 4.4-liter V-8 that produced 311 hp when it was launched in 2005. Since the engine was designed by Volvo and built by Yamaha, it’s a little less special than it might be, especially since its first appearance was in the family-friendly XC90.


Wiki Commons


However, when uncorked properly, as it was during Australian touring car racing, this V-8 is a monster. For a very small group of owners, it also found a new home in a proper mid-engined supercar in the form of the[Noble M600](https://www.hagerty.com/media/lists/9-out-there-supercars-of-the-21st-century/) . A British-built bruiser, the M600 uses twin turbocharging and stronger internals to pump out more than double the factory Volvo power from this Yamaha-made V-8.


The last stand for a proper Yamaha-tuned eight-cylinder found under the hood of the 2026 Lexus IS500 and LC500. A 5.0-liter V-8 with 472 hp, it’s one of the last naturally aspirated V-8s on the market, particularly in a sport sedan. When it’s gone, it’s gone. Perhaps its best application was in the criminally overlooked GS-F.


The closest look at what Yamaha would have done had it built an entire car with its own badge is one of the craziest might-have-been cars to ever reach the prototype phase. Starting in 1989, Yamaha supplied several V-12 F1 engines to teams like Zakspeed and Arrows. Not particularly competitive, the 3.5-liter V-12 did manage to score a podium finish towards the end of its run. But Japan was at the peak of its bubble economy optimism in the early 1990s, so Yamaha tried to build a road car around its halo engine.

Today, Yamaha can point to the 2000GT and the LFA as high-water marks of its engineering. The motor division of the company celebrated its 70th anniversary last year, and still offers a wide array of products. Yamaha products have won races and charmed fans. Somewhat bizarrely, the company can also claim victory in the Olympics, as it at one time had an archery supply division whose products won several gold medals.


It seems odd to think that a supercar and a piano could have much in common, but there is an overlap. For each to offer the best experience possible, they must be tuned by an expert. At Yamaha, home of the three tuning fork badge, that’s exactly what they know how to do.
