# Baseball, Hot Dogs, Apple Pie, and…

When I learned that we’d be devoting a whole digital issue of the magazine, for[Hagerty Drivers Club](https://www.hagerty.com/drivers-club/marketing) members, entirely to American cars in recognition of the USA’s 250th anniversary, I was reminded of a catchy 1970s jingle that many car fans over 55 can probably still sing to this day: “We love baseball, hot dogs, apple pie, and Chevrolet!”


If you’re not familiar,[here’s a link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9s7V8xpiUrs) . Fair warning—it’s a real earworm, even if it hasn’t exactly aged gracefully. Baseball endures, but hot dogs have probably lost some appeal now that we’re all a bit more health-conscious. And apple pie? I’ve always preferred pumpkin, but that’s just me.


The Chevrolet part still resonates, though. Who doesn’t love Chevrolet, Ford, or any of the other great carmakers that have been such a huge part of American life since the Model T rolled out in 1908? Cars remain one of the few things most of us agree on. We love buying them, working on them, talking about them, and, most of all, driving them.


That passion defines us. We’re a car-mad, driving-obsessed, curve-hugging nation, unlike any other in the world. Yes, many countries build automobiles, but none of them have the car culture we do. America itself is a land custom-made for driving, which is why I’ve always been fond of Henry Ford’s remark that he built inexpensive cars so people could “enjoy with \[their\] family the blessing of hours of pleasure in God’s great open spaces.”


In that promise, Ford gave shape to an idea of American mobility. By the time he devised the assembly line that spit out roughly 15 million Model Ts, that idea had changed life in America. Suddenly, distance wasn’t nearly the barrier it once was. Horizons beckoned. Limits fell away. Possibilities expanded.


Of course, Ford wasn’t alone—luminaries like Billy Durant, Ransom Eli Olds, Walter P. Chrysler, and others helped form the foundation of not only the automobile industry but also the American obsession with cars. Today, their legacy lives on in a new breed of leaders—Elon Musk at Tesla; Jim Farley, who is rebuilding Ford around car passion and has made “no boring cars” his mantra; and Mary Barra and Mark Reuss, who greenlighted a 1064-horsepower Corvette, brought Cadillac into F1, and are set to deliver GM’s sixth-generation small-block V-8 in 2027.


Some say the age of the car is waning. That’s nonsense. The revolution that began more than a century ago isn’t ending—it’s shifting gears, finding new rhythm in new hands. A younger generation is claiming the machines they love, from supercars and hypercars to older Broncos, SUVs, and the icons of Japanese performance.


Which brings me back to today’s *Hagerty Drivers Club* magazine, which is not only a tribute to American cars and innovators, it’s also our first-ever digital issue. Print editions are not going away—members will still get four gorgeous hold-in-your-hands paper-and-ink issues each year, along with two digital. Why digital? Because we have so much fantastic automotive video and web content we want to share with you.


The beat carries on. It always has. Each generation gathers its favorites then passes them forward like stories worth retelling. And with every passing year, the world of cars only widens—more to discover, more to love—so that, in every moment, we are living at peak car. This isn’t a passion that fades. It’s one that replenishes itself, again and again.


Case in point:[this edition of the Barn Find Hunter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kKPaDhH_XiE) , which is one of my favorite videos for[personal reasons](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/finding-a-sleeping-bear-dunes-f-100-took-me-back-to-my-childhood/) . In it, host Tom Cotter and team struggle to resuscitate a long-dormant 1966 Ford F-100 pickup that had been adapted to carry tourists through the massive sand dunes near my home in Northern Michigan. My dad drove one for a summer, and it meant the world to him. We searched nationwide for years, only to finally find one within 40 miles of where I live. We fixed it up and now show it off in local parades as a piece of Americana.


People love it because, of course, cars, trucks, and Americans go together like… well, you know.
