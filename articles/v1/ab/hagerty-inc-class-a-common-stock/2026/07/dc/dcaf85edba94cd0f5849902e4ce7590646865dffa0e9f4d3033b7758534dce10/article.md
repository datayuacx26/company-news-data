# Kinardi Line #7: Adorn Thy Toolbox

I’m a sucker for stickers. Always have been. Big ones, tall ones, small ones, but especially *dumb* ones. If there’s glib wording on one side and sticky stuff on the other, odds are I’ll slap it across the surface of my laptops, folders, binders, desk drawers, and car doors.


And especially on my toolbox.


I hadn’t thought much of this habit until last weekend. I stood there, stooped at my tool chest, peeling the waxy plastic cover off yet another[Flyin’ Miata](https://www.hagerty.com/media/people/colorado-is-where-miatas-go-to-fly/) sticker. They come packed into every order, big or small. Always a cartoon Miata, but usually a different one from the order before.


In time, these stickers have become[proof of a reluctant commitment to my $10 Miata](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/kinardi-line/kinardi-line-2-the-10-miata-ill-never-shake/) ; they also speak to Flyin’s ability to transform routine parts orders into thousand-buck bonfires that repeat themselves with alarming ease and frequency.


I found the right sliver of real estate and pressed the sticker firmly onto the side of the toolbox, swiping from the center to the edges so no bubbles were caught in the vinyl.


Stay tuned for the $200 clutch disc that became an $850 parts binge. At least I got another sticker.


Kyle Kinard


As I stood back to admire my handiwork, I wondered at this particular proclivity. “[Gnōthi seauton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Know_thyself) ,” I mused. “Whatever the heck that means.”


Years ago, I slapped the stickers on in haphazard fashion. Every parts-order prize found its rightful place until the front and sides of my toolbox were stacked with vinyl layers like sedimentary rock.


But one weekend a few years back—when I’d finally moved into my new garage and a clean slate felt apropos—I took to the toolbox with a razor blade. After an hour or two I’d scraped off nearly every last sticker, but couldn’t bring myself to remove them all. The ones I’d grown *really* attached to remained, which it turns out, were a surprisingly good few.


With a somewhat empty canvas left to fill now, I’m a bit less slap-happy with my stickers than before. The stickers that, uh, stick around, tend to do so for a reason. Usually that reason is a person.


At first glance, your favorite pony car shifter brand. At second glance, an affirmation that life is pain.


Kyle Kinard


Hagerty contributor Zach Bowman handed me this one from behind the desk of his office-slash-shop in Knoxville, where I happen to know there’s always a bottle of Tennessee whiskey in the bottom drawer.


I envy his work and fear his redfaced quickshot temper (the first one I enjoy up close; the second is best witnessed from a great distance). You’ll have enjoyed his words[on this site](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/of-miles-and-men-father-son-and-1700-miles-in-a-71-ford-pickup/) , I’m certain, but Zach’s work—which leans heavy into the poetic fragility and tragic beauty that fills our lives—doesn’t give a complete sense of the man.


To me at least, a sticker does that. Zach and I have often bonded over cheap beers, plus the shared belief that life is simply enduring bouts of financial, physical, and existential pain until you die, but getting the hell on with it regardless. “HURTS” sums it up in a tidy little wrapper.


And even if your only relation to this sticker was through a particular brand of performance shifters, the stupid pun might make you grin anyway.


Kyle Kinard


Kyle Kinard


Seriously, go buy Sam’s book.


Another friend. Yet another surfeit of stupid custom stickers. (A coincidence they’re both writers? Probably not). This one’s from Sam Smith, another voice who I’m positive Hagerty readers admire.


The first time I shared a car with Sam, it wasn’t ten minutes before we began riffing in effete British accents, doing impressions of seemingly dignified men who received carnal gratification from bludgeoning rare birds to death. Fast friends.


So when Sam cooked up a run of stickers to coincide with the launch of his book ([GO BUY HIS BOOK](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D2Q3P93Z?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback) ), they earned primo placement on the toolbox. The[Rosche sticker](https://www.hagerty.com/media/people/9-of-the-worlds-greatest-engine-designers/) rips, in particular, if you’re a car dork with Roundels on the mind.


If you don’t know Paul Rosche by name, you know his work. Especially the way it howls.


Kyle Kinard


Then there’s all the rest. A cartoon simulacrum of a W201 Mercedes track special I tested at Lime Rock. A souvenir from a trip to Daytona, for the Rolex 24, where I sat alone in the grandstands with Tom Kristensen to watch the sun rise. Then there’s an Indy Motor Speedway banner, where I went to[watch an honest-to-god F1 car go through its paces](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fNFx8ommEKk) , that jaw-dropping V-8 song cannoning off the empty grandstands.


The places, the people, the moments; they’re rendered here in vinyl and always at arm’s length.


Ask yourself—and be honest—How often do you pull out your wedding album? Once a year, *maybe* ? And how often do you reach for a toolbox drawer? If you’re like me, it’s at least once a day, but more like six or seven or eighty depending on how many bikes or carburetors are currently in pieces on the floor.


Go support the folks at “OHOAT!”, purveyors of weird and wonderful stickers. The place is run by a pair of solid, talented people.


Kyle Kinard


It’s a silly thought, but if[our scars can remind us that the past is real](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eHbNU9WuVgw&list=RDeHbNU9WuVgw&start_radio=1) , so do our stickers. In some way, they bear witness to the places, moments, and people who made us. My memory gets hazier every day. The stickers keep close the moments my grey matter would otherwise eject. Empty space on the box is a reminder to get out there and live, to bring back some stickers worth sticking.


Occasionally I think about what’ll happen to the toolbox when I’m gone. Most likely my son will roll it out of the mouth of the garage and set the thing on fire in the cul-de-sac—catharsis for all the half-baked bodges he’ll have endured at my hands.


I tell myself the Richard Scarry sticker was my son’s idea, but there may have been a nudge in the right direction.


Kyle Kinard


Or maybe he’ll keep the thing. Who knows?


Last week, the two-year-old boy smiled at the checkout lady and got a dinosaur sticker in return. He held it between his finger and thumb the whole ride home. When I finally lifted him out of the car seat, he held the sticker up to the sky and asked me, “Daddy, can I put the dino on the toolbox?”


“Sure buddy,” I replied, “there’s plenty of room.”


Kyle Kinard
