# Steam-Powered Chevelle Proves GM Would Try Anything in the 1960s

There’s a popular meme that makes the rounds whenever a scientific breakthrough in power generation is announced. In it, a character tells someone that they’ve come up with a fantastic new way to produce energy. The other person then asks, “New, or steam?” The inevitable answer: “Steam,” paired with a sad expression that indicates there’s nothing truly “new” under the sun.


As with the best humor, the joke lies in the truth of the matter. Coal, wood-fired, nuclear, and even some very early solar power stations all trot out variations on boiling water to rotate a turbine and generate power, making it one of the most time-tested forms of lighting up homes or spinning up industry. With the right engineering in place,[steam power](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/when-the-paxton-phoenix-tried-to-take-steam-mainstream/) is also very portable, as evidenced by its extensive use in both rail and water-going vessels during the era that bore its name.


Jay Leno points out the finer technical details of a Doble steam car.


Aaron Robinson


Although steamships and locomotives are a celebrated part of transportation history, steam-powered cars are considerably more obscure. The early days of the automobile saw steam, electric, and gas-powered vehicles scrambling for the same, limited market share, as few people could afford a personal car in the late 19th century. Even though steam vehicle development dated all the way back to the 1700s, technical limitations—poor thermal efficiency, the presence of an extremely heavy boiler, and steep maintenance requirements—saw steam eventually drop to the back of the pack as advances in internal combustion led to these vehicles eventually dominating.


Despite fading from the daily driving scene, however, steam power’s industrial use never went away, and by the time the late 1960s rolled around, it hovered at the periphery of the burgeoning alternative energy scene. At the same time, with the specter of federal regulations on tailpipe emissions threatening to bring muscle car madness to a halt, more than a few car companies were casting their nets far and wide to find future-proof technologies.


It turns out one automaker wasn’t just looking forward, but was also willing to reconsider innovations from the past. This is how, in 1969, General Motors ended up paying a man named Bill Besler to take one of its most popular family cars, the Chevelle, and convert it into steam power. If that sounds crazy to you, then consider this: He actually pulled it off.


## Besler: A Big Name in Steam


William J. Besler (R) with his brother George.


Popular Science/Recurrent Ventures


GM couldn’t have picked a better partner to push the limits of steam power. In fact, it wasn’t even the first car company to reach out to William J. Besler about the possibility of adapting steam to modern automotive needs. That honor belonged to Kaiser, which a decade beforehand had built an experimental version of the Manhattan.


Besler’s steam bonafides went well beyond the surly bounds of Earth, however, having been the first and so far only person to convert an airplane to steam power and then fly it successfully. A replica of the engine—which he stuffed into a Travel Air 2000—is[in the collection of the Smithsonian’s National Air and Space Museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/besler-steam-engine-reproduction/nasm_A19650253000) . Surely, a man who had overcome the weight limitations of steam power while lofting airborne could work wonders in a more humble passenger car?


So went the feeling at GM, in any case, whose research lab worked together with Besler Developments to see how far it could push steam on modern roads. The automaker also handed over a few guidelines, with the strictest being that the entire power plant could not be larger than the underhood area of the existing Chevelle platform.


This presented something of a problem for Besler, given that the most successful of traditional steam-car designs positioned the engine on top of the drive axle as a single unit, separate from the boiler (stowed under a long hood up front). It was an arrangement that offered better balance and use of space than GM’s all-forward requirement, and it’s[known as the “Doble” design](https://www.scribd.com/document/78447438/Doble-Steam-Car-00) , after the manufacturer that created it and sold it until the early 1930s.

## The Doble Double-Pump


How important was[the Doble engine](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/why-jay-leno-loves-steam-powered-cars/) to the world of steam transportation? It actually formed the basis of Besler Development’s entire operations, as Bill and his brother George bought out the company and its production facilities after Doble went out of business. That steam-powered plane? The steam-powered Kaiser Manhattan? All evolutions of the Doble design.


A Doble-designed water pump was mechanically fed, forcing water from the vehicle’s water tank into the steam generator. It is a critical component matched directly to the car’s firing rate and steam demand.


Doble Steam Motors


It’s only logical, then, that the engine the company used when converting the ’69 Chevelle was also Doble-based. In a[1982 interview with Special Interest Autos](http://kimmelsteam.com/docs/Chevy%20Steamer-poster.pdf) , Besler describes the setup as a “1970 Doble” (representing the year the work took place). It featured a “double-pumping” design, with piston movement in both up and down directions generating power as the steam moved from high-pressure to low-pressure, before being condensed into water prior to its return into the steam generator (which can burn many types of fuel, although for testing purposes Besler stuck with kerosene).


YouTube/SACA


Typically, a steam-powered car doesn’t require a gearbox at all—given its packaging into the drive axle—a feature that helped make them some of the smoothest-driving models in early motoring. Given that the Chevelle had to send its output rearward, the back half of its original V-8 engine was retained, preserving the connection to its three-speed manual transmission. Removing 50% of the engine block before converting it to steam power also provided the real estate for the rest of the drivetrain’s hardware, with ventilation cut into the hood and fenders.


## A Working Chevelle vs. a Cantankerous Pontiac


The end result was more of a success than most would think, given how far away the world of steam-powered cars feels from the current day. Once started, it took about 30 seconds for the Chevelle to generate enough steam power to get rolling (followed by 90 seconds to warm up the block), after which its 50 horsepower could pull up to 65 mph without much trouble. Surpassing that top speed, however, was not in the cards for the prototype. Emissions from the boiler were modest, and the water in the steam engine recirculated nearly indefinitely, making kerosene the only fluid that needed regular topping up. Testing revealed 15 miles per gallon from the steam generator.

General Motors called the Chevrolet prototype the SE-124 (after its displacement in cubic inches), and it wasn’t the only steam power play going on at the company at that time. Internally, GM’s own engineers were working on a similar conversion using a mid-size Pontiac platform.


[Dubbed the SE-101](https://books.google.ca/books?id=FyoDAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA84&lpg=PA84&dq=se-124+chevelle&source=bl&ots=6ZcDT4R9Iw&sig=wLDAFVzU86kJxMJG7UHhvos_ELc&hl=en&ei=F53tS6vSOYG-Npeq2OoL&sa=X&oi=book_result&ct=result&redir_esc=y#v=onepage&q=se-124%20chevelle&f=false) , it differed considerably from the Doble design in the Chevelle, adopting instead a “uniflow” steam engine that produced power by allowing steam to enter the cylinder and then expand in a single direction, exhausting it near the end of stroke travel. Unfortunately for GM, the uniflow design was beset by issues, including ponderous weight, a sketchy condenser implementation, and concerns over considerably higher production costs.


The SE-101 was an experimental steam-powered car built using a modified 1969 Pontiac Grand Prix alongside the Chevelle project known as the SE-124. Both concepts charted radically different engineering paths.

## Steam Cars Stumbled Where EVs Pushed On


History shows that General Motors abandoned any plans to pursue steam power past these two prototypes. This isn’t really surprising; while there were benefits to replacing ICE with steam, they weren’t overwhelming enough to justify the massive re-education program the company would have to undertake should it begin to offer sedans that had choo-choo heritage.


That doesn’t mean the Besler steam Chevelle was a failure. In fact, the vehicle went on to log more than 5000 miles of testing,[according to its current owner, steam enthusiast and educator Tom Kimmel.](http://kimmelsteam.com/1969chevelle.html)


There are plenty of parallels to be made between Chevy’s steam experiment and early implementations of battery-powered vehicles by Detroit’s Big Three. The past three decades are littered with examples of lead-acid EVs with paltry range and pitiful power, with the hopes and dreams of creative engineering teams propelling them toward bigger and better things. Steam power never quite had that momentum, making the Chevelle more of a pit stop than the shape of things to come.
