# Cars and Self-Image

Before I get into this, let me say that I despise stereotyping in just about any form. Most of us are far more complicated than a cheap soundbite about a single thing. I’m a guy who stubbornly holds onto the 30-year-old AOL email address through which I centralize automotive-related email traffic precisely because it’s my way of saying “If you think you know something about me because of the stereotype that all remaining AOL users are blue-haired old ladies with Jitterbug phones, the joke’s on *you.* ”


However, there’s no getting around the fact that the cars we choose to drive (or abjectly refuse to be caught dead in) project something about who we are, whether we want them to or not. The late folk musician Bill Morrissey has a song called “[Car and driver](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YevrGtidvpM) ” in which he cleverly serves up some of these common stereotypes (“My BMW draws applause / I am not bound by traffic laws / my Volvo wagon will seat six / it’ll run on diesel or trail mix / I’ll bet you a ten, even a fiver / you find the car, I’ll find the driver / It really ain’t no big deal / to know who’s inside that automobile”). Though that’s funny, I’d like to try to steer (heh) clear of the usual stereotypes of who is or isn’t driving Subarus and Corvettes and why I’m highly unlikely to ever own either vehicle (well, maybe a tastefully pinstriped[C3 Corvette](https://www.hagerty.com/media/buying-and-selling/1968-82-chevrolet-corvette-buyers-guide/) ), but let’s just say that there is no universe in which I’m *ever* going to be driving a Tesla Cybertruck. For both daily drivers and pampered passion vehicles, we are nothing without our deeply held automotive opinions.


Actually, I take that back. Let’s attack the BMW-driving stereotype head-on. I’ve daily-driven BMWs for nearly 45 years, not because of their advertising as “The Ultimate Driving Machine” for the affluent, and in spite of the longstanding bad image they’ve had for being piloted by hostile drivers. This all began in the yuppie years when the cars were known for having their high beams on at all hours (a problem perpetuated by BMW’s own advertising that frequently showed the cars with all their candlepower blazing), morphed into ridicule of people who didn’t know how to use their directionals, and (as per Morrissey’s lyrics) settled into the aggressive tailgating “a$$hole in a BMW” who tries to weave through dense traffic at triple-digit speeds. (Honestly, this behavior used to seem limited to BMWs, Porsches, and Corvettes, but now I see Priuses and hooptie minivans doing it. I hate it having become so ubiquitous—it’s dangerous as hell—but at least it takes the spotlight off BMW.)


As I’ve written, my[daily driver for the past decade](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/happy-10th-anniversary-you-daily-driver-you/) has been a 2003 BMW E39 530i with a 5-speed manual and the sport package. To me and others, this generation represents not only “peak BMW” but also the water’s edge of cars I want to own. The E39 doesn’t have those gawd-awful huge front kidneys (a friend of mine commented that “they look like the impression it would make if a fat naked guy fell butt-first into the snow” and now I can’t unsee it). I’ve read where people refer to the E39 as an “analog car,” and that’s ludicrous considering that it has 20 to 30 control modules depending on the option package. But at least it has no badly integrated diagnostic display that looks like someone ordered it from Temu, bolted it to the dashboard, and used 3D-shaded icons from Windows 3.1. It’s rear-wheel drive, so there are no front CV joints to need replacing. It’s got an old-school, mechanically driven water pump, not an electric one that requires pulling half the nose off to replace. It doesn’t have electrically assisted steering. And it’s now old enough that it’s an event when I see another one on the road.


I’ve kept the E39 for a decade because, to me, it’s the perfect daily driver. With its 230-hp, 24-valve inline six, it has exactly the right amount of power to move the car out of its own way but not so much that you keep goosing it just for the rush. The suspension is firm but not in the least bit jarring. It’s a very comfortable, quiet car with a great stereo. I can cruise at the 80-mph traffic speeds you routinely encounter on interstates and average about 25 mpg. It’s got fold-down rear seats, allowing me to slide things into the trunk that I couldn’t otherwise. And it’s not a “look-at-me” car. In fact, it’s almost invisible, except to other E39 owners.


A friend of mine recently sent me a link to a video titled “The psychology of people who still love old BMWs.” It’s obviously AI-generated, so the curmudgeon in me doesn’t want to include the link, but it actually does a decent job of hitting on many of the issues of engagement and ownership commitment that I and others love about older cars. And, incredibly, the video’s thumbnail picture shows a silver E39 just like mine.


Of course, the last thing I want is to be part of some trend. I don’t *want* the cars I love to become “hot.” I want them to be undiscovered treasures, like empty beaches you can only reach by driving a slightly-lifted old Suburban through bogs thick with mosquitoes.


But it does raise this issue of daily-driving “mature” cars. Between doing what most people do—daily-driving a sub-four-year-old, sub-60,000-mile, leased car—and[trying to daily a beloved classic](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/daily-driving-a-classic-can-be-more-trouble-than-its-worth/) (which I really don’t recommend, but hey, you do you), there is a vast gray area. My E39 530i is now a 23-year-old car with 225,000 miles on it, but as long as I address any fuel delivery issues (new fuel pump and filter), ignition issues (replacing the stuck coils when they begin misfiring), cooling system issues (keeping after the water pump, radiator, thermostat, fan, clutch, and hoses), and charging system issues (alternator and regulator), it’s a safe and generally reliable modern car.


Still, keeping a “mature” car that way is a commitment, and the people I know who do it (including me) justify it to themselves with a heady mix of iconoclastic bravado, economic calculus, and outright denial. I have several good friends who are joined at the hip with station wagons. One is on his fourth BMW E46 wagon, which are difficult but not impossible to find in a manual-transmission, rear-wheel drive configuration. Another loves the 1979–1985 Mercedes W123-chassis 300TD wagon, all of which were rear-wheel drive automatics. A third continues to be hooked on Volvo 245 RWD wagons built from 1975 through 1993, many of which were sticks. Part of the justification is that—particularly with the Mercedes and the Volvo—they’re fairly simple vehicles. But they’re also right-sized, satisfying to drive, and not some interchangeably-styled crossover SUV whose manufacturer you probably couldn’t tell if the badge was removed.


And yet, if you’re driving a mature or classic vehicle, there’s no denying that you’re driving around a statement that says “This car is more important to me than the convenience of having something newer and blander.” Whether there’s also a component that’s “ *BEING SEEN IN THIS CAR MAKING THIS STATEMENT* is more important to me,” and if so, whether or not you care, is for the owner to decide.


(That having been said, I saw a post on a vintage Lotus site where someone asked what the logistics would be about daily driving a[Lotus Europa](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/the-ups-and-downs-of-the-europas-nose/) *in Manhattan.* While I’m all for letting that automotive freak flag fly, my visceral reaction was “Dude, there’s a line, and this is *waaaay* over it.”)


I believe I’ve told this story before, but two cars before I had my current E39 530i, I had a BMW E39 wagon as a daily. Cool car, 5-speed, sport package, slightly hunkered-down suspension. But it was always breaking. In the end, I realized that my daily had the needs of an enthusiast car and thus could no longer function as my daily, and I didn’t *want* it as an enthusiast car, so I sold it. It wasn’t a difficult decision. I never regretted it. The car was never part of my self-image. I never thought of myself as “that guy daily-driving the BMW wagon” in the same way I think of myself as “that guy who’s owned his red BMW 3.0CSi for 40 years, has owned forty 2002s, and adores his two vintage Lotuses.” When the 530i gets too needy to be a daily and the time comes to cut it loose, I’ll be sad, but just as practical.


It’s your choice. If your self-image has you daily-driving a Fiero up to 200,000 miles like Marshall in How I Met Your Mother, I salute you. And if you reach the point where that makes no sense and you and your spouse think you need some new generic crossover SUV—hey, you fought the good fight as long as you could. Someone else on the left edge of that bell curve will be thrilled to buy it and see themselves driving something unconventional. Maybe a few years later, you’ll see them on the road, livin’ their dream, and think, “Look at that peacock. Look at that gadfly. *Ha!* I *mock you,* sir! I give him another six months until he grows up and buys a Kia Seltos.”


Which you utter, with a deep sense of self-loathing and shame, from behind the wheel of your Subaru Forester.
