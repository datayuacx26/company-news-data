# His Car Went Up In Flames, But Robert Wickens Is Ready to Race

As disasters go, this one didn’t merit a blip on an international scale, but to a small, second-year racing team, it was catastrophic.


On April 18, the DXDT Racing team competed in the Acura Grand Prix of Long Beach, the third IMSA WeatherTech Sports Car Championship race of the season, held on a tight little course carved out of the streets of downtown Long Beach, just south of Los Angeles. They finished sixth out of 17 cars in the GTD class, a respectable outing for the Chevrolet Corvette Z06 GT3.R.


After the race, some members of the North Carolina-based DXDT team stayed in California to prepare the Corvette for the next race, the StubHub Monterey Sports Car Championship, scheduled for May 3 at the WeatherTech Raceway Laguna Seca, a very different course from Long Beach. The week before the race, the team’s truck driver flew back to California, then headed north towards Monterey.


Lumen Digital Agency


He was barely past Bakersfield when he looked in the mirror and saw smoke pouring from the back of the trailer, on the driver’s side. He pulled over, grabbed a fire extinguisher, jumped out and ran to the rear. An overheated axle was now a full-fledged blaze.


“The truck driver did just about everything he could—he emptied a couple of fire bottles on it, and other truck drivers were stopping to help with their own fire bottles, but they just couldn’t find a way to extinguish the fire,” said Bryan Sellers, an accomplished racer turned competition director for DXDT. “He was able to disconnect the truck from the trailer and at least save it, but he said the transporter seemed to catch fire on the inside, and melted everything from midway back, including our race car.”


The team’s main toolbox and a handful of parts were located towards the front of the trailer, and didn’t burn up, but most everything else did, including the drivers’ gear—helmets, HANS devices, uniforms, shoes and gloves.


Lumen Digital Agency


But one of those drivers, Robert Wickens, a 37-year-old Canadian who grew up just a bicycle ride away from Canadian Tire Motorsport Park, the site of this weekend’s IMSA event, lost something important enough to possibly end his season after just one race. Wickens is a paraplegic, and uses a very sophisticated, computerized hand control system that allows him to steer, shift gears, accelerate and—this is the most important, and by far the most difficult to engineer—apply the brakes, with precisely the right amount of pressure.


There was just one copy of the new, experimental set of hand controls, and it was attached to the Corvette, which melted into the floor of the trailer and had to be scraped out. As far as Wickens knew, his season might have just ended with the Long Beach race.


Wickens may have begun his racing career on two wheels, bicycling up to his home track, but he knew even then that the plan was to race at CTMP on four, preferably very fast wheels. He climbed the ladder from karts all the way to IndyCar, where, rather astoundingly, he qualified on the pole in his first race with the series, at the 2018 St. Petersburg Grand Prix. He dove headfirst into his freshman season, impressive at every stop, earning the Rookie of the Year title at the Indianapolis 500.


Then came the ABC Supply 500, held on the big Pocono Raceway oval in Pennsylvania. He was battling up front with Ryan Hunter-Reay when their wheels touched, which was enough to send Wickens into the wall, then spinning up into the fencing, then back onto the track. Most of the body and all four wheels were gone. The crash looked too much like the horrifying wreck at Las Vegas in 2011, which cost two-time Indy 500 winner Dan Wheldon his life.


But Wickens survived, though his injuries were serious: His neck was fractured, as were both legs, both hands, a forearm, an elbow and four ribs. But worst of all was the damage to his spine. Wickens was a paraplegic.

In his mind, though, Wickens wasn’t done with racing. It was all he knew, and he refused to give up, maintaining a relentless rehabilitation schedule. Then, in the spring of 2021, that work paid off: Bryan Herta Autosport, a Hyundai racing team owned by Herta, a former IndyCar winner, owned a car fitted with comparatively rudimentary hand controls for driver Michael Johnson, who had been partly paralyzed in a motorcycle crash at age 12. Wickens was invited to test the car at a private session at Mid-Ohio Sports Car Course.


It was a rainy afternoon, and Wickens didn’t get many laps. But he got enough to convince himself, and everyone watching, that yes, he could still drive a race car.


He joined Herta’s team. Wins and a championship followed, as did an opportunity to move up from the four-cylinder Hyundai to the V-8 power of a Corvette Z06. His[2025 season with DXDT Racing](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/paralyzed-racer-robert-wickens-just-three-weeks-away-from-his-corvette-debut/) went well, as both Wickens and the fledgling team found their footing. For 2026, reunited with his affable, talented teammate Mason Filippi, who had also driven for Hyundai, the season was full of promise.


Then, the fire. And Wickens lost something far more valuable than his Nomex shoes and racing gloves. Corvette Racing, car builder Pratt Miller, and especially Bosch, the automotive electronics company, had taken hand controls to the next level, giving Wickens the ability to match his teammates outright. At Long Beach, Wickens qualified on the pole. Competition for the Corvette in the GTD class included cars like the Aston Martin Vantage, Ferrari 296, Lamborghini Huracan, and Porsche 911.


But that so-sophisticated Bosch system was essentially one of one, designed for Wickens. And it melted in the fire. “All of my hand controls that were in the car were unusable,” Wickens said. “All the spares that were on the truck were deemed unusable.” The steering, shifting and accelerating are controlled by comparatively simple technology. It’s the braking that was the massive breakthrough—earlier braking systems were more like on/off switches, when you really need to go from ultra-delicate to full pedal pressure.


IMSA Official


Bosch had figured it out once. Could they do it again? Indeed they did. “The first progression of the hand control system took about six months; this second version took like four weeks,” Sellers said.


We haven’t even mentioned that DXDT, which is owned by businessman and part-time racer David Askew, had to replace the car, too. It’s a refurbished Z06 GT3.R that DXDT had run in the SRO World Challenge series, which is completely separate from the IMSA WeatherTech series. In all, “It was quite difficult,” said Sellers. “We had a lot of help from a lot of people. Pratt Miller was very involved with the rebuild, and luckily they were able to put a rush on it to get it turned around.”


The car itself, minus the hand controls and Wickens, raced in the Sahlen’s Six Hours of the Glen, an endurance race held at Watkins Glen International Raceway in New York. That was on June 28, and the car finished 11th in a 20-car class. That race taught the team a lot, and that knowledge has been applied to Sunday’s race.


“I feel like we’re in a good place rolling into this weekend. It would be nice to really put on a show for Robbie and his hometown fans,” said Sellers, who, as a driver, has a class win at the Rolex 24 at Daytona and at Petit Le Mans, and two at the Twelve Hours of Sebring.


Lumen Digital Agency


As for Wickens, this race is circled in red every year. “This track was in my youth and my childhood. I grew up on the kart track, and I would ride my bike over to watch the American Le Mans Series race, and watch Ron Fellows and his yellow Corvette do its thing over the years,” Wickens said.


The 65-year-old track, still known as Mosport to most fans, was purchased in 2011 by a small group of investors, which included Fellows, a longtime Corvette factory driver. At 66, he remains one of Canada’s most beloved motorsports figures, operating the Ron Fellows Driving Experience at CTMP and other tracks using, of course, Corvettes, which Fellows co-drove to a pair of class wins at the 24 Hours of Le Mans, as well as an overall win at the Rolex 24 at Daytona in 2001.


Wickens ran fourth at CTMP last year, the highest finish the team has had in IMSA racing. “I think hopefully we can put on a good show for the fans, and finally give DXDT its first podium. Which would be well deserved, especially after all the setbacks that we’ve had.”
