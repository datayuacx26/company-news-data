# Piston Slap: Quick-Strutting or Restoring a Performance GM Suspension?

*Adam* writes:


Hi Sajeev,


I’ve been having a long mental debate since your[Fire the Parts Cannon](https://www.hagerty.com/media/advice/piston-slap/piston-slap-fire-the-parts-cannon/) post last summer. I’m currently working through a suspension renewal on my 2005 Buick LeSabre equipped with the Gran Touring (F41) suspension. *(Represented by the emblem I am holding in the cover photo. – SM)*


So far, I’ve replaced the rear air-leveling shocks with KYBs (keeping the old rear springs). I checked the rear trailing arms, but I’m not quite ready to pull the trigger on replacing those myself just yet—having to drop the rear part of the exhaust has me a bit skittish.


Lately, the car has felt pretty unsettled when going over highway expansion joints. I did the classic front-fender bounce test, and the front end isn’t settling down nearly as well as the rear does now with the new KYBs. With 125K miles and 21 years on the original front struts, I’m sure it’s time to replace them.


Here is my debate: Do I buy aftermarket Quick Struts (fully loaded), or do I rebuild the current assemblies to keep my original springs? If I go the Quick Strut route, I am currently looking at the KYB Strut-Plus.


My main concern is preserving the OE F41 spring rates. This car has always offered a nice ride while handling surprisingly well, lacking the typical wallow of a Cadillac DeVille. If I go with aftermarket Quick Struts, I’m worried the included springs will be generic, altering that perfect F41 balance.


On the flip side, my main concern with rebuilding the OE strut assemblies is having to wrestle with the “spring compressor of death.”


What are your thoughts? Is it worth risking bodily harm to keep the factory OE springs, or should I just go with aftermarket Quick Struts and the springs they provide?


*Sajeev* answers:


I recommend swapping your F41 performance springs onto new struts, because I am a fan of front-wheel drive barges that can actually take a turn. I smile every time I feed my 1989 Continental a twisty/bumpy road, but I also cry inside because a key part of its performance suspension (dual-mode shocks) tragically went away after removing its air suspension. I don’t want you to feel my suspension of disbelief (see what I did there?), but let’s pause to ensure everyone knows the terminology.


Rob Siegel


Monroe’s Quick Strut


Monroe


*Quick Struts* is a brand name owned by Monroe, and a *MacPherson strut* (above, left) is an integrated coil spring and damper assembly that attaches to the steering knuckle (or hub carrier, in the case of rear struts) at the bottom and the car’s body at its top. It’s been a common suspension setup in cars since the 1980s. One term is like Kleenex; the other term is like tissue paper.


The Monroe Quick Strut (or comparable) makes replacement easy, because there’s no need to separate the individual components like the damper, spring, top mount, bumpstop, etc. All you need to do is take the old assembly out and install an entirely new assembly, saving money on labor costs in the process. It’s a win-win for mechanics, shop owners, hobbyists, and motorists alike.


The High-Performance Oldsmobile LSS (not the Delta 88).


Oldsmobile


If only every part was this descriptive.


Rock Auto


There’s only one problem: many General Motors products have handling packages that are long forgotten. And they are long overlooked in the aftermarket. It’s easier for a company like Monroe to make a singular Quick Strut assembly for all GM W-body sedans, *subsequently telling the six people who care about their special handling packages to go pound sand.*


Unfortunately for Adam, he is one of those six people. And with this, hopefully we are all on the same page now.


Replacement coil springs (to go with a MacPherson strut) are sold online, but they do not differentiate between suspension options seen in the screenshot from Rock Auto above. I spent a fair bit of time searching various GM W-body forums and Rock Auto to see what suspension parts interchange and how the aftermarket treats things like Grand Prix GTPs versus a regular Grand Prix sedan. Or the Oldsmobile LSS versus the Delta 88. And the Impala SS versus an LS model. And the regular Buick LeSabre versus… *well, I think you get the point.*


2005 Pontiac Grand Prix GTP


Pontiac


The 2005 Pontiac Grand Prix was interesting, as RockAuto suggests there are unique struts for examples with 17+ inch wheels. Too bad they can’t tell you if the spring rates are different. The only place where a clear separation between suspension setups exists online is with replacement anti-sway bar bushings: The bushing diameters are different to accommodate larger or smaller bars, and picking the wrong one ensures a car cannot be reassembled and back on the road.


But since Adam is pretty passionate about this car, I have another recommendation. Get new coil springs from a vendor like[Coil Spring Specialties](https://www.coilsprings.com/) in the correct F41 spec, slam those on a new strut, and you can at least avoid having to disassemble the original struts.


In this scenario, you *will* have to pick up new top mounts and use the aforementioned spring compressor. (As we all know, the amount of energy stored in compressed springs is something that always deserves respect.) I know Adam is a DIY kinda guy, but this is one time when paying a shop to load a MacPherson strut assembly for you is worth every penny.


Meee-yow!


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


I will endorse that specific coil spring vendor, as I have used them three times for lowering three of the Mehta Family’s vehicles. One such example is this 1988 Mercury Cougar XR-7. Lowering a Fox Body (especially a non-Mustang) is a tricky affair for engineering concerns that are beyond the scope of this discussion, so getting a custom spring was certainly the right move.


A custom spring is probably also the right move for Adam. Maybe those 21-year-old Buick springs aren’t what they used to be, as rust and potholes can wear them out significantly. Is it worth trying to safely remove them from the MacPherson strut assembly when new springs can be made? And maybe you want the nose of the car lowered a fraction of an inch in the process?


You tell me, dear reader. *You tell me.*
