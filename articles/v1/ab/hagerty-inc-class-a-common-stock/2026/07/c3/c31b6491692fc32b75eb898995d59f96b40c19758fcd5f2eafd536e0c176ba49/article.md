# The 2001 Ford Explorer Sportsman Concept Was an Angler’s Dream Ride

By their very nature, SUVs are intended to be many things to many different groups of people. It’s right there in the name—Sport *Utility* Vehicle. More often than not, this mandate leads to products that are watered down, sometimes in a few ways, other times in many ways. That’s why, as enthusiasts, it can be hard to get excited about the latest SUV offering. We prefer cars with clear, focused goals.


The production world may sand down the edges of most vehicles, but in the world of concept vehicles, singularity of purpose still thrives. Case in point: The Ford Explorer Sportsman Concept from 2001.

Though I’m not good at it, I love fly fishing. Here in Northern Michigan, we’re spoiled with great rivers aplenty to stand in and let the stress of the day wash away. Naturally, the activity comes with plenty of gear, and toting it to and from the waterways I fish has made me appreciate the utility of a vehicle that can swallow rods, nets, coolers, packs, and more. My[Mitsubishi Montero](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/montero-in-moab-growing-to-love-an-overlooked-4x4-in-the-age-of-escape/) does a commendable job, as does my new half-ton pickup. But both would pale in comparison to the Explorer Sportsman Concept as far as a machine to aid my angling.


Introduced at the 2001 North American International Auto Show, the Explorer Sportsman Concept helped herald the arrival of the third-generation[Explorer SUV](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/first-look-2026-ford-explorer-tremor/) . Ford described it as a concept “designed to fulfill fly anglers’ dreams of finding the calm and unspoiled waters of the perfect wilderness fishing location.”


Ford Archives


While modern-day associations with that ideal tend to lean toward something with loads of ground clearance, plenty of underbody protection, and more payload capacity than you’d ever need (unless you were fishing for blue whales), the Explorer Sportsman concept skewed a little tamer as far as off-roading chops are concerned.


According to Patrick Schiavone, who was the chief designer for the Explorer Sportsman Concept and other Ford production trucks and SUVs at the time, that pivot was intentional. “Everything \[about SUVs\] was about rugged, off-roading at the time,” says Schiavone.


Ford’s marketing team had just wrapped up a round of market research around SUVs, and what they found was rather surprising. “\[The research\] came back that 80% of people who buy an SUV off-road. What we came to understand was that people didn’t even know what off-road meant. They thought, ‘Yeah, I take it off-road into my driveway. I take it off-road into the parking lot,'” Schiavone says with a laugh. “We were thinking, ‘Whoa, we’re not making quite the same connection that we thought we were making.”


Ford Archives


Instead of making the Explorer Sportsman as capable as possible in a rock garden, Ford’s designers opted for something that skewed a little more toward pack mule. There was still ample ground clearance, and those tires looked chunky enough to hold their own off-piste, but you can tell there were compromises made in favor of the SUV’s utility.


By focusing on a single outdoor activity, it gave the team a more defined idea of what to chase from a design and feature standpoint. They could have chosen one of the many forms of hunting, or cycling, or any other outdoor activity, but fly fishing met the moment thanks to the discipline’s traditional, methodical nature.


“There’s a cachet with fly fishing; there is a tradition, all the tools are so cool,” says Schiavone. “We knew we could do something that looked really, really neat and got our point across.”


Ford Archives


The designers went wild fitting the concept with all sorts of tricks and features that, speaking as an angler, would be genuinely useful chasing rising fish. Power running boards extended from beneath the vehicle to aid ingress and egress, but they also opened to reveal storage space for extra fly rods. The back portion of the roof rack could slide out to the side and fold down, making it easier to load bags and gear atop the SUV. (Straight up, this is brilliant and I think more companies should design racks this way.)


Ford Archives


It’s not quite a Land Rover Defender–style side rack—this one wouldn’t stay pulled down—but you can see the similarities.


Ford Archives


As Schiavone tells it, that roof rack was a big moment for the team, but not in a way you’d expect. “As much as we’re trying to practice or come up with concepts for auto shows to sort of ignite the passion of the general public, we also want to ignite the passion of the engineering community.”


It’s no secret that designers and engineers are normally opposing forces inside the halls of an automaker. Stylists want a body line to swoop a bit more; engineers have to push back and tell them that, for myriad reasons (production challenges, safety, durability, you name it), it can’t be done. In the folding roof rack on the Explorer Sportsman Concept, Schiavone and his team found a way to challenge the engineers and get two very different organizations rowing eagerly in the same direction. “You really love when you can get the engineers to play along with you,” says Schiavone.


Effective floor mats? No. Gorgeous ones? Absolutely.

As impressive as the concept’s exterior is, the interior takes things up another notch. Extensive use of Bloodwood, a vivid red hardwood found primarily on the moist coast of New South Wales in Australia, brilliantly offsets the subtle green exterior. Ford slathered it across the dashboard, the trim pieces, and even made floor mats out of it. Though most anglers catch and release their fish, the concept included a 30-gallon live fish tank bolted into the rear compartment. Were you out pursuing a catch-and-cook, this might come in handy.


Ford Archives


Perhaps the coolest touch is behind the front seats, where small packs fitted to the seatbacks can fold down, detach, and serve as fly-tying benches—replete with the special vises that hold hooks in place as you conjure fake bugs from feathers and animal hair. I’ve seen a few fly-tying stations inside vehicles over the years, but none as thoughtfully integrated as the ones on this concept.


Although many concepts wind up disappearing for good, this one made it out into the wild. In fact, this past May, it crossed the digital auction block on[Bring a Trailer](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/2001-ford-explorer-8/) , where it was bid to $24,888 but failed to meet reserve. According to the listing, the vehicle has an actual powerplant in it—a 2.5-liter Mazda inline-four backed by a four-speed automatic transmission. While that means it could theoretically move under its own power, the concept is not DOT- or EPA-certified, so it can’t be registered and titled anywhere in the United States.


Ford Archives


Not that it matters all that much. A vehicle like this isn’t intriguing because of whether or not you can wheel it to Walmart. Instead, it’s a fascinating look into a moment at Ford where the company realized that SUVs didn’t require massive off-road capability to be appealing. In some ways, the public reception—and the mere exploration of a different idea—helped accelerate the growth of the crossover SUV as we know it today.


Ford Archives


The same year that the Explorer Sportsman debuted, Ford released the Escape, a smaller crossover based on a front-wheel-drive platform that made no meaningful pretentions about off-road capability. Instead, it merely offered the things people like about modern crossovers—a higher seating position, a different silhouette from the sedans that ruled the roads at the time, and a more spacious interior.


Fast forward 25 years, and Ford doesn’t make a single sedan anymore—but it makes loads of SUVs. To say the Explorer Sportsman Concept was solely responsible for the SUV-heavy nature of Ford’s lineup today would be disingenuous, but it’s not a stretch to say some of the learnings around this project accelerated the progress toward the Ford we know today.
