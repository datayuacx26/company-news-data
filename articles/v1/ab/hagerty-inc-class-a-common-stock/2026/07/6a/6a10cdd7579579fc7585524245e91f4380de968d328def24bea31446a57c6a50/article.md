# The Mk2 GTI Proved Immortality Isn’t Just for the Beetle

Immortality can be a tough nut to crack. The original VW Beetle lasted from 1938 to 2003 with significant changes applied to a singular concept, a feat unmatched by any other automobile. But the 1970s ensured the Beetle would no longer live in many parts of the world, as air-cooled practicality made way for air-conditioned luxury. Long after production ended, the Beetle’s legacy remains unquestionable. The original Mk1 Golf took over as VW’s primary economy car, centered around a water-cooled engine and a crisp, two-box design penned by Italdesign. The Golf earned a die-hard following, but Beetle-like immortality is difficult.


Certainly not impossible, but definitely not easy. The Mk1 Golf made way for a redesigned Mk2 iteration, a mandatory update to ensure VW remained competitive in a brave new world of automotive retailing and an ever-expanding hatchback market. There are many more generations, but there’s just something about the Mk2 that hits that timeless and downright immortal level of automotive success.


Teeing up the second round of the Golf (see what I did there?) was a task that VW tackled hands-on, eschewing the consultative skills of[Italdesign](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/vellum-venom/vellum-venom-vignette-italdesign-ai-and-professional-servicing/) for an in-house effort that added a modicum of curves and contouring to the now classic two-box design. They also made the Mk2 move from the sub-compact to the compact class, with larger dimensions for length, wheelbase, width, and height. It was clear that front-wheel drive motivation was appealing to this market segment, and getting a little more Golf for a modest weight increase ensured more people felt the good vibes. It also proved that continuous improvement is now mandatory, as the initial run of the German Mk1 Golf ran its course from May 1974 to September 1983.

A Beetle it ain’t, but this Golf has some swing in its step. Its life cycle is a stark contrast to the retailing landscape that let the original Beetle go on without a significant redesign for decades. While South Africa loved the Mk1 enough to keep it around until 2009, other markets needed to enhance the original’s formula. You could get everything from a Diesel engine, a[Golf Rallye](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/vws-golf-rallye-g60-truly-was-the-golf-r-of-the-80s-and-90s/) all-wheel-drive homologation special, a light-duty off-roader called the Golf Country, and even an EV derivation known as the Golf citySTROMer. Most were not available in the United States, but we certainly appreciated Mk2 luxuries like power windows, power steering, and the benefits that come from local production at Volkswagen’s Westmoreland Assembly Plant in Pennsylvania.


While VW’s American assembly aspirations were temporarily dashed in 1988, the Mk2 Golf survived. Perhaps it even thrived, if not for modest sales (relative to mainstays like Ford’s Escort and Chevrolet’s Cavalier) but for carving out a premium niche for German design and engineering prowess. And that’s very well represented by the enthusiast-oriented GTI, whose formula truly made the Mk2 Golf into something special.


The GTI highlights made for a warm introduction of this new Golf to *Motorweek* watchers, as the show mentioned how global competition was eating Volkswagen’s lunch towards the end of the Mk1’s tenure. John Davis mentioned in his dulcet tones the Mk2’s enhancements, and noted how the GTI stands out thanks to its flush-fit headlights lifted from the VW Jetta sedan stablemate. It’s unfortunate that the GTI’s signature red accents were repurposed for copycat high-performance hatches from other manufacturers, but that steering column stalk-activated multifunction computer stands out to this day. In-car computing wasn’t a controversial take back then, and having an oil temperature reading was a blessing for the enthusiast seeking fun when the motor was ready for it.


The Mk2 GTI initially greeted American drivers with a respectable 100 horsepower, up from the base model’s 85 horses thanks to tweaks like an aggressive 10:1 compression ratio. *Motorweek* noted that the Mk2 is smoother and faster when putting the power down, if the handling is less like the stripped-down go-kart nature of the original Mk1 GTI. Four-wheel disc brakes and a smoother ride on rough American roads impressed in this road test, and left *Motorweek* wishing VW the best here in America.

But while the VW Golf was a strong seller, it was never intended to battle cheaper offerings from Chrysler (Aries), Ford (Escort), and Chevrolet (Cavalier) head-on. And the base car wasn’t the catalyst for America’s sport compact scene. Instead, along with “OGs” like the Honda CRX and Acura Integra, it was the GTI that carved out a unique niche, and it only improved with advancements like a twin-cam, 16-valve engine (123hp) in 1987.


But perhaps that motor feels a little belated in the United States, as we got hooked on Honda’s DOHC powerhouses with the introduction of the Acura Integra hot hatchback just one year before. *Car and Driver* once pitted a prototype 16v GTI against said Acura, and it lost by a marginal amount. Luckily, *Motorweek* did a similar shootout a little bit later, and the GTI turned the tables by earning the top spot.


And loyalists will rightly point to the Mk2 GTI’s merits, because American roads are a big enough tent for everyone. You will read passionate notes from the[Cavalier Z24 faithful](https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/this-cavalier-z24-reminds-us-that-you-dont-need-an-iroc-to-be-80s-bowtie-cool/) and from fans of Mitsubishi’s turbocharged Colt (née Mirage) if you look for them.


But what makes the Mk2 GTI so special is the DNA it shares from VWs past and present. You can say the second generation is too portly compared to the original, but it can carry more stuff and hold more people in superior comfort. It isn’t as refined, safe, or powerful as its Mk3 replacement, but it readily accepts the Mk3’s six-cylinder VR6 powerhouse. Doing so ensures modern motoring in an Mk2 is a breeze. There’s a bit of a[Goldilocks effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldilocks_principle) here, as the Mk2 GTI has the DNA available to be more than the sum of its parts.


Perhaps we Americans aren’t able to tell the Mk2 GTI’s story as comprehensively as others. We should be forgiven for our Civic duty to Escort all hot hatchbacks into our tapestry. But very few of these cars benefit from the Golf’s global appeal, paired with the powertrain upgrades to make a potential footnote in Automotive history into a rowdy barnstormer in modern times.


The Beetle was a fantastic foundation, but the Golf made that formula relevant in more modern times. Even better, the VW community’s ongoing enthusiasm for the Mk2 GTI is rooted in something truly special. They are ensuring automotive immortality, just not in a VW Beetle kinda way.
