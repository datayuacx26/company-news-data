# Porsche’s Transaxle Experiment Turns 50

It’s 50 years since Porsche did the unthinkable. The company, built on rear-engined sports cars, decided to put a motor up front instead.


The Porsche 924, which arrived in 1976,[wasn’t even originally a Porsche](https://youtu.be/6qLwd44C4iU?si=0i0UHW7ZmJaGzDgl) , but it did lead the company in a whole new direction—one which could have killed off the 911 completely.


The 924 started life as a commission from Volkswagen, known internally as EA425. Harm Lagaay was a designer on the project and recalls, “We literally had a white sheet of paper in front of us.”


Certain engineering and manufacturing parameters, such as the engine position, cooling systems, transmission, and seat positions were defined, but beyond that Lagaay and his team had free rein. “We thought it was the future,” he adds.


Indeed, it was, but not for VW, which pulled the plug on EA425 as the oil crisis advanced, ultimately favoring the Golf-based Scirocco instead. So, Porsche bought the project back, convinced that one key aspect of the design would define a new type of Porsche—the transaxle.


Conventional thinking would attach the gearbox to the engine up front, then send drive to the rear wheels via a torque tube and differential. That would tip the weight balance forward, of course, which was not a concept familiar to the firm.


Porsche’s design incorporated the transmission into the rear axle, moving some of the weight rearward. “The decision wasn’t reached at some arbitrary point, but at the beginning of development,” remembers Roland Kussmaul, former road-testing engineer, who describes the 924 as an “easy-to-handle, forgiving, fast car.”


The 924’s layout also allowed for room for four passengers and their luggage, with Porsche even billing it as a “family sports wagon.”


Porsche


Crucially, it was seen as a platform to build the brand on. With the 944 and 968 following the 924, the transaxle survived until 1995, while the range-topping 928 was even viewed by Porsche Chairman Ernst Fuhrmann as a replacement for the 911.


Customers saw things differently, however, and, as we all know, it’s the *Elfer* that is the only survivor of this period in Porsche’s history.


There’s still much to recommend in the transaxle cars, though. Cost of entry is much lower than any rear-engined Porsche, while Kussmaul is quite correct about the handling. So, which would be right for you? Hagerty’s experts have provided in-depth guides to each of them, but here’s a quick rundown.


## 1977-1988 Porsche 924


Porsche


The 924 arrived a year later in the U.S., initially with just 95 hp from its Audi-developed two-liter engine, and, at first, only with an automatic transmission. Soon, however, there’d be more power (110 hp) and the option to row your own gears with a dog-leg five-speed manual, which is the preferred choice. The most desirable 924s are the Turbos, with more than 140 hp, and the super rare homologation special Carrera GTs, which further boosted output to more than 200 hp. The last-of-the-line 924S, meanwhile, is probably the best all-rounder. In total, more than 150,000 924s were sold, and there are plenty still on the roads.[Check out our buyer’s guide](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/your-handy-1977-88-porsche-924-buyers-guide/) for more info.


## 1981-1991 Porsche 944


Porsche


The 944 was the punchier big brother to the 924. Powered by a 2.5-liter four-cylinder (that was effectively one bank of the 928’s V-8), it delivered 143 hp in standard trim and over 220 hp in the last of the Turbos. With a wider track than the 924 and racy flared fenders, it looked more potent, too. There was a cabriolet for those who wanted some wind in their hair, although there was quite a trade-off in terms of chassis rigidity, which dulled an otherwise excellent driving experience. By the end of its decade in production, the 944’s engine was, at three liters, the largest capacity inline four on sale. Buyers were plentiful and more than 160,000 944s were sold. A 944 “will even serve you well on the occasional road trip, your commute, or the odd track day… a Porsche for all seasons and all reasons,” concludes[our guide](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/does-the-porsche-944-deserve-more-love/) .


## 1977-1995 Porsche 928


Porsche


Porsche’s first V-8 made its debut in the big and beautiful 928. “A rear-engine layout was never even considered for the relatively heavy, large V-8 engine,” says Kussmaul. The transaxle was ideal, offsetting some of the weight of that eight-cylinder lump up front. It came at first in 4.5-liter capacity with 219 hp, growing through 4.7-, 5.0-, and 5.4-liter iterations to end its run in GTS form with 345 horses. Our pick of the bunch, though, is the ’87-91 S4, with its five liters and 316 hp driving via a five-speed dog-leg manual or a lazier four-speed auto. “The 928’s mature, high-performance driving experience holds up today,” reckons Eric Weiner in his[spotlight on the S4](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/the-1987-91-porsche-928-s4-might-be-the-best-of-the-breed/) .


## 1992-1995 Porsche 968


Porsche


The 968 was the last hurrah for the transaxle. It wasn’t a completely new car, but much more than just a facelift of the 944. The bodywork was softer, with a front end that echoed both 911 and 928, and a much-revamped interior. The three-liter inline four now came with VarioCam variable valve timing and 236 hp as standard. A six-speed manual was the driver’s choice, with a Tiptronic on the options list. It’s the rarest of all transaxle Porsches, with only 12,780 cars built, and truly, it’s the best of them, especially in Sport guise, and even more so, in stripped-out two-seater Club Sport form. We picked the 968 as one to watch on our[2022 Bull Market list](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/bull-market-2022/) , and owner Jamie Sowlakis remarked, “The car is a real underdog—undervalued and under the radar.”
