# According To You: The Best Money-Saving DIY Repairs

We asked you about your best money-saving DIY automotive repair, and you folks did not disappoint. We covered a lot of ground together, even mentioning my personal best: a heater core swap. Pulling a dashboard, dropping a steering column, and having the patience to do it all without breaking old plastic parts became a learned skill for yours truly, and I relished those moments once I realized how expensive the job was if I didn’t do it.


One truly outstanding response from last week’s question was the comment below. Car dealerships generally do not inform a reader of a DIY repair possibility, but this Volvo service department guided one of our readers to complete the task himself:


> “In defense of dealerships, when I took my 2004 Volvo XC70 to the dealership and told them the A/C wasn’t keeping the car cool any more, they checked the A/C and told me the cabin air filter was dirty and was choking off the air flow. The service writer told me they could replace it but the parts and labor cost would be about $80. He then told me I could buy a cabin air filter at an auto parts store for about $20 and it would be a ten minute job to replace it.” — *Eric K.*


So let’s have a look at some more doozies in the world of DIY repairs—I’ve selected several for your reading pleasure. But please do consider reading all of the responses in the original question, as we received a[full six pages of comments](https://www.hagerty.com/media/hagerty-community/what-diy-repair-saved-you-the-most-money/comment-page-6/#comments) . There isn’t enough space to publish them all here, so let’s start with the opposite of the comment above:


## The Dealership Alternative


Warner Bros | YouTube


**William M:** There was a cam phase error on my wife’s 2014 Cadillac SRX. Dealer said a camshaft was bad and quoted $6500 for repairs. I bought a cam position sensor for $35, which solved the problem. That was 175,000 miles ago.


**Gregory H:** I had “Ford points” from a new F-150 purchase a couple years ago. I used them on a semi-synthetic (that’s what they use) oil change. I asked and calculated that if I paid for that 6-quart oil change, it would have cost $130.00. I’m not kidding! I can do and do do (hey, that was fun to write!) a full synthetic Mobil oil change myself for $40-ish. Stealerships are out of their minds.


**Miguel:** I changed the rails on the sunroof of my F-150; the dealer quoted me just short of 3 grand. They claimed the entire sunroof assembly needed to be replaced, requiring dropping the headliner. I bought the rails online for less than $75.00, and about 1 hr of watching YouTube videos and 1 hr of labor. My first thought was 50+ years of oil changes, that has really added up!


**Larry D:** The BMW dealer wanted big money (four figures) just for front brake pads and rotors on my ’22 X2 M35i. I did just the pads myself (rotors were fine). I happened to find a closeout Wagner set from Rockauto for $20. They’ve been fine. Huge win!


**DickR:** Replacing the ABS hydraulic control module (the box with the brake lines coming out under the hood) on my 2008 Honda Accord. From Honda, the part alone would have cost nearly $2K. I could see the dealer charging anywhere from $500 to $1000 for labor, plus sales tax on all that would have made the job cost nearly as much as the car was worth.


I found a source online that sold rebuilt modules for all kinds of cars. I think the rebuilt unit cost about $300 delivered. The replacement job that I did wasn’t nearly as daunting as it seemed. It worked just like new and never had a problem. My cost ended up being probably less than 10% of what a dealer would have charged.


**Dan P:** I did a basic soldering job on my kid’s 2001 Toyota Highlander that fixed the A/C. The wires behind the temp switch had simply broken and needed to be soldered to reattach. The dealer wanted to replace the entire center control console. Pennies and minutes instead of $1000+. And I learned all about why flux is so important in delicate soldering jobs!


**Sal E:** Blend door actuators on my Mustang. Dealer wanted over $600. A magnetic screwdriver, a little body contortion and 40 dollars in parts…done. Although my shoulder still hurts. Wonder what that’s gonna cost me!


**Nick:** My 2004 CRV developed a slow second-to-third shift problem two weeks after a dealer oil change. Dealer “diagnosed” as complete transmission rebuild at $3000. I did some investigation and found a sticking second-to-third shift solenoid at a cost of $75. Problem solved.


**alphagearhead:** I have a 2005 Bentley Arnage which is a gold mine of DIY savings. A few years ago, both front windows failed to respond to the switch; I could hear a relay click but no motion. The proposed repair would have been ~$5000 at the dealer. Instead, I carefully removed the door panels, and whacked the window motor with a small hammer while my son held the window switch. The windows have worked fine ever since; apparently they can just get gummed up from sitting, so now I cycle the windows every time I drive the car.


The front spring isolators were falling apart. After some investigation, I found a ~$25 Energy Suspension isolator that would fit in place of the $400 each OEM part, along with Rancho shock boots in place of the $250 each OEM part. I took the spring/dampers to a local off-road shop to get the springs reinstalled for another $40. All in, a little under $200 spent, vs again, several thousand at a dealer.


I also always recommend having the factory service manual if available.


## A Steal of A Deal?


Lexus


**Toms57:** The 2005-2006 Lexus LS430 power door locks notoriously fail. I know, there were four copies in our fleet. I was shopping for a fifth LS430 and the local Lexus dealer had a low-mile garage queen. The car was already reasonably offered and the dealer was getting phone call inquiries on it while I test drove it. The passenger-side power door lock wasn’t working, and the replacement part alone for the Lexus “soft close” doors listed at over $1100, plus labor.


Using this and a few other minor items that needed repair, I negotiated over $2000 off the offered price and bought the LS430. The dealer hadn’t prepped the car yet, so I returned a few days later to complete the sale. After completing the purchase from the dealer manager, I showed him a small motor I had in my coat pocket that cost $5 and explained I could fix the door lock actuator in about two hours with the $5 motor that is installed inside the Lexus plastic power door component part that lists for $1100. The look on the Lexus dealer’s sales manager’s face still makes me laugh.


**Binksman:** A $0.30 hose clamp. A person I work with reached out to me on a Friday night who knew I hauled scrap on the side and asked me to take their 1996 Grand Cherokee from the patient parking lot before Monday morning. The transmission had given out on his way to work. Knowing well how ’90s-era Chrysler automatic transmissions often have a Karmic reappearance as an emergency parking brake, or boat anchor, I said sure. Before I got there with my trailer on Saturday afternoon, he had purchased a new Hyundai, so he just gave me the Jeep (he even paid the title transfer costs the state required). I winched it onto the trailer and took it to the farm.


Before I even unloaded the Jeep, I noticed it was leaking transmission fluid from a cooler hose. I grabbed a hose clamp, refilled the fluid, and gifted the Jeep to my wife for her to use with our newborn. We used that Jeep for another 10 years and 80k miles.


## Economical Engine Enhancements


Kyle Smith


**Wayne:** My money-saver was rebuilding the engine on my ’71 Riviera. I had some basic tools and purchased the others I needed along with new bearings, etc. I must admit I had limited knowledge and read books and got tips from mechanically adept friends. The biggest I got was when I started it up and it actually ran! I could now burn rubber with that big 455 engine in almost top shape.


**Darwin O:** Overhauling my 82 911SC engine. Had a “triangle of death” oil leak, so out came the engine. While removing the heads, I found five broken head bolts, although the car seemed to be running fine. Short story, I put in new rings, head studs, oil lines, billet chain tensioners, drilled and tapped a bunch of broken exhaust studs, etc. Problem arose when it was time to put the intake and injection on and put the engine back up. I had a serious conniption about getting the car running again, so it went to the shop for that. Still, I saved myself some major bucks. I must have done my part right because it runs great.


**ParaboTech:** Now I have a good one. I saved a boatload back in 1991 when I replaced the 2.8-liter V-6 in my 1984 S-10 Blazer with a 3.1-liter from a 1992 Firebird. It required a lot of modification to wiring harnesses and special customization to accommodate the GM VATS anti-theft key system. I got it all working pretty well and drove it for almost 10 more years before I sold it. I’m sure that engine swap would have cost several thousand dollars and more than the value of the Blazer. That is probably one of the biggest DIY auto projects I have done.


**Mike L:** Replacing the cylinder heads on my ‘56 Caddy. Was a huge job for me, but with a great shop manual in one hand and my trusty old Craftsman tools in my other hand, I pulled it off in my own garage with perfect results. Very satisfying!


**[Greg Ingold:](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/gregory-ingold/)** Subaru head gasket repair is by far the winner for me. Years ago, I had head gasket problems on an ’08 Forester, and calling around, it was going to be a $2500 repair on the cheap end. For around $750, I got upgraded MLS gaskets, had the heads resurfaced and valves re-ground, a new timing set and water pump, as well as any other hard-to-access seals while the engine was out. Took about a week to do with machine shop lead times.


**Salsa96:** Right now I’m rebuilding the engine in my 240Z. I’m pretty sure I’m saving a bundle, but I also like doing the work, so that seems to offset the whole thing.


**William B:** Probably doing a complete rebuild on a Ford 289 in my Sunbeam Tiger. It has been a while, but in today’s money I would have $2000 in parts as opposed to $15,000 having it done by a shop!


**Michael D:** R&R engine on 2005 BMW 325iT—the head gasket blew and estimate from shop was $5K to replace. This happened just as COVID quarantines started. I was in a traveling sales position and my employer was paying me to not go anywhere and I always wanted to do this job. Found a low-mileage used M54 for $1200 shipped to my door. Took about three months, and miracle of miracles, got it done and buttoned up; it started first time. Great experience. Nothing scares me now—except for the heater core…


**Tom B:** Rebuilding my own engines. Predominately VW, but hey, I saved myself tons of money, not to mention the satisfaction of doing it myself. I think many folks know of which I speak!


**Warren S:** Put new modern rings in my Model A engine, complete valve job; I have that equipment. Fitted all bearings to .001, new gasket set, runs like a million bucks, starts instantly; total spent was $187. Far too many spend FAR too much on these engines, and for what? I’ve done this stuff since 1948, been there, done it. You do not need an ohmmeter, Plastigauge, or a micrometer. Do it yourself and learn.

**Lifeisrich:** Instead of replacing the 3-inch-diameter, foot-and-a-half-long rubber intake tube from air cleaner to intake manifold on my ‘91 Mazda Protégé DOHC, I repaired the leak by slopping on Shoe Goo, and it lasted another five years.


## Thanks, YouTube!


**Scott B:** Rebuilding the instrument cluster on our 1984 Corvette. Parts from Batee Corvette Parts (LCD polarizing film, board pin connectors, power supply, LED Lights, photo sensor kit), their YouTube videos, and an Auto Restoration Degree from McPherson College is all it took. Fully functioning display is a work of art at night.


**Rob M:** The ex had a 2016 Cayenne a few years back; the Porsche dealer quoted thousands ($4k?) to do a front brake job. I watched a couple YouTube videos and bought a set of (12 point?) star bits. I think the dealer parts, including some sensors, were around $800, only took a couple hours in the driveway to get it done.


**Eric:** My biggest money-saving project was replacing a blown-out spark plug in my 2000 Ford 5.4 Super Duty. I watched several YouTube videos and decided that was a doable project. After spending about $300 on a couple of tools I needed and the Cal-Van insert kit, I did it myself (even though it took me twice as long as the mechanic in the video). Very satisfying, and probably saved myself at least $500 in labor. Unfortunately, I just blew another plug out coming home from a swap meet this past weekend. Oh well, once again!!


**ed:** I replaced a front wheel speed sensor on my ’13 Mercedes SLK 55. It was $30 for the part and a good bit of YouTubing on how to find the wiring harness disconnect. Once found under the fender liner, a funky connector ring that would only come off a certain way, only on a Tuesday, only with a full moon. I’m sure the dealer would have been much more efficient, but now I know, and I figure I can do it on anything.


**Bruce F:** I had a 2010 Audi A5 with the turbo four-cylinder that was burning 1 quart in 300 miles. Google revealed a YouTube video about using Berryman B12 to soak the rings for 24 hours. After treatment, it burns 1 qt in 4500! Amazing result! The Internet rocks!


**Rocco417:** Pulled the dash on my ’13 Corvette C6 to replace the HVAC actuators I shorted out. All three cost less than $100. The labor was going to be over $2500. I didn’t need any special tools, just a lot of patience, and the YouTube video with step-by-step instructions.


## Completely Bushed


Rob Siegel


**Tom W:** So many feats of PITA fixes to choose from, but one good example was changing the rear crossmember bushings in my driveway on one of my eight or so Nissan Z31 300ZXs. I used multiple scissor jacks precariously supporting the many pieces making up the rear IRS. When I finally removed it, I carried it into the house to present to the wife to show off my great accomplishment.


A friend had a press in his shop down the street and allowed me to pop the old ones out and press in the new ones… A successful mission in the list of many. I’m 74 now and limit my repairs to minor items, oil changes, and brake pads, etc.


**JR:** For me, it was rebuilding my complete front suspension system, including new control arms, spindle, hub/rotor, coil spring, all bushings, tie rod ends…you get the picture. The car is 57 years old (1969), so a lot of it needed complete replacing and did NOT want to come apart. Not sure if I ever want to do that again, but I said that after the last one I did. Either way, I am the typical guy with an old car (that I love) and I would have hated having to pay someone to do that, unless of course if I had Elon’s money.


## Turn Key Restoration


Broad Arrow


**JW:** Complete re-wire of my wife’s ’70 Mach1 with a Painless chassis wiring kit. Took a week but was worth it!


**William H:** Rebuilding the valve bodies of the active suspension, hydraulic lines, and repairing the top hydraulics on a SL500.


**Richard P:** There is no way I could afford to own my brass era cars if I had to pay others to work on them. I have just completed many hours of work machining a new set of kingpins, spring shackle pins and bushes and tie-rod end pins for one car.


The only cost was the steel bar. If I had had to pay someone else, the labor cost would have been horrendous.


**Roger S:** Without question, I saved the most money by doing almost all of the rebuild of my Grabber Blue 1970 Mustang Mach 1 Q code (non-ram air 428 CJ) four-speed car. It was an empty shell when I got it, and the only body parts that are original now are the quarter panels, deck lid, rocker panels, roof, hood, and one rear frame rail/torque box.


I didn’t paint it, but I did everything else, which included replacing the entire undercarriage from the taillight panel to the core support with all new Dynacorn parts, all new suspension pieces, a complete new interior, rebuilding the engine and four-speed toploader, installing the drivetrain, glass, body trim, etc.
