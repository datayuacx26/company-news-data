# Original Owner: Don Knop’s Mustang Stable Started With a Blue GT in ’65

While the Mustang GTD is generating lots of buzz for its staggering $325,000 starting price and record-setting[6:40.385 lap time](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/ford-mustang-gtd-competition-nurburgring-time/) around Germany’s Nürburgring, Mustang history offers a number that it will never top: *1.3 million* . That’s how many 1965-1966 Mustangs Ford sold. The early Mustang’s sales averaged about the same per month as the current model moves in a year.


Think of the stories those million-plus cars could tell. Don Knop’s was one. With 323,000 miles on the clock and more coming, the Nebraska man’s 1965 Mustang GT served as a daily driver, family car, salesman’s car, and, briefly, an autocrosser.


“It was the car I had when my wife, Carol, and I first dated,” Don said. “We drove it in our wedding in 1969 and took it to California on our honeymoon. Back then, the speed limit in Nevada was ‘reasonable and proper,’ so of course we drove 90 all the way through the state.”


Don and Carol Knop drove the Mustang in their June 1969 wedding and to their California honeymoon.


Courtesy of Don Knop


A Montgomery-Ward air conditioner Don installed before that long trip caused the car to overheat, so when they returned, he installed a custom three-core radiator. The Mustang then made numerous trips to California for visits to see Don’s grandmother, along with family trips to Kansas City. Knop even towed snowmobiles up to Centennial, Wyoming, a couple of times. Over his 61 years with the Mustang, Don has rebuilt its 289-cubic-inch V-8 three times; replaced rusted floors, frame rails, and body panels; painted it and replaced the interior twice, and updated the chassis as needed.


Knop rebuilt the Mustang’s engine a third time in 2019, along with the engine compartment.


Courtesy of Don Knop


Knop towing snowmobiles to Centennial, Wyoming in 1975.


Courtesy of Don Knop


Don replaced all floorpans in 1991 and the rear frame rails and other parts in 1992. He gave the car its second repaint that year.


Courtesy of Don Knop


“I always did all the mechanical and body work myself in my two-stall garage, including the painting,” he said. “Although I made some improvements to make my ’65 more fun and easier to drive, I’ve kept the overall appearance the same as it was when I drove it off the showroom floor.”


For the Mustang’s most recent rebuild in 2019, Don rebuilt the engine for the third time, replaced rusted inner fenders, replaced many suspension parts, and installed rack-and-pinion steering. For this rebuild, he had a deadline to meet. “I had promised Carol I would have it off the jack stands by her 71st birthday. I took it off at 11 p.m., so I just made it,” he said.


Don kept a promise to have the Mustang finished by his wife Carol’s birthday in October 2019.


Courtesy of Don Knop


Sadly, Carol passed away in October 2024. An 80th birthday party that she’d planned for Don at the Museum of American Speed next to Speedway Motors in Lincoln, Nebraska, went on in 2025 as a tribute to her, with 200 people attending. “I still have all those fond memories of her in our Mustang,” shared Don.


The ’65 GT is one of three Mustangs in Don’s fleet. A[1966 Shelby GT350H](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/legends-of-the-gt350h-fact-fiction-and-hertzs-rent-a-racer/) he bought in 1969 served as a daily driver and an autocrosser for many years. It’s one of the 85 or so built with the four-speed. He bought a 2007 Shelby GT in 2009, and still takes to the cones with his daughter Kellie and grandson Max.


Don, daughter Kellie, and grandson Max all autocross Don’s ’07 Shelby GT.


Courtesy of Don Knop


There’s a fourth Mustang in the family. Forty years ago, Don and Carol bought their son, Corey, a 1965 GT when he was in high school. Don restored it a couple of times, most recently in 2023 as a restomod.


## **From Corvair to Mustang**


Knop has done all the mechanical, body, paint, and interior work on his Mustang for 61 years.


Courtesy of Don Knop


While attending Southeast Community College in Milford, Nebraska, Don stopped his ’63[Corvair Monza Spyder](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/klockau-classics/this-1964-chevrolet-corvair-monza-spyder-offers-turbo-charged-action-with-sport-to-spare/) in its tire tracks in October 1965 when he saw an Arcadian Blue Mustang GT parked outside Milford Motors. It had just the options a 19-year-old gearhead would want: 225-hp A-code 289 cu.-in. four-barrel V-8, four-speed stick, GT Equipment, tinted glass, and white sidewall tires.


“Larry TeSelle was the salesman,” Don said. “He hopped in the passenger seat and let me take it for a drive. Milford was a small town, just a few blocks to the highway. I still remember running it through the gears going west out of town, then turning back and doing that again. I was hooked. I had to have that car.”


Knop’s Mustang was light on options, but it has the good ones.


Courtesy of Don Knop


The list price was $3032.15, including the $76.75 destination charge. If there was a discount during the early Mustang’s buying frenzy, it was probably because this car was a demo with some miles. Don does not remember exactly what he paid, but he said the dealer gave him $1500 for the turbocharged Corvair, and he borrowed the rest from his father, who ran the family farm.


“The Corvair was fun, but the Mustang was head-and-shoulders above it,” Don said. “The Mustang didn’t have power steering or power brakes, but I was young and strong, so that was OK.”


He began piling on the miles and tried the Mustang on a quarter-mile drag strip in Omaha, 75 miles away, where he says he narrowly lost to a Chevelle. He gave autocrossing a try on a course set up on a nearby go-kart track, an experience that sparked his passion for the sport.


## **Factory Basics**


1965 Ford Mustang fastback in front of the Ford Pavilion at the 1964 World’s Fair in New York.


Ford


The 1965 Mustang was one of the U.S. auto industry’s greatest acts of redemption, arriving less than five years after Ford had mercifully put its biggest failure, the Edsel, out to pasture. Ford had, however, already discovered the value of market segmentation, first with the 1958 four-seat luxury Thunderbird, then the compact Falcon for 1960, and then the intermediate Fairlane two years later.


Championed by Ford Division General Manager Lee Iacocca, product planners pursued a low-priced sporty coupe to capture an emerging “youth market.” Basing the new model on the inexpensive Falcon helped sell the idea to the boss, Henry Ford II, who imposed a low and strict budget.


The Mustang’s spring 1964 arrival caught the initial wave of baby boomers graduating high school that year. Yet, the sporty-looking car instantly appealed to all ages, and Ford saw demand quickly exceed projections. By the following spring, three U.S. plants were building Mustangs.


Ford


The first Mustang’s $2400 base price got the buyer an irresistible design with an upscale bucket-seat interior. About a hundred dollars more replaced the sleepy inline-six with a peppy V-8. Adding one of the 289 cu.-in. four-barrel V-8s allowed the desirable $168 GT Equipment option that added dual exhaust, special handling suspension, quicker steering ratio, front disc brakes, grille-mounted fog lights, and a five-dial instrument cluster. The package dressed the exterior with chrome “trumpet” tailpipe extensions poking through the valance panel, rocker panel stripes, and GT badges. The chrome styled steel wheels were a separate option, which the dealer added to this car.


The top-performance option was the K-code 271-hp HiPo 289, though it was expensive and not commonly ordered. The Mustang quickly established racing cred within Ford’s “Total Performance” program. A specially prepared trio entered in the 1964 10-day, 4000-mile[Tour de France Automobile](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/ford-mustang-success-at-the-1964-tour-de-france/) brought home a class victory. Caroll Shelby’s GT350 thrilled driving purists on the street, and the[GT350R](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/1965-shelby-gt350r-an-old-friend-heads-to-market-again/) would clinch the SCCA B-Production road racing championship in 1965-1967.


Most Mustang buyers were not racers but simply captivated by the affordable “baby Thunderbird,” as Iacocca once described the car.


## **Building His First Car at 12 Years Old**


Left: 12-year-old Don (standing) in June 1957 with the go-kart he built. He would drive five miles to visit best friend Kenny (at the wheel). Right: Don’s Grandson Max in the same kart a few years ago.


Courtesy of Don Knop


Don began wrenching on cars when he built his own at age 12. “I was kind of a C student in school,” he said. “I just couldn’t remember the assignments. But I was very good, mechanically.”


Inspired by photos of Soapbox Derby racers he saw in a magazine, he found a 1×12 board on the family farm to use as a main chassis member. He had some fun coasting down a hill that ran from his dad’s workshop, past the chicken house, tractor shed, and barn.


“But then I had to turn around and pull it back up the hill,” he said.


His father fixed that, buying him a used garden tiller with a three-quarter horsepower Briggs & Stratton motor, including a frame and band clutch.


“I did just about all the work mounting the motor on that one-by-twelve,” he said.


For his car’s first test run, the boy started the motor and engaged the clutch with a hand lever he’d made. After shooting across the concrete floor and out of the workshop, the little car crossed some ruts in the ground, breaking its 1×4 wood rear axle.


“When my dad came home from the field, he saw the damage and helped me put a two-by-four axle in,” Don said. “I had so much fun with that car. I would drive it to my friend Kenny’s house about five miles away. I still have it at the farm in the hog shed.”


## **Mustang Family**


Don bought his ’66 Shelby GT350H in 1969 as a daily driver and started autocrossing it in 1971. Seen here in 1972.


Courtesy of Don Knop


Don bought his Shelby GT350H from a used car dealer for $2100 in March 1969, three months before marrying Carol, a schoolteacher. The car needed some mechanical work, which he did himself. The ’65 GT became Carol’s daily driver, and Don used the Shelby to commute to his job as a tool-and-die maker in Lincoln. He got serious about autocrossing the Shelby in 1971 and continued with it for over 20 years.


A new Ford Elite replaced the Shelby as a daily driver in 1976. Working for Lincoln Machine, Don moved up from machinist to manager, and when a new buyer took over, they asked him to take his knowledge and experience on the road as a salesman. His territory covered central Nebraska and Iowa, the state where he’d lived as a young boy.


He loved driving the ’65 Mustang and used it for many of his sales trips. “My customers liked to see me drive up in the car,” he said.


The Mustang in the Milford, Nebraska car show in 1979.


Courtesy of Don Knop


Don retired the Mustang from regular driving in 1992, with 279,000 miles. He then spent a year, on and off, replacing the rear frame rails, trunk floors, and other rusted areas. He also replaced the rear springs and gave the car its second repaint, this time with base coat/clear coat.


He retired the ’66 Shelby from competition in the 1990s, replacing it with a ’94 Camaro Z28 bought from a friend and then racing it for 14 years. Don nearly retired from autocrossing after that but did take the ’66 Shelby out of its own retirement to run in the 2008 Nationals in Topeka Kansas in its old class with modern Mustangs.


Don took his Shelby out of its retirement to run it in the 2008 SCCA Autocross Nationals in Topeka, Kansas against some newer Mustangs.


Courtesy of Don Knop


When the SCCA moved the Autocross Nationals to Lincoln Air Park in 2009, Don couldn’t resist jumping back in, buying and prepping a 2007 Shelby GT. Shelby America’s Las Vegas facility added Ford Racing bolt-ons, including a cold-air induction setup, more aggressive ECU calibration, performance exhaust, and suspension upgrades to the stock Mustang GT foundation. Those Shelbys, along with Mustang GTs with the same parts added, became dominant in SCCA F-Stock.


“We’re having a ton of fun competing in autocrosses, and against each other,” Don said about the family racers. “Kellie raced it in national championships for years. Toward the end of last year, Max was consistently getting better times than his mom and me.”


Don said his Shelby GT served as a template for the SCCA’s Club Spec Mustang class, including a slew of allowable suspension upgrades. The single rule set allows one car to participate in Autocross, Time Trials, RoadRally, and RallyCross.


17-year-old Max has been getting faster than his mom and grandfather in the Shelby.


Courtesy of Don Knop


At the Nebraska Region SCCA Annual Awards Banquet last March, Don accepted trophies for winning the autocross Master Class (for ages 62 and up), along with his fifth regional Autocross Driver of the Year award for having the most points across all classes. The group also surprised him with the Jerry Doctor Lifetime Achievement Award, named for the man instrumental in getting all the SCCA programs set up in the region and making the region run smoothly.


Since 2019, Don has allowed the[Museum of American Speed](https://www.museumofamericanspeed.org/) to keep his ’66 Shelby in an SCCA racing display. He takes it out a few times a year to drive and perform some maintenance.


Knop’s ’66 Shelby is on display at the Museum of American Speed in Lincoln, Nebraska.


Courtesy of Don Knop


## **Father and Son Mustang Reunion**


In March 2022, Don offered to help Corey get his ’65 GT back on the road. It had been in storage near Don’s home for eight years after Corey moved to Colorado. Don expected to freshen the engine and do a bit of fix-up but quickly realized it needed much more.


“One thing led to another, and then the only thing left on the body was a few wires under the dash,” he said. “This was by far my most extensive restoration.”


Corey’s Mustang emerged in June 2023 fully renewed and upgraded, including four-wheel disc brakes. They were needed to rein in the BluePrint 302 with Holly Sniper fuel injection that dyno’d at 409 horsepower. Don did all the mechanical and body work, plus painting prep. A friend did the final painting in his body shop. Don made a fun discovery during the work: Corey’s car was No. 675506, and his was 674248, just 1258 cars apart.


“Ford was building around 1500 1965 Mustangs each day,” Don said. “Corey’s car was probably built the same day or within a day of mine.”


Don restored his son Corey’s ’65 GT (right) in 2023 and then followed him 500 miles to Colorado in his own ’65.


Courtesy of Don Knop


After the restoration, Corey flew to Lincoln to retrieve his revitalized car. The next morning, father and son drove their ’65 Mustangs 500 miles to Niwot, Colorado.


“What a fun trip that was,” he said.


Don never misses a chance to drive his blue ’65 GT in good weather, and he plans to keep on going.
