# When Broken Down in the Boonies, Get Creative and Rely on Community

A good friend of mine is a retired executive with, as expected, strong executive skills. He has applied himself to organizing bi-annual road tours for his small group of car enthusiasts that make a three-day loop up and down the Appalachian Mountains. This has been going on for years, and I was invited to join the group about six years ago.


The rule used to be that you had to bring a “classic” car, and in 2021 my 1991 BRG Miata qualified, being over 30 years old. This was a bit of a bitter pill for me to swallow, because it feels like about three years ago that I was looking at[clay models of Miatas](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/the-first-hand-story-of-the-first-mazda-miata/) in the design studio… this is how time compresses exponentially once you age past the half-century mark.


Scott C.


Requiring classic cars for the tour invites a sort of danger for the group, in that not all the cars might make it home. Particularly for our British and Italian classics, there tends to be a “not going gently into that dark night” decaying halo around the electrical and coolant and braking, etc. systems in these older classic cars. Crankshafts and pistons rarely break, but ancillary support systems such as ignition points and slave cylinders are clearly at risk. Nonetheless, we optimistically pull them out of their garages and put them into a three-day trial-by-fire (well, hopefully not), lest they be labeled “garage queens.” On top of our cockeyed optimism, being members of the Hagerty Driver’s Club with towing privileges emboldens us further.


Scott C.


One such trip started with two first-generation Miatas (no real risk here), a Porsche 993 (similarly, we can trust new-ish Bosch parts to make the journey), a BMW 3 Series coupe, an old Alfa Romeo Spider, a ‘71 Porsche 911S, and a 1958 Austin Healey Bugeye Sprite that had been thoroughly worked over. Like a middle school dodgeball team, we had a mix of strong and weak players to contend with.


Scott C.


The trip was to traverse two-lane roads wandering through the Appalachian Mountains where cell coverage was spotty at best. To wit, all of us carried spare parts and tools in anticipation of some sort of roadside episodes. The logic for this game is “if you bring it, you won’t need it,” so mission-critical systems such as fuel and ignition were backed up with NASA-level parts redundancies.


Scott C.


The Alfa was the first casualty, not even making it out of the hotel parking lot that Saturday morning. Some errant crack in the aged cast aluminum oil pump housing had emerged on the first leg and caused most of the black lubricant to puke itself onto the tarmac overnight while we slumbered. Despite lots of JB Weld and prayers, it was not to be, and time of death was called early Saturday morning. Like soldiers getting ammunition off wounded compatriots, we scavenged the Alfa’s trunk for repair items we might need for the remaining cars. Not polite, but prudent.


Scott C.


We set off with the remaining fleet, the Alfa driver now resigned to a passenger seat as ballast. The weather was perfect, the roads were clear, and the tour began in earnest. It being Spring, there was a bit of roadkill to contend with and we quickly came up with a metric for ground clearance in the group. The Bugeye was good for one squirrel in the road, but not the average crow. The two Porsches could clear a crow if you winced, but not a possum. The BMW was easily good for a possum but not a fawn, sad as that sight was. Speed through the corners was inversely proportional to the size of the carrion you could straddle, so the Bugeye won that test every time.


Our code was to stop at each stop sign or stoplight that involved an actual turn, so as to allow the group, well, to re-group. Between the slight traffic and our individual appetites for aggressive driving (largely within the generous speed limits), this meant that every thirty minutes or so we collapsed the rolling Slinky of cars. As a rule, our tolerance for waiting is around ten minutes. At an average speed of 50 mph, ten minutes equaled just over eight miles, and that is about as far as someone should fall behind.


Scott C.


As it came to be, one stop lasted fifteen minutes with no sign of our buddy and his six-figure-value ’71 911. Was it off the road due to inertia and bad choices? Had some 50-year-old system failed the owner (brain- or car-related)? Conjectures only lead to stress, so we generally hope for the best and then go looking. As mentioned, there is no cell coverage in most of these areas, which greatly exacerbates any rescue operation. Not only can you not call for mechanical assistance from your friends, but you also can’t call a tow truck. It is like being back in 1971, except that this 911 would have been more reliable back when the Lunar Lander was still under warranty.


We sent out our own Donner-party-esque rescue mission: Two of us in separate cars backtracked our route, hoping to not find the 911 in a ditch. These early Porsches are known to be “doctor killers” (as were Beechcraft Bonanzas back in the day) due to very poor “lift throttle” dynamics in fast sweeping corners. The simple trailing arm suspension would cause the outside tire to toe-out on lift throttle engine braking, and the high rear weight bias would slingshot the back of the car off of the road. Experienced 911 pilots developed enlarged anatomical features to be able to keep their gas pedal depressed (no matter how much it spooked their inner core) in a fast corner to keep this demon from pitching them off of the road in a snap-oversteer maneuver.


Scott C.


After a 20-minute search, we found our friend and his concours-condition jade green 911 safely on the side of the road, all body panels intact, much to our relief. It was the car and thankfully not the driver, that had a self-inflicted wound and needed attention. A quick interview revealed that his alternator light had come on and he had the sense to pull over and wait for help. Since early 911s have a common belt running the alternator and the cooling fan, it would have been foolhardy to try to drive on the battery alone—if it were his belt that had broken, he would have seized his high-compression air-cooled engine in short order.


Inspection of the engine revealed that the alternator/fan belt was not connected to the upper pulley, largely because the upper pulley was missing. Its core was still bolted to the common shaft for the fan/alternator, but the outer V-belt pulley rim part was nowhere to be seen. Luckily, air-cooled Porsches have sheet metal all around the engine bay, intended to keep hot undercar air from reaching the cooling fan’s upper intake. This sheet metal has the parallel oft-used function of catching parts that fall off of the top of Porsche engines, in this case catching the shattered perimeter of an aftermarket alternator pulley installed by the previous owner. I cited this failed pulley in an earlier article on the[endurance limit of aftermarket parts.](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/trust-but-verify-every-car-part-has-its-limit/)


We were hundreds of miles from home and repair facilities, and deep out of cell coverage, so towing was not an option. Being on the side of the road in the middle of nowhere in an expensive classic car is a pretty bad situation; you can’t just leave it there overnight unattended. We let the engine cool off as much as ambient temps would allow and gently drove it about a mile to the first spot we could find to park it off of the road. This turned out to be a motel made up of a collection of vacation cabin-ettes, so we found a spot and told the front desk we’d be parking a collector car there for a few hours while we made a repair. Stage one of the fix was underway—the car was safe and we could focus our bandwidth on the repair.


Scott C.


Back assembled as a group, we put our collective executive skills to work on how to fix this seemingly simple problem. Four of us were degreed engineers, so we were not going to let this be a stumbling block to our weekend tour. Towing the car home was out of the question, as it would be an admission of failure from the get-go. Getting a new pulley was out of the question this far into the boondocks. Somehow, we needed to repair the failed aluminum pulley.


One of the more observant non-engineers reminded us that a snow ski resort was just a few miles away, and conjectured that they must have a maintenance shop with some sort of tools that might help. Finding a TIG welder in the boonies is a long shot, but this was a very good plan given our options. A few minutes later, as the sun set over the forests surrounding us, we arrived at the ski resort en masse with the remaining running cars. It was well past skiing season, but we were hoping there was a skeleton crew around.


Showing up in an eclectic group of sports cars is a good calling card when you are trying to flush out a car guy in any location. It was easy to find the security guard—because he was a huge man in a small golf cart that looked like a circus act involving a bear. This very large and Sasquatch-ian guard listened to our plight, and with barely a word, quietly led us to a kindred spirit right away at the maintenance shop of this resort. We held the fractured aluminum pieces in front of the mechanic and pleaded our case.


Lucky us. I have found over my decades of being around car/airplane/motorcycle guys (and gals) that we all share a certain pluckiness when it comes to solving common mechanical problems. There is some sort of excitement when things break, because you get to fix them, as odd as that sounds. Frankly, without this pluckiness, this hobby would be far too frustrating, since old machines come with their fair share of things to attend to.


Scott C.


Scott C.


Our new friend sadly stated that he did not have a TIG (Tungsten Inert Gas) welder suitable for aluminum welding, and that his MIG (Metal Inert Gas) welder did not have compatible wire for the same, so we were getting nowhere. He did have confidence in brazing it, however, so we reassembled the fractured center hub into the pulley’s outer ring and Dremel-ed it clean. Sadly we did not know what alloy the aftermarket amateurs used for this pulley, but whatever it was it would not accept brazing no matter how hard he worked, skilled as he was. It looked like a dead end.


Scott C.


Scott C.


We started brainstorming about alternate pulleys or pulleys that could be adapted to fit, just to get the car back to our hotel base. After thirty minutes of discussion and silly ideas, the huge-but-silent security guard piped up from the dark back corner of the room and quietly stated that his uncle had two junk Karmann Ghias in his barn. We all looked at each other and smiled. This was to be our path forward; we were certain.


There is an old saying in the automotive engineering world: If a German engineer has a problem, he throws 100 years of history at it; if an American engineer has a problem, he throws a $100 at it; if a Japanese engineer has a problem, he throws 100 junior engineers at it; if an Italian engineer has a problem, he goes to lunch…


Scott C.


Knowing that Porsches and Volkswagens share more than a little DNA (both being originated by Dr. Porsche Sr. himself), there was more than a remote chance that a Karmann Ghia alternator pulley would fit a 911 alternator shaft. Why redesign a shaft once it has proven itself in service—this is a common and useful philosophy in German automaking. A common shaft means a common pulley mounting hub. Our newest friend drove over to his relative’s barn and soon returned with a dusty steel alternator pulley from a mid-seventies Karman Ghia. And, lo and behold, it bolted right up to the Porsche alternator as expected.


The belt was not suited for the odd diameter of the VW pulley, and it was O’-dark-thirty by this point, so the next morning we visited a nearby[Tractor Supply](https://www.tractorsupply.com/) store to find a wide V belt that was around 2 inches longer in length (the regular auto parts stores didn’t have one wide enough). A bright green John Deere belt turned out to fit perfectly, and the assembly of all of these parts was a thing to behold.


Rob S.


Scott C.


Our friend’s precious 911 was back on the road and continued the tour and even made it through the three-hour trip back home without incident. The correct parts were ordered and installed, and now his high-value car is back to OE levels of reliability, rescued by a broken VW hiding in a barn deep in Appalachia.


So, if you ever find yourself in need of a roadside repair for your classic, think out of the box and listen to even the most unlikely solutions—sometimes the answer comes from the quietest corner of the room.


Scott C.
