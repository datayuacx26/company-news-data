# Against All Oddities: My Eagle is a Nest of Surprises

My half-lifelong dream of procuring an AMC Eagle has been realized,[as you may have read](https://r.search.yahoo.com/_ylt=AwrhdgTfomBqLAIAm7tXNyoA;_ylu=Y29sbwNiZjEEcG9zAzEEdnRpZAMEc2VjA3Ny/RV=2/RE=1785927647/RO=10/RU=https%3a%2f%2fwww.hagerty.com%2fmedia%2fopinion%2fagainst-all-oddities%2fagainst-all-oddities-eagle-has-landed%2f/RK=2/RS=KIOuZLIhib1gGt4x2bOFORZmYWY-) . When it finally came, fulfilment came more easily than I thought, figuratively rolling out of its nest and falling to my feet. Of course, there was a catch: It had been heavily tinkered with. ‘Round these parts, that’s a risk worth taking.


During my 17 years of pining for an Eagle, I was aiming for one in completely stock form: a Sport model wagon with a five-speed. I’m now convinced that nearly all such examples are dead or stashed away in some Rambler Ranch scenario (or owned by commenters), because I don’t remember the last time I saw an Eagle on the road.


It was a crime of opportunity to take a modified one, and I originally wasn’t too enthusiastic about dealing with someone else’s work. I have had a bit of time now to reconcile myself to what I’ve purchased and analyze the degree of buyer’s remorse. Results: precious little remorse, really. The Eagle has been a relatively constant supply of pleasant surprises and sighs of relief. As I merge onto I-77 at this very moment, I am connected to Bluetooth while my phone charges wirelessly and I’m verbally feeding article notes into my phone. At present, my chances of getting home seem pretty damn high. Expectations exceeded, indeed.


What are the baseline expectations, you may be asking, for a $1500 effed-with Eagle? For starters… low. I certainly didn’t expect that I’d be able to commute in it on days when I actually have meetings in the morning and things to do in the evening. I mean, I packed a rudimentary toolkit just for good measure (and to ward off the evil spirits), but, knock on wood grain, I haven’t needed it.


So, as I’ve started looking into this thing more deeply, what have been the good and bad surprises? Plop into these mismatched, tufted leather seats for a rundown.


## The Eagle Build Book


First of all, this car came with a build book—a nice leather-bound one, actually. It’s even the same color as the interior. Was this intentional? I think so. The content inside it reminds me of a much more condensed version of the documentation that Herr Bender provided when I purchased the Hobby 600, aka the camper van my wife and I later lived in. If there are as many quirky nuances to this thing as that coachbuilt Italian camper van, I’m screwed.


Furthermore, I see notes from a string alignment, some hand-drawn wiring diagrams, circuit diagrams for an amp, and evidence of a ton of very thorough brake work that I had no idea was done. Not having to deal with brakes in the future is a major plus. Oh, and it’s got a front locker and part number callouts and lash settings for the front and rear diff rebuilds! Oh, and the rear end has been swapped from the dinky AMC 20 unit to a Chrysler 8¼-inch rear end from a Jeep Cherokee.


Three cheers for care *and* documentation at a $1500 price point! I will endeavor to continue where the seller left off.

## Comfort and Practicality


As you’d expect from anything with couch-like seating, this thing is insanely comfortable. The front seats are like recliners, and the power steering is feather-light despite the massive tires. I must admit, though, that I’m absolutely roasting while in the car week. Outside temperature has been in the mid-90s, with all the humidity you’d expect of a foothills North Carolina climate. Every day, I’ve had to take a cold shower before bed.


In the good-news column, I do have four functioning electric windows that go down and back up—twice as fast as any other electric window I’ve ever seen. They’re borderline dangerous. The Realtree camouflage-covered sun visors help a little bit, but, God save me, I will need some air conditioning in this thing. I did reach out to the previous owner, and he said that he still had the condenser in his garage, which he’d be happy to fork over.

On the subject of general practicality, the flat-folding seats are really handy, though the three-inch lift makes it a little difficult to get things into the relatively high hatch opening. That being said, I’m unwilling to sacrifice the sexiest taillight design of the 1980s for less heaving. The Eagle has a very low profile and a sturdy roof rack designed for it; details in the build book show cut lengths for Uni-strut industrial cabinet-making material. The low-slung nature of the Eagle makes this usable even when lifted.


In other positive tidings, the two-inch receiver that I figured was solely for looks is backed by a solid piece of I-beam bolted to the frame. It’s a very clean installation, actually. I will be using that later tonight to pull a fallen tree across my yard.


What a find.


## **On to the powertrain**


I can confirm that the oil leak the previous owner disclosed is, in fact, no better or worse than expected. I still have no idea where it’s coming from, but the level on the dipstick moves appreciably during my commute.


The first day I drove it to work, a crowd formed around the AMC. We all sort of agreed that the leakage came from a combination of the valve cover and rear main seal, and that it would be prudent to first address the valve cover and then see if the leakage is reduced to a more sensible level.


Even with all four windows open, I smell that sweet, uncombusted oil mist and a very distinctive blow-by aroma. If I consult the big logical flowchart of what to do next in my brain: *IF blow-by AND rear main seal THEN… engine swap* .


[Matthew Anderson](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/manderson/)


Further digging through the build book shows me that, in addition to the camshaft, the timing set and head gasket were also done. That makes me happy, of course, but a little sad at the same time, because the chances of it being only a valve-cover leak are ultimately lower.


I have started adding “Jeep 4.0L manual transmission” to my saved searches.


Additionally, I have noticed that there is a five-speed Eagle SX/4 at a salvage yard, local to work, that has the Sport steering wheel and manual pedal assembly. I think I could get there and back during lunch.


## **Ok, So Now What?**


The plan is to keep driving it until the oil leak gets appreciably worse. Then I’ll decide what direction to go with the engine.


If I’m honest, a 4.0-liter swap using components from a low-mileage Jeep sounds pretty appealing, especially if it involves fuel injection. I suspect I won’t want to invest too much in the air-conditioning system until I know what serpentine drive I might be using. If I’m going to do all that, I wonder how much harder an LS swap would be? Or maybe a Toyota Supra 2JZ?


I’m just about home now. The heat and PCV fumes are perhaps polluting my thoughts. But a weekend-warrior engine swap might be in my not-so-distant future.
