# 2026 GMC Yukon AT4 Ultimate Review: Denali in Danner Boots

Recently, my wife was in the market for a vehicle to replace her beloved but 15-year-old V-6 Toyota RAV4. (I lost the argument to get a minivan, given our 1-year-old and second baby on the way.) Some kind of three-row family SUV made the most sense—a[Grand Highlander](https://www.hagerty.com/media/new-car-reviews/2024-toyota-grand-highlander-review-un-minivan/) or Mazda CX-90, perhaps. But in the interest of scientific rigor and thoroughness, I also borrowed a new 2026 Yukon AT4 Ultimate from GMC’s media fleet. A big, luxurious body-on-frame full-sizer with a six-figure window sticker was not on our radar, but this was a chance to experience what a much fatter budget could bring home.


Hagerty associate editor Nathan Petroelje wrote a phenomenal review of the[GMC Yukon Denali](https://www.hagerty.com/media/new-car-reviews/review-2021-gmc-yukon-denali/) back in 2020, when the current-generation car was new. His piece focused largely on the rig’s then-new independent rear suspension, praising its composed but cloud-like ride and declaring it the “best development to come to GM’s full-size trucks in many years.” Though the Yukon got a refresh for the 2025 model year, it was largely an aesthetic- and equipment-focused overhaul, which is where this review will mostly focus.


Chris Stark


Since the AT4 trim first debuted astride the Sierra 1500 pickup in 2019, GMC’s off-road sub-brand has propagated like Montana mansions. The Yukon got it in 2021, and these days, you can get an AT4 version of everything short of the Hummer EV and the Savanna van. The AT4 Ultimate is an all-new trim as of last year, bridging the gap between the off-roady AT4 and the all-singing, all-dancing Denali luxury machine. Suspension-wise, that means an adaptive air ride that can boost ground clearance from the standard 7.8 inches to 9.8 inches in a pinch, plus MagneRide dampers. The wheels are 20-inchers on chunky all-terrain tires, giving the AT4 Ultimate a distinctive stance compared with the Denali’s flashy 24s. Underneath you’ll also find a front skid plate and an electronic limited-slip differential to go with the standard 6.2-liter V-8 and 10-speed automatic transmission. Our specific test Yukon featured Night Vision ($2000), plus a Sterling Metallic paint finish ($495), for a total of $103,190 including destination fees.

With the AT4 Ultimate, GMC clearly wants to capitalize on the industry-wide demand for more rugged luxury vehicles, all somewhat adjacent to the[Land Rover Defender](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/new-defender-octa-offers-everything-everywhere-all-at-once/) formula. And why not bundle both characteristics into one trim? Together, the Denali and AT4 accounted for roughly half of all GMC U.S. retail sales in 2024, with the Denali accounting for 30 percent and the AT4 for 20 percent. “The AT4 trim has become a significant piece of GMC’s business since its debut,” a GMC spokesperson told me. “The AT4 and AT4 Ultimate trims have comprised nearly 20 percent of Yukon sales this year.”

So what are these big spenders getting for their dough? Refreshed Yukons all received redesigned front and rear ends, but like a boxy, 5800-pound Kinder egg, the most tantalizing goodies are inside. On top of the new tablet-like 16.8-inch infotainment screen and 11-inch all-digital cluster, the AT4 Ultimate adds an “Obsidian Rush” interior: three rows of full-grain leather seating (front-row occupants get 16-way controls with massaging functionality), contrast-color piping on the thrones, and Ash Burl wood accents everywhere you look. Not quite Denali levels of elite material quality, but the details are damn good—particularly the laser-etched topographical patterns on the open-pore wood. If Cadillac is looking to sway the Silicon-Valley rich with its haute-couture fabrics and top-notch tech, the AT4 Ultimate is after the Jackson Hole, ranch-chic rich. It works.

What a body-on-frame SUV like this really gives you is a sense of excess. It is bigger, wider, more spacious, and higher-riding than most daily situations demand. Exterior aesthetics aren’t proportionally pleasing, I admit—the Yukon AT4’s ride height and big tires exaggerate the sense that it is a bit too tall relative to how long it is.


I got over that pretty quickly. One day of kid-loading and grocery hauling, towering over most other members of traffic and never fearing for a moment that basic errands would overload the trunk space, a little voice in your head starts to whisper, “ *You could get used to this.* ” The second-row seats are genuinely adult-sized, and they individually slide to accommodate larger items or convenient ingress/egress. Even the third row shows little sign of material cost-cutting. When the in-laws visit, and you venture out for dinner, the Yukon can swallow everyone comfortably. It’s particularly useful that all second- and third-row passenger seats can be controlled electronically via the trunk access, which means you can avoid futzing around with opening doors and yanking latches in tight parking spaces.

GM’s 420-hp and 460-lb-ft small-block V-8 underscores the Yukon’s sense of ease. Torque is healthy and hits low in the rev range, delivering drama-free grunt and a pleasantly thunderous sound. It’s far from the most enthusiastic or vibrant application of this engine, though; the transmission is so eager to upshift that the revs never meaningfully rise unless you’re stomping on the throttle. It gives the impression of a somewhat insipid and uninspired powertrain, despite the topline numbers.


Chris Stark


It’s the ride quality that blows you away with this Yukon. MagneRide is magical, offering perpetual control and stability without entirely isolating the driver from the road. There’s a bit of side-to-side head toss typical for a big truck-platform SUV, but it’s never unsettling, and before long I was hurtling over speed bumps at 40 mph just to see at what point the suspension would protest. (It didn’t, so I gave up that gambit.)


Less satisfying is the steering, which is where this AT4 Ultimate setup falls flat. Steering is dead as roadkill until you’re 30 degrees or so off-center, and there seems to be a half-second delay before any inputs result in meaningful front-end response. Supple, precise steering, in concert with great suspension tuning, can create the illusion of driving a much smaller SUV—[Cadillac’s Escalade](https://www.hagerty.com/media/new-car-reviews/first-look-review-2023-cadillac-escalade-v-esv/) consistently manages this feat. The Yukon, however, handles like the massive rig that it is. I suspect the culprit here is squishy tires with acres of sidewall (275/60R20) and a pronounced ride height. Brakes are generally solid, except on downhill stretches where a fair bit more pedal travel and foot pressure than you might expect is necessary to stop the nearly three-ton Yukon’s momentum.

Where the AT4 Ultimate may find its stiffest competition is with the standard Yukon Denali, which costs about $84,000 to start. While that variant skips such niceties as the air suspension, 18-speaker Bose sound system, and SuperCruise driving assistance (some of this can be optioned à la carte), a Denali enjoys the benefit of… being able to show off and tell people you drive a Denali. That one word, like “Rolex,” is a shorthand for elite status, in a way that “AT4 Ultimate” or “Grand Seiko Evolution 9” don’t as readily convey. Or, if you’re a real baller, there’s always the Denali Ultimate that can cost many thousands more than our test vehicle, simultaneously making sure people know it via unmissable 24-inch wheels.


Chris Stark


Alas, my wife and I enjoyed a bit of dress-up but opted not to sell our house and buy a GMC Yukon AT4 Ultimate. It’s probably for the best. But I can nevertheless see the appeal, particularly if I lived in a secluded mansion with gravel- or dirt-road access—and wanted to race up the driveway at lurid speed without waking the toddler asleep in the back seat. GMC likely sees its AT4 and Denali customers as belonging to distinct buckets, and the AT4 Ultimate is a way to entice that more off-road audience to spend a bit more. It’s savvy, it’s smart, and it leverages the current-generation Yukon’s inherent polish and sophistication when it comes to the chassis. No doubt we’ll see GMC rolling out this formula to several other models in due time.


## 2026 GMC Yukon AT4 Ultimate


**Highs:** Astoundingly polished ride. Distinctive interior that does a more-than-decent Denali impression. Highly functional as a family vehicle if you have 3+ kids and can’t possibly imagine buying a minivan.


**Lows:** Lifeless steering. Powertrain could use a little more sparkle. Can’t tell people you drive a Denali.


**Takeaway:** With the AT4 Ultimate, GMC has found another savvy way to slice the trim pie. This combo of ruggedness and luxury should sell well.
