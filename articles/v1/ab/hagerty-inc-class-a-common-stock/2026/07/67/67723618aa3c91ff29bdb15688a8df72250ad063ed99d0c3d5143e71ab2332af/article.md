# 5 Old Favorites Losing Value in a Cooling Market

We’re well into the driving season, and another update to Hagerty Valuation Tools is here. Last week we discussed cars that saw remarkable increases in value. Unsurprisingly, supercars were the clear winners… again. The rest of the collector car market tells a completely different story, though.


Our[Hagerty Market Rating](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/the-collector-car-market-continues-to-cool-as-summer-heats-up/) continued to soften, falling to its lowest rating since 2011, all while well-established enthusiast vehicles cooled in value. Among those, here are five of the most significant (as calculated by average across all conditions).


*Have questions about how we arrived at these changes? You can read more about the methodology behind the Hagerty Price Guide[here](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/how-the-hagerty-price-guide-is-made/) .*


## 1976-1986 Jeep CJ-7 -22%


Jeep


The Jeep CJ-7, produced from 1976 to 1986, represented a significant evolution of the CJ line while staying true to the utilitarian, open-air off-road experience that had made the nameplate an American institution since the post-war years. Compared to its predecessor, the CJ-5, the CJ-7 featured a stretched wheelbase of 93.4 inches, improving interior room, ride comfort, and high-speed stability without compromising the vehicle’s off-road agility. Engine options over the years ranged from a 151-cid four-cylinder and 258-cid six-cylinder up to the AMC-sourced 304 V-8. New to the line was a three-speed automatic transmission, a choice which likely ruffled a few feathers in the day, but was a commercially smart move, broadening the model’s appeal beyond the hardcore off-road crowd. The CJ-7 also famously spawned the Renegade and Golden Eagle appearance packages, which leaned into the era’s love of bold graphics and decals, making them eye-catching icons of late-seventies truck culture. Today the CJ-7 occupies a special place in the hearts of Jeep enthusiasts. Rugged enough to be a potent trail machine, simple enough to be wrenched on by an amateur, and with unmistakable styling to turn heads anywhere.


The CJ’s popularity and importance are undeniable, which makes a 22-percent average drop noteworthy. Earlier in the year, we observed mixed results, with examples changing hands above and below recommended values. This latest update has brought a clear shift, with values solidly on the decline—CJs have been transacting lower than expected, or simply failing to sell, or remaining on the market. Classic trucks and SUVs are a mature market by this point, so this drop represents a potential shift in what was previously the hottest segment of the hobby. The latest drop puts good-to-excellent examples of the CJ-7 back into the mid-teen-to-mid-$20,000 range on average. This represents a new opportunity for buyers who have been sidelined by the somewhat rapid upswing in CJ values over the past few years. But this is also a sign to watch the classic truck and SUV market for further movement or opportunities going forward.


## 1968-1972 Buick GS -18%


Buick


Buick’s Gran Sport reached the peak of its powers during the 1968-1972 model years, establishing itself as one of the most formidable and underappreciated muscle cars of the era. While Pontiac had the GTO and Chevrolet had the Chevelle SS, Buick offered something slightly more refined without sacrificing any of the brute force that defined the muscle car wars. The high-water mark of the GS line was the introduction of the 455-cid V-8. Producing a standard 350 horsepower, the 455 Buick’s claim to fame was its massive torque output. The top-tier Stage 1 package added 10 horsepower, but pushed torque to a staggering 510 lb-ft, making it one of the torquiest production cars of the era. Available as both a hardtop coupe and a convertible, the GS also offered the GSX package in 1970, which was available in either Saturn Yellow or Apollo White and added bold stripes, a rear spoiler, and front air dams. Buick’s reputation as an “old man’s brand” caused the GS to fly somewhat under the radar, but that same reputation has made well-optioned examples a relative bargain in the collector market compared to their Pontiac and Chevrolet counterparts.


The case of the Buick GS is a curious one; while much of the muscle car market has posted mixed results, the GS is the only one that presented a clear direction. Just about every example we tracked on the market sold for a reduced price from what we’ve grown accustomed to seeing. From standard Gran Sports to the GSX, they all experienced a drop. This brings up the question of whether this is an isolated change or a sign of more drops to come across GM A-Bodies or beyond. Only time will tell whether the Buick GS represents an early warning sign or simply an anomaly in an otherwise stable market. What is clear is that buyers are becoming more discerning, and the days of any muscle car commanding a premium simply by virtue of what it is may be fading. For collectors looking to sell or opportunities to buy, the GS’s performance on the market warrants attention.


## 1950-1967 Volkswagen Type 2 Transporter -16%


Volkswagen


Produced from 1950 to 1967, the Type 2 Volkswagen is one of the most iconic and culturally resonant vehicles ever built. Conceived by Ben Pon, a Dutch VW importer who sketched the original design on a notepad in 1947, the Type 2 was built on the same rear-engine, air-cooled platform as the beloved Type 1 Beetle. Available in a vast array of configurations. Including the single- and double-cab pickups, utilitarian Kombi, and the Samba with its panoramic windows. The Type 2 was as practical as it was charming, offering businesses and families alike a versatile, economical hauler. Produced initially in Wolfsburg and later at a dedicated plant in Hanover. By the late 1960s, the van had been adopted as an unlikely symbol of the counterculture movement, its rounded, friendly shape making it synonymous with the era’s spirit of freedom and wanderlust; a connection so strong that it defines the vehicle’s legacy to this day.


We discussed the VW Transporter in this context not long ago, and here we are again. The 16 percent average drop is fully representative of the top-tier Sambas. Over the years, these things exploded in value, commanding well over $200,000 for a minty example. Nowadays, Sambas are expensive but below the $200,000 threshold. You can still expect to drop close to six figures for a good one, which is indeed properly expensive. The real news here is that not every expensive vehicle is thriving in the current climate, and even established collector models are struggling. The Samba’s slide is a reminder that no market, however storied, is immune to gravity. For years, these buses defied logic alongside their price tags, but the current climate is proving that passion alone cannot prop up enormous asking prices indefinitely.


## 1970-1972 Chevrolet Corvette LT1 -15%


Chevrolet


Available from 1970-1972,[LT1-equipped Corvettes](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/1970-72-chevrolet-corvette-lt-1-market-spotlight/) represent one of the most driver-focused configurations in the early history of America’s sports car. While the big block engines of the era receive much of the attention, the LT1 was the purist’s choice. (Quick aside: GM naming conventions are a bit convoluted. The LT1 package was an RPO, or Regular Production Option, and had no dash. The engine of the same name that it came with *did* have a dash.) Speaking of, these Corvettes came equipped with a high-revving, LT-1 350-cid small block that produced a factory-rated 370 horsepower in 1970. (Rated power dropped in subsequent years due to the way horsepower was measured as well as a compression drop in 1971). Developed with heavy input from Zora Arkus-Duntov, the LT-1 featured solid lifters, an aggressive camshaft, 11:1 compression ratio, and a large Holley four-barrel carburetor sitting atop an aluminum intake. This yielded potent performance while offering better weight balance that big-block Corvettes simply couldn’t match in terms of overall driving dynamics. Production numbers were relatively modest—fewer than 5000 LT-1 Corvettes were built across the three model years—which has made them the most desirable non-big-block C3s.


It was less than a year ago that we were discussing big gains for LT1 Corvettes, but the tables have turned. Over the past quarter, several great examples have hit the market and sold at prices well below expectations or failed to sell at all. Depending on year, this has put driver-quality examples at or below the $50,000 mark and in the $60,000-$70,000 range for a show-grade car. Whether this represents a correction of a sharp increase earlier or the beginning of a more sustained trend for early C3 Corvettes remains to be seen. What is clear is that the window has closed to cash in on the bump and enthusiasts who sat on the sidelines may have a new opportunity to jump in and snatch up an LT1 ‘Vette at a better price.


## 1966-1977 Ford Bronco -12%


Ford


The first-generation Ford Bronco, produced from 1966 to 1977, was Ford’s answer to the growing demand for compact, capable off-road vehicles. Positioned as a direct competitor to the Jeep CJ and International Scout, and designed by engineer Donald Frey, the Bronco was built on a short 92-inch wheelbase that gave it exceptional maneuverability on rugged terrain. Under the hood, buyers could choose from a straight-six or a 289 V-8 (later a 302), and the standard four-wheel-drive system made it a genuine go-anywhere machine right out of the gate. Over its first generation, the Bronco earned an enthusiastic following among off-road enthusiasts and outdoorsmen alike.


First-generation Broncos have been some of the longest-appreciating vehicles we’ve tracked over the past decade. Over time, trucks have matured as collector vehicles, and we’ve likely hit the tipping point where most enthusiasts who wanted a Bronco as a driver or as a base for a restomod have picked one up, and competition is less stiff. The 12 percent average drop in values we observed in the past quarter represents a significant downward shift for these trucks. Don’t get us wrong, these are still pricey machines for what they are; expect to still plonk down $50,000 or more for a driver-grade example of a truck with all the complexity and comfort of an old Massey Ferguson tractor. Because Broncos have been market leaders for so long, this is one that we will be watching closely in the coming months for hints to where the broader collector car market is headed.
