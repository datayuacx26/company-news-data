# Why Are These Four Wolfpack Rides Customs the End of the Line?

They are only four cars in an auction that features hundreds. And while we firmly believe that every car has a story—that’s what we do here, after all—these four, called the Wolfpack Rides collection, may be over-the-top outrageous, but their combined story is, well, poignant.


They go up for sale this afternoon at the[Mecum Auction](https://www.mecum.com/) in Harrisburg, Pennsylvania. Owner Paul Carlevale will be there; he doesn’t live far away. He will be sorry to see these cars go, but they leave behind memories. Good ones, mostly.


Carlevale is 65 now, but like many of us, he fell in love with cars as a teenager. Not so much with the first one, a hand-me-down Volkswagen Beetle: “It traumatized me, you know? After that I had to have something cool.”


So it was promptly followed by a car that was more Carlevale’s style: A[Pontiac Can Am](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/1977-pontiac-can-am-gto-sticker-package/) , the 1977-only model offered only in white. They were powered by the same blisteringly powerful 6.6-liter T/A V-8 that came in my black and gold *Smokey and the Bandit* -like ‘77 Trans Am. It was rated at, um, (can this be right? 200 horsepower? Huh!). They sounded good, though.


Anyway, “That Can Am was the start of it all,” he said. He rebuilt the engine, made it go faster, and—this was a long time ago, kids, don’t judge!—”You remember when we used to jack the rear end up and paint it white? Use air shocks to get some elevation, and the cops would come along and measure how far the bumper was off the ground, and they told you it was illegal?” He laughs. Even over the phone, I know the look on his face.


“Back when I was a kid,” he says, “you learned to do everything. It was common then to be an automotive jack of all trades.” In high school, he took trade classes, and that continued afterward. “I enjoyed working with my hands. The joy of taking something and making it work, and making it yours, is kind of cool, you know?”


When he and the Can Am were done with each other, he moved on to a souped-up ‘68 Chevrolet Camaro, then a ’70 Chevy Nova, and that was followed by—well, lots of vehicles. Let’s fast-forward a bit in his life story.


Carlevale, who owns a construction company, was pleased when his two sons—Gus, the older one, and his little brother Dominic—showed a similar interest in the automotive world that he had. Together, they began building custom cars, right in their home’s garage. The cars were carefully constructed, not just quickly assembled to sell. “That’s not what this was about,” Paul says. It became a family thing, a bonding exercise. They built quite a few cars. Which one does Paul believe is their most successful? “I don’t think I’ve succeeded with any of them,” he says. “They’re all a work in progress.”


Not surprisingly, the age-appropriate sons were also quite internet-savvy, and about 10 years ago, Gus decided to begin posting about the builds online, under the name Wolfpack Rides. “It was inspired by my oldest son, and I wanted to support him—of course, supporting him in something that I love to do, too, isn’t that hard, right?” It took a while, but they gradually found an audience—107,000 subscribers on YouTube, 250,000 followers on Instagram, 147,000 followers on TikTok.


The most famous Wolfpack car is a blown, narrowed, 2000-horsepower ‘68 Mustang they nicknamed Helleanor, a tribute to the pair of “Eleanor” Mustangs that gained stardom in the 1974 movie *Gone in 60 Seconds* (a ’71 Mustang was used in that version) and again in the 2000 remake, where Nicholas Cage drove a ’67. A[33-second video](https://www.tiktok.com/@wolfpack_rides/video/7354391376877866283) of Helleanor just backing out of a garage has 6.6 million views on TikTok.


Atop[their Instagram page,](https://www.instagram.com/wolfpack_rides/) there’s an eight-car sample of their builds, including some of the cars that go to auction today.


But first, you should know this: After a run that lasted about 15 years, these are the last of the Wolfpack Rides customs. We’ll explain why in a minute.

My personal favorite of the offered-up four is a[1967 fastback Mustang named Rocket.](https://www.mecum.com/lots/1175007/1967-ford-mustang-custom-fastback/?aa_id=790805-0) It’s powered by a blueprinted 528-cubic-inch Ford big-block V-8 engine, topped with a new 8-71 blower. The transmission is a strengthened C4 automatic with a built-in trans brake, mated to a Strange 9-inch rear axle with 3.73 gearing. The interior is sparse but purposeful. The enormous barely-treaded rear tires, as well as the front tires, are mounted on Weld wheels. Paint is candy apple red, with white stripes.

And there’s[Zeus, a black 1969 Camaro convertible](https://www.mecum.com/lots/1175004/1969-chevrolet-camaro-custom-convertible/?aa_id=790810-0) . It was one of their earlier builds, which took nearly four years to complete. It isn’t as extreme as Rocket, but it’s a runner: The engine is a 707-cubic-inch V-8 with a Donovan aluminum block that somehow fits under the hood, mated to a Lenco four-speed manual transmission with a Gear Vendors overdrive. Inside, there’s Vintage air conditioning and reupholstered Dodge Viper seats. The leather covering the interior and roll bar is hand-stitched. The top is power-operated. Much of Zeus is hand-fabricated, which doesn’t show from a distance, but you can tell something doesn’t look quite normal about this Camaro, and I mean that in a very good way.

The most radical of the quartet is[Sinister, a 1970 Dodge Charger](https://www.mecum.com/lots/1175005/1970-dodge-charger-custom/?aa_id=790808-0) that looks the way the original big-winged, long-nosed Charger Daytona should have. Alliteratively speaking, it’s painted a popular but polarizing Plum Crazy Purple, and with 1100 horsepower at the rear wheels, it’s quicker than any of the original Daytona models, thanks to the 572-cubic-inch V-8 with a ProCharger F-3 supercharger, feeding a Rossler-tweaked GM Turbo 400 automatic transmission with an air shifter and a trans brake. The black leather bucket seats are from a Dodge Viper, and there’s air conditioning, front- and rear-facing cameras, and electric power steering.

Finally, this older-school[1969 Dodge Charger doesn’t have a nickname](https://www.mecum.com/lots/1175006/1969-dodge-charger-custom/?aa_id=790906-0) ; perhaps the buyer can think of one. The engine is a 440-cubic-inch V-8 with an 8-71 BDS blower and a beefed-up four-speed manual transmission with a McLeod dual-disc clutch. It also has a nitrous oxide system, but it has never been used. The rear axle is a Moser-modified Dana with 3.55 gears. There’s air conditioning with power steering and brakes. Outside, that’s a House of Kolor Tangerine and Solar Gold paint job.

These cars are “some of the best I’ve ever had,” Paul says. “I drive them with the confidence that I’m going to get there and get back home.” They average over a thousand horsepower, “Which is pretty stout, but we’ve managed it quite well.” The cars are built to stay cool even on hot days, “and they run right on the fuels available today.” They are tuned to like, if not love, E85. Daily drivability, he says, “all comes down to making good choices.”


Paul gave up racing long ago, preferring to drive his cars on the street. But if he meets somebody at a red light, “I’ve got enough to keep up with the traffic, you know? It’s nice to have a little horsepower in your pocket.”


Courtesy of Mecum Auctions


Paul owned a shop for eight years, “and we did everything—paint and body, interior, the whole deal.” It was called Carlevale’s Custom Cars, and it became regionally famous. You could walk in and maybe see a little of everything, from an Austin-Healey to a drab green, six-wheel antique military transport truck to a yellow ’51 Mercury lead sled.


Now, “I have no interest in working for the public. I do it for my own enjoyment. But I doubt I’ll be doing it anymore. Not in my lifetime.”


Courtesy of Mecum Auctions


But why? “The real reason I’m selling these cars is because I lost my son Dominic last October,” Paul says. Dominic had graduated from college and married, and he and his wife, Erin, founded and ran their own business. They had two little girls. Dominic Carlevale would have been 33.


Add to that the fact that older brother Gus and his family have relocated to Florida. Paul has no one to build cars with now, and there’s no point in keeping all these. “I have five granddaughters, no grandsons, and I doubt the girls will be wanting to wrench on these muscle cars. I’m getting up there, and it’s time to move on.”


Today may be difficult. “Wiping the cars down, getting them ready to show, I look at all the work we’ve done, everything that’s in them—work we did over years, not months. It’s been kind of nice walking out and deciding which one I’ll drive today. I don’t know how I’ll feel when they’re gone. But I also know I need to lighten my load, pay attention to my granddaughters, and my new wife, you know? I’ve got to slow down.” I’m not sure if he’s trying to convince me, or himself.


Courtesy of Mecum Auctions


Regardless, he insists that, “Once the car passion bites you, you’re in. It is what it is. You think you can get away, but you can’t.” Especially when each car is like looking at a photo album, full of family pictures. That’s probably why he and Gus have held back two cars. One, as you might have guessed, is Helleanor, the Mustang. The other is a 1968 Dodge Charger. That car is with Gus in Southwest Florida, while Paul and Helleanor remain in Pennsylvania.


Paul insists that he will remain a car guy, even though—pause here for effect—he now drives an *electric car.* At least it’s a Porsche Taycan. “Hey, you gotta keep up with the times!” he says, with debatable enthusiasm, considering that there’s an exclamation mark at the end of that sentence.


Anyway, though he claims that he’s laid down his wrenches for good, “Helleanor is the most problematic car we ever built. Hey, I may not be entirely done after all!” That exclamation mark is legit.
