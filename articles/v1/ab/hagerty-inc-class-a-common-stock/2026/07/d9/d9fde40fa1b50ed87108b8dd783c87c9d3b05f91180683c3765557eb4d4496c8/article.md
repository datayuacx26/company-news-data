# Off the Rack: Affordable Classics Designed by Three Italian Icons

Everyone loves a bargain, especially when it comes with a designer label. The entire fashion industry is predicated on the concept of top designers who create haute couture collections in limited quantities at stratospheric prices for the most discerning clients (read: the richest), creating a brand aura that allows them to sell higher-volume ready-to-wear—also known as off-the-rack—apparel and accessories for the general public.


Once upon a time, the auto industry operated in much the same way. Masterpieces of design (and engineering and performance) such as the Lamborghini Miura and the Ferrari Daytona were the province of the rich, while the rest of us enjoyed them only in pictures in magazines, as we might have admired Elizabeth Taylor in a Givenchy gown in a tabloid photo. And although neither Lamborghini nor Ferrari offered affordable cars for the masses, the men who designed their unattainable cars liberally peddled their skills to mainstream automotive manufacturers across the globe. To continue the fashion analogy, these arrangements were akin to fashion designers today making guest appearances at Uniqlo, the chic yet highly affordable retailer. These occasional collections feature the perspective and talent of the best stylists at fast-fashion prices.


Valentino tastes on Uniqlo budgets are nothing new to car enthusiasts, as evidenced by the automobiles we’ve gathered here. Hailing from three manufacturers on three continents, all were conceived by some of the most storied designers—or *progettisti* , in Italy. Each of these cars bears the design language of legends, but, just like when they were new, they don’t require a second mortgage on your house to put into your garage.


## 1971–89 Fiat X1/9: A Fiat for Every Man


Blair Bunting


**Designed by Bertone**


Nuccio Bertone saw great success with the Fiat 850 Coupe and Spider in 1965. When he returned to Fiat’s co-director of design, Oscar Montabone, in the late 1960s with a proposal to make a mid-engine sports car, Montabone wanted none of it. “All you want to do is build a poor man’s Miura,” he said with a sniff, according to Luciano Greggio’s book, *Bertone* . Nuccio’s dream of forging a relationship with Ferruccio Lamborghini similar to that between Pininfarina and Ferrari had flamed out with Ferruccio’s waning interest in the car business. It looked as though his attempt with Fiat was destined to do the same.


Fiat issued marching orders for only two new design concepts on the existing 128 chassis, one front-engine and one rear-. Nuccio was told he could produce a mid-engine design if he wanted to, but not to hold his breath about it being made. Fiat was historically a front- or rear-engine company—the 850 and its 500, 750, and 800 predecessors were all rear-engine cars.


With Marcello Gandini, then chief designer at Bertone, at the helm, the mid-engine undertaking was in capable hands. Gandini had already designed the Miura for Lamborghini and went on to create numerous mid-engine cars, including the Alfa Romeo Carabo concept and the Countach. Nuccio’s persistence and Gandini’s designs wore Montabone down, but Nuccio didn’t get everything he wanted by the time the X1/9 went on sale in the U.S. in 1973. He’d hoped to make the car bigger than the Spider to appeal to American buyers. Nuccio expressed concerns that it would be too small and considered more of a toy that landed “in the hands of students and secretaries.”


The Fiat X1/9’s instrument panel is a pure period piece.


Blair Bunting


Its richly colored leather seats with fabric inserts have timeless appeal.


Blair Bunting


“The X1/9 was my first new car. I bought it in college in 1980,” said Rick Polhamus, one such student and owner of the brown X1/9 you see here. “It was black-on-black, and it was great. I never had to drive anyone to parties because there was no room.” His dad told him that this was the only time in his life that he wasn’t going to need four seats, so he might as well buy something with two. Polhamus only sold that original car because he moved to sweltering Palm Springs, California, for a job and needed A/C.


A few years ago, when friends started buying project cars, Polhamus got the itch to own an X1/9 again. His new car, a 1980 model, arrived on a truck after he bought it from an online auction site for $5200. “As it was coming down the ramp,” he recalled, “I thought they’d made a mistake because it was so much smaller than I’d remembered.” Nuccio’s size-matters concerns were clearly well-founded.


Gandini had joined Bertone at 26, apprenticing under fellow design legend Giorgetto Giugiaro for a year before Giugiaro left to open his own studio (we’ll get to him shortly). One glance at Gandini’s Autobianchi A112 Runabout concept car and the DNA connection to the X1/9 appears clear. It struck the same clean-cut silhouette but wasn’t as sharp a wedge as, say, the Lancia Stratos. It sported the same beak as the Runabout, with a long overhang and pop-up headlights instead of the bizarro headlight pods placed on the concept’s C-pillars. The X1/9 wore an integrated spoiler up front and had a targa hardtop that was stowed in a special compartment at the front of the car. The Runabout’s tiny windscreen was replaced with traditional A-pillars and a proper windshield.


Sharp exterior lines define a surprisingly comfortable cabin.


Blair Bunting


This new car retained its factory code name, X1/9, derived from Fiat’s convoluted project numbering system intended to keep auto journalists off the scent of new products. When Montabone finally presented it to Fiat owner Gianni Agnelli, he loved it. “Don’t touch anything,” Agnelli reportedly said. “I like it very much the way it is.”


“The name Bertone didn’t mean anything to me necessarily,” Polhamus admitted about his X1/9’s origins. “Later, I understood what that meant. It just validated my feelings about it. In 1980, nothing looked like it.” To him, it was revolutionary. “The angular wedge with its sharp edges made it stand out,” said Polhamus, who was studying architecture when he bought it. The sticker price of the 1980 X1/9 was about $8190, or some $34,000 today.


By 1980, the engine grew to a 1.5-liter four-cylinder that made 75 horsepower after Fiat ditched the Weber carburetor for fuel injection. It’s a smooth driver, with well-apportioned gear ratios and short throws. It revs high even with a five-speed and is so low to the ground, it makes you feel like a cat zooming down San Francisco hills on a skateboard.


Flickr/Alden Jewell


“The X1/9 drives like a Formula 1 car,” said F1 world champion Emerson Fittipaldi when he first reviewed it for Italian magazine *Quattroruote* . “Only rarely have I come across this kind of honest roadholding.” He cooed about its weight distribution and neutral handling but did criticize its lack of torque.


Although the X1/9 is petite, it’s surprisingly practical, with enough trunk space in the front and rear to accommodate a weekender bag or two. A trunk lock in the driver’s door jamb, which utilizes a secondary key, keeps your belongings secure. And it looks so good, you might never put the roof on. “Even in the winter, the thermal cover \[the cocoon effect of the compact cabin\] is so efficient, as the warm air from the heater gets trapped, you could be driving around in a T-shirt,” Polhamus said.


Despite Polhamus having regrets about selling his original car, buying a replacement in modern times did not break his bank. Hagerty data pegs a 1980 X1/9 at about $10,700 for a #3 example.


## 1987–93 Cadillac Allanté: Detroit Returns to Turin


Blair Bunting


**Designed by Pininfarina**


Few automotive origin stories are as outlandish, nonsensical, and expensive as the Cadillac Allanté’s. Looking for a sleek two-door to compete with the Mercedes-Benz SL and Jaguar XJS, Robert Burger, then Cadillac’s general manager, looked to Italian coachbuilder Pininfarina for help. Burger wanted something open-aired and European-looking to sit atop the Eldorado architecture.


Battista “Pinin” Farina—“pinin” in northern Italian slang means youngest—started his company in 1930 after separating from his brother’s Stabilimenti Industriali Farina coachworks. Upon opening the doors, Pininfarina built bodies for Rolls-Royce, Lancia (whose founder, Vincenzo Lancia, helped Battista start his company), Hispano-Suiza, and Fiat, among others. It was the 1947 Cisitalia 202 Coupé, with its low hood sweeping back from an elegant nose, that put the coachbuilder on auto manufacturers’ wish lists. In the early 1950s, the relationship between Pininfarina and Ferrari began producing cars, culminating in milestones such as the 250 GT SWB, the Daytona, the Testarossa, and the F40.


The Allanté’s clunky steering wheel, set against a backdrop of one of GM’s 1980s asymmetrical instrument pods, must have seemed like a good idea at the time. It does at least provide good sightlines to the tachometer and speedometer. Oh, that gearshift lever.


Blair Bunting


It was this mystique that GM executives sought to highlight a new era of design and technology for Cadillac. And the partnership had some history—Pininfarina and Cadillac had already worked together on the Eldorado Brougham in 1959. Thirty years later, the renewed collaboration resulted in the Allanté and what Cadillac manufacturing operations director Bill Buschman called “the longest assembly line in the world.”


Step one: The chassis and wiring harnesses were flown from Detroit to a newly built factory just outside Turin, the northern Italian epicenter of automotive manufacturing.


Step two: The Italians constructed the Allanté’s body panels, married them to the chassis, and then painted them.


Step three: Cars were then transported to San Giorgio, about an hour-and-a-half drive from Turin, where they were wired up and trimmed with carpet and upholstery.


Step four: The finished bodies were trucked back to Turin, where they boarded one of[two specially designed Alitalia and Lufthansa 747s](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/heres-what-happened-to-the-boeing-747s-that-moved-cadillac-allantes-across-the-atlantic/) , which flew them to Michigan, where the Detroit-Hamtramck Assembly Center installed their powertrains. At first, the planes could accommodate 56 cars. After a retrofit, they squeezed in 59.


Step five: Finished cars drove a 1-mile test track before they rolled onto a fleet of carriers to dealers. By the time it reached a dealership, an Allanté already had more than 7000 miles on it, mostly in the air.


The transatlantic production process that produced the Cadillac roadster is one of the most fascinating examples of Italian-American automotive collaboration.


GM


When Tom Rohner, owner of the 1993 Allanté we drove and photographed, first heard this story, like many, he was intrigued. “They’d put four or five Allantés together on the line at the \[Pininfarina\] factory, then spit out a Testarossa,” Rohner said. “Both models went through the same paint shop.”


In 1987, Rohner drove his father’s brand-new Allanté and was hooked. Within a year, he’d bought a 1988 model. A couple of years later, after a move to San Diego, he and his dad bought a parts car, as repairs were already proving expensive. Once everyone in the national club found out about it, his phone started ringing off the hook. Before long, Rohner owned six parts cars and started a club. Now he self-publishes a magazine called *Roadsters by Cadillac* .


“I’ve dismantled 66 Allantés myself,” Rohner said. So extensive is his knowledge, he can tell instantly if someone with the wrong mechanical background has worked on a particular car.


Blair Bunting


Blair Bunting


Exterior lines from Pininfarina’s Italian design masters have aged far better than the instrument panel.


Blair Bunting


The Allanté was the most expensive American car, with a base price of about $50,000 in 1987, some $145,000 in today’s dollars. It had to feel special, so Cadillac threw everything at it. The 1987 model had an advanced version of Bosch’s anti-lock braking system. A three-mode automatically adjusted suspension came in 1988. The 1990 Allanté had the brand’s first CD player and was the first front-wheel-drive car with traction control. Cadillac used the Allanté to test features before putting them in higher-volume cars.


The sumptuous, bolstered leather seats are luxurious to the touch. The December 1986 issue of the British magazine *What Car?* suggested the Allanté was “built for two six-foot Texans… the Recaro seats are designed for their posteriors, so they’re big rather than hip hugging.” The fully carpeted trunk holds golf bags aplenty—this alone should have been American catnip. Given the number of buttons on the dash, you might expect the Allanté to take off back to Turin on autopilot. From the leather-covered shifter with its hand-stitching to the curved dash nook on the passenger side, the Allanté worked the details right.


Flickr/IFHP97


Flickr/IFHP97


Mario Vernacchia, Pininfarina design manager for the Allanté, executed an exterior that defined the simple elegance of the period—even Vernacchia’s Ferrari Mondial was considered an example of excessive restraint.


Sadly, for all its innovations and design pedigree, the Allanté wasn’t a hot ticket. The lofty sales goal of 6000 units a year was never met. In 1987, in addition to Rohner’s dad, only around 3000 others bought one. It wasn’t until the final year of production, 1993, when Cadillac installed the more powerful Northstar V-8, that sales peaked at slightly under 4700 units.


The Northstar allowed the Allanté finally to deliver on Burger’s sporty European premise. Sprawling majestically under the hood with its four cams and 32 valves, it made 290 horsepower, an upgrade from the 200 horses of the Allanté’s 4.5-liter, which had already replaced the original pushrod 4.1-liter V-8 and its 170 horses in 1988. After the Northstar went in, only the Viper and the Corvette had more giddy-up among American roadsters at the time.


Marketplace/BlakeWingrove


Twist the key and the V-8’s exhaust burble sounds exactly as the exterior looks, enough to turn a head but perhaps not snap a neck. The Northstar engine answers the throttle pedal’s call well, even if the suspension leans to the cushiony end of the touring spectrum during a 7.2-second voyage to 60 mph. The Northstar V-8 is notorious for costly head-gasket failures, but Rohner demurred. Of the almost 100 Cadillacs with Northstar engines he’s owned, he’s never had a head-gasket problem. In all his years working on them, only two customers needed to have the head gasket redone.


“They made improvements to the car every year,” Rohner said. “And when they got it just about right, they quit making it.” General Motors excels at this enterprise, deleting cars including the Buick Grand National, the Pontiac Fiero, and the Chevrolet Impala SS right when they were getting good. They’re at it again with the Cadillac Blackwing engine heading into retirement before the end of this year.


Rohner credits the Allanté’s semiautomatic convertible top design for some of the car’s sales woes. “It requires more manual labor than stump removal,” he said. He demonstrated the cumbersome 1-plus-minute choreography to retract the roof by awkwardly pulling things up and pushing other things down, all while leaning over the car. You won’t hear him complain too much, though. “I rebuild at least two of these rear pull-down units a week,” he said, pointing to the motorized contraption that secures the top in place when reemployed. “I send the designer a Christmas card every year.”


In today’s market, Hagerty values an Allanté at approximately $16,200 (in #2 excellent condition). So yes, the Allanté is now an Italian-designer steal.


## 1992–97 Subaru SVX: All-Wheel Magnifico


Blair Bunting


**Designed by Giugiaro**


After cutting his teeth in the late ’60s on the softer, curvier shapes of the Iso Grifo and the Maserati Ghibli, Giorgetto Giugiaro started pinching and triangulating cars for the wedge era. The noses of his Maserati Boomerang and Alfa Romeo Iguana concepts looked as if they could scrape ice off a windshield. After stints at Bertone and Ghia, Giugiaro branched out on his own in 1968, founding Italdesign Giugiaro.


Though known for his pricier collector car designs and noteworthy concepts, Giugiaro’s later work regularly appeared in the showrooms of whoever would pay to keep the lights on. We can thank him for the Volkswagen Golf Mk I and Scirocco, the Fiat Panda, the first-generation Impulse from Isuzu, and even the original Lexus GS.


Subaru teamed Giugiaro with staff designers Tetsuya Hayashi and Hiroshi Sarukawa to transform the polarizing mid-’80s XT sports coupe. The result: the flagship SVX coupe, which was designed in Italy and engineered in Japan, with the concept debuting at the 1989 Tokyo Motor Show. The production model was introduced in the U.S. in the summer of 1991 as a ’92 model.


The SVX’s cabin, with its double-decker stereo hiding discreetly behind a wood door, has aged well.


Blair Bunting


In 2001, David Barnblatt saw this red 1997 SVX on eBay Motors, advertised as the last new production example, with only 200 miles and an asking price of $50,000. A decade later, the same car showed up again at a Subaru dealer in Oakland, California, with only 36,000 miles on the odometer and a far more reasonable $7700 price. It reminded Barnblatt of the late ’80s JDM (Japanese Domestic Market) forbidden fruit he’d read about in car magazines, except now it was produce he could finally afford.


“I was blown away by the SVX’s quality and design,” Barnblatt recalled. “It was not like a normal Subaru. This was built to a different standard, more like a Mercedes. The way the doors closed, the interior, everything felt special.”


Interior suede-like trim was a rare touch in the 1990s.


Blair Bunting


The SVX sports squinting headlights, a heavier back end, and a massive greenhouse. A lower body line creates a crease in the front fender that transitions to the windowsill then continues to the top of the rear decklid—all in the name of accentuating the wedge. The car came with two distinct pairs of wheels, the blades of each being specific to the right and left sides of the car, an element both practical for cooling purposes and pleasing to the eye. The optional integrated rear wing looks sculptural in nature.


The most striking feature of the SVX might be the unconventional “glass-to-glass” canopy, a design similarly found in Giugiaro’s 1983 Renault Gabbiano concept. The lower part of the window retracts into the doors, yet the upper glass remains fixed onto the doorframe. This allowed for excellent visibility while maximizing aerodynamics and giving it a singular look. The side windowsills sit noticeably lower behind the delicate A-pillars, giving off jet-fighter cockpit vibes. Early models had a black roof, which added to the effect. “Subaru has not built a homogenized blahmobile,” *Road & Track* gushed in a 1992 review.


The cabin was designed to wrap around the driver—another aircraft attribute. “With the half-window open, when it’s raining, no water gets in the car,” Barnblatt said. You’re protected by the front piece of stationary glass.


Even the engine bay looks aesthetically pleasing, not overrun by wires and hoses. You’re met with Subaru’s big, torquey EG33 engine, a 230-hp, 3.3-liter horizontally opposed flat-six, which made power akin to the contemporary midrange trims of competitors such as the Dodge Stealth and the Nissan 300ZX.


The 3.3-liter horizontally opposed flat-six engine provided grand touring, rather than sports car, vibes.


Blair Bunting


From the driver’s seat, the SVX delivers on its touring car résumé, with tall gearing and a silken ride, all while the engine hovers at 2000 rpm. It provides great outward visibility, the A/C blows perfectly, and it can go 400 to 500 miles on its 18.5-gallon fuel tank. Its speed-sensitive steering, which adds weight at speed, predates traditional Sport drive modes that achieve a similar effect. All-wheel drive was standard on most models; a cheaper front-drive option was available for a brief period, but few bought it. At a time when most sporty coupes were offered with a manual gearbox, the SVX came only with an automatic.


Subaru produced some 25,000 SVXs from 1992 to 1997, of which about 14,000 came to the U.S. Only 600 were made in the final year as production wound down. Originally, the SVX didn’t come cheap, with a sticker price of $37,000—a fair chunk of change in 1992. Adjusted for inflation, that’s $87,000, making the SVX one of the most expensive Subarus ever produced.


Hagerty Valuation lists an excellent #2 SVX at about $18,200. Barnblatt concedes there might not be much of a market now for his Japanese/Italian unicorn. “Kids who bought them and thought it would be a fast car or something they could drift are disappointed.”


***


Naturally, we’ll always love the supercars in posters we taped to our walls as teenagers, the same way we might daydream of grand Lake Como villas, aged bottles of Brunello di Montalcino, or an Armani suit. But there’s also something transcendent about Italy’s attainable charms—a walk through a piazza at dusk, a scoop of strac-ciatella gelato, or a drive in an affordable designer sports car. These pleasures might be inexpensive, but they’re by no means any less Italian.
