# Taxi! The History of NYC’s Iconic Yellow Cabs

The reviews are in for Jennifer Lopez’s most recent rom-com outing, *Office Romance* , and sad to say, but it appears not to have quite risen to the occasion. The usual set pieces are in place, but something is missing. It’s not the star power of Ms. Lopez, and it’s not the fault of glowering leading man Brett Goldstein, who provides an effective foil to JLo’s character.


That missing element? Almost certainly a yellow Crown Victoria taxicab. You just can’t have a romantic comedy set in New York without a proper yellow cab showing up as the backdrop for heartbreak or a reunion, or as part of a race to the airport before love’s hourglass runs out. Not just rom-coms either: Be it an episode of *Seinfeld* or *Law & Order,* a yellow Ford taxicab is part of popular culture’s view of the NYC landscape. Or at least it used to be.


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


As of two years ago,[the last holdouts for the Crown Victoria taxicab](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/there-are-two-crown-victoria-taxis-left-to-preserve-the-old-ways-of-nyc/) hung up their medallions, making way for the taxi of tomorrow. Somewhat uninspiringly, these were supposed to be a series of minivans from Ford, Nissan, and Chrysler. Somehow, a yellow Toyota Sienna just doesn’t make for a good movie poster.


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


However, as much as the Big Apple modernizes, it remains both a real place and a fictional place, the latter fixed in our imaginations. There, both the Checker and Crown Vic cabs still ply the boroughs, depending on whether it’s the grubby, mean streets of the 1970s or the cheerful and sanitized environment of *Friends* . If you’re visiting NYC, you’ve got two choices: *walkin’ ovah heah* , or getting a cab. Let’s dive in on the latter.


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


## Electric Avenue in the 19thCentury


Hop in your non-DeLorean time machine and head back to the late 1800s, and most folks you’d talk to would be astonished to find out that electric vehicles weren’t yet the default choice in the future. Especially so in New York City, where Edison’s lightbulb was the flame of the future, leading the way for a thoroughly modern metropolis.


But, like other cities around the globe, there was quite a lot more horse dung around the streets of NYC than was desirable. It was all well to envision a world transformed by the dreams of Jules Verne and H.G. Wells, but quite another thing to step off the curb into a steaming pile of manure. All over your best spats.


Starting in 1897, the Electric Carriage and Wagon Company launched a fleet of a dozen Hansom taxicabs in the city, NYC’s first taxis not drawn by horses. Early EVs like this weren’t uncommon—they were well-known enough to show up in some of F. Scott Fitzgerald’s writing—but they were a very expensive way of getting around.


They were also pretty old-fashioned, at least in terms of layout. The driver sat in an elevated position at the rear, just as in a two-wheeled horse-drawn hansom, with the passenger riding up front. The powertrain was futuristic, but the construction was still old-timey wagon-building.


Electrobat taxicabs on the streets of NYC, 1898.


Unknown/Public Domain


Still, in some alternative universe, the electric cab became a defining feature of New York, in the same way the trolley car is shorthand for San Francisco. By 1899, there were a hundred electric taxis on the street, and shortly after the turn of the century, a thousand of them.


Unfortunately, the financial markets of the early 20th century could teach a yo-yo some lessons in instability. A market crash and a fire at the building where the Electric Carriage and Wagon Company kept their machines rewound the clock, and it was back to horse-drawn cabs on the streets. While New Yorkers considered themselves as cosmopolitan as any European city-dweller of the time, there was a certain amount of frontier attitude among the city’s cab drivers.


Rather than set rates, you had a fleet of independent operators out there to charge whatever they could get away with. When this happened to one Henry A. Allen, a well-connected son of a stockbroker, the resulting grudge ended up kicking off the use of the metered taxicab in NYC. Allen was so incensed about being charged $5 for a three-quarter mile ride (almost $200 today), he decided to get into the taxi business himself.


1910 Darracq taxi drivers queue in a busy road in New York City.


National Motor Museum/Heritage Images/Getty Images


Traveling to France, Allen lined up a fleet of imported Darracqs, outfitting his drivers in sharp-looking uniforms and instituting fare metering. Used to being gouged, New Yorkers quickly adopted this more fair-minded option, and Allen might well have made his fortune. Sadly, poor labor relations turned into basically all-out warfare, and he eventually folded up shop.


## A Checkered Past


By the 1930s, yellow taxicabs plying New York’s streets were a common sight, with fleets from both GM and Ford. However, there was one particular manufacturer that would become synonymous with the yellow taxicabs of New York, Chicago, or any major city in the US: Checker.


Checker’s Byzantine corporate history in the 1920s and 1930s makes *Succession* look like *Winnie the Pooh.* There were power plays and suspected stock fiddling and hostile takeovers, but through it all, founder Morris Markin somehow managed to keep his hands on the reins. By the time the U.S. entered WWII, Checker was a fully independent taxicab manufacturer that had built just shy of 30,000 cars.


National Motor Museum/Heritage Images/Getty Images


By this time, NYC had introduced the medallion system, limiting the number of licensed taxicab operators in the five boroughs. Signed into law by Mayor La Guardia, the Haas Act capped the number of legal cabbies at around 17,000, a little over half of what totals had been during the Great Depression. The cap would have some unintended consequences decades later, when a taxi medallion could be worth as much as a million dollars.


Before that, though, let’s take a look at the Checker Taxi and its consumer twin, the Marathon. With production peaking in the early 1960s, the Checker A11 became the default New York taxicab of the ’60s, ’70s, and ’80s. And not just to New Yorkers either, but to anyone who watched the million stories of the Big Apple play out on TV or the silver screen.


Robert De Niro opening the driver’s door on his Checker cab on the set of the film *Taxi Driver* , in New York City, New York, 1975.


Vinnie Zuffante/Getty Images


The Checker A11 looked like a 1950s car, and little changed until it went out of production in the early 1980s. In 1967, legislators ruled that every medallion-carrying NYC taxi had to be painted yellow, and the banana-hued Checker found itself the least-likely showbiz star of them all.


Engines were borrowed from Chrysler, then Chevrolet, mostly not-very-thrifty inline-sixes and V-8s. For all the “follow that car!” onscreen chasing, even a V-8-powered Checker is a leisurely old boat and doesn’t exactly handle like a Corvette.


Dan McCoy/National Archives


Still, the slab-sided looks made it an icon, whether it was being driven by Travis Bickle or Phoebe Buffay. Or by a cast of misfits getting yelled at by a young Danny DeVito. Joni Mitchell’s *Big Yellow Taxi?* That’d be a Checker. Nowadays, examples of the civilian version, the Marathon, are far more common than the working cabs themselves, which basically got driven hard then scrapped afterwards. If you’re going to produce a period drama set in the 1970s or 1980s New York, you can’t do it without a few yellow Checkers in the background.


## Queen of New York City


With[Checker taxi production having wound down](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Eo74UeLsQrE) by 1982, the hunt was on for another taxi to take up the mantle of default NYC cab. There’d be a host of fill-ins, just as not every yellow cab in the city was a Checker, but it wasn’t until the late 1990s that we got another one that really stuck in the public imagination.


Giovanni Porta


Specifically, that was the P70 extended-wheelbase Panther platform Ford: the Crown Victoria. Popular as a cop car for its toughness and simplicity of service, the Crown Vic was a favorite of fleet operators, and a longer wheelbase version was a hit with taxicab companies.


With over two decades in operation as the NYC cab of choice, yellow Crown Vics became part of the furniture of the city. Just seeing one is enough to have the *Seinfeld* slap-bass riff play in your head.


Giovanni Porta


This particular one is a 2011 P70 Crown Victoria, retired as NYC cab 3D68. It’s an owner-operator car rather than a fleet example, one reason why it is so well-preserved. Another is that its current owner, Giovanni Porta, a Panther platform enthusiast with a couple of them, including a Mercury Marauder, has gone to some lengths to track down authentic New York yellow cab details.


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


For instance, like all cabs, this car has yellow trouble lights hidden behind the grille. If a cabbie is having trouble with a passenger, he or she can activate these to warn any police in the area that something nefarious is happening onboard.


[Brendan McAleer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/bmcaleer/)


A Crown Victoria is a charmingly old-fashioned car to drive, which is why you get so many enthusiasts who like old police cars. The longer wheelbase adds a plusher ride, and even if the 215-hp V-8 doesn’t exactly make it a four-door Mustang, it’s got plenty of lazy torque on offer. Porta drove this car clear across the country to the West Coast after buying it, stopping at various national parks along the way. If you’re interested, the fare for such a trip would cost you about $10K.


With the last Crown Vics leaving NYC cabbie service in 2024, even with special dispensation, there’s a bit of a void left behind. The Toyotas that have picked up the slack do the job well, but they’re not really iconic. Perhaps, in time, they’ll become more beloved.


Gary Hershorn/Getty Images


In the meantime, there’s an unlikely option in the offing. At this year’s New York Auto Show, Kia showed off a yellow cab version of its PV5 electric van, complete with full wheelchair accessibility. It’s just a box, really, but with that classic yellow livery, it’s exactly like what a modern city taxi cab should look like.


NYC’s first proper taxicabs were electric, so maybe its future ones will be too. Time will tell what the city will fall in love with next. You just can’t fake real chemistry on screen, or on the streets.


Giovanni Porta
