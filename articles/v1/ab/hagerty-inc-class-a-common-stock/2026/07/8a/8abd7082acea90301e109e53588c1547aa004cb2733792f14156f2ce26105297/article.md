# Don Garlits: Father of Swamp Rat, UFO Enthusiast, and Unequivocally the Greatest Drag Racer Ever

We’re standing near the entrance of the Don Garlits Museum of Drag Racing, located in Ocala, Florida. It’s a comprehensive, full-day history lesson, packed with dozens of famous drag racing cars, many of them driven by Garlits, known to fans as “Big Daddy”—we’ll explain how he got the very appropriate nickname in a moment. We’re winding up a lengthy interview with him, during which we were dazzled by his ability to recall names, places, dates, and details from a career that spans 75 years.


He is much easier to talk to than he was a few months ago. Garlits, understandably, was stone deaf, so you would shout questions. Once that no longer worked, he began using his phone, attached to an iPad—you would state your question, and the phone would transcribe it. He’d read on the iPad, and he’d answer. The process took a while. But he got a cochlear implant above his still-barely functioning right ear for Christmas, and you no longer have to shout. “He told me it was the first time he’d ever heard my voice,” a young museum clerk said.


As I typically do, I’m ending the interview with a trademark boilerplate question: “Don, is there anything I haven’t asked you?”


We are, incidentally, surrounded by members of the museum staff, as well as customers who did not know that their $22 admission would include an audience with Big Daddy himself. They all eavesdrop with interest.


Garlits considers my question for a moment, then takes a half-step toward me. He looks me in the eye and says, in a low, very composed growl: “You didn’t ask me about the *aliens* !”


I step back, shocked and surprised, but probably not for the reason you think. Oh, I knew about the aliens, all right, and I definitely didn’t ask about them. But did Garlits recall a particular story I wrote years ago? Just how good *was* his memory? And that almost under-his-breath growl—why did it sound so familiar? It clicked. Clint Eastwood. You know: “Go ahead. Make my day.” Maybe it’s a generational thing: Eastwood is 96, Garlits turned 94 in January.


Garlits’ longstanding interest in aliens—extraterrestrials, flying saucers, Area 51, classified government secrets, and especially UFOs, which Garlits has personally seen four times—wasn’t that big of a secret, but nobody had written much about it. Long ago, 20 years or so, I learned that in addition to the expected, brand-friendly website of www.dongarlits.com, there was a second one, www.garlitsdon.com.


Click on that one, and this is what it said: “Aliens: What You Never Really Wanted to Know! By Don Garlits, UFO Investigator.” It was page after page of what he had learned in his years-long investigation—personal experiences, interviews, history—all very entertaining, and it dovetailed with his adjoining assertion that the Apollo moon landings never happened. I wrote a story for a magazine that mentioned the website, and two days after it came out, garlitsdon.com disappeared. It’s still gone. Did I cancel his website? And did he remember that? Was he angry?


Don’t think so. We talk about aliens, but merely friendly chit-chat. Over and done. The rest of this story will be about Don Garlits, not UFO Investigator, but The Greatest Drag Racer Ever.


ISC Archives/Getty Images


Big Daddy was there at the start. And against all odds, he’s still here.


Garlits was born in Tampa on January 14, 1932. His father was an engineer for Westinghouse, who quit his job and moved south for his health. Growing up, Don loved cars, but it wasn’t until his shop teacher brought a copy of a 1949 *Hot Rod* magazine to class that Don’s imagination expanded beyond the basic cars-as-transportation concept. He took a genuine and immediate interest in the magazine’s philosophy and developed a revised cars-as-hot-rods world view. Or at least a Tampa view.


Back then, drag racing was two cars, stopped at a traffic light, squealing tires down Main Street when the light turned green. You hoped there were no cops around, and you secretly prayed nothing happened that might qualify as a *Rebel Without a Cause* movie moment. You know—the driver’s education filmstrips that showed a mother pushing a baby carriage into the intersection, then skid marks, and finally two caskets, one regular-size, one tiny. Cool guys sneered when they watched the movie, but that sort of thing sticks with you.


In June 1950, Garlits and his hot-rod buddies finally got a chance to do some informal but legitimate drag racing at the nearby Zephyrhills airport, a U.S. Army Air Force facility during WWII that was passed on to the town of Zephyrhills. Once a month, they’d close down a runway to use for drag racing.


Garlits, and his 1940 Ford convertible with a Mercury engine, was at the head of the line. By the end of the first day, the fire was lit.

Garlits began building engines and cars that would go faster and faster. A junkyard Cadillac engine soon replaced the Mercury in his ’40 Ford.


About that time, he and his wife, Pat—they had met at a popular lake in 1952, and were married February 20, 1953—were driving to a planned picnic near Lake Wales, just north of Sebring, when they spotted a sign that said “Drags Today,” with an arrow pointing down the dirt road leading to the drag strip. They detoured and never made it to the picnic. Garlits won his first trophy. Yes, he still has it.


Garlits at rest—an unusual sight—in his office at the Don Garlits Museum of Drag Racing in Ocala, Florida, 50 miles south of Gainesville.

If the drag-racing fire was lit in 1950, by 1954, it was raging. Garlits was never satisfied, always planning his next mechanical move. There was a ’36 Ford coupe, then a 1927 T roadster with a Mercury engine that he built under a tree in the front yard. He correctly reasoned that extra weight was a bad thing, so he stripped the T of everything but the body. Then one day, the body went.


Meanwhile, drag racing was getting respectable. The National Hot Rod Association, the drag equivalent of NASCAR, was formed by Wally Parks, then the editor of *Hot Rod* magazine. This year, the NHRA is celebrating its 75th anniversary, beginning in March with the Gatornationals in Gainesville, Florida, right up the road from Garlits’ museum.


By 1957, he and his friends had massaged their on-the-fly design into an approximation of a slingshot dragster—the driver was in the very back, legs straddling the transmission. The engine, a Chrysler Hemi, had been moved as far to the rear as it would go. Bigger tires were mounted inches from the driver. Frame rails from a junkyard ’31 Chevrolet cradled the engine and led to the skinny front tires. It did, indeed, look a little like a cocked slingshot. And like the dragsters in *Hot Rod.*


Garlits wanted a car that was proper-looking, a representative for his business, Don’s Speed Shop, which was painted in big letters on the side of the dragster. The rest of the car was gloss black, a consistent Garlits trademark, as was a gold cross painted right in front of the cockpit. On November 10, 1957, the dragster went 176.4 mph and covered the quarter-mile in 8.79 seconds.

Garlits had built and driven the world’s fastest dragster, and the first one to top 170 mph. That this occurred in Florida, and not California or *at least* in Detroit, caused much peeing of pants in the national drag-racing community. Garlits and his car were getting so much publicity that a racer from Detroit began posting disparaging comments in drag-racing publications, starting a semi-friendly feud, and claiming that Garlits was nothing but a “swamp rat” from Florida.


Garlits liked the name, and his car was christened the Swamp Rat 1. With each new car, that number would increase by one.[Ed Iskenderian](https://www.hagerty.com/media/people/celebrating-ed-iskenderians-104th-birthday-with-the-man-himself/) , who owned a camshaft-grinding company south of Los Angeles and was a legendary entrepreneur and pot-stirrer, placed ads in magazines like *Hot Rod* and racing weeklies like *Drag News* touting Garlits’ ever-increasing performance benchmarks, invariably insisting that Isky Cams were one big reason for his success, especially at the expense of the California contingent. Iskenderian died in February at 104; when I talked to him last September, he told me to give Garlits his regards.


Garlits’ fame spread from coast to coast, but he had yet to race farther west than Texas, and eventually he, wife Pat, and little brother Ed made the trip to California. It was humbling, a learning experience. But the next time they went back, Garlits dominated.

Garlits’ fame continued to expand, though the cars slowed down—the NHRA instituted a gasoline-only rule from 1958 to 1964, but still Garlits went fast even without nitromethane. In 1962, he was racing at Indianapolis, a tough town. He’d gone there with Pat and their two young daughters, and the other racers began calling him “Daddy” Garlits. When Garlits won, the announcer, Bernie Partridge, said on the microphone, “Guess we’ll have to call him ‘Big Daddy’ now!” The Indianapolis newspaper picked it up, and the name stuck.


For much of his career, Garlits followed the money, cherry-picking rather than chasing season championships. He raced with the NHRA, the IHRA, the AHRA, and anywhere else he felt like running: Big Daddy was always welcome. Well, by promoters; racers weren’t always so pleased to see him.
All the while, Garlits couldn’t stop thinking, couldn’t quit innovating, constantly went in search of one less tenth of a second, one more mile per hour.


Alvis Upitis/Getty Images


Sometimes it got him in trouble. On March 8, 1970, at Lions Drag Strip outside Los Angeles, he was racing the Swamp Rat XIII, a conventional slingshot—driver in the back, engine just ahead of him, and straddling the transmission. This transmission was an experimental two-speed unit that Garlits was trying to perfect.


As he was leaving the starting line under full power, on the final run of the day, the transmission exploded. The dragster broke apart right behind the engine. The rear portion of the Swamp Rat, where Garlits was sitting, levered straight up, into a 90-degree angle from the car, then slammed onto the pavement, separated from the engine and the rest of the car. The explosion sent hot oil and metal shards into the crowd, nearly severing a teenage boy’s arm.


And the splintered transmission cut off half of Garlits’ right foot. It was bad. Compounding it, the ambulance crew slammed the door on what remained of his foot. He was out for the rest of the season.


It wasn’t an entirely new idea, but it wasn’t until Garlits lost part of his right foot in this exploding-transmission incident at Lions Drag Strip near Los Angeles in 1970 that he was compelled to perfect a rear-engine car that moved the drivetrain behind the driver. It turned out to be better in every way.


The Denver Post/Getty Images


While he was in the hospital, he began designing a rear-engine dragster, where any catastrophic drivetrain failure would occur *behind* the driver, not in front. That design had been tried multiple times before but had always failed. Back in Florida, moving around his shop in a wheelchair, Garlits and his crew of T. C. Lemons and Connie Swingle started work on Swamp Rat XIV.


To no one’s surprise, Garlits lost in the first round when he debuted the car back at Lions Drag Strip the next season. But Garlits is an unopposed genius when it comes to figuring out what’s wrong and fixing it, and soon he started winning, and by the end of 1971, the advantage of a rear-engine Top Fuel dragster was apparent to everyone. Within a couple of years, most every new Top Fuel dragster was rear-engined.


By 1986, Garlits was up to Swamp Rat XXX, and it was performing remarkably well, winning the NHRA championship, the first dragster to top 270 mph. (Note that Garlits was also the first piston-powered drag racer to go 180, 200, 240, and 250 mph. Jet-powered dragsters had gone faster, but they were exhibition-only.) The nose of XXX had a snake’s head appearance, flaring out around the tiny front wheels, then angling down, designed to maximize aerodynamics.


Racing in the NHRA Summernationals at Englishtown, New Jersey, the front wheels lifted slightly off the pavement as Garlits launched, but he kept his foot on the accelerator, eager to beat fellow Florida racer Darrell Gwynn, just starting his career. Suddenly, about halfway down the track, the front wheels lifted even more. Air caught under that wide nose and blew the car straight up, pointing at the sky, as even the rear wheels briefly left the ground. Swamp Rat XXX rotated 180 degrees and slammed down onto the asphalt, aiming back toward the starting line.


Swamp Rat XXX does a “blowover” in Englishtown, New Jersey. It happened a second time in Spokane, Washington.


ISC Archives/Getty Images


The engine was still running, and the car drove out through a cloud of smoke, Hollywood-like, made a slight left turn, and stopped. Garlits, shaken, emerged from the car. The crowd exploded into cheers and applause, convinced Garlits drove out of that cloud on purpose, ever the showman. But he said later he was simply a passenger and had no control over what happened. “What those people witnessed was a 20th-century miracle,” he said. “My guardian angel looking after me.” Swamp Rat XXX went to the Smithsonian.


A second, and even worse, blowover crash happened a year later at Spokane, Washington. Garlits had some injuries but healed up and then retired to the television booth to become a commentator. He attempted a comeback a few years later but had to quit after a race in Atlanta in April 1992, when detached retinas in both eyes made him see spots. It was caused not by the launch of his Top Fuel dragster, but by the deceleration, the 4g shock that occurs when twin parachutes unfurl at the finish line to jerk the dragster to a stop. Doctors told him he would be risking the loss of his sight completely if he continued racing.


Still, he qualified for a few more races, the last in 2003, when he was 71. His best speed: just over 323 mph. More recently, he built an electric dragster, hoping to be the first to hit 200 mph. Swamp Rat 38 went 189 mph, and that’s all it would do. Not bad for an 87-year-old driver.


Lately, Garlits has been staying closer to home, which isn’t far from the museum, though it’s likely he’ll do some personal appearances for the NHRA’s 75th. That museum is a revelation: Garlits either saved or bought back many of his historic cars, and he has displayed not only them, but cars made famous by other racers, including inventory from other forms of motorsports.


That the museum even exists is literally an answered prayer, he says. Garlits’ first attempt at being a museum curator failed: He built it so far off the beaten path, on his family homestead, that nobody could find it. Wife Pat said that to succeed, it needed to be located off either Interstate 4, which runs from Tampa to Daytona Beach, or even better on I-75, which starts in South Florida and ends in Northern Michigan, at the border to Canada. Pat added up how much they could spend on land located near an I-75 exit: “She told me we could write a check for $80,000,” Garlits says.


They knelt by their bedside and prayed. They began looking for land, but what they found started in six figures, often ended in seven. They made one more trip north on I-75 and pulled off at an exit south of Ocala. There was nothing there but a gas station and a turquoise-roofed Stuckey’s. But there was vacant land there that would be perfect. A tiny cardboard sign in a ditch said it was for sale and listed a phone number. They drove to Stuckey’s and made a phone call. A surly woman answered: Yes, it was for sale.


“How much?” Garlits asked. “It’s $80,000,” she said.


Not long after, Garlits was walking his property when a man approached him at the fence line. “I own this property next to yours,” the man said, “and I’d like to buy your land. I’ll give you $180,000.” Garlits told him it wasn’t for sale at any price. The man asked why: “Because God found this land for us,” Garlits replied. The man’s last name was du Pont. Yes, he was one of those du Ponts. The museum opened in March 1984. It’s located on 16 acres, and the display area has grown from 10,000 square feet to 65,000.

Garlits has an office there, and a garage where he can work on his cars. Unless he’s on the road, he is in the office or the garage most every day. “See those file cabinets over there? They hold all my personal racing pictures,” he said, pointing behind his desk. “And they are filled up. Thirty-eight Swamp Rats, and there were cars before we started naming them. My 1940 Fords, my ’36 Fords, my roadsters, my flathead dragsters, and then we get to the Swamp Rats. I’m in the process of scanning all those files, and I’m down now to the third drawer over on the far side, up to 1984. I’m trying to identify every picture—it’s easy telling what the car is, but it’s a lot harder to figure out where the car is—sometimes I’m looking for some little clue in the photo that gives it away. I’m putting all those pictures on my little Samsung hard drive.


“On top of that, I’m in the process of writing my life story— *Don Garlits, The Official Autobiography* —I’m at 85,800 words, and I’m up to 1987, and I’m just getting ready to have the big wreck that took me out of racing.” There have been multiple books written about Garlits. It will be interesting to see his own take.


Staying busy is important to Garlits, and one of his answers to the inevitable question: What’s your secret?


Cameron Neveu


“I can’t stop working. You stop working, you die,” he said. There are so many memories, bittersweet ones. Garlits lost his wife, Pat, in 2014. They were married for 61 years. “She was the love of my life,” he said. A man isn’t supposed to outlive his wife: Ask me how I know.


His younger brother, Ed, died in 2020. Most of his friends are gone. From his high school graduating class in Tampa, he is one of two who “are still vertical,” he said.


Garlits has, at least, retained his sense of humor.


“You have to keep moving, use every part of your body,” he observes. Including, as it turns out, the penis. “It’ll shrink right up!” he says. He knows this because “I’ve seen it with my own eyes!” No, not on Garlits, but on his brother, who was in a nursing home. “He had a nice one. But one day they were changing his diaper. All he had left was this little button! You’ve got to use everything, or you’ll lose it.


“Sorry,” he says, “but the filter’s gone at 94. I just say whatever in the hell I want.”


Cameron Neveu


Garlits also advises fiscal responsibility. “I’ve got to save for my future! I’m going to be old someday! I’m not going to any nursing home, I’m gonna have a private nurse come to my house and take care of me, 24 hours, around the clock.” He laughs. We all do.


I first interviewed Garlits 48 years ago at the long-gone Hallsville Drag Strip, located right outside the town of Hallsville in East Texas. Like a lot of the top drag racers back then, Garlits often did match races at smaller tracks around the country to earn extra money, maybe $2500 a weekend, when the major series were taking a weekend off. To draw a good crowd, the promoter for a second-tier track, where the usual participants raced hot-rodded, home-built ’55 Chevrolets or Ford Mustangs or Pontiac GTOs, would hire a pair of big-name drivers to match race each other in their 2000-hp Top Fuel cars.


On this particular weekend, in April 1978, Garlits raced Paul Savadin, the owner of Paul’s Crank Shop in Devine, Texas, near San Antonio. Savadin may not have been nationally famous like Garlits, but he was well known in Texas and regarded as one of the best crankshaft guys around—he built them for Garlits.


It was billed as the local boy takes on Big Daddy and—well, loses. But even then, it made for good bragging rights for Savadin, comparable to the time East Texas boxer Donnie Fleeman stepped into the ring with Muhammad Ali—who was still Cassius Clay then—in February 1961. Fleeman was knocked out and promptly retired, but lived another 50 years after that fight, plenty of time to let everybody in Midlothian, Texas, know that their Donnie Fleeman was the first pro fighter to last seven rounds with the soon-to-be Greatest Of All Time.


In 1967, Garlits grew a beard, vowing not to shave until he ran the quarter-mile in less than 7 seconds. He finally did in September when he ran a 6.77 at the U.S. Nationals in Indianapolis, where he shaved on the starting line, surrounded by fans.

Garlits today at 94.

Don Garlits is to drag racing what Muhammad Ali is to boxing: the best. The difference is that pro boxing as we know it has been around since 1882, when John L. Sullivan became the first American heavyweight champion. A popular joke suggests that drag racing began when the second car was built, but the hobby became a sport in 1951, when *Hot Rod* magazine editor Wally Parks founded the National Hot Rod Association.


The NHRA’s 75th celebration began at the Gatornationals on March 8 and will conclude 19 races later at the NHRA Finals in Pomona, California, on November 15. We’re 108 years too late to hear John L. Sullivan talk about the early days of boxing, but we still have Don Garlits to talk about the birth of professional drag racing.


And, of course, UFOs. May the force be with you, Big Daddy.
