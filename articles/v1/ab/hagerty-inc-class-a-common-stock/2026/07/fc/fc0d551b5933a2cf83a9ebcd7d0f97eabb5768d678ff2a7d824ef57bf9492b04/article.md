# The Eagle Vision and the Story of Chrysler’s LH Cars

As soon as I saw John McIntosh’s mint-condition 1993 Eagle Vision roll into my local cars and coffee, I felt acutely aware of how long it’d been since I’d seen one so nice. Like many old sedans and family cars, Chrysler’s original LH platform trio—the Vision, Chrysler Concorde, and Dodge Intrepid—are rare now, but seeing a really nice one in 2026 offers a taste of how exciting they were when they were new in 1992.


\[ *Editor’s note: After you’ve learned about the story of the LH cars, check out Benjamin Hunting’s story on the[Lamborghini Protofino concept](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/the-lamborghini-portofino-concept-paved-the-way-for-chryslers-cab-forward-aspirations/) for a deep dive on the roots of the LH’s design.* \]


The LH’s sleek cab-forward shape and ultra-low beltline made Ford’s redesigned 1992 Taurus, then the best-selling car in America, look positively conservative. The roomy cabin, filled with then-modern-looking plastic, looked just as radical and offered roominess previously reserved for full-size cars. Chrysler spent $1.5B creating them, a pricey gamble considering its floundering fortunes at the time, but it paid off.


Alex Kwanten


After a year of PR hype, the LH trio landed like a lightning bolt in the fall of 1992. The one-two punch of the LH cars and the[ZJ-series Jeep Grand Cherokee](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/jeeps-original-grand-cherokee-tackled-country-roads-and-country-clubs/) vaulted Chrysler to design leader among the Big Three, banishing memories of recycled, stretched K-cars and setting the stage for a string of smash hits like the Neon, the 1994 Ram pickup, 1995’s “Cloud car” compacts, the 1996 minivans, and the PT Cruiser.


Customers loved them, and McIntosh is such a fan that he also owns a matching 1995 Dodge Intrepid SE. “Every time a Concorde comes up for sale, my friends suggest adding one of those too,” he says. He graciously let me take them both for a spin, offering a great opportunity to look back on the history of the LH platform and see how they hold up today.


## AMC and the Far-Out Future


There are really two threads to the LH story, the first of which was a major shift in design thinking that started when Tom Gale became head of design in 1985. The second is Chrysler’s 1987 acquisition of AMC, but both happened against the background of the big cuts and restructuring at Chrysler under CEO Lee Iacocca in the early 1980s.


Stellantis


On the design side, Gale says, “We were always what I’d call ‘two late,’ emphasis on the numeral, for ‘two years late.’ Even going back to the Elwood Engel days, we would answer in 1968 to what GM had done on mid-size cars in 1966, and things just kept marching forward that way. Engel and later Dick Macadam did wonderful things, but the company had been through real turmoil even before the loan guarantee days, and we just didn’t want to do that again.


“After I took over in ‘85, and I don’t take sole credit—it’s always a team effort—we endeavored to be proactive rather than reactive. Designers live in a world that’s four or five years ahead of everybody else, and we forced ourselves to think further out.” Since Chrysler didn’t have the funds to tool up entirely new cars in 1986–87, Gale’s designers focused on concepts like the Lamborghini Portofino—many of them truly radical for Chrysler.


Lamborghini Portofino concept


Stellantis


“Eventually, Iacocca asked, ‘If we can do concept cars like this, why can’t we do production cars like this?’ and I said, ‘Well Lee, that’s the question we’ve been waiting for you to ask.’ To his credit, he was more comfortable with landau roofs, white walls, and wire wheels. I don’t mean that in a demeaning way. His marketing background was that when you needed a special car, you’d dress it up with stuff, but he could see that the times were changing and he got on board.”


But of course, stylists need mechanical designs to work their magic on.


Michael L. Abramson/Getty Images


On March 9, 1987, Chrysler announced that it would buy AMC from Renault. At the time, the best assets Wall Street could see were the Jeep brand and the company’s new factory in Bramalea, Ontario. But AMC also brought talent and ideas, like longtime AMC designer[Vince Geraci](https://www.hagerty.com/media/people/remembering-vince-geraci-stalwart-of-amc-design/) , executive and sales maestro Joe Cappy, and ex-Renault exec François Castaing.


Castaing, working with Bob Lutz (who joined Chrysler in 1986), brought the idea of platform teams, pioneered at scrappy, understaffed AMC. The process of pulling together small, dedicated squads from design, marketing, engineering, and other groups to focus on one product family transformed, and greatly accelerated, the company’s development process. The LH cars took just 42 months to get from approval to showroom.


Alex Kwanten


Late in the LH’s development, engineering manager Glenn Gardner relayed in a 1992 interview that the team wanted to change the driving position. As the styling was set in stone, there were few ways to change it, so they lowered the floorpan by 9/10th of an inch, carefully working around the underbody components. Gardner credits the relationships of the platform team for making it happen. “In the old days, they would have said it’s too late, we can’t do it.”


The AMC acquisition also came with a new car, the Renault Premier, and it soon became clear that this design spoke to the future in ways reworked K-cars could not. Based on the French Renault 25 but heavily reworked for Americans by AMC (and ItalDesign), the Premier, powered by either an AMC four-banger or the[PRV V-6](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/could-this-turbo-engine-have-saved-delorean/) , began rolling off the line at Bramalea in September 1987 for the North American market as the Eagle Premier.


## The Eagle Premier and Cab Forward


Stellantis


“I loved the Premier,” says Jason Vines, who was, back then, the freshly appointed head of PR for the new Jeep-Eagle division, created both to integrate the AMC/Jeep dealer network and to give standalone Jeep dealers other products to sell. “At the time I thought the Premier was the best product we had, quality-wise, and the most in-tune with the market. When it was new, we set up a program for journalists called the ‘Eagle Challenge,’ to drive it back to back with the Accord and Camry, and it always did well.”


But Eagle, a name partly drawn from AMC’s last model and partly from Lee Iacocca’s imagination, was a new brand with an inconsistent lineup. “The other initial Eagle car, the \[Renault-sourced\] Medallion, was a piece of sh*t,” Vines says. “Plus, there was too much badge engineering. In the beginning, the products were to be exclusively for Eagle, but then there was a Dodge Monaco version of the Premier, which the Dodge dealers said they could sell a lot more of than the Eagle Dealers, which they did not. All of the other Eagles were shared with Mitsubishi or Plymouth.”


The Premier sold well initially and then soon flagged, but it had all the hardware to be a winner, so it became the basis of the LH.


Alex Kwanten


“The LH was all developed at Highland Park,” Gale says, “but they kept the packaging and platform layout of the AMC car, which is what gave it that long snout and the north-south engine. One thing that we all liked was that the crash structure and crush zones allowed us to move the windshield further forward … it let the A-pillars go forward, which made for easier entry and exit.” This created a very airy, open interior, and the team also moved the rear wheels back, creating a huge back seat that was easy to get in and out of.


Alex Kwanten


The design not only created a great canvas for the ideas Gale’s team had been working on, which were showcased in concepts like the 1990 Eagle Optima (“I wish I had trademarked the name, because Kia got it.”), but also gave it a marketing hook. According to Gale, it was PR man Tom Kowaleski, another AMC vet, who came up with the “cab forward” label. “These two words instantly explained the whole point of the design to people.”


While philosophically based on the Premier, the LHs had lots of new hardware. The climate-control system was benchmarked against the one in the BMW 7 Series. Four-wheel antilock brakes and a multilink rear suspension were standard. Power came from the then-still-new 3.3-liter OHV V-6 designed for the minivans or, on the top trims, a brand-new 214-horsepower 3.5-liter SOHC V-6, which had almost as much power as the Ford Taurus SHO.

Pushing the wheels to the extreme corners of the car and giving it a wide track all but guaranteed stability and a smooth ride, but Castaing was also a former Renault Sport tech director who had won Le Mans with the company and spearheaded its 1970s F1 program. He and Glenn Gardner had high standards for how the LHs should drive: They had to handle well, but also be easy, quiet, and smooth to live with. Both reportedly beat the hell out of prototypes at the proving grounds in all kinds of weather.


Alex Kwanten


Alex Kwanten


There would only be three mainstream models. The Concorde and Intrepid were easy to style, drawing on inspiration from the Portofino, Optima, and themes from the Viper. “We started with the crosshair grille, which we picked up from our own history, and it was easy to make a cohesive style,” Gale says. “The Concorde had more visual frills; all three used soft 1990s forms throughout. The Vision was sort of a Concorde in a sharper suit.”


I asked Gale about whether there were plans for a Plymouth LH, but there really weren’t. “I would’ve known if anybody knew, but there really wasn’t anything terribly serious.”

## The LHs Are a Hit


If the behind-the-scenes action was exciting, Chrysler’s forward-facing image in 1990 and ’91 was not.


Profits had been slumping since 1986, its cash reserves had shrunk by 50% in those two years, and there was constant speculation about Iacocca’s succession plan. “Everything we had was relatively long in the tooth,” Gale says. “And I was part of the team that went around the world selling shares to investment banks with \[then-Chrysler CFO\] Jerry York, and we had a tough time selling at even a modest price.”


In August 1991, the company held its annual press preview. “Internally, we called that one our ‘tread water’ preview,” says Vines, “because we had nothing. We had a new engine for the *old* Ram pickup, which was like 25 years old. But we were excited because we knew what was coming.”


Alex Kwanten


That fall, Chrysler geared up for a $70M ad campaign to introduce the new cars at the 1992 Detroit show, alongside a preview of the LHS and New Yorker, which would arrive a year later. They got a warm reception from critics and consumers alike, though after the show reporters mentioned that some Chrysler folks had joked that LH stood for “Let’s Hope,” which the *New York Times* and a few other places translated to “Last Hope.”


When they hit dealer lots that September, the cars were immediately popular. In the first year, the trio sold just over 147,000 units, which was slightly below Chrysler’s target, but once word was out, 1994 sales surged past 220,000, plus 80,000 units of the fancier senior LH models, the New Yorker and LHS. Counting those cars, more than 1.2 million first-gen LHs were sold through the end of 1997. “When we went back to Asia to sell shares in 1993,” Gale says, “we quickly sold the whole book at a substantially higher price.”

## Whatever Happened to Eagle?


There were only two things that didn’t gel: First, the New Yorker just wasn’t as popular as the LHS, so it only lasted into 1996. And second, the Eagle Vision never really found a market, though Chrysler did keep investing in it, adding the manu-matic “Auto Stick” system in 1995 and prepping a replacement for 1998, which was previewed as the 1995 Eagle Jazz concept. Ultimately, it became the Chrysler 300M.


With the AMC acquisition a distant memory by 1997, the Eagle brand withered and was wound down in 1998, with the popular Talon being the last model to go. “We were Saturn before Saturn,” Vines says. “Let’s start with a bang and then give you nothing.” Eagle just never formed a cohesive identity because of all the badge engineering, which made establishing any real identity difficult, but in Vines’ view, management also wasn’t willing to give Eagle variants of some of its most popular ideas, like a minivan.


Alex Kwanten


That same year, the DaimlerChrysler merger happened, and after the initially bright headlines, it soon became clear the merger blunted much of the momentum and the culture of agility that the LHs had ushered in, with Lutz and Castaing departing, among others. Plans for a Plymouth revival were scrapped, and two new cars, the Prowler and PT Cruiser, became Chryslers. “I’ll just be bold and say it before you ask,” Gale says of the merger: “That was the ruination of our company.”


Gale stayed, and he ultimately led the creation of the rear-wheel-drive LX cars, which were the antithesis of the cab-forward idea. Why the change? While the LHs had been perfectly timed for 1993, he says, by the early 2000s a large front-drive car basically meant a Buick LeSabre. Consumers thought more of—and were willing to pay more for—rear-drive large cars, which also dovetailed well with Daimler’s hardware.


## LHs Today


Alex Kwanten


John McIntosh was wowed enough by the LH when it was new to actually go to a dealer and check them out. “I went in 1993 in my ‘81 200SX,” he says, though he couldn’t afford an LH at the time. Nearly 30 years later, and after he had gradually acquired an eclectic collection of K-cars, BMWs, Buick Reattas, Yugos, and other things, a friend sent him a Craigslist ad for the Vision. “I thought, ‘Oh yeah!’ and went to drive it. The car had 40,000 miles on it, and the seller only wanted $3500, so it was a low-risk buy.


Stepping into the Vision, I was immediately struck by how open and airy it feels compared to modern cars, with their thick A-pillars. It feels genuinely huge inside, in front and in back, even compared to the Mercury Grand Marquis in my own driveway.


Alex Kwanten


The gray plastics of both the Vision and the Intrepid look a little dated now, but their soft, oval forms immediately transport you back to the ’90s. At the time, compared to the angular forms and disco-era dashboards of the K-cars, the LH’s cabins seemed as radical as their exteriors. Everything in them is ergonomically designed and easy to use, too. There’s a little self-diagnostic “message center” in the Intrepid, and its white-dial gauges still look fantastic. Both cars still seem modern 30 years later, and they drive that way, too.


Alex Kwanten


The Vision came only with the bigger 3.5-liter V-6, and the Intrepid SE is mechanically almost identical. While not as quick as modern cars, they still have 214 horsepower and handle well for cars with three-decade-old suspension bushings. There’s plenty of steering feedback, and the 16-inch alloys, far out wheels in 1993 but on the small side today, still leave enough tire sidewall to smooth out the bumps, helped by the car’s giant 113-inch wheelbase. It’s not a sports car, but it’s fun to drive, and quiet, with relatively little wind and road noise.


You could easily daily this car, and McIntosh has put 10,000 largely trouble-free miles on the Vision. This usability is one reason why so few survive; many 1990s “normie” cars have simply been run till they can run no more.


Alex Kwanten


The Vision was also always rare in comparison to its stablemates—sales peaked at 22,678 cars in 1993, and ultimately 94,550 were built. Despite this, none of the LHs are worth very much, so if you’re looking for one, McIntosh advises holding out for the nicest one you can. Even the best early LH in the world probably isn’t worth more than $7500, which is a bargain for something so visually striking and with a pretty illustrious history.


“These cars were a godsend,” Vines says. “They didn’t do what the minivans did, but they finally got us respect in that segment.”
