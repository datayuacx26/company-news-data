# The Collector-Car Market’s Widening Split, in Two Charts

One of the business world’s biggest buzzwords of the past year or so is the “K-shaped” economy. It refers to strong growth in one part of the economy, and slow or stagnant growth in another. For example, last year Moody’s Analytics reported that the top 10% of earners accounted for nearly half of[consumer spending](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-16/top-10-of-earners-drive-a-growing-share-of-us-consumer-spending) in the U.S., which was the highest share Moody’s has ever recorded. In other words, the top of the economy is driving most of its growth.


The collector car hobby is not the economy, of course, but there have been some similar trends in recent months, in which high-dollar cars make staggering jumps in value while many more mainstream vehicles are either dropping or treading water, and not keeping up with inflation. The metrics that make up our[Hagerty Market Rating](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/the-collector-car-market-continues-to-cool-as-summer-heats-up/) have been demonstrating this trend with every monthly update recently.


There is another view that shows the widening gap between the most expensive cars and the more common ones: When looking at auction sale prices, the gap between *average* sale price and *median* sale price is widening. Shown on the graph below are the 12-month rolling average and median sale prices at live auctions in North America over the past decade.


First off, a quick explainer for the folks who didn’t take statistics. In a set of sales prices, for example, average (aka “mean”) combines all the prices, then divides the sum by the number of prices. The result is a single dollar figure that is a representative of that set of prices. Median also seeks the middle, but to calculate it, you arrange the prices by smallest to largest, then find the price in the very middle, with half of the prices below it and half the prices above it.


Sometimes average and median can be similar or even identical. But outliers will throw off an average more than they will a median. Let’s say eight cars sell for an attainable $35,000 each, but two cars sell for an expensive $180,000. The average of all comes out to $64K, while the median stays at $35K.


That’s a big difference, but the gap on the graph above is much larger, and it’s growing. Both average and median sale price at auction have been trending up since 2020, but the average has been growing at a faster clip. This suggests a relatively small but growing number of high six-, seven-, and eight-figure sales are pulling up the average, while the majority of collector vehicles continue to trade in the mid-five-figure range.


Historically, average price has always been significantly above median in the automotive auction world. Every live auction has at least one headline car that draws attention to the sale, makes headlines, sells for a much larger amount than the rest of the consignment list, and pulls up the average. But the number of those headline cars is growing, as are their sale prices relative to the more mainstream stuff crossing the block at the same auction.


This is just one measure, but it matches what we’ve been seeing elsewhere, from supercars making the biggest gains in the[Hagerty Price Guide](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/5-cars-gaining-value-in-an-ever-widening-market/) to high-end sales activity propping up the Hagerty Market Rating, which measures the overall health of the collector market. Whatever heat there is in the collector car market in 2026, it’s mostly at the top.
