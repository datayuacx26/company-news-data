# Piston Slap: Nobody Suspects the Horn Button!

*(We are running low on reader questions to feed the Piston Slap series, so email us at pistonslap@hagerty.com to keep us running with a full tank!)*


*George* writes:


Sajeev,


Some time back, the horn on my van began blowing for no reason. I pulled the power line to the horn. I figured the relay had probably decayed, so I bought another relay. I got that in a couple of weeks ago and tested the circuit out. I got power on the line when I hit the horn button, so I hooked up the horn. Nada. I removed the horn and connected it to my lawn tractor battery. Nada. So I bought a new horn and installed that.


Everything worked perfectly until about 8:30 PM. The horn began blowing again for no reason, so I pulled the power line again. A day or two later, I checked the line, and there was no power on it. I did not think to check the battery at that time, and with the temperature over 100 degrees, I won’t be doing that soon.


I think the problem must be caused by the wire between the horn button and the relay shorting out to ground. What’s causing it to do that without any outside action? There’s also some possibility of a problem with the horn button, but, again, what’s causing this? This is a 1961 Econoline van, so there aren’t places for rodents to hide. If the line is shorting out, I think the relay will have drained the battery before opening up again.


Any suggestions you might have for troubleshooting are welcome. I can’t very well just hook it all back up and wait for the problem to occur, nor am I likely to pull on my jeans and run out late at 2:00 AM if it happens then.


*Sajeev* answers:


The horn button, *it’s always the horn button* .


***These things are wear items, folks!***


This is a sore subject for me, as I’ve had to deal with failing horn buttons that trigger late-night freak-outs on my 1980s Ford products. They fail after 25 or so years because the insulating pad—between the copper contacts that connect to activate the horn—inside the button is made out of foam. It’s very similar to the stuff used in headliners, and it rots away just like that ceiling fabric.


I was forced to DIY the horn repair with leftover packaging material from electronics shipments, but these days, the foam is reproduced and sold as a “horn button repair pad” online. That’s not fair: I spent far too much time “con-foam-ing” (sorry) packaging material to the bizarre contouring of Ford’s copper horn contacts. But I will get over my trauma, because it’s lovely to see the aftermarket making a restoration part for a known wear item.

But this information isn’t entirely relevant to George and his ’61 Econoline. So let’s exchange the word “foam” for the words “horn ring contact and brush.” This spring-loaded part likely loses its resilience after 65 years, triggering the horn as it fails. I’d be far less springy after 65 years of getting pressed in anger by an agitated motorist, too!


George, do yourself a favor and get a new “horn ring contact and brush” from anyone selling[Dennis Carpenter](https://www.dennis-carpenter.com/cars/electrical/horn/c20z-13a821-a-horn-ring-contact-brush-assemb?srsltid=AfmBOoppdB5NGa6zuw6dqJ5XG3N-zHr-TN4aS2WDAgsh-vtgoDGmM3x6) reproduction Ford parts. The image above suggests that disassembly is pretty easy, making this restorative effort a piece of cake. Fingers crossed on that!


Doing this is much cheaper than getting an entirely new horn button assembly. Even better, you get to keep your “Econoline” branded button in the process! *(My quick search for replacement horn buttons suggests they only make replacements with the Ford emblem.)*
