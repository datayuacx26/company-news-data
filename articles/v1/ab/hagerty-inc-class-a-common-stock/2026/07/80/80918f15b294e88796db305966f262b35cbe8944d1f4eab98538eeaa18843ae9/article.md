# The Deadliest Decade: The Battle for Aero Supremacy

Thus commenced the Battle for Aero Supremacy. Not another race passed where a new wrinkle wasn’t introduced. It wasn’t immediately clear whether this was a testament to the technical ingenuity of Formula 1 or to one-upmanship run amok. Wings moved farther forward and farther back. They grew wider and taller, then wider and taller still. They were attached to the bodywork. They were attached to the hubs. They were fixed. They were adjustable. The system operated by compressed air. Or by electrohydraulics. Or via cable or bungee cord. The drivers moved the wings as they saw fit, or the wings moved automatically, depending on whether the brakes were applied, or fifth gear was selected, or the moon was in the seventh house of the seventh sun and Jupiter was aligned with Mars. By the fall of 1968, every car sported wings varied enough to confound an ornithologist.

Jacky Ickx, cornering in a Brabham Cosworth BT26A at the 1969 Spanish Grand Prix, was in second place when his rear wing failed. After it was replaced, he returned to the race but retired with rear suspension failure, a result of the previous mechanical problem.


David Phipps/Sutton Images/Getty Images


Some outsiders wondered if it was all just a game of monkey see, monkey do, and skeptics suggested that the major benefit of wings was that they made the drivers feel more confident. The teams knew better. At Monza, a wingless BRM emerged from Curva Grande—the intimidating sweeper at the end of the pit straight—at 156 mph. With a wing, the exit speed was 168 mph. Tire temperature, meanwhile, climbed from 130 degrees to 160 degrees, which demonstrated how much more grip was being developed. Lap times were an even better barometer. Bruce McLaren reported early in the wing-development game that aero aids were worth one to one-and-a-half seconds a lap. By the final race of the season in Mexico City, the fastest lap, recorded by Jo Siffert in a high-wing Lotus 49, was nearly four seconds faster than[Jim Clark](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/family-photos-from-the-65-indy-500-solidify-a-shared-respect-for-jim-clark/) had gone in an aerodynamically clean version of the same car the previous year.


Wings were great, everybody agreed. Exactly why, nobody knew for sure. “We didn’t have any wind tunnels,” said Herbie Blash, a longtime FIA official who was then a mechanic at Lotus. “It was just, ‘Stick this on’ and ‘Stick that on.’[Colin \[Chapman\]](https://www.hagerty.com/media/member-stories/a-life-with-colin-chapmans-old-lotus-eleven/) was flying his own plane, so he obviously understood aerodynamics to a certain degree. But we had no real technical knowledge about what would work and what wouldn’t work.” And what empirical data existed was derived almost entirely from what happened when new untested parts were deployed on race weekends.


Fast-and-dirty prototype development has a lot going for it, but it also means taking the bad with the good. In the case of wings, failure *was* an option, and it occurred early and often. By the time the teams reached Rouen for the French Grand Prix, Chapman had soured on the small, vestigial wing that debuted at Monaco. Now, in typical Chapman fashion, he’d fitted his Lotus 49Bs with the biggest, tallest, most outrageous wings in Formula 1. In the garage, young Jackie Oliver, who’d been hurriedly drafted into the team after Clark’s death, pushed on the airfoil, which made a disquieting *boinggg!* sound as it visibly vibrated on the slender mounting struts. “Is that all right?” he asked. To which Chapman scornfully replied, “Lad, when you sit on an airplane and you look out the window when it takes off, the wing is flexing because that’s what keeps it from breaking.”


Oliver shelved his doubts, and when he headed out for practice, he was pleased to experience “fantastic” levels of grip—briefly. On his third flying lap, streaking past the grandstands at 190 mph, he closed on Richard Attwood’s BRM, which was just coming out of the pits. As Attwood moved over to allow Oliver to pass, the turbulent air behind him tilted back the wing of the Lotus so that it was generating lift instead of downforce. Uh-oh. The rear wheels levitated off the ground, and the car walloped a low wall so hard that the gearbox, transaxle, and rear wheels broke off as a single assembly, leaving Oliver dazed and confused in what was pretty much the only part of the chassis that hadn’t been destroyed.


Skid marks and wreckage show how fortunate Jackie Oliver was to escape a practice run crash in a Lotus 49B, French Grand Prix, 1968.


David Phipps/Sutton Images/Getty Images


Chapman, who had a fractious relationship with most of his drivers unless they were named Clark or Andretti, blamed Oliver for losing control. Despite the accident, and even though several observers told Chapman that they’d seen the Lotus’s wings flexing conspicuously at speed, he opted for even higher airfoils at the next race, Brands Hatch. The wings survived the British Grand Prix; in fact, Oliver would have won if his transmission hadn’t disintegrated while he was leading. This time, John Surtees was the one with wing woes. After the freshly mounted airfoil on his Honda RA301 snapped off, he finished the race with the naked struts blowing in the wind and a set of bald rear tires, thanks to the sudden lack of grip. At the U.S. Grand Prix, the rear wing of Jochen Rindt’s Brabham BT26 collapsed at both ends so that it looked like a tepee built with an erector set. At the next race, in Mexico, a strut on local hero Moisés Solana’s Lotus 49B broke, forcing him to pit to have the wing ripped off and trashed.


By the time the 1969 season began in South Africa, F1 was deep in the throes of wing mania. Lotus, Brabham, and Matra all showed up with biplane arrangements—two sets of high wings, front and rear. During practice, Kyalami looked more like an airport than a racetrack. The wings of two of the Lotuses and both of the Brabhams failed during practice or the race. Still, Jackie Stewart won in a biplane-equipped Matra MS10, outrunning Hill in his twin-wing Lotus. At Montjuïc Park, the twisty street circuit in Barcelona hosting the Spanish Grand Prix, Chapman reasoned that he needed even bigger wings. After practice on Friday, Lotus mechanics worked into the night fabricating 6-inch-wide extensions with fiberglass and foam to either side of the rear wing. “No testing, no scrutineering,” said mechanic Beaky Sims. “It shows what you could do back then.”


Jochen Rindt, in a Lotus Cosworth 49B, was leading the race by a huge margin when he suffered a rear wing failure at over 160 mph at the Spanish Grand Prix, 1969.


David Phipps/Sutton Images/Getty Images


Despite the lack of testing, the Lotuses qualified 1-3 in the hands of Rindt and Hill. Rindt led from the flag and walked away from the field. As he started his ninth lap, Hill’s wing collapsed after he crested the bump on the front straight at 160 mph, and his car was demolished when it rammed into the guardrail on the left and ricocheted into the Armco on the right. Hill was unhurt. But as Sims ran out from the pits, Hill saw the wing sagging on Rindt’s car. “His wing is going! His wing is going!” he yelled. “Beaky, get back to the pits and tell Chapman to bring him in.” Too late. When Sims was halfway there, he heard a huge crash as Rindt’s wing failed. Rindt cannoned into Hill’s wrecked Lotus and then vaulted onto the guardrail. If the launch angle had been slightly higher, he would have landed in the crowd. As it was, Sims said, “The nose of the car was bent up like a banana. We couldn’t get \[Rindt\] out, and we could hear the battery shorting, and we could smell fuel. Finally, Graham got astride the car and just *yanked* him out.”
