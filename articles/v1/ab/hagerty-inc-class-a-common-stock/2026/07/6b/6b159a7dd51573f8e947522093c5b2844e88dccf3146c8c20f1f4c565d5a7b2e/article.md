# Why Packard Still Matters

On a hill just behind The Lodge at Pebble Beach on California’s Monterey Peninsula, the world’s most exclusive traffic jam starts before sunrise every third Sunday in August. A queue of some 200 meticulously fettled machines sit bumper to bumper, waiting to roll onto a golf fairway and bask in the boundary where commerce and technology intersect with art. Among them in 2025 were 13 Packards, for the simple reason that there are always Packards on the lawn at Pebble Beach, because staging a Pebble Beach Concours d’Elegance without a Packard is like staging *King Lear* without the king.


Drive almost any antique car dating from the earliest years to about 1940 and, at some point, you are bound to be asked if it’s a Packard. Nearly 70 years after the brand disappeared from showrooms, it is the one name from the classic era that average people can recall at a moment’s notice. Packard’s fame lives on in old gangster movies, in stories that fathers and grandfathers tell at family gatherings about the cars they admired when they were kids, and in car shows where wide-eyed children gaze up at towering chrome grilles that seem to scrape the heavens, their parents unconsciously replanting the seed in the next generation by reading a placard and saying out loud, “Oh yeah, that’s a Packard.”


Packard used several mascots over the decades, perhaps most famously the “chrome doughnut lady,” as some call the Goddess of Speed (top, left), a tribute to the Greek goddess Nike, which appeared in various forms on Packards from 1926 to 1950.


Matt Tierney


If there were a Mount Rushmore for cars, a Packard grille would be at top dead center. Fully a quarter-century into the 21st century, a Packard remains the bluest of blue-chip American classics, the quintessential car from the before times, the one that best epitomizes the elegance and formality of an earlier age and the old trope that “they sure don’t make ’em like they used to.” Machines bearing that famous script logo have roamed all the continents and defended democracy in the air and at sea. You don’t even have to ask the man who owns one, per the company’s famous ad tagline, because everyone already knows that Packard was the best America had to offer in a period when its empire was ascendant and its citizens seemed confident that it always would be.


> On a Mount Rushmore for cars, a Packard grille would be top dead center.


“They were just a remarkably successful high-quality luxury firm in the era when a stand-alone luxury marque could survive,” affirms Chris Bock, chief judge at Pebble Beach and a strong advocate for keeping Packard as one of the first brands to be given a standing class at the event. That was in 1983, and more Packards have won Pebble’s coveted Best of Show award than any other brand except for Duesenberg. Says Bock, “I hear people saying, ‘Why would we celebrate Packard instead of Cadillac?’ and then I have to point out that there are more classic Packards extant than all the other luxury marques combined. They outsold most of the other marques from the classic era. It’s easy to defend.”


“They used the best materials, they had the best craftsmen, Packard was the soft-spoken boss of the road,” said Ross Myers, a dairy farmer from Boyertown, Pennsylvania, who brought to Pebble a ’32 V-12 roadster. “I like fixing up stuff, and if you’re going to spend all the time to fix up an old car, I like it to be something special, and Packards to me are special.”


“Everybody who is into classic cars wants a Packard,” said Stephen Babinsky of Automotive Restorations in Clinton Township, New Jersey. A serial Packard owner himself, he was on the lawn that morning overseeing the pre-judging prep of a dark blue Dietrich-body ’34 1107 Twelve Coupe restored in his shop. “Of course everybody wants a Duesenberg, too, but they are a different level. For somebody who wants to get into the hobby, Packards are the way to go because they’re really good cars and they’re affordable. They are just well engineered, and they’re good driving cars, especially the early ’30s stuff. I have Cadillacs, I have Chryslers, I have all kinds of sh*t, and the Packard is just the best all-around car.”


Matt Tierney


A new car bearing the Packard name hasn’t sold since 1958, yet the brand remains as relevant as Bentley, Bugatti, and Chevrolet in the hearts of collectors. What makes it so special has something to do with the magnificent cars and engines and a lot to do with the people behind them, most of whom didn’t bear the name Packard. In fact, Packard is further proof that men such as Henry Ford and Walter P. Chrysler, founders who remained with their companies long enough to see their names grow into carmaking institutions, are rarities in the annals of auto history. Louis Chevrolet lasted barely four years in his namesake startup before selling out, David Dunbar Buick just seven years. The engineering-minded James Ward Packard and his business-savvy brother William Doud Packard lasted only a decade in the car business before handing over the enterprise to others.


Matt Tierney


Descendants of early English colonial settlers, the thriving Packards of elysian, well-to-do Warren, Ohio, were already successful in various fields. The brothers ran a bustling factory producing incandescent lamps, wiring, and other electrical devices while their sister, Alaska Packard Davidson, was destined to become the first female investigator at the FBI (and one of the last until 1972, after being fired in 1924 by newly installed director J. Edgar Hoover).


A trip to France in 1896 saw James Ward return with a de Dion-Bouton motorized tricycle, a stubborn and idiosyncratic device which seemed determined to uphold every crass French stereotype. Packard’s growing interest in cars undaunted, he then bought a car from Alexander Winton, his Scottish neighbor up the road in Cleveland. It proved to be hardly more reliable. According to *Packard: A History of the Motor Car and the Company,* by the editors of *Automobile Quarterly* , Packard made numerous trips back to the factory to get his motor carriage fixed and to suggest engineering changes. Finally, in 1899, in a scene that would play out again some 60 years later between Enzo Ferrari and Ferruccio Lamborghini, Winton exploded and told Packard that if he thought he could do better, he should damn well build his own car.


Packard thought about it, then poached several of Winton’s key employees. His new auto firm, The New York and Ohio Company, produced its first car, the Model A, in November 1899 (the name was soon amended to The Ohio Automobile Company). A harbinger of future Packards and their renowned durability, that first car still exists, a gift from James Ward Packard to his alma mater, the engineering school at Lehigh University, which maintains it to this day in running condition.


The new design featured a number of firsts for the auto industry. As a spark-fired internal combustion engine revs up, the timing of the spark needs to advance, and the standard practice into the 1920s was to affix a lever on the steering wheel that the driver could advance manually to keep the engine running properly. Being electrical men, the Packards endowed their car with automatic spark advance years before it became common. There was a rev limiter built into the distributor to prolong engine life. Orders began to trickle in as word got around town and the region. Legend has it that the company’s famous slogan, “Ask the man who owns one,” was born on a busy day in 1901 when the office secretary asked James Ward for a reply to a letter from a potential buyer in Pittsburgh inquiring about the car’s features. “Tell him I’ll be over to talk with him—no, wait, tell him to ask the man who owns one.”


Right about so many things in 1899, by 1901, Packard was already falling out of step with the fast-moving industry. Yes, Packard was an early American adopter of the steering wheel to replace the tiller. The company also established the industry’s first training school for service mechanics. But the founder of the company that would later be famous for its V-12s believed that “more than one cylinder on a Packard would be like two tails on a cat—you just don’t need it.” James Ward held that a simple, reliable engine placed under the seat, which kept the weight centralized and low, with more of it on the rear axle for traction and stability, was better than putting a larger, more complex one under a removable cover out in front of the driver, as was rapidly becoming the industry standard.


William Doud Packard pilots the company’s second car, the Model B, past the original plant in Warren, Ohio.


Bettmann Archive/Getty Images


Besides, James Ward and William Doud’s enthusiasm for the project seemed to be waning as their first interest, the growing electrical components business, demanded more of their attention. It’s thought that officially changing the name from the Ohio Automobile Company to the Packard Motor Car Company in late 1902 was an effort to make it easier to raise capital through share sales and, if necessary, move the operation out of state. No doubt they were influenced in part by the son of a prominent Detroit railroading family who had decided to become a major investor in Packard, the first of several outsiders who would build Packard into what it would become.


Henry Joy’s arrival was the beginning of the end for Packard the carmaking brothers and the start of Packard the automobile institution. An ardent automobilist and fan of the product, Joy somehow convinced James Ward to hire a new chief engineer, Frenchman Charles Schmidt, who brought to the company fresh ideas already being tried in Europe. The proverbial two-tailed cat, the Packard Model G, arrived in 1902. Joy brought in other wealthy Detroit investors, and soon the rumor spread that the whole company would soon be decamping for the embryonic Motor City.


By all accounts, Joy’s Detroit mob was amply funded and well organized, meaning Packard had a solid financial backing at a time when the industry was rife with penniless dreamers and honey-tongued charlatans. Joy had big ideas for Packard, the main one being the construction of an enormous new factory to replace the scattershot workshops in Warren where the cars had been built to that point.


Given Joy’s ties to Detroit, a move was inevitable. Joy purchased the Weber farm, a 67-acre cow pasture at Grand Boulevard and Concord Avenue just northeast of what was then Detroit’s city limits, and hired the architect Albert Kahn, the already respected German immigrant whose industrial designs for Ford, Chrysler, and General Motors would become iconic. The newspapers estimated the new plant would increase the city’s labor pool by at least 1000 and drive up local property values. Joy figured his future king of car companies needed a suitably impressive castle. One early visitor to “the Boulevard,” as the new Packard headquarters came to be known, was no less than Henry Ford, whose own Packard limousine was stolen off the streets in Detroit in September 1916. The auto magnate went in person to retrieve the chassis and engine numbers in order to file a complete police report.


“The Boulevard,” as insiders called Packard’s sprawling plant in Detroit.


Universal Images Group/Getty Images


Packard’s model designations by letter gave way to model designations by numerical series, a practice the company would use for decades, eschewing the more traditional use of model years that came with a relentless imperative for annual changes. The two-cylinder engines grew into four, then into six, then, in 1915, into the 12-cylinder Twin Six, the start of Packard’s long quest for the smoothest, most refined horsepower possible.


“They didn’t get lost in the weeds trying to reinvent the wheel,” notes John Bothwell of Douglas and Company, a prewar specialist in Costa Mesa, California, who has worked on a number of Packards. “They stayed with conventional wisdom: When T-head engines were the thing, they had T-head engines, when everybody was doing twin-cylinder engines, that’s what they did. They weren’t trying to reengineer stuff, they just went with the trends, but they focused on doing what they did at a very high quality.”


By 1912, a $6200 Packard limo was an avatar of American wealth.


Universal Images Group/Getty Images


The titular president of the company, James Ward had long since become a passenger on his own train, and in 1909, he and William Doud stepped back completely from the auto business. Henry Joy would soon be gone too, a victim of his boundless enthusiasm for distractions that the Packard board found exhausting. The search for the perfect man to run the enterprise took a year and ended when the next great figure in Packard’s history stepped into the story.


Alvan Macauley—he hated when people misspelled it “Alvin”—was a 42-year-old patent lawyer from West Virginia who was already feted in Detroit’s industrial circles for reviving the fortunes of what would become the Burroughs Adding Machine Company. He did so in part with a lightning move of the factory and its 400 workers from St. Louis to Detroit that lost only two days of production. By 1916, Macauley was the president of Packard, a position he held along with chairman of the board for the next 32 years.


The original garages at the Packard Proving Grounds Historic Site in Shelby Township, Michigan, are a perfect backdrop for an assortment of Packard’s elegant products. Opened in 1928 as the first of its type in the auto industry, the facility is now a 17-acre museum and event space.


Matt Tierney


The papers called him “tight-lipped and bespectacled,” and “the personification of conservatism.” It was said that he never lost his temper, that his favorite food was turtle soup, that he could both milk a cow and mill his own furniture, and that he hand-carved cigarette cases to give as gifts to friends. Besides a wood shop in which he spun out chairs and cabinets and miniature furniture for his grandchildren’s dolls, Macauley’s Grosse Pointe mansion contained rare tapestries, artwork by the great masters such as Rembrandt, and an enormous library. He could recite long literary passages from memory, and his favorite book was *Lorna Doone* , a 19th-century romance epic by the English author Richard Doddridge Blackmore.


Which was slightly ironic since Macauley found long names annoying, a reason he dropped his own first name, James, to make his signature quicker to sign (a practice he encouraged in subordinates). His management philosophy was summed up by an inscription that hung for years in the Packard executive offices: “I know of no more stricture that can be passed upon the manager of any business than to have it said of him that in all his direction and handling of men, he developed mediocrities. We are on earth that we may ourselves succeed and enable others to succeed; the manager who develops among his organization the greatest number of successful men will himself enjoy the greatest measure of success.”


Packard and Alvan Macauley are inextricably linked, Macauley having successfully helmed the company through the Great Depression and two world wars. He was the person the average Packard buyer wanted to be.


Packard Proving Grounds Historic Site


Urging Congress to lower tariffs just months before the Great Crash of 1929 got him on the July cover of *Time* magazine. In a rapidly growing Detroit that was a melting pot of immigrants, Macauley was an ardent America-firster, telling his shop-floor workers that advancement at Packard was dependent on learning English. They apparently revered him, partly because experienced craftsmen who grew too old to mill wood or machine metal were given easier jobs within the plant rather than turned out in the street. His stature among the rank and file climbed further when it was reported that his salary of $256,000 in 1929 was whittled down to $30,000 in the lean year of 1933. Unlike many of today’s executives, Macauley shared the pain when business was bad.


> Macauley was the embodiment of Packard’s customer.


American newspapers helped make Macauley a household name by occasionally running his bylined column in which he opined on the economic and industrial issues of the day. His overriding message was one of optimism. Business was always looking up, from Macauley’s perspective, so long as men of good character were willing to do hard work. As chairman of the National Automobile Chamber of Commerce, one of several industry trade groups he chaired during his career, Macauley weighed in on everything from the shortage of parking spaces in Philadelphia to “the evils” of the trade-in scheme.


Matt Tierney


Contrary to his colleagues at other companies who were pioneering planned obsolescence with annual model changes, Macauley exhorted Packard owners never to trade in, but to keep their cars until they were entirely spent. Otherwise, he said, it only encouraged the industry to produce cheap, disposable cars, a waste of money and resources. “A homely but exact parallel might be found in a man at a fruit stand,” he wrote in a 1927 column that reads like one of Franklin Roosevelt’s folksy fireside chats. “A nickel for an apple, one bite, and then another apple might catch his eye because of a brighter color. One bite from that, after payment of another nickel, and then an unusual plumpness might intrigue him into the purchase of a third.


“The remainders of the first two apples are as sound and just as good in every way as before the bites were taken, but they have been stigmatized and their value lowered because of use. They are used apples.” Macauley wondered that people who were so careful with their nickels by eating apples down to their core were nonetheless “prodigal with their hundreds of dollars” by trading in perfectly good automobiles.


A 1925 Model 333 embodies a period when many of Packard’s rivals disappeared, unable to survive the era of the Model T and mass production.


Matt Tierney


Relentlessly upright, always immaculately dressed, a pillar in the business community, and apparently without vices, Macauley was the embodiment of Packard’s idealized customer. While the Hollywood glitterati bought Packards clothed in flamboyant bespoke coachwork by Dietrich, Murphy, or Hibbard & Darrin, many ordinary bankers and businessmen bought Standard Eights because they thought they were—or wanted to be—Alvan Macauley. And in the next great figure in Packard history, chief engineer Jesse Gurney Vincent, whom Macauley brought over from Burroughs in 1912, the company had the man to create the cars to fit the image.


Matt Tierney


An Arkansan who dropped out of school in the eighth grade to work as a blacksmith and a machinist, Vincent would eventually attach his name to more than 400 patents. He wasn’t at Packard long before drafting a 35-page memo outlining the advantages of a V-12 engine. “From the time when the first practical car made its first run on the road,” Vincent summed up, “there have been three things which every motorist asked for—more range of ability, greater smoothness, and less noise.” These attributes came to define Packard’s guiding principles through its golden years of the ’20s and ’30s.


Though Vincent would be associated with the development of four-wheel and power brakes, air conditioning, and hypoid gears, as well as one of the industry’s first vehicle proving grounds, his first love was engines. Anchoring the company’s reputation as the “Master Motor Builders” were perfectly balanced straight-sixes and -eights and whispering V-12s with hydraulic valve-lash adjusters for ultimate silence. An avid pilot, Vincent was a co-architect of Packard’s World War I mainstay, the light but powerful Liberty aircraft engine (an effort that earned him the rank of colonel in the U.S. Army Signal Corps). With Macauley’s enthusiastic support, he also developed the first diesel aircraft engine. And he worked with champion boat racer Gar Wood to stuff multiple Liberty engines into racing hulls in the 1920s, an effort that directly led to the company’s marine engine business.


The 1942 Model 2021 was Packard’s last expression of elegance before the war.


Matt Tierney


With the next war looming, Packard accepted the enormous job of building the Rolls-Royce Merlin aircraft engine under license after Ford passed on the contract, judging the hand-built 27-liter V-12 to be “unsuitable for mass production.” Vincent wrote one of his legendary memos, enthusiastically endorsing the project and figuring the company would need a separate plant employing 14,000 workers to fulfill a wartime demand that would see more than 55,000 Packard-Merlins produced.


Delicate negotiations with the protective British over the design dragged out for months, their armed services demanding features in which the U.S. War Department had no interest. Everything from thread pitches to block castings to carburetor types to supercharger configurations were points of contention. While Packard didn’t completely redesign the Merlin, it made so many changes that its version resembled the original about as much as a Tennessee twang resembled a Liverpool scouse. The same mother tongue, but that’s about it.


Chief engineer Jesse Gurney Vincent was interested in boat racing, which led directly to Packard building huge marine engines. Examples of Packard cars and engines can be seen at the Packard Proving Grounds Historic Site in Michigan.


Matt Tierney


At the same time, Packard cranked out thousands of the 4M 2500, a 2490-cubic-inch, 1200-hp V-12 marine leviathan. Three of these engines in the 80-foot Elco *PT-109* propelled a young Lieutenant (JG) John F. Kennedy and his crew to almost 50 mph while patrolling the Solomon Islands. Over in Europe, General Dwight D. Eisenhower shuttled to and from his headquarters in an army green Packard sedan, and Joseph Stalin was such a fan that he ordered his own domestic limo maker, ZiL, to exactly copy Packard’s designs. Knockoff Packards built by ZiL and, later, GAZ, ferried the party elite in the Soviet Union for decades.


> Packard cranked out thousands of the 1200-hp V-12s.


After the war, forces that had been pushing Packard to lower the drawbridge with less expensive cars gained momentum. The introduction of the “junior” Packards of 1937, with prices starting at $795, an unfathomably low sum for a new Packard, led to the Boulevard’s best sales year ever: 109,518 cars. The success of the juniors caused Packard to rethink its antiquated and somewhat artisanal production methods and embrace mass manufacturing techniques that lowered costs. These changes served Packard for a time when the peace came.


However, some bad product decisions, waves of labor strikes, and postwar shortages and inflation started Packard’s long slide toward its disastrous 1954 merger with Studebaker. While new Clippers and Caribbeans remained status symbols in the nicer parts of town, Packard, the independent luxury automaker, could not compete against the Cadillacs, Lincolns, and Chryslers that had huge manufacturing and sales organizations powered by the million-unit sales of mass-market brands to sustain them. It hung on much longer than its brothers in the once-formidable “Three P’s” (Packard, Peerless, and Pierce-Arrow), but eventually time and progress caught up to Packard with an anticlimactic decision in 1958 by the Studebaker-Packard Corp. to drop the Packard name altogether.


When asked, owners and restorers are hard-pressed to come up with specifics as to what made Packards stand above. “They just drive a little easier, the brakes are a little better, the shifting is easier, the machine work is a little tighter,” said Babinsky, the New Jersey restorer, before admitting, “Honestly, I don’t know what the combination is.”


Packard was the last independent American luxury manufacturer when it merged with Studebaker in 1954, having outlasted its peers and outsold for years even well-funded rivals such as Cadillac and Lincoln, which were just luxury divisions of much larger automakers.


Matt Tierney


Owners of cars from this era talk a lot about how Packard used solid brass and bronze in their door handles and other fittings, whereas other luxury brands used cheaper pot metal, a metal alloy of various low-cost materials such as zinc, lead, and copper that is cooked up in a “pot.” It’s a reputation for quality “that has never gone away,” said Craig Handley, president of the marque’s oldest national owner organization, the 73-year-old Packard Club. “What attracts people still, after all these years, is that reputation. All the histories of the industry portray Packard as the Rolls-Royce of America, that it was the ultimate car.”


> They just drive a little easier, the brakes are a little better, the machine work is a little tighter.


Ultimately, what keeps Packard relevant is its story as much as the machines. They say that nothing lasts forever, and to be sure, Packard’s full run was a mere 59 years, meaning it has been gone longer than it was alive. But if you’re wondering how it’s possible that old guys who were excited about Packards in the 1960s got replaced by new old guys who were excited about Packards in the 1980s, and how that cycle keeps repeating to this day (such that the Pebble Beach Concours today still receives far more Packard applications than it can accept), then consider that the story of one of America’s great car companies still fascinates us. It still inspires people to keep retelling it and to keep sinking their time and money into preserving the cars. And it’s looking like it always will.
