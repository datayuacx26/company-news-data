# How to Solve a Midlife Crisis with the Car You Already Have

The more time I spend in my almost 50-year-old body, the more I understand why a midlife crisis is inevitable. Daily stressors have a cumulative effect, and it can get overwhelming in ways I’ve never experienced before. A release valve of some form is needed. Having accomplished most of my childhood aspirations at this point in my life, I need nothing to survive and thrive. But I definitely *want* something. A red Corvette, an air-cooled Porsche, or a lifted 4×4? I genuinely don’t know, but I need something.


Make that *needed* , past tense. Commissioning a graphics package from an artist for my 2011 Ford Ranger XLT resolved my impending midlife crisis. I now have a nebulous sense of excitement and satisfaction in my truck. I mean, it’s still the same old rig: honest, reliable, and downright fun to drive. But now it feels right for me in ways I cannot explain. I enjoyed the creative process even more than the finished product. This feeling can’t be replicated by a trip to a dealership, either.


But my story isn’t one of self-aggrandizement or even self-reflection. Instead, it’s a tale of how to make a mass-produced vehicle into your own creation, and the lessons I learned along the way.

A custom project gets complicated when an artist has to turn a customer’s vague requests into a final product. But I had the right person for the job, and it all started with a factory photo of a 1993 Ford Ranger.


## The Photo That Started It All


1993 Ford Ranger XL Sport


Ford


Full-size American and many[Toyota](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/why-the-80-series-land-cruiser-is-the-most-notable-4x4-of-the-90s/) trucks are having a moment—values are increasing and reproduction graphics are readily available—but the second (1993–97) and third (1998–2011) generation Ford Rangers aren’t riding so high. Though there are readily available graphics if I wanted to make a Ranger Splash or STX, I initially wanted something more like the[Free Wheeling graphics](https://www.hagerty.com/media/design/vellum-venom-vignette-a-bronco-cannot-change-its-stripes/) seen in the 1970s and in today’s Bronco and Bronco Sport. However, the Free Wheeling aesthetic isn’t correct for the 1990s-era sheetmetal found on my truck. Plus, there’s no kit designed expressly for the compact Ford Ranger. Further research of Ford-blessed graphics from 30-ish years ago was needed, and I struck gold.

The treasure was found in the 1993 Ford Ranger brochure, as the XL “Sport” model came with a[Jazz disposable cup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jazz_(design)) color scheme atop geometry that has the feel of a 1990s[Taco Bell](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/design-inspiration-90s-taco-bells) . The vibe probably had a short lifespan back then, but these days it’s an homage to the 1990s that delights nostalgic millennials. And that pleasure is much like the feeling I have driving my vintage 2011 Ranger in a sea of more modern trucks from the same decade. Pairing the two made sense, at least in my mind.


As I have done with decades of[OEM Plus](https://www.hagerty.com/media/tags/oem-plus/) upgrades, I figured it’s wise to leverage the hard work performed by the designers who get paid six-figure salaries to make these trucks in the first place. Even so, there was plenty of work ahead to reproduce long-forgotten graphics from the 1990s. I realized it would be nice to leverage a graphic designer to make it more like something available with 2010s-era technology.


## The Culmination Of Graphic Design


Rico “Mr. Culmination” and the final product.


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


I used to know him as[Mr. Culmination](https://www.instagram.com/culmination.htx/) on social media, and at the end of last year we became friends at a dinner with mutual friends. His real name is Rico, and his day job is making industrial signs for corporations and commercial designs for office spaces. That résumé suggested he had the skills and the ability to implement a project. His custom Scion FR-S is a promotional billboard for his artistic talent. The more I learned about the mythical “Mr. Culmination,” the more I was sold on the content of Rico’s character. Here’s his story in his own words:


> “As a young boy in America, it’s difficult not to be into cool cars. However, what really turned me into a car enthusiast was the purchase of a Ferrari Enzo poster back on March 10, 2004. This happened at my elementary school’s book fair, but my intention was to get a science book. Unfortunately I needed $5 more for a book, so I used what my mom gave me to get the Ferrari poster instead. I still let my mom know that I could have been a scientist, but instead I became a car guy because of a $5 difference!
>
>
> Being in the car scene inspired me to design, as I wanted to create cool art for my vehicles. I’ve always loved seeing ‘modernized’ designs on retro builds. When the Ranger was presented to me, it was an easy ‘Yes, let’s do it.’ It became a very exciting part of my work that challenged me to create something beautiful to look at.”


After dinner, Rico took a snap of my truck in the parking lot. It was dark outside, but that’s all he needed to get the ball rolling. Going forward, we spoke via text and DMs on Instagram. I kicked things off with the above photo of the XL Sport, but I wanted Rico to run with this seed and make it blossom as his own creation.

The color palette of the Jazz disposable cup wasn’t working for me, so I used AI image generation to turn that 1993 Ranger into something akin to my vision. AI did a good job tweaking colors to my commands, even if it butchered the graphics and the truck, likely to avoid copyright issues.


I shared the AI photo with Rico, but unfortunately I didn’t make it clear that AI was only intended for the color palette. His work needed to hinge on the original 1993 image, so time was wasted in the miscommunication. People shouldn’t work for free, and I wanted to make up for his wasted efforts. But Rico was extremely generous with his time, suggesting he was fitting me in between jobs.


*(The whole job set me back around $900, but don’t expect every commissioned graphic designer to be this generous with their talents. Again, nobody should work for free.)*


Having the unintended extra time was good, as I had another graphic that needed installing. I bought this “Ford” tailgate graphic over 10 years ago for dirt-cheap on eBay, because I hate the F-150-sized, OEM tailgate emblems on the last of these compact Rangers. I was glad to de-badge/repaint the tailgate when the opportunity presented itself, in the form of a hit-and-run in a parking lot. After, the tailgate looked naked, and this decal was perfect.

Turns out this tailgate decal was only for 1993–94 Rangers, just like the side graphics I chose for my project. Perhaps Ford regretted the Taco Bell–themed body sides just as much as it did Ford product leaving the factory without a blue oval emblem affixed to its posterior. Whether that’s true or not is irrelevant, as that notion further cemented my mission.


Months passed, and Rico came through with the goods. He took the 1993–94 XL Sport graphics as a foundation, removed the “Sport” verbiage from the doors, changed the colors to a mix of grays and reds, extended the pattern further down the bed, and made it flow to the taillight contouring at the end of the body. This was everything I wanted, and more: Rico made the graphic his own with color gradients never seen in 1993, and added white borders for extra pop on the silver body. I liked his vision and asked him to take it to the next stage of creation.

Rico obliged, visiting my home with test prints and color swatches to ensure I would be happy with the final product. I decided the red needed to start off bright, worthy of my alma mater (University of Houston). White worked great with the bright red elements, and the gray area was offset with a splash of pure black. Rico initially had more black on the design, but I insisted on gray levels that started at the charcoal present in my truck’s OEM-plus Alcoa wheels.


The design was finalized after a few moments looking at colors and test prints in direct sunlight. The final product may not appeal to the general public, but the artist and this particular customer were sold. So the 1993–94 Ranger-inspired design for my truck went to the printers, and I reserved a day for Rico to install his masterpiece.

Designing it right is one thing, but getting it on sheetmetal is another. This graphic is quite complex, and therefore not easy to install. Rico spent nearly a full day in my garage, on a day with enough humidity to make installation a challenge. Because this is a continuous design from panel to panel, he said, “a small 0.25-inch error in the front can cause the whole project to be halted or forced to be redone completely.”


I suspect Ford’s 1993–94 Ranger graphic is less complicated for this reason; the design changes significantly between the cab and the bed, likely for ease of installation at the factory. Rico made installation harder for himself, but perhaps that’s a mark of an artist who is truly dedicated to his craft.


> “As each section went on, the truck began to gain more life, especially at the reveal when we removed the masking paper. Seeing the vinyl contour to the curves of the truck just made it look more lively.

I think he nailed it. This is precisely why I wanted someone like Rico behind the project. I wanted to commission an artist, someone who understands the assignment and isn’t afraid to take my original idea to a higher level.


Rico suggested that he always had an idea of how the final product would look, but even he was surprised at how good the graphics look, especially from vantage points you can’t experience on a computer screen.

While commissioning custom graphics for my Ranger is my equivalent of buying a red sports car, I genuinely craved the opinions of relevant third parties. The first was my father, who barged out of his house upon my arrival to exclaim, “Wow, did you buy a new truck?”Honestly, I could have stopped there. Dad’s exclamation of surprise and delight was worth every penny, every laborious hour of Rico’s precious time.


Then I reached out to my friend Denny, a local who’s made a name for himself with his[Ultimate 1980s Garage](https://www.facebook.com/TheUltimate80sGarage) . I will go on a limb and assume his midlife journey is much like mine. Denny’s love includes bringing Houston’s Rad Era community together without gatekeeping or cliquey drama. One of his events was the perfect place to put my graphics to the test, so I made a point to capitalize on the platform he created.


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)

I texted Denny to ask for a prominent spot at his next show, making sure to include a photo of my Ranger and the XL Sport as justification. He actually remembered these graphics when they were new (this only adds more credibility to his arsenal of automotive knowledge). “I thought maybe they did a red version!” He texted back. “This looks sweet!”


Retro-graphic approval from Denny is akin to a blessing from Jay Leno, so I arrived at the show early and was allowed to park the Ranger wherever I wanted. It no longer felt wrong to park my humble truck amongst blue-chip cars from the 1980s and 1990s and the latest flights of fancy. Luckily for my ego, the Ranger turned heads to the point that folks walked around it to see the graphic’s full effect.

I think Rico’s mission to make my truck a “great conversation starter” is fulfilled. While I lacked the nerve to solicit opinions from strangers, I did enjoy the overwhelmingly positive feedback from my friends in attendance. It was a diverse group: men and women, Generation X down to Generation Z, and fans of all automotive marques.


However, I soon craved more validation. My brother loves cars like his sibling (his influence is why I’m writing here), but he’s a tough customer. He had remained silent for weeks on the matter of the Ranger’s graphics, so upon his arrival at Denny’s show, I flatly asked for his opinion. I set the tone with a little self-deprecation.


“So, how much or how little do you hate the graphics I put on the Ranger?”


“No, no, I think it works well for you,” he dryly remarked.

Later on, he generously let me switch keys so I could drive his car show–appropriate sports car back to town. Too bad I wasn’t wise to his game. You see, he was playing 4-D chess. Less than a minute after he slid behind the wheel of the Ranger, a showgoer and his son finished their walkaround and complimented him on “his” truck.


“Hey, that’s a really nice Ranger!”


My brother thanked the man with a big smile on his face. With that, he let out the clutch and drove away. As the Bassani exhaust’s baritone note floated off into the horizon, I was left in a void of disappointment. I’ve been waiting since 2011 to get such a remark from a stranger.


The key to his off-the-shelf sports car felt heavy in my pocket, like an albatross around my neck. I was crestfallen.


*Oh dear,* I thought. *I fear a second midlife crisis is approaching…*
