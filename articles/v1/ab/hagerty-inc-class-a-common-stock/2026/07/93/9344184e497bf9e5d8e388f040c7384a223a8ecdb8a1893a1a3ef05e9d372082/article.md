# Never Stop Driving #192: When Will We Have Our Own Goodwood Hill Climb?

This past weekend, some 200,000 folks attended the annual Goodwood Festival of Speed (FOS) in the U.K. There they watched—and heard—hundreds of cars race up the hill-climb course that cuts, for just over a mile, through the Goodwood estate.


When the Duke of Richmond, Charles Gordon-Lennox, started the hill climb on his family’s ancestral estate in 1993, he expected a few hundred of his racing enthusiast friends to attend. Instead, more than 20,000 people showed up, and it quickly became an annual pilgrimage for many car freaks.


The Red Arrows Air Display and Mazda sculpture at Goodwood Festival of Speed, 2015.


Gary Hawkins/Sutton Images/Getty Images


Back in 2015, the first year I went to the Festival of Speed, I drove a Mazda IMSA race car up the Hill. The course, which curves its way, lined with barriers of straw bales, through Goodwood’s beautiful grounds, was crazy simple, and the run was over in about a minute. Yet the event attracted all sorts of celebrities—racing and otherwise—and the crowds fought miles of traffic to cheer from numerous grandstands. What surprised me was the plethora of new cars, both production and concepts, on display. The FOS was like a hill climb with an auto show off to the side.


The FOS concept is genius: Combine an exciting event in a cool location where attendees can see and hear new and historic cars roaring by with the new-car booths and displays that we are accustomed to seeing in big-city convention halls. Even in the sweltering heat of the West Sussex summer, Goodwood was packed.

At the time, I was working for *Road & Track* , which is owned by Hearst Corporation. The Hearst family story is fascinating: George Hearst made a fortune in the California gold rush and later acquired the *San Francisco Examiner* newspaper as settlement of a gambling debt. The paper passed to his son, William Randolph Hearst, to run. William struck gold too, but in publishing, and he used a good chunk of his fortune to build, over nearly three decades, a sprawling California estate in San Simeon called Hearst Castle. His life, and the Castle, were fictionalized and dramatized in the movie *Citizen Kane.*


Today, Hearst is a privately held $13.5 billion conglomerate—still family-controlled—whose magazines, newspapers, and television stations are just a fraction of an empire that spans financial ratings agencies, healthcare software, aviation data services, and a stake in ESPN.


Aerial of Hearst Castle, which sits atop a hillside along the Central California Coast, May 19, 1997.


Bob Riha, Jr./Getty Images


I was so enamored with the Festival of Speed that I pitched an idea of creating a similar event at Hearst Castle. I reasoned that it’s a perfect location because there’s an ideal hill-climb road, a mansion, gorgeous scenery, and even an airstrip. Plus, the Castle is about midway between Los Angeles and Monterey, two major hubs in the car world. The idea didn’t make it far up the flagpole, and I never got a chance to pitch it to the family, which decades ago donated the castle to the State of California but still owns the land around it and has some influence over its use.


David Crotty/Patrick McMullan/Getty Image


Since then, I’ve learned that dozens of individuals and consortiums of car people have tried to create an American version of the Goodwood Festival of Speed. I spoke to one group, for example, that was trying to create something at the Vanderbilt estate in Asheville, North Carolina. Hill climbs, as motorsports events, have enjoyed a bit of a revival here in the U.S. The Sports Car Club of America (SCCA)[now sanctions an entire series](https://www.scca.com/pages/hillclimb) of the time-based events, and there’s even a hill climb not far from Hagerty HQ,[in Empire, Michigan](https://empirehillclimb.racing/event) .


Jayson Fong


There’s hope, though: Earlier this month, the creation of the[Goodwood Road Racing Club of America](https://grrcamerica.com/) was announced, as was a partnership with Willow Springs International Raceway. The brand has arrived on our shores, then, but there’s no word yet of whether a hill climb will be among the events on their docket. For now, I continue to dream of finding someone who will create a major event that’s part hill climb, part auto show, and all awesome. Step one is finding a landowner willing to turn over an estate or other suitable property for a motorsport event, which is much harder than it sounds—so if you know someone, please share in the comments. In the meantime, my colleague Aaron Robinson[summed up all the Goodwood events](https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/the-goodwood-trifecta-how-do-the-dukes-three-car-events-compare/) and you can see highlights from this year’s Goodwood FOS[hill-climb challenge here.](https://youtu.be/v5Ip1YeZaN4?si=Xaw4bVU4DwCkL4t5) Ford’s wildly winged Super Mustang Mach-E posted the fastest time up the Hill, and the crowd favorite was probably Travis Pastrana in the wonderfully goofy Subaru Brat.
