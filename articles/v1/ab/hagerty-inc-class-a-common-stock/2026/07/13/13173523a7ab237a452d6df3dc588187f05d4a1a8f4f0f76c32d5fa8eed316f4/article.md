# DeSoto Suburban: Chrysler’s Questionable Station-Wagon Alternative

Mrs. Dozie Herbruck, wife of my high-school classmate Dave, mentioned at a recent reunion that her family had a three-row DeSoto sedan when she was a kid. I didn’t know such a car existed. Could she come up with a photo or two of the vehicle, and some childhood memories of it?


“Your request for a picture of our DeSoto sent me down memory lane,” she later responded via email. “We probably had it in the early ‘50s. I looked through all my mother’s slides and found pictures of every boat we owned, every fish caught, every beach visited but, alas, not one picture of the DeSoto. Apparently, my mother was as embarrassed by that car as my sisters and I were.


“One sister remembers packing us all into it for a vacation to visit relatives in New Hampshire and driving over the scary Bear Mountain bridge. My brother thinks it was a Suburban sedan, semi-automatic with fluid drive. He remembers driving it (illegally, of course) and said he shifted it by pressing down on the accelerator. My other sister said she would hide when being dropped off at school because her friends all had station wagons and we had this ‘hearse.’”


Her anecdotes might explain why DeSoto built so few Suburbans through its six model years. They might also explain why, as far as I know, no other Chrysler (or other) brand offered anything like the DeSoto. The story of the DeSoto Suburban turns out to be more complex, and interesting, than I anticipated.


## History


Stellantis


Named for the Spanish conquistador Hernando de Soto, the DeSoto Motor Car Company was founded in 1912 as a subsidiary of Zimmerman Manufacturing, a builder of horse buggies based in Auburn, Indiana. DeSoto was later acquired by the[Auburn Automobile Company](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/glenn-pray-lost-everything-to-keep-auburns-and-cords-alive/) and in 1928 was reborn as the DeSoto Division of Chrysler Corporation, which planned for DeSoto to compete in the mid-price class with GM’s Pontiac and Oldsmobile, Ford’s Mercury, plus Hudson, Willys, and Studebaker, one step below Dodge and above Plymouth.


Mecum


A total of 81,065 DeSoto Sixes were built in 1929, a first-year U.S. production record that was not eclipsed until the 1960 Ford Falcon. Chrysler reversed the market positions of its mid-priced marques in 1933, re-ranking DeSoto above Dodge in hopes of boosting the latter brand’s sales. Unfortunately, DeSoto’s shorter-wheelbase versions of[Chrysler’s streamlined 1934 Airflow](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/1934-chrysler-airflow-future-early/) proved unpopular. Unlike Chrysler, which continued to offer more traditional models in addition to the radical Airflow, DeSoto was hobbled by that Airflow design until new Airstream models arrived for 1935.


## Stretch Sedans


With the resumption of civilian auto production following World War II, DeSoto’s 1942 models were reborn as 1946 models. For the 1947 model year, the long-wheelbase (139.5- vs. 121.5-inch) three-row Suburban sedan was added as DeSoto’s top-of-the-line, flagship offering. As the sales guide proclaimed, the Suburban “establish\[ed\] a new trend in motoring comfort”:


“Even while easily seating nine persons, there is still more luggage space available than in a conventional sedan. Seating and carrying capacities are instantly interchangeable to suit varied requirements. Both first and second seats are full-sized and adjustable, the second having a 21-inch range, controlled by levers on both sides. Third, or auxiliary seat, is a substantial straight-across cushion with divided back and can be quickly flattened to become part of the floor or partially lowered to form a barrier between the luggage and the front compartments.”

The long adjustment travel of the second-row seat enabled it to be pushed far enough forward to create nearly seven feet of flat cargo floor, which was durable, laminated fir wood with chrome-plated skid strips. To accommodate heavy loads, the floor of the car’s chrome-railed rooftop luggage rack was made of sturdy plywood over a reinforced steel-on-steel roof panel. “It all adds up to a multi-purpose car of superb beauty, as modern as tomorrow,” the sales guide concluded. DeSoto’s Suburbans were initially powered by a 237-cubic-inch inline-six good for 109 hp (later increased to 112 hp, then to 116 hp with a displacement increase to 250 cubic inches for 1951) through a standard three-speed manual or optional Fluid Drive two-speed semi-automatic transmission.

The Suburban’s primary target was the upscale buyer with a large family who wanted the practicality of a station wagon with the styling, luxury and handling of a sedan. Offering exceptional interior and cargo roominess compared to typical competitors, plus all-steel construction in an era when station wagons typically had high-maintenance, rot-prone bodies made of wood, the Surburban also appealed to hotel and airport shuttle and taxi services. Some even became DeSoto limousines. Unusual for U.S. automakers, DeSoto offered no station wagon at the time and touted the Suburban as a better alternative.

While it earned some good press for innovative design, versatility and cargo and towing capabilities, the Suburban’s relatively high price of $2175 (about $33,000 in 2026 dollars) helped limit its sales to 3339 units in 1947 and total production to 7054 through ’48, according to Stellantis data (see chart below). The latter half of 1949 brought Chrysler Corporation’s first new postwar models and, for 1950, DeSoto’s first station wagon. Suburban production totaled just 2795 units. The new “Carry All” model accounted for all but 108 of them, with both second- and third-row seats folding flat to extend the cargo floor to the back of the front bench. An eight-passenger sedan (the sales guide called it the “Seven-Passenger”), with foldable, “adult-size” jump seats between the front and rear benches, accounted for another 186 units.


The 1950 model year saw 4498 DeSoto Suburban Sedans and 825 Eight-Passenger Sedans built, while Korean-War years 1951–52 added 2192 Suburbans and 768 Eight-Passenger Sedans. In 1953 and 1954, Stellantis reports that 338 and 307, respectively, of the latter were built, but no Suburbans. Given the ravages of rust and the relative unpopularity of these cars (Herbruck’s father donated the car to the Red Cross when the family “finally got a real car,” in her words), very few remain today as collector pieces.


## The End


Mecum


In 1952, DeSoto added a series of Firedome models with 276-cubic-inch V-8[Hemi engines](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/hemi-chrysler-drew-dome-forged-dynasty/) . For ’53, the automaker dropped its long-running Deluxe and Custom trim names and designated its six-cylinder cars “[Powermaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeSoto_Powermaster) s.” For 1955, all Chrysler Corp. models were redesigned with Virgil Exner’s “Forward Look,” and DeSotos, with impressively soaring tailfins and triple taillamps, sold in record numbers. A 1956 DeSoto even served as the Indianapolis 500 pace car. The ‘57s also did well, but the 1958 economic downturn hurt sales of all mid-priced cars, and DeSoto sales were down 60% versus 1957, marking their worst year since 1938.


After sales continued to plunge through 1959 and 1960, Chrysler canceled its DeSoto marque after the 1961 model year. In all, more than two million passenger cars and trucks bore the DeSoto (aka De Soto) brand in North American markets, of which more than 16,000 were long-wheelbase Suburbans. While Chevrolet’s Suburban truck-based wagons have been built and successfully sold since 1935, DeSoto’s 1947–52 Suburban Sedans were an early precursor to today’s hugely popular car-based SUVs and crossovers.
