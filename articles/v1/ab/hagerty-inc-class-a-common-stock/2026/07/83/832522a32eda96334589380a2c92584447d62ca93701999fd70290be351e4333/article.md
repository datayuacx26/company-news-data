# Factory Tuned: We Drive Ford’s 800-HP Mustang, 700-HP F-150, and 300-HP Maverick

From the tall perch of a Ford truck, the long stretch of pavement ahead of me at Michigan International Speedway looks inviting. Ford brought media out to sample a host of new kit from the new Ford Custom Garage, and it set up a makeshift drag strip on which for us to roast tires and abuse brakes. There are worse ways to spend an afternoon.


A Ford product planner imagines a scenario in which the owner of an F-150 enjoys the muscle of his V-8 engine at first, but later thirsts for even more fizz. Courtesy, perhaps, of a 3.0-liter Whipple supercharger and a lowered suspension to handle the additional nose weight and give the truck an appropriate stance. With my left foot pinning the brake pedal, I floor the gas and listen to the whine of the blower as the engine revs up, primed for take-off. All that’s left to do is lift off the brake and hang on; the 4×4 behemoth, which Ford calls the F-150 FP700S, just barely scrabbles for traction before hooking up, launching down the straightaway with its nose canted to the sky.

The FP700S is a dealer-installed, ready-to-go package that combines the supercharger kit with 22-inch wheels, aero and cooling tweaks, a host of styling upgrades, and lowered suspension. It’s pricey: $26,895 including installation, but Ford stresses that this type of considered, warranty-backed modification is what some customers—those wary of the aftermarket—want.


Ford


Writ large, Ford aims to meaningfully expand its after-sales footprint. “We want to democratize customization,” Matt Simpson, Ford’s Executive Director of Customization, told media at MIS. Almost half of Ford buyers customize their car in some way, which represents an opportunity. It doesn’t take a business genius to figure out that increased investment across the after-sales portfolio will leverage existing parts and capabilities to encourage higher transaction prices. (For our money, the one to get is the single-cab FP700S with rear-wheel drive and one of the lower trim levels. It’s scrappy and raw, like a modern-day F-150 Lightning sport truck.)


Existing efforts include Genuine Ford Accessories and Ford Racing parts. The former encompasses functional pieces like floor mats and roof racks, while the latter covers performance-car hardware like wheels, crate engines, ECU tunes, and superchargers that can be purchased à la carte. Critical, Simpson said, is the under-30 demographic that is customizing their vehicles at a higher rate than other age groups, and craving bigger modifications. A new limited-edition “Horizon Series” model will arrive each year, starting with a very pink High Desert Bronco capped at 1000 examples.


Ford Custom Garage is where all of the Blue Oval’s catalogue comes together into a “turnkey” package which people can buy pre-fitted, straight from the dealer. The Custom Garage approach—which includes dedicated performance packages for the F-150, Mustang, and Maverick, as well as more design-focused packages for a wider range of models—targets people who want a personalized vehicle but don’t want the hassle of third-party or DIY parts installation. And, like the aforementioned FP700S suspension kit, these packages have been engineered to work together as a unit, explained Simpson: “We make sure it is a complete vehicle with its own reason for being, not just a set of parts.”


Ford


Speaking of *raison d’etre* , take the Mustang FP800S. The Mustang customization team considers it their “distillation of the best enthusiast Mustang,” and we can see why. For one, it’s the only way to get a supercharged Mustang with a manual gearbox; the[Dark Horse SC](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/mustang-dark-horse-sc-will-make-795-hp-660-lb-ft-of-torque/) is auto-only. For another, the FP800S pumps out 800 hp and 615 lb-ft of torque thanks to Whipple’s Gen 6 supercharger, and adds new springs and dampers, a custom MagneRide calibration (tuned passive dampers are also offered), adjustable stabilizer bars, upgraded half-shafts, and a performance cat-back exhaust with quad Borla tips, and an air/oil separator. It also stands out from any other Mustang, thanks to striking graphics and a unique 19-inch flow-formed wheel design—10.5 inches wide up front and 11 inches in the rear.


The full host of upgrades, not including tires or installation, costs $18,500 and can be added to any Mustang GT. (Given the scope of hardware here, installation for all of the above could be in the tens of thousands of dollars.) Ford will honor the warranty if installation is handled by a dealer or any ASE-certified mechanic.


Ford


We start off in a stick-shift FP800S. Power smacks you right in the face, right off the line, sending the Mustang ripping out of the pits and MIS and onto a short road course that Ford set up for media. The whine from the supercharger adds a delectable layer to the familiar roar of Ford’s 5.0-liter Coyote V-8. Big, meaty tires (305s up front and 315s in the rear) maintain enough grip to keep the pony pointing straight, though not without a bit of steering correction. We increase our pace with each lap, learning to ease into the throttle in anticipation of the heady low-end torque. The 10-speed automatic model we pilot next feels more or less identical, albeit with early gears that seem shorter. Shifting with the paddles is crisp and seamless.


Brakes are nice and grabby, with a predictable progression through the pedal travel. There’s a touch of body roll, enough to give the driver a sense of weight transfer, but never so much as to upset the car. The Mustang is not a light car these days—the Dark Horse is about 4000 pounds—and the FP800S feels no different. Still, the setup is solid: remarkable at maintaining composure during aggressive changes in direction, and the traction- and stability-control systems in Sport mode do a wonderful job of saving one’s bacon without obviously cutting power or killing momentum. Shout out to the Recaro seats, which keep the driver glued into position yet feel all-day comfortable.

Next up: an autocross in… Mavericks. Yes, Mavericks—the SV300T package brings to the fore what the team’s product planner called “a modern hot hatch.” To that we say, *not quite.* These are still tall, long, relatively heavy pickup trucks compared with true sport compacts like the discontinued Focus and Fiesta. And they have the brake dive and body roll to show it. With that said, it’s hard to be cynical about a Maverick with 300 hp, 350 lb-ft, and a snarling Borla exhaust. The thing pops and cracks like a bag of cherry bombs. And while the powertrain is less refined than that of the standard Lobo—turbo lag is pronounced, to say the least—it has a lot more fizz and personality to make up for it. In that sense, the SV300T does the moribund SVT moniker proud.

The heart of the SV300T package is the turbocharger, borrowed from the Mustang’s 2.3-liter four-cylinder. Add to that a Ford Racing intercooler, 20-inch wheels, lowering springs (10 mm lower in front, 30 mm lower in the rear) and shocks from the Maverick Lobo, and unique stabilizer bars and bushings. The suspension in this kit is slightly different from a Lobo, and that model benefits from brake-based torque vectoring on the rear axle. Standard Mavericks equipped with the SV300T also get a transmission calibration much like the Lobo’s, turning the eight-speed automatic into a seven-speed. XLT and Lariat versions also swap single-piston front calipers for two-piston units. Ford suggests not skipping the $745 4K tow package, which comes with a dedicated transmission oil cooler and stronger cooling fans.


Tire fitment is still up in the air, but the Michelin Pilot Sport 4S summers we sampled on the SV300T were magic. They make the most of the Maverick’s prodigious acceleration, but all that lateral grip quickly launches you sideways in a seat not up to the task. Better bolstering would go a long way here, something that the Fiesta ST and Focus ST had in spades thanks to chunky Recaros.


Ford


Without all of the graphics doo-dads (something you can do if ordering parts à la carte), a 300-hp XLT would be one hell of a sleeper. But the Lobo eats its lunch in hard cornering, where its brake-based torque vectoring on the rear axle helps the car rotate and put down power, rather than just cutting it.


Cost for the SV300T package: $5000, not including tires and installation. Mavericks Lobos ordered this way through dealers will be subject to between 13 and 14 hours of labor, or about 18 hours for the XLT and Lariat. That will add several thousand dollars to the final price, but when the package officially becomes available this fall, Ford expects to also offer an all-in price that customers can pay for Mavericks pre-equipped by a third-party upfitter. One assumes, or at least hopes, that option will be cheaper.


No matter how you slice it, this is a good time to be a Ford enthusiast.
