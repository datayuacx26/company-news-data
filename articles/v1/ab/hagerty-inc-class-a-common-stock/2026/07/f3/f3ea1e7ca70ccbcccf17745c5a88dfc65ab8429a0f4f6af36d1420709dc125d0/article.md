# Never Stop Driving #194: The Robot Arrival

Who’s the better driver, the average American or a Waymo robot? According to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, the answer isn’t even close—the robots are far safer. According to a recent IIHS[report,](https://www.iihs.org/research-areas/bibliography/ref/2374) Waymo vehicles in driverless operation were involved in crashes worthy of a police report 68% less often than human drivers in the same areas and time periods. Waymo’s rate of rear-ending another vehicle was 91% lower, and its vehicles’ rate of being rear-ended was 40% lower.


None of us should be surprised. The robots don’t eat while driving or have a pet crawling in their lap or scroll TikTok or get behind the wheel drunk or high. Furthermore, I think that, as cars have become increasingly refined over the years, drivers are less careful because they’re more insulated from the feeling of risk. So, as much as I love driving, I’d rather my three kids, who all now have driver’s licenses, share the road with bots than idiots.


Unsplash/Josh Sonnenberg


I’m reminded of the famous Hemingway quote, from *The Sun Also Rises* , about how bankruptcy happens: “Gradually, then suddenly.” I think we’re at a similar point with autonomous cars. An old friend of mine, a former racer who loves to drive, tells me that Tesla’s recent update to its autonomous software, now called[Full Self-Driving (Supervised)](https://www.tesla.com/ownersmanual/model3/en_us/GUID-2CB60804-9CEA-4F4B-8B04-09B991368DC5.html) , is much improved. In the past, his Model 3 in autonomous mode felt like a shaky new driver, gingerly navigating the roads, but now the system is such a confident and commanding pilot that he routinely relies on it to navigate the dog-eat-dog traffic-clogged roads of the Northeast. After one such three-hour journey, he felt less tired and stressed thanks to his robot driver.


Tesla updates the software on its cars wirelessly and automatically, one of the innovations Elon Musk brought to market. Earlier this summer, v14.3.4 updated the driving assist features, and it wasn’t just my pal who noticed: Many[users reported a dramatic improvement](https://www.basenor.com/blogs/news/tesla-update-v14-3-4?srsltid=AfmBOooTPjKUEA_cR3RoCOly7QWn2ig7Nlz_SPsWM2ZsL1MCjwmQQu0v) in the confidence of the robot driver, even in low-visibility weather. Tesla owners also discovered that the interior screen has new messages when the car is nearing a destination like “Will pull over near destination” that passionate Tesla fans—there are still legions of them out there—believe inches Tesla toward a robotaxi future.


[During an earnings call earlier this year](https://www.reddit.com/r/TeslaFSD/comments/1rloq1w/elon_musk_your_tesla_with_fsd_could_make_you/) , Musk pitched the eventual ability of private owners to add or subtract their vehicles to the robotaxi fleet his company is building. The additional income could offset the cost of the car, he predicted, like Airbnb does for homeowners. The cheapest Tesla, the Model 3, starts at less than $40 grand, or some ten thousand less than the average price of a new car. Full Self-Driving (Supervised) is a subscription service[that costs a heady $99 a month.](https://www.tesla.com/support/full-self-driving-subscriptions#cancelations) There currently is no long-term contract for FSD so it can be canceled at any time.


Unsplash/Yan Ots


Let’s take a step back and marvel at how we can buy a reasonably priced car and then get it to drive itself for $100 a month. That’s amazing, right? Musk reminds me of Ferdinand Piëch, the Volkswagen Group chief whom I wrote about last week: an incredible engineer and innovator who has, like all of us, normal human imperfections.


I have mixed feelings about these autonomous developments. Of course, the autonomous genie is out of the bottle and there’s no putting it back in, but there’s a difference between commuting and the type of pleasure driving I so often engage in. Machines have been taking over human labor for over a century. When’s the last time anyone scrubbed their clothes on a washboard?


Common sense tells us robot drivers are safer, and the data prove it. We can certainly imagine a dystopian future in which driving is outlawed in the name of greater safety. Regulation, however, rarely keeps pace with technology. I asked Claude, the AI assistant, where improved autonomous cars are taking us.


The software spit back the thinking that’s coalesced over the last decade: We’ll see more autonomous cars and perhaps less car ownership in dense urban areas over the next five years. Maybe that’ll mean less congestion. Driven cars, the machine said, will coexist alongside the bots for a very long time. The bot even mentioned the horse industry, for good reason. Horses didn’t disappear after the car took over, and today, about a million people in the U.S. work in the horse business, which is reportedly a $180 billion industry.


I say it’s high time to take the wheel from the uncommitted and the careless and unclog the roads for those of us who love to drive. On this day, that’s my optimistic future vision!
