# 10 Oddball Cars at the 2026 Monterey Auctions

Monterey Car Week is right around the corner, and this event brings together some of the world’s rarest and most expensive metal (and fiberglass, and carbon fiber). Some are on display. Some are for sale. Some are just parked on the street. Mixed in among the six- and seven-figure stuff, though, are some truly weird and wonderful automobiles, and these are some of our favorites every year in Monterey. There is so much stuff that we rarely see, never see, or never even knew existed.


The 2026 Monterey auctions look like they will once again be a goldmine for lovers of the strange and the obscure. Below are 10 cars, all crossing the block in Monterey, that fill the quirky quotient.


## [1989 Porsche 911 Carrera Targa “Rijkspolitie”](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/ql26_r0043/1989-porsche-911-carrera-targa-rijkspolitie)


Broad Arrow


**Broad Arrow**


They don’t dial 9-1-1 for emergencies in the Netherlands. Nope, when the Dutch need help from the authorities, they dial 1-1-2. Nine-one-one does have some cop-centric connotations over there, however, because for more than 30 years the Dutch traffic police patrolled the country’s highway system in[Porsches](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/for-more-than-30-years-the-dutch-police-was-one-of-porsches-best-customers/) .


Because Dutch highways didn’t technically have a speed limit in the 1950s and ’60s, the authorities needed a competent rapid response vehicle. So they started using Porsche 356s in 1962. Specifically, they used 356 cabriolets, so officers could stand up and direct traffic without having to exit their state-issued German sports car.


Andrew Newton


Broad Arrow


Early 911s weren’t available as cabriolets, but when open-topped 911 Targas came out in 1967, the *Korps Rijkspolitie* started snatching them up. The one up for auction in Monterey is a 1989 model, showing 156,826 kilometers (97,447 miles) but benefiting from an engine rebuild 2500 miles ago. Police equipment includes a Targa bar-mounted blue light, rear-facing loudspeaker, rear Hella lights, front Bosch horns, and a plywood shelf behind the front seats to store helmets, boots, lighting, radios, tools, and a fire extinguisher. There are also additional switches up front along with a Teletron radio unit mounted below the ashtray.


It reportedly did five years of service with the police, and has been in the U.S. since 2024. Broad Arrow’s estimate for it is $125,000–$150,000, which is condition #1 (concours) money for a standard[’89 Carrera Targa](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/porsche/911/1989/1989-porsche-911-carrera?id=aCn1I000000CxX3SAK) in our price guide.


## [1991 Bancroft Roadster by GFG-Exklusiv-Cars](https://cars.bonhams.com/auction/31959/preview-lot/6178060/1991-bancroft-roadster-chassis-no-3800011a000042-engine-no-r1161551135/)


Broad Arrow


**Bonhams**


The build year noted on this swoopy blue thing is not a typo. It really is from 1991. A neoclassic à la[Excalibur](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/excalibur/series_iii/1976/1976-excalibur-series_iii-base?id=aCn1I000000DJvESAW) or Zimmer, it was conceived by an American named Hugh Bancroft and built by a coachbuilder in Westphalia, Germany, called GFG-Exklusiv Cars GmbH.


According to Bonhams, just 10 were built out of a planned 300. Buyers could specify either a Mercedes-Benz V-8 or Mercedes-Benz V-6, and on top is a mish-mash of 1930s coachbuilt styling cues formed in aluminum or steel. This one has the V-8, and is reportedly offered from its second owner, who bought it from Hugh Bancroft’s family. They put it in climate-controlled storage in Costa Mesa for about 30 years and kept the mileage to just over 1000, so it’s a 35-year-old car that also looks like a 90-year-old car that also looks like a new car.


## [1999 Vector SRV8](https://rmsothebys.com/auctions/mo26/lots/r0087-1999-vector-srv8/)


RM Sotheby's


**RM Sotheby’s**


[Jerry Weigert’s](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/hagerty-magazine/jerry-wiegert-and-the-vector-supercar/) star-crossed supercars, aka Vectors, are all weird and wonderful. Every single one of them. They’re also rare, and they hardly ever come to market. This one is from very late in the Vector story.


Weigert’s firm had been taken over in 1993 by an Indonesian outfit called MegaTech, which subsequently bought Lamborghini in 1994. Vector’s main product under MegaTech, introduced in 1996, was the M12, which was essentially a[Diablo](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=lamborghini+diablo) in Vector clothes. It was all short-lived, however, as MegaTech sold off Lamborghini to Audi and Vector to a pair of entrepreneurs named Randy Welty and Tim Enright.


Josh Mackey/RM Sotheby's


Josh Mackey/RM Sotheby's


The SRV8 was essentially Vector’s last hurrah, built on a repurposed M12 chassis but with a 5.7-liter Chevrolet LS1 V-8 and Porsche five-speed in place of the Lamborghini V-12. Advertising its return to all-domestic power was a new badge with the stars, stripes, and Lady Liberty, ringed by “Vector Aeromotive” in blue text. The styling was signature Vector, but with generally softer edges, new scoops, and fixed headlights instead of pop-ups.


Sadly, Vector shut its doors not long after the SRV8’s debut in 1999. The car remained a one-off prototype, and the last functional Vector ever built. Welty gave it to his son as a 16th birthday present, and while most 16-year-olds would have promptly wrapped such a car around a tree, the younger Welty kept the SRV8 until 2025 before selling it to the consignor. All-original, the last-of-all Vectors shows 864 miles. RM Sotheby’s has a $350,000–$450,000 estimate for it.


## [1957 MV Agusta 1100 D2 Autocarro](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/ql26_r0175/1957-mv-agusta-1100-d2-autocarro)


Broad Arrow


**Broad Arrow**


MV Agusta is a name mostly associated with racy three- and four-cylinder motorcycles, but the company got its start in aircraft production and in the postwar years built cute little commercial vehicles like the 1100 D2 seen here. Powered by a 1079-cc, 27-hp twin-cylinder diesel of MV Agusta’s own design, it could carry a ton, and buyers could have it in flatbed, van, and bare-chassis configurations. Production lasted from 1957 into the early ’60s, with only around 2000 examples built.


This one boasts a full restoration and a Cognac leather interior, and there are enough wheel chocks to fit three motorcycles up on that lovely pine deck. There’s currently one up there already, and it’s included in the sale, though according to Broad Arrow it’s not a ready-to-ride MV Agusta 750S but a non-functioning “salesman’s display.” Regardless, the estimate for the whole adorable package is $175,000–$200,000.


## [1970 Pontiac One](https://www.goodingco.com/lot/1970-pontiac-one?sortBy=ENDING_SOONEST&pageNumber=4)


Gooding Christie's


**Gooding Christie’s**


It started life as a stock 1970 Firebird, but over the course of four years it became… this. Conceived as “a vision of the Firebird decades ahead of its time,” it was first sketched out by Harry Bentley Bradley, a former GM stylist best known for his work on Mattel’s Hot Wheels line.


Gooding Christie's


Gooding Christie's


Gooding Christie's


GM Technical Designer Dave Crook brought the drawings to life, giving this Firebird-of-the-future a lower ride height, rear wheel skirts, half a dozen hood scoops, a pointed nose, and a mildly customized interior. Underneath is a standard, and not-so-futuristic, 350 V-8 and automatic. According to Gooding Christie’s, the finished Pontiac One caught the attention of Bill Mitchell, then GM’s Vice President of Styling, and he liked it so much that there was talk of making it an official GM display car. Nothing came of it, though, and it remains the only example built. There’s no represented history of what happened to it in the five decades since, but it looks to be in good shape and has a $200,000–$250,000 estimate for Pebble Beach.


## [1970 Volkswagen Beetle “Wrought Iron” by Rafael Esparza-Prieto](https://rmsothebys.com/auctions/mo26/lots/r0116-1970-volkswagen-beetle-wrought-iron-by-rafael-esparzaprieto/)


Karissa Hosek/RM Sotheby's


**RM Sotheby’s**


The term “rolling sculpture” gets thrown around a lot in this hobby, especially in museum exhibits and glossy auction catalogues, but this wacky VW is as much of a rolling sculpture as any high-dollar Bugatti or Duesenberg. The story starts with a blacksmith from Mexico City named Rafael Esparza-Prieto. A parts store owner in Zacatecas, a few hours north, wanted something eye-catching to put up front and draw people in, and he hired Esparza-Prieto for the job. The blacksmith then took a Beetle, cheap and plentiful in Mexico at the time, crafted intricate skeletal bodywork in swirly wrought iron, and then painted it white.


Some employees at the Volkswagen plant in nearby Puebla caught a glimpse of the lacey-looking Bug, and just before the 1968 Olympics in Mexico City, VW commissioned Esparza-Prieto to make two more copies. These caused enough of a stir that the company ordered another batch of 18 cars to use as promo displays at dealerships. Esparza-Prieto later moved to the U.S. and finished two more wrought-iron Beetles, bringing the total to 23 known examples. This one, built on a 1970 chassis, was on display in California dealer Vasek Polak’s showroom, and RM Sotheby’s has a $75,000–$125,000 estimate for it. Another one of Esparza-Prieto’s creations sold at auction back in 2019 for[$78,400](https://rmsothebys.com/auctions/tg19/lots/r0004-1970-volkswagen-beetle-casa-linda-lace-by-rafael-esparzaprieto/) .


## [1952 Avions Voisin C31 Biscooter by Snecma](https://www.goodingco.com/lot/1952-avions-voisin-c31-biscooter-pb26?sortBy=ENDING_SOONEST&pageNumber=0)


Gooding Christie's


**Gooding Christie’s**


In the prewar era, Gabriel Voisin built beautiful aircraft- and Art Deco-inspired luxury cars; some of the most memorable vehicles of the 1930s. After the war, though, the car market in Europe was hungry for small and cheap cars, not big and expensive ones. So he designed the Biscooter, complete with basic drivetrain and equipment to keep the cost down, while using aluminum for the chassis and body to keep the weight down. Styling doesn’t appear to have been much of a priority.


Voisin’s new postwar parent company didn’t see the potential, but he licensed the design to a Barcelona company that sold about 12,000 examples. The one in Amelia is represented as the 10th in a series of 15 prototypes, and was formerly in the collection of Peter Mullin. Gooding sold it two years ago for[$44,800](https://www.goodingco.com/lot/1952-avions-voisin-c31-biscooter) , and it has a $50,000–$70,000 estimate for Pebble Beach.


## [1970 Citroën M35](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/ql26_r0086/1970-citroen-m35)


Broad Arrow


Broad Arrow


**Broad Arrow**


For much of its history, Citroën committed itself to forward-thinking, outside-the-box engineering. This resulted in some very clever cars over the years… and quite a few weird ones. The M35 certainly fits the bill, with the bleary-eyed but charming face of the Ami family car mated to a steep fastback coupe tail and huge windows, all built by French coachbuilder Heuliez. The styling is certainly strange enough, but the M35’s underpinnings, and its history, are also delightfully unconventional.


Starting in the ’60s, Citroën hooked up with German firm NSU to build rotary engines under the brand name Comotor, and Citroën’s M35 was essentially a beta test for the engine. The little coupe used Citroën’s signature hydropneumatic suspension to flatten the bumps, was fitted with front disc brakes, and at its heart was the new single-rotor engine that could scoot to 90 mph. Citroën completed 267 examples and entrusted them to select customers as part of a long-term evaluation program. The customers reported their experiences back to the company, and rear windows even read: “This Citroën M35 rotary-engine prototype is undergoing long-term testing in the hands of a Citroën customer.”


The usual rotary engine maladies (high fuel and oil consumption) plagued the experimental cars, along with reliability issues. M35 production quietly ended, and Citroën’s massive investments in the failed Comotor program contributed to its 1974 bankruptcy. This M35 was reportedly on static display from 2004 to 2013, and then made its way to the U.S. where it spent some time in the Peter Mullin Collection and Museum before selling to the current owner. Broad Arrow has a $30,000–$40,000 estimate for it, and there’s another, twin-rotor-powered[Citroën GS Birotor](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/ql26_r0087/1973-citroen-gs-birotor) in the same sale with a $20,000–$30,000 estimate.


## [1965 Attica](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/ql26_r0177/1965-attica-cabrioletta)[Cabrioletta](https://www.broadarrowauctions.com/vehicles/ql26_r0177/1965-attica-cabrioletta)


Broad Arrow


**Broad Arrow**


Greece isn’t the first, the second, or the thirtieth country we usually think of when it comes to car manufacturing, but a few cars have rolled out of the birthplace of democracy. One of the better-known Greek brands was Attica, named after the region where Athens is located. If that doesn’t sound Greek enough, the head of the brand was a guy named Georgios Dimitriadis.


Starting in the early 1960s, Attica built a version of the German Fuldamobil microcar under license, powered by various 200-cc engines. The bubbly little Attica became very popular in its home country, but according to Broad Arrow, just two were built in this “Cabrioletta” beach car body style. This is one of them, and it was fully restored in Athens about a decade ago. Similar in concept, style, and cuteness to a[Fiat Jolly](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/fiat/600/1959/1959-fiat-600-jolly?id=aCn1I000000DIR5SAO) but much rarer and more obscure, it has an $80,000–$100,000 estimate.


## [1959 Chrysler Scimitar Town Car Phaeton by Reutter](https://www.mecum.com/lots/1176484/1959-chrysler-scimitar-town-car-phaeton/?aa_id=795983-0)


Mecum


**Mecum**


Two of my favorite oddball cars are the otherworldly creations of[Bruce Baldwin Mohs](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/mr-mohs-and-his-amazing-motor-cars/) , and[Ford’s Edsel](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=edsel) . If you smashed them together, they’d come out looking something like this special coachbuilt Chrysler. Called the Scimitar because of the upswept panels on its sides, it is one of three built for the 1959 Geneva Motor Show. One was an “All-Purpose Sedan” and one was a “Two-Door Convertible,” while this “Town Car Phaeton” is a four-door with a retractable hardtop.


Mecum


Mecum


Designed by Brooks Stevens and funded by the Olin Mathieson Chemical Corporation to promote its aluminum business, the Scimitars featured anodized brushed aluminum in high-corrosion areas. Underneath was a[Chrysler New Yorker](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/search?q=1959+chrysler+new+yorker) platform with a 413 cid V-8 (the taillights, though, are from a[’58 Impala](https://www.hagerty.com/valuation-tools/chevrolet/bel_air/1958/1958-chevrolet-bel_air-impala?id=aCn1I000000Cvi2SAC) ). Actual construction of the bodies was entrusted to Karosseriewerk Reutter in Stuttgart, and they must have been a fun departure from the Porsche 356 bodies that Reutter was busy building at the time.


All three show cars were on display at Amelia Island in 2020. Mecum doesn’t have a presale estimate for the Town Car Phaeton, but its sibling, the All-Purpose Sedan (which looks more like a wagon), just sold at auction a few weeks ago for[$156,800](https://cars.bonhams.com/auction/32353/lot/61/1959-scimitar-ex-all-purpose-sedan-chassis-no-100678-engine-no-1181/) .
