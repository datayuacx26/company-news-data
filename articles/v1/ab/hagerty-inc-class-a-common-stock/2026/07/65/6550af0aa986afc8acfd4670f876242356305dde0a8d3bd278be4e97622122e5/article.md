# The Hidden Costs of Owning a Collector Vehicle

A few months ago, a friend of mine—let’s call her Andrea—texted me some pictures of sunbeaten first-gen Jeep Wagoneer husks rusting in a field in western Wyoming, several hours from her home in Montana.


“What do you think of these?” she asked.


Eight months pregnant, Andrea and her partner were on the hunt for a cool-mom car. Though Andrea has worked in the automotive industry most of her life, she doesn’t know the first thing about cars, aside from their vibes.


Produced from 1963 to 1991 (broadly unchanged during those three decades, aside from the “Grand” prefix), few rigs are as vibe-y as an[SJ Wagoneer](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/before-buying-1984-1991-jeep-grand-wagoneer/) . During its heyday, the slab-sided and sometimes wood-paneled SJ was the runabout of well-heeled horse-people and the Secret Service alike. They feel official and accessible at the same time.


Mecum/Andrew Thompson


“You should just set your wallet on fire and throw it in the open window,” I texted back.


I knew that even a perfectly kept or well-restored full-size Jeep can be a basket case, prone to leaking somewhere and breaking something. Even though Jeep built well over a quarter-million SJ Wagoneers, parts aren’t terribly easy to find. I also knew that neither Andrea nor her partner would tolerate getting stranded during a rural Montana winter.


Unlike most people, Andrea heeded my advice and walked away from the SJs. Four weeks later, her son was born and she was thankfully too busy to revisit the cool-mom-car debacle.


Her misguided, if understandable, interest in a vibe-y vintage truck got me thinking, however. Those unanointed few—those who have yet to suffer the trials and tribulations of classic-car ownership—simply don’t fully grasp the many hidden costs of owning a collector vehicle. I say this, mind you, with not a whiff of judgment; I have spent well above six figures repairing, rebuilding, keeping, and maintaining vintage vehicles over the last 22 years.


With that in mind, I figured I would try to lay out all of those hidden costs of classic-car ownership, not with the aim of dissuasion but rather to prepare a new buyer for the inevitable. Few hobbies are as rewarding as ours. If you’re on the fence about purchasing your first classic, by all means buy that thing. Just know what you’re getting yourself into.


## Space, the First Frontier


“Buying the car is just the first of many checks you’re going to write,” says Jim Pickering, a[Hagerty contributor](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/project-cheap-camaro-an-introduction/) , collector, and former mechanic. “And I don’t care if it’s the nicest car in the world. It can be the best restoration of the highest-quality car—it’s still going to have needs. It’s like buying a house. The best house is still a project. And a car is the same way.”


Consider, for instance, storage.


Unsplash/Valeriia Neganova


“The thing I think people don’t think about enough is space,” Pickering says. “Where are you going to put this thing? How are you going to keep it safe? How are you going to keep it out of the weather? The reality of that tends to be outside car peoples’ heads.”


Space is the first factor people should noodle on when considering a collector vehicle. It’s one that I didn’t truly consider until speaking to Pickering. When I bought[my ’76 International Scout Traveler](https://www.hagerty.com/media/driving/the-suffering-is-the-point-on-the-trail-in-a-scout-with-needs/) , I had a house with a two-car garage. But not even that space was sufficient for storing and rebuilding it. Luckily, my father had a modest barn sitting unused on his property, where I was able to keep the Traveler during the year-long rehabilitation. It still lives there.


Nick Jaynes


Car collector and retired auto journalist Jeff Zurschmeide has built two shops in his life to store his cars—each at a cost of $30,000. If you don’t own a home with space for a shop, or you can’t afford 30 grand for one, you’ll still want to find a place to get your car out of the weather. No matter what part of the country you live in, the[elements can wreak havoc](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/what-happens-when-cars-sit/) on a collector vehicle. So, getting it inside is essential.


And if you do have a garage, are you prepared to kick your daily driver—or, perhaps more importantly, your partner’s—to the curb to hide your vintage ride from the elements?


[Eric Weiner](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/eric-weiner/)


“Your best bet by a long shot—and that doesn’t mean it’s cheap, but it’s cheaper—is just a storage unit,” Zurschmeide says. “I ended up spending $600 a month last winter on a big storage unit out here in Tillamook, Oregon, and I’d imagine in urban areas it’s even more expensive.”


So, factor storage and a space to work on your car into your budget.


## Working on It


With a space for the car, the next thing to consider is who is going to work on it when it inevitably needs service or repairs. I’ve gone both routes—paying someone else and doing it myself. Neither is cheap.


Nick Jaynes


Nick Jaynes


If you choose to work on it yourself, you’ll need know-how and tools. Even if you don’t factor in the hours you’ll spend working on the vehicle, DIY is still an expensive venture. With my Traveler rebuild, I went in with a full suite of automotive repair tools, having worked as an apprentice Volvo mechanic during journalism school. Over the 13 months I rebuilt the Traveler, I still spent more than $3500 on new tools just to get the job done. And tools are just the visible cost; the invisible one is time. Not just the wrenching, but the hours spent becoming enough of an expert on your specific vehicle to track down the right part—and to know it’s the right part before you buy it.


If you’re not mechanically inclined or you don’t have the wherewithal to DIY the job, you’ll need to find someone to work on your vehicle. And finding that person—let alone someone reliable, competent, and honest—is a feat unto itself. In the last two decades that I’ve been buying and selling vintage vehicles, the pool of shops that will even take in a vehicle produced before 1990 has shrunk significantly.


Cameron Neveu


Depending on how oddly specific your car is, the mechanic you do find may be booked out for months. And when you finally get it in, and they figure out what’s going on, you and they may be waiting for months for the correct parts to arrive. During that time, your car very well may be parked outside in the elements. When it’s finally done, you might be facing a four- or five-figure repair bill.


Servicing an old car isn’t like taking your Honda to the dealership, where mechanics work on book time, and the amount of time each job takes is pre-set by the OEM, regardless of how long it takes the tech to complete the job. Repairs on old cars take as long as they take—and you’ll be paying the shop’s hourly rate for every hour that ticks by. It can add up fast.


## Insurance


“If you’ve already encountered Hagerty, you pretty well solved the insurance problem,” Zurschmeide says.


Unsplash/Roger Starnes Sr.


He’s right, and that’s been my experience, too. I’ve insured my collector vehicles with Hagerty since 2013—long before my byline ever appeared on its pages—largely because most standard auto insurers simply don’t know how to cover a vintage vehicle.


Consider my friend, whose wife inherited a 16,000-mile V-12 Jaguar XJS. Their standard carrier was charging them monthly roughly what Hagerty now charges them for an entire year. That gap isn’t just a better deal—it’s a fundamentally different product: a limited-use, guaranteed-value collector policy rather than standard daily-driver coverage, which is part of why it costs less and fits the car better.


No matter who you insure it with, however, those costs need to be factored into your collector-car budget.


## The Unobtainium


Matt Tierney


If you have your eyes on a rare vehicle, it shouldn’t come as a surprise that the[parts supply will be equally, if not more, rare](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/good-parts-for-some-classic-european-cars-are-getting-scarce/) . Some pieces simply may not be available. Or, if they are, they’re astronomically expensive compared to a similar component for a more common car.


Conventional wisdom says parts scarcity is an orphan-brand problem: Buy something weird and pay the price. Jim Pickering’s example turns that on its head.


Pickering points to the 1965–66 full-size Chevrolet, a car GM built by the millions, where the brittle interior plastics have dried to eggshell over 60 years and almost nobody reproduces them. Want to swap a four-speed into a ’66 Impala? You’ll need the correct console, unique to the four-speed cars and nearly impossible to find.


Mecum


“You can’t find one,” Pickering says. “And if you do, it’s going to cost you $3000. That kind of thing happens all the time.” The lesson: Production volume is no guarantee of parts availability, and even mass-market classics hide costs that never show up on the asking price.


But Pickering is optimistic that the parts problem is fixing itself, and the reason is generational: Owners aging into the hobby now grew up with computers and can often fabricate what they can’t buy. He watched a friend restore a 1991 Dodge Cummins pickup, for which grilles and interior plastics no longer exist. He simply 3D-printed the missing pieces himself.


[Chris Stark](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/chris-stark/)


“It’s probably less of a consideration, and it will continue to be less and less as we move along,” Pickering says, “because now you have people 3D printing parts and making their own stuff.” The skills, as he puts it, are different now than they were back in the day, which means the category of truly unobtainable parts is quietly getting smaller.


## Buy Right the First Time


This loops us back to that first check Pickering mentioned at the outset, the one you write to purchase the car. Because how much everything hurts that comes after depends a great deal on which car you buy in the first place. As Zurschmeide says, quoting the old collector-car line, “There’s nothing more expensive than a cheap Ferrari.”


The wisdom there, of course, is that you’re not actually saving yourself money by getting the cheapest version of the car you want. Even the best car will have needs.


Cameron Neveu


If that feels contradictory, it’s not. Buying the best example you can afford is how you keep the purchase from becoming the most expensive part; a cheap, needy car will bleed you on the way in. But no example, however pristine, spares you the ongoing bill—the checks keep coming regardless. Buy the best you can afford, yes, and know that even then, it’s going to cost you more than you imagined.


Remember, with classic-car ownership, the journey is more important than the destination. This is true of both the ownership process as well as any given drive. You might not make it to your intended destination, but with eyes wide open, the story that arises from the breakdown detour will be far better than your originally plotted path.
