# Picture Car Confidential #44: Right-Hand-Drive Reticence

Long ago and not so far away, I had a job at the *Wall Street Journal* . My duties included scripting daily business reports for radio. These scripts would often include brief summaries of stock market indices and commodities prices, with a few words of informed speculation (by others older and wiser than myself) as to why the market was up or down, or why the price of corn, wheat, or hog belly futures had jumped or fallen. Over the course of months, I learned that sometimes there were real causes—skyrocketing feed or fertilizer prices, for instance—and other times the movements were more what you might call vibe-based. One day, for example, the Pope got shot, and markets dropped. “OMG, the Pope has been shot!” the analysts said, “this is terrible.” Which it was. But the next day the market shot back up. “Pope, shmope,” the analysts said. “What’s he got to do with the economy anyway?”


I came away from such daily zigzagging with the sense that the movement of markets, while sometimes grounded in fact, was often emotional. Market peaks and valleys often corresponded to unrelated events or, even, innate human elation and depression cycles. All of this led me to my current obsession: trying to figure out how and why classic car values sometimes seem to go up or down based on actual events, while other times, the cause seems to be because some influential someone woke up on the wrong or right side of the bed and told their friends.


To get to the bottom of it, I spoke with John Wiley, Hagerty’s Director of Valuation Analytics. He spends his days tracking markets, so he’s a deeper thinker than I am, which is why I write a column and he knows what he’s talking about. Wiley studies the role of demographics—the population aging into and out of things they liked when they were young, for instance—as well as the role of currency exchange rates and tariffs. As these are the things that keep his mind whirring through the night, perhaps he could offer clarity.


Andrew Newton


On the demographic front, Wiley said that while cars like the MGs and Triumphs I loved as a lad are[taking it on the chin lately](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-indexes-trend-upwards-despite-a-picky-market/#h-british-car-index) (many who remember them from way back when are aging out), American muscle of the 1960s remains relatively strong. Why’s that, I ask, while thinking about the upside of cheaper MGB GTs and other of my childhood dream cars? “A lot of it is just that the domestic vehicles can be modified, and it’s sort of okay to modify them, and not too hard. The parts are cheap, and they’re available. This is true even for \[older models\] like ‘55 Chevys. My experience with such things is that people are usually happy for the modifications.”


Flickr/SenseiAlan


Turning my sights overseas, I wondered whether new tariffs—which can add 10, 20, or 50 percent of the purchase price to a buyer’s bill—shouldn’t depress the market for self-imported cars. Turns out, on this subject Wiley suspects that the tariffs have had an effect on the volume of classic cars imported from abroad, but notes that the government has begun withholding data, which makes quantifying the effect of such surcharges a less certain affair. Using recent price declines in a popular JDM favorite, the Nissan Skyline of the 1990s, as an example, he suggests two possibilities:


“Either the values got ahead of themselves recently and there’s been some sort of reset, or there’s a concern that it’s just going to be too hard to get parts, so that could be part of it.


“But,” he added, “this stands in contrast to the Porsche and Ferrari markets, which seem to be very robust, and that includes \[owner-imported\] ‘rest of world’ vehicles. If you have a paint-to-sample Porsche of any era, that’s a pretty valuable car. So, it is kind of a strange dynamic in which the imports from Japan are less popular, but the imports from Germany and Italy, at least of those prestigious makes, are still pretty valuable.”

“Well, that leaves us Rover and Peugeot owners pretty far out to sea,” I tell Wiley ruefully. Expanding on the “what about me?” theme, I ask him, “And what about right-hand-drive cars?” and proceed to relate the tale of the time, in the face of a long-forgotten financial catastrophe, when I once thought semi-seriously about selling my 1963 Lotus-Cortina. A very early car (the 137th or 139th car built, I can’t remember which), the badge on its hood (I mean, bonnet) reads “Consul,” as the very first Cortinas still paid homage to a predecessor English Ford, thus launching as Consul Cortinas. One interested party who phoned was excited beyond belief before becoming deal-killingly disappointed when he realized that the car was right-hand drive. Yet all Mark I L-Cs were right-hand drive, I explained, except for the 1966 model year, when a few LHD ones trickled out of Ford’s factory. Fun as it remained, like Shelby Mustangs, which became less special as the Sixties progressed and volume increased, my L-C was not like a regular non-Lotus-ized Cortina, with their all-steel (not aluminum) panels, cart-sprung rather than the original Chapman-penned coil-sprung rear suspension and no close-ratio gearbox. The caller didn’t care.


Jamie Kitman


Worse yet, Wiley reports, that’s the way most Americans feel about RHD. “I was looking at the Hagerty Price Guide and, for 1950s and 1960s Rolls-Royces and Bentleys, their right-hand-drive deduction is 40 percent. And, according to the guide, even late ‘90s Acura Integra Type Rs originally sold in the U.S. transact for around 20% more than the JDM Honda Integra Type Rs, which are right-hand drive.” How do I say “Gulp…” in Japanese?


Skylines are one exception, he notes, “because there is no alternative. They didn’t make left-hand-drive versions. Basically, it doesn’t seem to affect the market.” He adds that Toprank, the Skyline importer run by Los Angeles-based Brian Jannusch, has told him that “people get used to it. The perception is, if there’s no alternative, they’ll deal with it.” (Another time, while acknowledging its drawbacks, I can enumerate the instances in which RHD can be to your advantage.)


Chris Stark


Could the disparity owe something to differing audiences for the marques and their socio-economic statuses, I ask? “Yes. What’s peculiar to me is that if we’re to make a bunch of generalizations, you could say that Skyline owners might be more interested in cryptocurrencies, whereas the Porsche and Ferrari buyers are willing to pay a lot as they benefit more from the artificial intelligence, large language model boom.”


“That sort of assumes that crypto has tanked a bit, whereas the AI bubble has yet to burst?” I offer.


“Right,” he concurs.


It reminds me—for old times’ sake—of an old Wall Street adage. *“The markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.”*
