# A Surprising Diagnosis

Most of the pieces I write either tell a story about a particular car or event, or explain how a certain automotive component or system works, or step you through how to do a certain repair. In most of them, I’d like to think that my readers probably learn something. And during that, if they find me entertaining, so much the better.


Today, though, we’re just going to get weird.


This, of course, has to do with the ’69 Lotus Elan +2, because Lots of Trouble Usually Serious and all that. For the past month, on and off, I’ve been trying to diagnose a rough-running problem that only occurs under certain conditions. I’d rather not go into detail on the problem itself because once I find and fix it, it’ll make a great article. For today’s piece, all that you need to know is that on a hot Sunday, I’d test-driven the car on the highway four times, trying to recreate the problem with added instrumentation in the car to catch things in the act, each time returning home to set up the test in a different way, and found nothing.


Like nearly all carbureted vintage cars, this one originally had a mechanical fuel pump, but when it had an at-home restoration 13 years ago, the PPO replaced it with an electric pump. There are plusses and minuses to this—electric fuel pumps are better at priming the carbs, but so many new unbranded parts are absolute junk—so I had ordered an inexpensive replacement electric fuel pump that looked the same and had the same pressure rating to keep in the trunk as a spare. I was holding off swapping it in as part of the test because I’d found nothing wrong with the existing fuel pump, but not being sure what else to try, I installed the new one.


Out with the old, in with the new.


Rob Siegel


As I was heading to the highway for a fifth test, the heat of the day caught up with me (long story why I wasn’t using the Elan +2’s[electrically-driven air conditioning](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/the-hack-mechanic/lotus-elan-2-air-conditioning-project-the-electric-compressor/) ), and my mind and body unmistakably said “No. Go home. Do it tomorrow.” I listened, and turned down a side street instead. And it was a good thing I did because, about a hundred feet later, a loud rhythmic racket began emanating from the engine compartment.


My first thought was that the engine threw the belt. I pulled over immediately, crept forward maybe 30 feet until I was somewhere shady, and looked.


As I’ve described elsewhere, I installed a high-output alternator into this car to run the electric A/C compressor, then installed a timing-belt-like Gilmer-drive toothed pulley set and toothed belt so the belt wouldn’t slip during high loads. I saw that the one-inch-wide belt was sitting slightly further forward on the toothed alternator pulley than usual, but the belt tension still felt about right. The Elan +2 has a Lotus-Ford Twin-Cam engine in it with a bespoke Lotus head that you *really* don’t want to overheat and crack, but there was no evidence of hot-running; I didn’t see or smell any wetness indicating a coolant leak. I was maybe a mile from my house, so I elected to get back in the car and drive it very slowly and carefully home while watching the temperature gauge like a hawk. So I did. The noise continued unabated, but the temperature gauge stayed in the middle.


Nothing looked obviously amiss on the toothed belt on the Gilmer-drive pulley set.


Rob Siegel


While limping the car home, I looked at the cigarette-lighter voltmeter. It read 11.9 volts, indicating that the alternator wasn’t charging. A- *ha!* Most likely scenario: The high-output alternator I’d bought and installed last September had crapped out, and I was hearing something inside it being chewed up. I relaxed a bit, continued home, put the car in the garage, poured myself a big glass of lemonade over ice, sat down at the laptop, and began to compose an email to the vendor I’d bought the alternator from, as I was still inside the 12-month warranty period. But then I thought, “No, you’re not certain that the alternator died. In the morning, when everything has cooled down, pull the belt off and spin the alternator pulley. If it’s seized or crunchy, then it’s bad, but if it spins smoothly, you’ve got more work to do.”


The next morning, I was greeted by a text from my son saying that the house stank of gas, he’d looked in the garage, saw fuel dripping from the trunk of the Lotus, and put a pan under it (good boy). I chuckled to myself about the fact that my wife Maire Anne has an exquisitely sensitive sense of smell, *hates* fumes from the garage coming into the house like this, and part of our back-and-forth about whether we should move out to the country and buy a place with a detached 50×80-ft metal outbuilding was that it would eliminate this little problem. That little marital joke notwithstanding, obviously I needed to deal with the fuel leak immediately.


The dripping was coming from a threaded NPT fitting on the gravity-fed inlet side of the newly-installed $16 Amazon fuel pump. I could see the Teflon I’d wrapped on the threads. Had I forgotten to tighten it? I grabbed a couple of wrenches and found that it was already pretty snug. I snockered it down further, but the drip continued. Was the leak from the screw-in filter that the pump came with? Was the NPT joint itself cracked? Don’t know. And yes, I had fired up the pump and visually inspected it for leaks after I installed it. So I removed it while mumbling to myself about how all new unbranded parts are junk, and reinstalled the 13-year-old fuel pump that had not actually caused me any documented problems. Yet.


I didn’t want to make the same mistake with the reinstalled pump and just assume everything was okay without a test drive, but then I remembered—the alternator. Right. Need to pull the belt off so I could spin the pulley.


As soon as I had the toothed belt in my hand, I burst out laughing because I saw that most of the teeth had been sheared off.


Well, *there’s* yer problem right there…


Rob Siegel


Before I threw on the spare belt, I gave the alternator pulley a spin, and felt nothing crunchy. It was thus likely that all of the engine compartment racket was due to the nearly-toothless belt slipping, though whether it had been failing for months or did so suddenly is a mystery for another day.


I needed to take the car for a test drive to check both the alternator and the reinstalled electric fuel pump, but my daily-driver 2003 BMW E39 530i first needed to be moved out of the driveway. I parked it in the street, then gave the Lotus a spin around the block. The cigarette-lighter voltmeter immediately jumped to about 14 volts, indicating that the alternator was charging the battery again, and the alternator was quiet both at idle and during the test drive. I verified that no fuel was leaking, backed the car into the garage and put it in the left rear spot as I still had unresolved issues to deal with, and returned the E39 to the driveway.


I then went inside, was about to post the photo of the toothless belt to social media because it was funny, and realized that I didn’t have my phone in my pocket like I always do. No problem—I went into the house, grabbed our cordless house phone, and took it into the garage (that’s the only reason you still have a landline and a cordless phone—to find your cell phone, right?) and called my phone.


The call immediately went to voicemail.


Hmmmn. That’s odd. I was certainly possible that the phone’s battery had died, but since I knew I’d just taken the photograph of the belt, it was unlikely. I didn’t expect my wife’s cell to behave any differently (both it and mine are four-year-old iPhone SEs), but I borrowed it anyway and tried. The call again went to voicemail. I tried sending a few texts, but it didn’t raise any audible “ *ding!* ” in the garage or the two cars I’d just been in.


I went to the laptop and used Apple’s “find my phone” feature, and it reported that my number was offline.


Great. A lost non-responsive cell phone.


I’m certain that every one of you has gone through this with a phone, wallet, keys, or all three. You go from “Where was the last place I had it?” to “Look on every single horizontal surface” to “Look bloody everywhere.” I *had* just been in both the Lotus and the BMW E39, so the odds that I’d put the phone on the seat and it had slipped into the black-hole-of-a-crevice that we all know exists between the seat and the door sill or the console, but if it was there, it was truly invisible. I walked up the driveway and to the street where I’d parked the E39. Nothing.


Damn.


And then I remembered—I knew *exactly* where I last had the phone. It was when I took the photo of the sheared belt. Had I done something stupid like laid it somewhere in the Lotus’ engine compartment? There are very few truly horizontal surfaces there, so I’ve been known to put tools on the valve cover or the skinny flat area on the upper radiator tank. I looked with the droplight, but came up empty.


And then I heard the phone ding a few times as if it was suddenly receiving the texts I’d sent it. Unfortunately, the dinging didn’t continue for long enough for me to localize the sound.


Frantic, I grabbed the house cordless phone again and dialed it. This time it rang. I traced it to the Lotus. I stuck my head inside the cabin. Nope, not there. I realized it was coming from the engine compartment. It took several rings for me to find it.


There it was, wedged between the exhaust headers and the steering column mount. If you look, you can see that the case actually melted.


You can see why I didn’t see it.


Rob Siegel


It made absolutely perfect sense. Due to the high temperatures, the phone had overheated and shut off, but once it cooled down, it came back on. Miraculously, the phone is fine. The $9 case took all the damage.


A better view of the melted case.


Rob Siegel


So, while I’m still scratching my head about the car’s intermittent rough-running issue (and I promise I *will* write about it once I solve it), at least I totally nailed this iPhone-died-and-came-back-to-life diagnostic mystery. With everything else going on, I’m calling it a win.
