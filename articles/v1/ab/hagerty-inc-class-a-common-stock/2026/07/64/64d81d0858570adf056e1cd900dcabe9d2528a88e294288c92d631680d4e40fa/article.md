# Before the Turbos, AMG Built a Simpler, Subtler V-12–Powered CL63

There was a time when AMG cars felt like slightly unhinged interpretations of otherwise sensible Mercedes machinery. They weren’t quite factory specials in the modern sense, but something closer to what might happen if an enthusiastic engineering department were left alone overnight with a few strong espressos.


The Mercedes-Benz CL63 AMG belongs squarely to that era. Hang on, you might say—the CL63 is a V-8, a mid-2010s bread-and-butter machine that is maybe a little special but not, you know, *special* . And you’d be correct, but that’s not the CL63 we’re talking about here. Nope, this one’s a naturally aspirated V-12. Just 26 were built, and production lasted all of November 2001.


It arrived at an interesting moment in AMG’s evolution. By the early 2000s, the once-independent tuning house had been fully absorbed into the Mercedes-Benz corporate structure, and its products were becoming increasingly integrated into the official model range. Yet cars like the CL63 still retained something of AMG’s earlier spirit: large engines, subtle visual aggression, and a slightly mischievous sense of excess.


Not long afterward, the philosophy would change. The supercharged and later bi-turbo monsters that followed—most famously the CL65 AMG—ushered in a new era of AMG: faster, louder, and considerably more theatrical. The CL63 therefore occupies an unusual niche. It is both modern AMG and old AMG at the same time.

To understand the CL63 you first have to understand the C215 CL-Class itself. Introduced in 1999, the C215 was Mercedes’ flagship coupe, derived from the contemporary W220 S-Class but styled as a sleek, pillarless grand tourer. It represented a major philosophical shift for Mercedes. Gone was the monumental overengineering of the earlier C140 coupe; in its place came lighter construction, more electronics, and a new focus on technological sophistication.


Chief among those innovations was[Active Body Control (ABC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Body_Control) , a remarkable hydraulic suspension system designed to virtually eliminate body roll and pitch. By monitoring the car’s movements through a network of sensors and adjusting hydraulic pressure at each wheel, ABC could actively counteract the forces that traditionally cause large luxury cars to lean in corners. In theory, the result was impossible: limousine comfort with sports-car body control. In practice … well, we’ll come to that.


The CL63 AMG was powered by a 6.3-liter naturally aspirated V-12, a development of Mercedes’ M137 engine, which as a 5.8-liter unit powered cars like the S600 sedan and CL600 coupe. Unlike later AMG units that increasingly relied on forced induction, the CL63’s powerplant remained resolutely old-school, with large displacement, natural aspiration, and plenty of mechanical presence.


Like most Mercedes V-12s of the era, the engine employed an aluminum block and aluminum cylinder heads, with a 60-degree bank angle chosen to ensure inherent mechanical balance. Each cylinder head housed three valves per cylinder—two intake and one exhaust—a design Mercedes had used extensively throughout the 1990s to optimize airflow while maintaining durability.


The valvetrain had single overhead camshafts per bank, while dual spark plugs per cylinder ensured more complete combustion. This twin-spark arrangement was a hallmark of many Mercedes engines during the period, particularly those intended for large luxury vehicles where smoothness and efficiency were paramount.

AMG’s modifications went well beyond increasing displacement. The engine received revised camshaft profiles, altered intake geometry, and recalibrated engine management, all designed to improve airflow and throttle response. The intake system itself was optimized for the larger-displacement engine, ensuring that the additional capacity translated into genuine gains in torque and power.


And the results were substantial for the time. The CL63 produced around 444 horsepower at 5500 rpm and approximately 457 pound-feet of torque, figures that placed it firmly among the most powerful luxury coupes of the early 2000s. But numbers rarely capture the real appeal.


What distinguished the engine was the way it delivered that performance. Unlike the turbocharged V-12 that would follow in the CL65, the naturally aspirated M137 unleashed its torque in a broad, linear sweep. There was no sudden surge of boost, no dramatic spike in acceleration—simply a steady and relentless wave of power.


That power was transmitted through the company’s five-speed 5G-Tronic automatic gearbox, a unit widely regarded as one of Mercedes’ most durable transmissions. While not as quick-shifting as modern dual-clutch systems, it provided smooth and unobtrusive gear changes.


Performance figures were suitably impressive for the era. The CL63 could reach 62 mph in around five seconds, with top speed electronically limited to 155 mph, though the engine clearly possessed the strength to push beyond that figure if allowed.


However, the CL63’s engine was not designed to be dramatic or flamboyant. It was designed to
deliver immense performance with minimal fuss. That’s precisely what makes it feel like one of the last truly old-school AMG engines. Shortly afterward, AMG moved in a very different direction. The 6.0-liter CL65 AMG replaced displacement with twin turbochargers, which boosted power to well over 600 hp and torque to levels that bordered on the absurd.

It was faster, unquestionably. But it was also louder, more aggressive, and far more theatrical. The CL63’s naturally aspirated V-12, by contrast, embodied a philosophy of big capacity, smooth power delivery, and effortless speed. In other words, it was a luxury engine first and a performance engine second. And perhaps that is why it remains so appealing today. If you can find one.


The 26 cars built were destined for the European and Asian markets, and none of them ever made it to the States officially, so it’s hard to put a price on them. The last one to sell publicly appears to be one that[sold in 2022](https://www.classic.com/m/mercedes-benz/cl/c215/cl-63-amg/) on Classic.com for $51K.


There are some caveats for those fortunate few who own a CL63. That ABC suspension, for example, is a bit like a drunk PhD student: unquestionably clever, but prone to expensive mistakes. ABC relies on a large number of high-pressure hydraulic components working together perfectly, and over time these parts inevitably wear. If the hydraulic pump goes, that’s a couple grand. If the struts leak, they can cost upwards of $2500 each. The accumulators—otherwise known as ABC spheres—are around a thousand bucks, and if there are problems with the valve block, budget around $3000. Deferred ABC maintenance is part of the reason why so many “normal” C215-generation CLs are available for so little.


Look for an ABC fluid flush every two to three years in the paperwork, and accumulators typically need replacing every 60,000 to 80,000 miles. It’s part of the reason why so many ‘normal’ C215 CLs are available for so little.


So, sure, there is some potential for great pain. But then, with a glorious naturally aspirated V-12 before you and the knowledge that you’re one of the exalted few piloting it, we reckon you’ll find the pain tolerable.


Looking back, the Mercedes-Benz CL63 AMG represents a fascinating turning point in AMG history. It arrived at the moment when the brand was evolving from[a slightly eccentric tuner](https://www.hagerty.com/media/buying-and-selling/brutal-amg-cars-rm-sothebys-essen-auction/) into a fully integrated performance division within Mercedes-Benz. Its enormous V-12 reflected AMG’s traditional approach to performance: big engines, effortless power, and understated styling. The CL65 that followed would push the concept much further, ushering in the era of turbocharged excess that now defines much of AMG’s identity.


For those who appreciate the earlier philosophy, the rare CL63 AMG represents the end of an era.
