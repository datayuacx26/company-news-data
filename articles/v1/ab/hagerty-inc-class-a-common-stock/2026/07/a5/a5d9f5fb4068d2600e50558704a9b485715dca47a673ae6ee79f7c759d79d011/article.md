# Offy: Four Letters. Four Cylinders. Four Decades as the Engine to Beat.

Chew on that for a bit. We now live in an age when memes are born, flourish, and are forgotten over the course of a week. So how did the Offy manage to spend four decades as the undisputed heavyweight champion on everything from the bricks of the Indianapolis Motor Speedway to the pavement of the Milwaukee Mile to the dirt on bullrings buried deep in flyover country? Well, technical excellence was one reason. Being in the right place at the right time was another. But mostly, the Offy thrived because it was a unicorn, a felicitous match of form and function and an ideal weapon for an era when road racing was for wimps and oval-track racing was the sine qua non of American motorsports.


“I won three features in one day in an Offy-powered midget—Labor Day in 1963,” Mario Andretti recalls. “Later, when they turbocharged the Offy, before they put a limit on the \[boost\] pressure, you could just wind the sh*t out of it, and it was a characteristic of that engine that it could withstand it. I mean, look at how reliable it was, for so many seasons, over so many decades. OK, sure, you blew an engine now and then. But if you rebuilt the engine in the proper time frame, you could run it forever, and that reliability factor was something that wins you championships. So yeah, it was hard not to love that engine.”


The Offy bears the name of master machinist and former shop foreman Fred Offenhauser, who opened his own small business in South Central Los Angeles in 1933. Initially, he kept the lights on at his cramped, scruffy shop by rebuilding truck engines. But in 1934, he began selling four-cylinder race motors branded as Offys, and fellow Southern Californian Kelly Petillo scored the first of the engine’s 27 wins in the Indy 500 the next year. The motor displaced 260 cubic inches and made about 250 horsepower, which allowed Petillo to qualify at a speed of 115.095 mph.


Fred Offenhauser (L) and Louis Meyer (R), Los Angeles, 1956.


Klemantaski Collection/Getty Images


Nearly a half-century later, the same basic engine still reigned supreme thanks largely to the addition of a turbocharger to goose power output to levels unimagined by its original builders. In 1973, with the manifold pressure—which measures how much extra air is being rammed into the combustion chamber, usually in terms of pounds per square inch or inches of mercury—registering God-only-knows how much boost, Johnny Rutherford used a turbo 159-cubic-inch Offy to turn a 200-mph lap during practice for the Indy 500 and qualify on the pole at a record speed of 198.413 mph.


“In those days, there was no limit on the manifold pressure,” recalled engine builder Roger Bailey, who also built the Offy that Rutherford drove to its last Indy 500 win in 1976. “We had a 120-inch manifold pressure gauge, and we bent the needle. Our Heenan & Froude 490 dyno pegged out at 1350 horsepower, and that engine on the dyno went way past that. We’ll never know the true horsepower. But Rutherford always said that he could spin the wheels on the short chute at the Speedway.”


It’s almost impossible to overstate how dominant the Offy was during its long stretch at the top of the American open-wheel mountain. The record book shows that 255- and 270-cubic-inch models won every Champ Car race except Pikes Peak from October 1946 to August 1963. And from 1953 to 1960, every car that qualified at Indy was either an Offy or a Novi, which had been built initially at Offenhauser’s shop. To this, add thousands of wins chalked up by smaller versions of the engines designed for midgets and sprint cars. No other race engine was so successful for so long. None has even come close.


Note the complex plumbing of the turbo Offy that propelled Joe Leonard and his Parnelli VPJ-1 to the top of the Indy-car championship in 1972.


Cameron Neveu


Over the years, the Offy vanquished countless other contenders to its throne: the Sampson V-16, the Big Six and Little Six of Art Sparks, the Novi, the four-cam Indy Ford, flathead Fords, and various other stock-block motors, not to mention grand prix engines from Maserati, Alfa Romeo, Mercedes-Benz, and Ferrari. Conventional wisdom says that the final nail in the Offy’s coffin was the development of the Cosworth DFX. Which makes sense. After all, the Cossie was a thoroughly modern, turbocharged take on the esoteric Formula 1 motor that had dominated F1 for decades. So, of course, it was better than the Offy, right?


Well, yes and no. During the late 1970s, Indy-car racing branched out to road courses, where the Offy, designed and optimized for ovals, clearly wasn’t competitive. Then, the engine was weakened by ever more draconian limitations on fuel allotments and manifold pressure. The ostensible goal was to reduce speeds, but as a practical matter, it prevented crew chiefs from screwing up the boost pressure of the Offy to stratospheric levels that would have caused the eight-cylinder Cosworth, with its tiny ports and valves, to blow up like a Roman candle. So in a very real sense, the Offy was a victim of politics as much as superior technology.


Engine builder Stewart Van Dyne was there when the Offy was laid to rest, and he’s got his own take on the cause of death. “Rules,” he says simply. Sad but true. Here’s the story behind how it happened.


The gleaming pair of cam boxes were a signature feature of all Offys.


Cameron Neveu


We all know the paradox of the[Ship of Theseus](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/the-ferrari-of-theseus-philosophy-and-a-1-87m-pile-of-parts/) . If every part of the ship has been replaced, is it still the same ship? It’s tempting to ask the same question of the Offy. Over the years, the engine benefited from countless upgrades, from magnetos to electronic ignition, carburetors to fuel injection, and more. But what’s truly amazing about the venerable four-banger is how much remained the same. The Offenhauser’s last official race was a 500-miler at Pocono in 1982. If you were to remove the turbocharger assembly and a few ancillary components from the Offy in the back of Jim McElreath’s winged Eagle, you’d see a tall, narrow, Y-shaped powerplant immediately recognizable as a sibling to the engine in Kelly Petillo’s high-riding front-engine Wetteroth chassis at Indy in 1935.


Whether the engine had been built during the depths of the Great Depression or at the height of the disco craze, it always featured a handful of signature elements: first, a pair of slender cam boxes shrouding overhead camshafts operating four intake and exhaust valves per cylinder. Second, an integrated block and head that eliminated the need for a failure-prone head gasket. Third, a barrel-shaped aluminum crankcase fitted with bronze bulkheads known as webs that gave the Offy its legendarily stout bottom end. And fourth, a tall gear tower housing a cluster of gears that ran the camshafts, the oil pump, and the magneto/distributor with far greater precision than chains or belts ever could.


The red Joe Hunt magneto was a Bendix Scintilla aircraft unit specifically adapted to the normally aspirated 255-cubic-inch version of the engine.


Cameron Neveu


Many of the features found in the Offy have been rendered passé by modern advances in metallurgy and machining. But one of them remains the gold standard in contemporary high-performance engines: the four-valve double-overhead-camshaft valvetrain and pent-roof combustion chamber. Ironically, this DOHC arrangement is the oldest of the bunch, dating back to the pioneering years of automobile development.


Nicolaus Otto received a patent for the four-stroke internal combustion engine in 1876. Plenty of progress was made over the next quarter-century, but there was still no shortage of disagreement about which designs worked best. In an era before computers, much less computer simulations, it was impossible to know exactly what happened inside the combustion chamber—how smoothly the air-fuel mixture flowed into the cylinder, how efficiently it was burned, and how easily the spent gas escaped through the exhaust port. Theories abounded, but trial and error was the only way to determine what worked. Which is why racing was so important to the development of the nascent automobile.


Nowadays, racing is a form of entertainment that exists primarily to amuse fans and give manufacturers a platform to market their products. Since the 1970s or so, sanctioning bodies driven by the twin imperatives of safety and parity have been forced to promulgate rules that slow cars down by clipping wings, limiting engine speeds, and so on. A century ago, by way of contrast, racing was a laboratory for wild-hair experimentation. True, a lot of would-be innovations turned out to be losers that were quickly swept into history’s dustbin. But most of the car features we now take for granted were first proved on the racetrack.


Peugeot is the world’s oldest automobile manufacturer, and one of its early models shared first prize at the world’s first automobile race, run from Paris to Rouen in 1894. In 1911, in preparation for the freshly revived French Grand Prix, the company decided to build a brand-new race car. But instead of entrusting its design to its in-house engineering department, the assignment was given to three factory race car drivers, augmented by a brilliant draftsman by the name of Ernest Henry. The four men had no formal training. On the contrary, they were empiricists who operated by the seat of their pants. Skeptical Peugeot engineers dubbed them Les Charlatans. Imagine their surprise when this group of apparent poseurs unveiled a design that changed the course of automotive history.


An internal combustion engine is essentially an air pump that trades heat for energy. All things being equal, the faster you get the air-fuel mixture into a cylinder, and the more efficiently you burn it before the spent gas flows out of the exhaust port, the more power you’re going to make. As with everything in life, there are trade-offs. If you want an engine that breathes better and spins faster, you’d better be prepared to pay a premium and accept a higher possibility of catastrophic failure. Which is why automobile manufacturers primarily concerned with generating profits often spec engine designs that are suboptimal from a performance perspective.


> Skeptical Peugeot engineers dubbed them *Les Charlatans* .


Consider the[groundbreaking flathead engine that Ford introduced](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/fords-flathead-v-8-gave-power-to-the-people/) in 1932. This is the unit that brought V-8 power to the masses and served as the technological foundation of the hot-rod movement. To cut costs and minimize complexity, the valves—two per cylinder—were located side by side in the block rather than the head and opened by lifters actuated by short rocker arms attached to a camshaft located just above the crankshaft. (Technically, this is known as an L-head engine. If the valves were located on either side of the cylinder, it would be a T-head. But both are flathead or side-valve designs.) The upside? Easy and cheap to manufacture. The downside? Strangled airflow and a compromised combustion chamber.


After World War II, Ford’s lunch was eaten by a new generation of overhead valve motors from General Motors. The signature feature of these OHV engines was valves actuated by long pushrods. Although the valvetrain was more complicated and expensive than the flathead’s, putting the valves in the head over the cylinders permitted the design of a more efficient combustion chamber. Then[Chrysler raised the bar](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/hemi-chrysler-drew-dome-forged-dynasty/) by creating a combustion chamber whose top was shaped like the dome of a planetarium. A crossflow head with an intake port on one side and the exhaust port on the other promoted better breathing, and a spark plug located at top dead center of the dome optimized combustion. Engines built to this design were originally sold in 1951 as FirePower units, but we know them now as Hemis.


A hemispherical combustion chamber allows the use of large valves that produce big power. The drawback is that a hemi head can accommodate only two valves per cylinder, and that’s a problem. Remember, an internal combustion engine is essentially an air pump, so a critical factor in how well it performs is what’s known in engineering-speak as volumetric efficiency. This translates roughly into how close you can come to completely filling the capacity of the combustion chamber with the air-fuel mixture flowing through the intake port. If increasing volumetric efficiency is the goal, then four valves are obviously better than two. The downside is that four valves won’t fit inside a hemi head. Instead, you have to use what’s known as a pent-roof head that’s made up of five angled surfaces (as in a pentagon), rather than a hemispherical dome.


Believe it or not, Les Charlatans figured all of this out way back in 1911. And they had another profound insight. They didn’t want to bury the camshaft in the block and open the valves with long, heavy pushrods that would limit engine speed. Instead, they located the camshafts above the cylinder head and opened the valves with L-shaped cam followers. The car they designed was built around a four-cylinder, 16-valve, twin-cam engine whose architecture is still pretty much the go-to solution to this day for everything from an entry-level Honda Civic LX to the 416-hp Mercedes-AMG CLA45 S.

For the record, Les Charlatans didn’t invent the DOHC design. But they’re the ones who proved that it worked. In its debut in 1912, the brand-new Peugeot was driven to victory in the French Grand Prix by Georges Boillot, who was one of Les Charlatans. The car finished 13 minutes ahead of a Fiat that had been, until then, considered to be the most sophisticated race car in the world. The next year, another member of Les Charlatans, Jules Goux, won the Indy 500 in an upgraded version of the Peugeot. He, too, outdistanced the second-place car by 13 minutes despite pausing during pit stops to revive himself with infusions of champagne.


The Americans immediately realized that the French interlopers had a better idea. In 1915, a Peugeot was acquired by “Wild Bob” Burman, who’d set a land-speed record in 1911—41.732 mph—in the ex–Barney Oldfield Blitzen Benz. Burman marauded from coast to coast until blowing up the motor. To rebuild it, he enlisted the aid of a man whom historian Griffith Borgeson would later describe as “the greatest creative figure in the history of the American racing car.”


Like Les Charlatans, Harry Arminius Miller was an intuitive and protean self-taught genius. Although later photos depict him as a dapper raconteur with a rakish fedora and a waxed mustache, he came from sturdy Midwestern stock, the son of a German immigrant, born in a small Wisconsin logging town. Miller’s formal education ended when he quit elementary school to work at a bicycle shop. Fascinated by machinery, he was a classic tinkerer who learned how things worked by taking them apart. But unlike most do-it-yourselfers, he wasn’t content merely to put them back together. No, he aspired to build a better mousetrap, whether it was a simple fuel pump or a W-24 marine engine for hydroplane racing.


During his formative years, Miller ran a bicycle shop, repaired steam engines and milling equipment, designed and built a motorcycle, patented a spark plug, and worked at several car companies. He learned the rudiments of racing while preparing an early Olds for competition, and a stint serving as the shop foreman at an ironworks company taught him the latest thinking in metallurgy and casting techniques. At some point, he turned his attention to a problem that has bedeviled anybody who has ever worked with carburetors: how best to regulate the mixture of air and fuel. Eventually, he patented a unit that used a rotary barrel to progressively expose fuel jets as the throttle was opened. Although his so-called Master carburetor didn’t work well at part throttle, it was perfect for high-performance driving. Besides establishing his credentials in the racing world, his experience selling carburetors whet his appetite for more ambitious motorsports projects.


By 1915, Harry A. Miller Manufacturing employed more than 50 people, and his shop just south of downtown LA became a hub for a burgeoning cadre of professional racers. Later that year, he designed and built the first of several single-overhead-cam four-cylinder engines. In 1920, he unveiled the first of his magnificent straight-eights, featuring a DOHC valvetrain clearly inspired by Burman’s old Peugeot. Miller soon emerged as the colossus of American race car design and production between the wars, and the shadow he cast over the sport didn’t disappear until the Offy finally faded away in the early 1980s.


The Henry Ford


The Miller factory was a soup-to-nuts operation; virtually every single component of his race cars was built in-house. And what amazing cars they were! Designed for high-speed, banked board tracks where the throttle was rarely cracked, the chassis were impossibly slender, measuring only 18 inches wide at the cockpit. Later, Miller introduced a front-wheel-drive version especially for Indy. By eliminating the driveshaft, Miller was able to lower the cockpit and make the bodywork even cleaner aerodynamically. But the secret to the success of his cars was the engine under the long, sleek hoods.


The first of Miller’s straight-eights displaced 183 cubic inches. Then came a 122-cubic-inch version of the same engine. But his masterpiece was a supercharged 91-cubic-inch (1.5 liter) straight-eight that produced speeds that almost beggar belief. In 1927, Frank Lockhart clocked a lap at 147.7 mph around a board track in Atlantic City. He used the same car, totally unstreamlined, with four exposed tires, to go 171.02 mph on a short course laid out on the dry lake at Muroc, California. The speed Lockhart achieved with a 1.5-liter Miller was only 32 mph short of the record that Henry Segrave had set recently in a purpose-built LSR car powered by not one but two 22.5-liter airplane engines.


> Virtually every single component of his race cars was built in-house.


The Henry Ford


In addition to being an engineering genius, Miller had the soul of an artist, so he was determined to build not only the fastest cars and engines but also the most beautiful ones. The purity of its fundamental design, coupled with a variety of unnecessary aesthetic flourishes, transformed the Miller 91 race car into an industrial jewel. As Mark Dees wrote in a caption in his authoritative history, *The Miller Dynasty* , “It was designed to be as light and efficient as the knowledge and materials of the time would allow, and the early example shown here has seldom, if ever, been matched for its architectural beauty and truly elegant simplicity.”


But Miller wasn’t a one-man band. Two collaborators were especially critical to his success. The first was Leo Goossen, a formally trained engineer and draftsman who was able to transform Miller’s esoteric concepts into detailed industrial drawings. Quiet and self-effacing, projecting an image more like an accountant than a stalwart of the racing world, Goossen would be a principal player in the design of every purebred American racing engine over the next 50 years. The other critical employee was shop foreman Fred Offenhauser, whose Teutonic precision allowed Miller components to be machined to tolerances that were unheard of at the time. Offenhauser also had the business moxie that served as a counterweight to Miller’s cost-be-damned approach to commerce—which was a major reason that the company always seemed to be teetering on the edge of financial disaster.


“Miller didn’t have any money,” says Gary Schroeder, a longtime midget racer and racing-parts supplier whose father, Gordon, was a famous builder of steering boxes and torsion bars and owned various Offy-powered race cars. “He didn’t have state-of-the-art machinery, just clapped-out tools run by overhead belts and pulleys. But he had some really clever people working for him, so the tolerances on the engines were amazing. They don’t have any gaskets on them. I mean, there are no gaskets anywhere.”


Miller wasn’t the only American with money trouble after the stock market crash of 1929. Expensive and exotic, the Miller 91 was all dressed up with no place to go as the Depression spawned new forms of less expensive, shorter-track racing. Miller reluctantly agreed to develop a four-cylinder engine that could be sold at a more affordable price. As it happened, he already had a four-banger in his portfolio: a marine engine that had been created a few years earlier for the 151-cubic-inch hydroplane class. While enlarging it to 200 and then 220 cubic inches, Miller and Goossen replaced the SOHC valvetrain and hemi head with the 16-valve twin-cam arrangement and pent-roof architecture familiar from the straight-eight engines. Although it didn’t carry the nameplate yet, this was, in effect, the first Offy.


> The Offy was built by racers, for racers, and there’s a method to its madness.


Four-cylinder Millers finished 1-2-3-4 at Indy in 1934. Alas, this proved to be the swan song for Harry A. Miller Inc., which had been forced into involuntary bankruptcy the previous year. In lieu of back pay, Offenhauser spirited away assorted tools and machines, along with the patterns for the 220 inline-four, which he immediately began producing at the shop he opened after Miller went out of business. The following year marked the first Indy win for an engine badged as an Offy. (Confusingly, four-cylinder engines built at the Miller factory before it closed won the 500 in 1936 and 1938.) Goossen, who went to work for Offenhauser, always maintained that the “big” Offys were effectively rebranded Millers.


Unlike their big brothers, Offys built for midget racing had only two valves per cylinder. This early model is a 97-cubic-incher equipped with a pair of side-draft Riley carburetors.

For the record, Offenhauser also commissioned Goossen to design a new engine to take advantage of the craze for midgets, which were the most popular form of open-wheel racing during the Depression. To save time and money, Goossen based it on half of a 183-cubic-inch Miller straight-eight. Unlike the twin-cam valvetrain in the “big” Offys, the small engine had a hemi head and a single-overhead cam. But this mighty midget was badged as an Offy, and it was downright unbeatable for decades.


Offenhauser moved to a bigger, better-equipped shop—though still in South Central LA—in 1941 and prospered doing defense production work during World War II. In 1946, he sold his business to three-time Indy winner Louis Meyer and his one-time riding mechanic Dale Drake. With Goossen providing engineering expertise, Meyer-Drake Engineering thrived in the postwar era while building three different sizes of engine: 270s and then 255s for Indianapolis and the rest of the Championship Trail, 220s for sprint cars, and 110s for midgets. Technically, there were no rules saying you had to run an Offy. But if you wanted to make a living in American open-wheel racing, you either got an Offy or found another line of work.


An Engine For the Ages: With its tall gear tower and slender block, the Offy was perfectly symmetrical, so intake and exhaust manifolds could be swapped from side to side, depending on the crew chief’s preference.

These days, most purpose-built race engines come directly from automakers or specialist engine factories. They’re designed by mechanical engineers working with cutting-edge computers and multimillion-dollar budgets, they feature components formed by sophisticated mills and tooling, and they’re assembled in clean rooms by teams of meticulously trained technicians. In the IndyCar world, teams lease engines from Honda and Chevrolet, which provide fully built motors and the technicians to work on them. It’s very much a plug-and-play operation.


Not so in the 1930s and ’40s. Or the ’50s and ’60s. Well into the 1980s, racers either bought engines from specialists such as Cosworth and Traco or built them from components sourced from parts catalogs. Teams were responsible for optimizing performance, doing routine maintenance, and making repairs when something broke. There was a lot less money in racing back then, so engines tended to be run hard and put away wet. When something failed, which happened a lot more often back then, engines had to be fixed on the fly in crappy conditions at county fairgrounds and rudimentary bullrings. So during an era when even well-funded teams rarely had more than a crew chief and one or two mechanics, racers prized engines that were tough, dependable, uncomplicated, and economical.


Can you say Offenhauser?


“It was a tractor engine more than a race engine—a big, heavy motor with big, heavy parts, and it didn’t turn too fast,” said Jim McGee, who worked on naturally aspirated Offys during his years at Leader Card Racers in the 1960s before becoming one of the winningest crew chiefs in Indy-car history. “But it was reliable and cost-effective. You had to rebuild the four-cam Ford and the Cosworth every two races, but you could go four races or more with the Offy. You could probably run it 1000 miles, and then all you had to do was take it apart, Magnaflux it, and put the pieces back together, and it was ready to run 1000 more.”

The Offy isn’t a simple engine—far from it—and its more idiosyncratic design features require some annoying mechanical gymnastics. For example, since the cylinder head isn’t detachable, as with conventional motors, boring and honing the cylinders can be tricky, and a valve job requires a special tool and technique. Installing the crankshaft is an even more time-consuming operation since the crankcase is a one-piece unit. But the Offy is nowhere near as finicky as, say, the Wilbur Shaw–driven Maserati 8CTF that won Indy in 1939 and 1940. “In the Boyle Special,” says Dan Rosenau, restoration manager at the Indianapolis Motor Speedway Museum, “between the throttle pedal and the second carburetor, there are 17—no, make that 18—bell cranks, rod ends, bushings, and bearings. It’s the most complicated throttle linkage I’ve ever seen.”


The Offy was built by racers, for racers, and there’s always a method to its apparent madness. Take, for instance, the integrated cylinder block and cylinder head. The primary benefit of this unorthodox design is eliminating the need for a head gasket. That doesn’t sound like a big deal today, when blown head gaskets in purpose-built race engines are an exceedingly rare event. But this wasn’t the case when the Offy was designed in the 1930s, thanks to far inferior gasket materials. And gasket failures would have been an issue in the 1970s, when turbocharged Offys were producing more than 1000 horsepower, or roughly four times as much as the original engine.


Kelly Petillo spent his life savings to build this car, and Fred Offenhauser provided an engine on credit. Petillo won not only Indy in 1935 but also the AAA national championship.


Courtesy Indianapolis Motor Speedway


The monoblock design also eliminates the distortion to the cylinder bore that inevitably occurs when you clamp the cylinder head to the block and torque down the head bolts. “That distortion will happen even when everything’s perfect,” says Frank Honsowetz, who restored a variety of Offys during his tenure running[Ed Pink Racing Engines](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/ed-pink-the-old-master-has-passed-away-at-94/) . “And it doesn’t have to be much. A human hair is about three-thousandths of an inch in diameter. And if you move the bore 1 thou, that’s enough to hurt the sealing to the piston rings.” Today, again, thanks to advances in metallurgy and fastener design, this is no longer a gigantic deal. But it used to be, and the Offy solved it by casting the head and block as a single piece and getting rid of head bolts altogether.


But the most unusual feature of the Offy was the one-piece barrel-shaped crankcase, which forced mechanics to employ techniques that weren’t taught in Engine Assembly 101. To install a crankshaft in a conventional engine, the block is turned upside-down and the oil pan is removed to expose the bottom end. The crank is then lowered onto semicircular main journals and bearing inserts that will support it and allow it to rotate freely. Next, semicircular main bearing caps are bolted to the main journals, fully encircling the crank. Once the oil pan is reattached, you’re good to go. Obviously, this glosses over all the work that goes into the process. But in big-picture terms, the process is easy-peasy.


Installing a crankshaft in an Offy is a much more complicated and labor-intensive operation. To begin with, there are no main journals or main caps. Instead, the crank was ringed by stout bronze discs known in Offy lingo as webs. After these are set in place on the crankshaft, the empty crankcase is heated to at least 200 degrees either in an oven or by an acetylene torch with a rosebud tip. The case is then stood up on its nose, and the fully dressed crankshaft is lowered down into it through a just-big-enough hole in the back. The webs are clamped to the case while the aluminum is still hot. The so-called interference fit formed when the metal cools and shrinks is stronger than simply bolting the components together. This process is a pain, literally—it’s almost impossible to avoid burning your hands and wrists while turning wrenches inside the narrow access hole on the side of the heated case. But as they say: No pain, no gain.


> It was a tractor engine more than a race engine–a big, heavy motor with big, heavy parts.


A one-piece crankcase is significantly sturdier than a standard split case with main caps. This is an especially powerful advantage when the engine is an inline-four with a flat 180-degree crank, which sends cylinder pressure straight up and down in a vertical plane and puts the main caps under tremendous loads. Although the problem can be addressed by anchoring the main caps with four or even six bolts, a one-piece crankcase is an even better solution. With its barrel case and webs secured via interference fit, the bottom end of the Offy was virtually impervious to vibration even after engines started making 1000 horsepower and more. “As long as you got your clearances right, it was just rock-solid when you slid it together,” says Mike Devin, who built countless Offys as an Indy-car crew chief before becoming the technical director and director of competition for USAC.


Robustness was one of the Offy’s most compelling attributes. But in racing, the goal isn’t merely to finish; it’s to finish first. So during the years when everybody was running an Offy, crew chiefs were constantly tinkering to find an advantage, however small. The most inventive guys were known as “Offy witches,” and their black magic ranged from minor tweaks like employing different cam grinds for different tracks to major upgrades such as creating a “double pumper” lubrication system with a pair of oil pumps. Creative crew chiefs even mounted Offys in a “lay-down” position, as much as 90 degrees from vertical, to lower the hood line of their hulking front-engine roadsters.


The most common area for experimentation was bore and stroke. Some engine builders favored a longer stroke (coupled with a smaller bore) to maximize torque and acceleration off the corners. Others preferred a shorter stroke (and bigger bore) to allow engines to spin faster and develop more top-end horsepower. Camshaft grinds, too, were open to interpretation. “There were a lot of choices, and everybody had different ideas for different tracks,” Schroeder says. Back then, remember, races were run on dirt and pavement, short tracks and long, and the condition of the surface—heavy, slick, whatever—was another factor to take into consideration.


> The most inventive guys were known as “Offy witches.”


The improvements produced by these variations were measurable in terms of race results but only incremental when quantified by speed. When the first Kurtis KK500A roadster appeared at the Speedway in 1952 with a 270-cubic-inch Offy, Bill Vukovich was the fastest qualifier at 138.212 mph. A decade later, in a Watson roadster powered by a 255 Offy, Parnelli Jones took the pole at 150.370 mph. An increase of 12 mph in 10 years didn’t seem like much. But it was enough, as long as the Offy was the only game in town.


The engine on the stand is one of the 255-cubic-inch normally aspirated Offys that dominated Indy-car racing in the 1950s and early ’60s. By 1972, Joe Leonard’s Samsonite-sponsored car packed a 159-cubic-inch version augmented with a massive turbo.


Cameron Neveu


Everything changed in the early 1960s, when regulations allowing far bigger stock-block engines rendered Offys increasingly uncompetitive in sprint-car and midget racing. Then, in 1963, Jim Clark finished second in the 500 with a 255-cubic-inch version of the 289 Ford found in the Cobra. The next year, after a $10 million development program, Ford returned with a four-cam V-8 that had 75 horsepower and 2000 rpm on the Offy. An Offy eked out a providential win at Indy in 1964, thanks to A.J. Foyt, but Clark triumphed with a four-cam Ford the next year. In 1966, only nine of the 33 starters ran Offys, and none of them were competitive. Adding insult to injury, Louis Meyer split with Dale Drake to take a job running the Ford engine program, and the shop moved to a new facility in Santa Ana, not too far from Disneyland.


It looked like it was time to put the tired nag out to pasture. But it turned out that, thanks to some new technology, there was still some life in the old warhorse.


The most straightforward way to get more power out of an internal combustion engine is to pump more air through it. This can be accomplished in two basic ways: You can either increase engine speed by spinning it faster or you can force more air into the cylinder during each intake stroke. The Offy was already maxed out in terms of revolutions per minute. So that left Door Number Two as the only option in what was turning out to be a one-sided battle with the four-cam Indy Ford. And that was fine with crew chief Herb Porter.


Herbie Horsepower, as he was known in the USAC community, was a high priest who worshipped at the altar of forced induction. During World War II, he’d served as a staff sergeant overhauling P-40s and P-51 fighters, and he developed a deep appreciation for the superchargers on their screaming Merlin V-12s. After the war, he fitted a sprint car with a Roots-style supercharger in 1949 and, later, a centrifugal blower. Encouraged by the speed of these cars, he built a blown Offy that qualified on the front row at Indy in 1952 (the car was named, fittingly, the Miracle Power Special). But it crapped out after 20 laps and was classified 33rd and last. Like the heroes of Greek myth, Porter’s supercharged cars tended to be spectacular but short-lived, and he took a hiatus from his adventures on forced induction.


Up close and personal with the ingenious mechanical fuel-injection unit invented by Stu Hilborn. The “piss and squirt” system debuted in 1949 on a 270-cubic-inch Offy driven by Jimmy Jackson.


Cameron Neveu


A supercharger creates horsepower by forcing more air into the cylinders. It’s typically driven by a belt, gear, or chain run off the engine, and this drive mechanism exacts a penalty by sapping a certain amount of power. A turbocharger, by way of contrast, is driven by exhaust gases that would otherwise flow out the tailpipe, so the extra power it produces is more or less free. The downside was that it took time for the turbo to spool up, so there was a lag between depressing the throttle and picking up speed. It was also difficult to tune the turbo to work well across a broad range of engine speeds.


Nevertheless, turbos started to appear on trucks and tractors in the 1950s, and in 1962, General Motors was emboldened to apply the technology to the Oldsmobile Jetfire. Engineer Bob DeBisschop was hired by Garrett AiResearch to make it work. It didn’t, or at least not well enough to sell in appreciable numbers. Throttle lag was a drag, and the turbo didn’t do much at low speed. On the other hand, it worked really well at high speed, so DeBisschop thought the turbo might have a future on big ovals where engines spent most of their time in a relatively narrow rev band, and he found an ardent ally in Herbie Horsepower.


Aided by Stu Hilborn (of fuel-injection fame), Porter and DeBisschop fitted an off-the-shelf TE06 turbo designed for an Allis-Chalmers diesel tractor to a spare Offy. Before long, they were making 600 horsepower on the dyno. Yowza! In 1966, Porter crammed the engine in an elderly Watson roadster, with the turbo and a crude Rube Goldberg–like network of large pipes and tubes hung out the side of the cockpit. With Bobby Grim behind the wheel, the car whistled down Indy’s front straight at 200 mph and belched flames on overrun in the corners. Grim described it as “an absolute plumber’s nightmare of two sets of injector nozzles, one adjusted for unblown, the other for when the blower kicked in. Which meant that at low speeds, the roadster was a slobbering idiot—pop, spit, slobber, it barely ran at all. Then all of a sudden, BLAM! The turbo kicked in, and it was hold-onto-your-ass.”


In 1968, driven by Bobby Unser, an Eagle powered by a Herb Porter–built Offy outlasted Lotus turbines and four-cam Fords to become the first turbocharged engine to win Indy.


Courtesy Indianapolis Motor Speedway


Although Grim managed to qualify—barely—he was eliminated in the big opening-lap crashfest. The addition of a wastegate and other detail upgrades improved drivability, and Lloyd Ruby scored the turbo Offy’s first USAC win next year. In 1968, Porter worked with DeBisschop and Jud Phillips on Bobby Unser’s turbocharged Eagle. Unser won Indy, and Offys powered nine of the first 11 finishers. By 1969, Ford was forced to start turbocharging its formerly unbeatable four-cam motor. But in a weird twist of fate, the old-school, heavy-duty design of the Offy—especially the stout bottom end—allowed it to absorb stratospheric levels of boost better than the Ford. The Offy also breathed better as the power levels rose.


“The problem with the four-cam Ford was that they designed it with ports the size of a four-cylinder 255 Offy, and they were too big for an eight-cylinder engine,” says Van Dyne, who built the turbo Offy that propelled Mark Donohue to winning Indy in 1972. At first glance, bigger ports seem like a good idea since they’d theoretically allow more air into the cylinder. But intake ports aren’t analogous to the windows in a house. The difference is that the size of the cylinder affects the velocity of airflow through the port, with bigger cylinders able to suck in air faster than smaller ones. This increases the volume of air being combusted during each power stroke. The Ford had big ports, but the cylinders were too small to efficiently suck in air through them. In fact, many teams inserted what they called port stuffers to better match the size of the ports to the size of the cylinders.


Thanks to the insurrection launched by Herbie Horsepower, Offy was able to reclaim its throne. Ford shuttered its Indy-car operation in 1971 and sold all of its equipment to A.J. Foyt. And from 1972 to 1975, Foyt was the only driver to win an Indy car race with anything other than an Offy. This streak came to an end at Pocono in 1976, when Al Unser finished first with a four-cam Ford, but not the four-cam Indy Ford. Instead, he was using a brand-new turbocharged iteration of the Cosworth DFV, which had dominated Formula 1 since being introduced in 1967.


The Cosworth belongs in the great engine pantheon alongside the Offy. The DFV won 155 races from 1967 to 1983, and the Indy-car derivative, designated the DFX, won another 153 races. Light and compact, it was strong enough to serve as a stressed member of the chassis, and it made good power through a wide range of engine speeds. Designer Keith Duckworth’s most influential technical innovation was placing the valves near the top of the combustion chamber, at a narrow angle from vertical, rather than at the sides, which used to be common practice. This improved the shape of the combustion chamber and allowed the use of flat-top pistons, which improved flame travel and promoted better combustion. Also, the narrow-angle valves translated into a straighter—and, therefore, more efficient—flow of air through the ports.


The included valve angle of the DFV—that is, the angle between the exhaust and intake valves—was 32 degrees. In the Offy, designed during an era long before the benefit of narrow-angle ports were understood, it was 72 degrees. Facing an existential threat in the form of the DFX, the Offy empire struck back. An Offy derivative known as the D-G-S, for Dale Drake, Leo Goossen, and Art Sparks, reduced the included valve angle to 44 degrees, and it was followed by another engine—the last of the Offys—with an included valve angle of 38 degrees. The improvement was dramatic. But the Offy’s major enemy wasn’t the Cosworth; it was the rule makers.


In 1974, reacting to the fuel crisis and the gruesome Indy 500 the previous year, USAC instituted regulations designed to slow cars down. Manifold pressure, which had been unrestricted, was limited to 80 inches of mercury. Lower fuel allotments were a special challenge for the Offys because crew chiefs tended to run them rich, which hurt fuel economy, to prevent pistons from melting. Manifold pressure was cut in 1976 and again the following year, down to 42 inches. This was perfect for the Cosworth, which would have been a hand grenade with the pin pulled at 80 inches. But it yanked the rug out from underneath the Offy.


The Offy won its last race in 1976, the D-G-S in 1978. By that time, it was painfully clear that the four-cylinder engine was long past its sell-by date. John Drake, who’d taken over the business after the death of his father in 1972, decided to build a V-8. After Goossen’s death at the end of 1974, ex-BMW engineer Hans Hermann was hired to design it. The Drake V-8 ran a few races in 1979 and showed some promise, but it was too little, too late. “Money was an issue,” says Van Dyne, who had been hired by Drake to build the engine. “And the bar-room banter really hurt us.” Drake shut the doors for good in 1979.


A few backmarker teams continued to race the Offy until 1982. The engine last appeared at Indianapolis in 1983, when an Offy-powered car practiced but failed to make a qualifying attempt. Nearly a half-century after the first example came out of Fred Offenhauser’s humble shop in Los Angeles, the Offy was finally silenced. But what wonderful noise, in so many cars and on so many tracks, it had made for so many years!


“The normally aspirated engine was nearly bulletproof if maintained well and not too badly abused,” historian Gordon Eliot White wrote in his marvelous book, Offenhauser. “The engine had no particular weakness. It could survive horrendous piston speeds, severe vibrations, even liberal doses of nitromethane for qualifying. When turbocharged, it would hang together under pounding that would destroy stock-block engines and the Ford four-cams of the 1960s.


“It was,” he concluded, “the engine for its age.”
