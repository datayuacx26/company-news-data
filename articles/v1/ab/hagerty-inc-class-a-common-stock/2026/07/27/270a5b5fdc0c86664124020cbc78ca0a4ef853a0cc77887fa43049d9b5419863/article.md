# The Deadliest Decade: The Most Lethal Racing Incident You’ve Never Heard Of

Yellow River Drag Strip was an outlaw track.


Not an outlaw in the sense that it was illegal or a lone pariah that operated beyond the norms of polite society. On the contrary. Yellow River was one of a legion of scruffy drag strips that dotted backwaters all over the country, from the overgrown pastures of the rural South to the featureless sprawl of the exurban West. “A lot of them were back in the woods, and you needed a map just to find them,” said Don Prudhomme, who visited just about all of them during his long career. What made these tracks outlaws was the fact that they weren’t sanctioned by the National Hot Rod Association or the American Hot Rod Association. Instead of big-time events featuring full fields of national stars, they catered mostly to local run-what-you-brung competition—a motley mixture of fair-weather drag racers in street-legal cars and semi-pro ringers with more moxie than money.


Dick Brannan in the Bob Ford 427 SOHC Mustang was a leading factory-backed figure at the time, representing Ford Motor Company’s drag racing program in mid-’60s sanctioned Funny Car competitions.

For outlaw tracks, the money shows were “booked in” slates of match races hyped through newspaper ads, flyers, and radio spots exhorting listeners to “BEEE THERRRRRE!!!” NHRA and AHRA races featured a series of elimination rounds until the two last remaining cars faced off in the final. A typical match race, on the other hand, involved two headliners who were usually paid a flat fee to race heads-up in a best two-out-of-three format. On special occasions, to boost attendance, promoters would put together a “Chicago-style” show with four racers competing on a round-robin basis. And that’s exactly what drew 5000 fans to Yellow River Drag Strip in Covington, Georgia, on Sunday, March 2, 1969.


The Onenota Star


The attraction was a quartet of[Funny Cars](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/the-serious-business-of-the-funny-car-engine-wars/) featuring blown, big-block, nitro-gulping engines in tube-frame chassis covered by fiberglass, flip-top bodies that resembled muscle cars of the era. Good ones blasted through the quarter-mile in 8 seconds while going out the back door at more than 180 miles per hour. They weren’t quite as fast as Top Fuel slingshots, but they were more squirrelly going down the track—and more popular with the fans. This made them naturals for the match-race circuit, where spectators were eager to root for their Mopar, Ford, and GM faves.


The three out-of-town invitees at Yellow River were Arnie Beswick in the Tameless Tiger Pontiac GTO, Malcolm Durham in the Strip Blazer Corvair, and Frank Oglesby in a Cougar owned by “Dyno Don” Nicholson, who’d recently switched to Pro Stock after deciding that Funny Cars were too hairy for his taste. The local stud was Georgian Huston Platt, who campaigned his Camaro as the Dixie Twister. The chassis came from the Logghe Stamping Company, a top Funny Car builder of the day. Although Chrysler Hemis were the engines of choice in this period, Platt’s mill was a Chevy V-8 bored and stroked to 498 cubic inches and topped by a GMC 6-71 supercharger.

The drag strip consisted of a narrow, two-lane furrow of asphalt cut through a woody Georgia hollow that now houses a mobile home tract about 35 miles east of Atlanta. Although there were chain-link fences and guardrails near the start line, nothing separated the spectators from the rest of the track other than shallow dirt berms. The shutdown area was particularly iffy, with a curve right in the middle. The conditions weren’t ideal, to put it mildly, but they also weren’t especially dubious by the standards of the day, which were, admittedly, not very high. As Platt said later, “There wasn’t a track in 10 states that was qualified to run those cars back then.”


By mid-afternoon, the crowd was liquored-up and rowdy. Several PA announcements requesting that spectators move away from the track were ignored. At 2:20 p.m., Platt staged in the right lane for his run against Oglesby. In front of him, the drag strip was flanked by two unbroken lines of people stretching to the finish lights. Platt launched cleanly. But about 1000 feet into the run, he heard a loud bang. He immediately popped his parachute. Although there is a brief clip of amateur video of the wreck, it’s too blurry to show exactly what happened. According to drag racing lore, a foolhardy fan moved toward the track to grab a beer can and was swallowed up by the billowing chute. For whatever reason, the Dixie Twister angled to the right and arced over the dirt berm like a crowd-surfing rock star and thudded into a tightly packed mass of humanity. Contemporary reports say the car was going 150 mph when it hit.

In and of itself, the crash wasn’t serious. If there had been a decent barrier in place, Platt would have walked away unscathed—which, in fact, he did. But because there was no spectator protection, the results were cataclysmic. Eleven spectators died at the scene, and a twelfth died later in the hospital. The youngest was 3 years old, the oldest 65. The rest were in their teens and 20s. More than 40 people were injured.


This was the worst tragedy in the history of American motorsports.


Yet behind this simple, declarative sentence is a long, complicated story. Although there was plenty of regional coverage of the accident, the Yellow River catastrophe wasn’t a big story in the rest of the country. Today, amazingly, it’s almost completely forgotten. Even drag racers who were active at the time have only a hazy recollection that something really ugly occurred on a really sketchy drag strip somewhere far, far away. This is a comment less on the fallibility of memory than the barbaric safety record of drag racing in the 1960s. Sure, 12 dead and 40-plus injured in a single incident are appalling numbers. But this was an era when the drivers of Top Fuel rails and Funny Cars were killed as regularly as Social Security checks were cut. To understand why carnage was so commonplace requires looking back at the roots of the sport.
