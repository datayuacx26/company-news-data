# 183-MPH Land Speed Lincoln Mark VIII Is A Sub-$20K Dream Come True

Time is running out on telling firsthand stories behind Lincoln’s Mark VIII luxury sports coupe, but occasionally we get to do just that. From its indium-plated grille to that aluminum differential housing, the 1993–98 Mark VIII’s flagship status was the leading edge[of a profitable knife](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/ford-was-interesting-before-globalization-made-its-mark/) wielded by Ford back in the early 1990s. Its 32-valve engine ensured future successes with Cobras and Coyotes, but there are whispers of Ford engineers blasting down I-94 in prototype Mark VIIIs at illegal speeds, shocking fellow motorists with almond-thin HID headlights shooting a previously unseen form of daylight down the road. It’s fair to say that engineers in the Mark VIII’s orbit are rock stars in their own right, one even earned[a feature story](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/homegrown-the-v-8-dream-machines-of-a-retired-big-three-engineer/) for our Homegrown series. But most Mark VIIIs have a short lifespan on this planet, often used and forgotten while BMWs, Cadillacs and Mercedes-Benzes elevate to the highest ranks in automotive fandom.


Thankfully one special prototype survived the Mark VIII’s faded glory, likely because it highlights a skunkworks effort that led to a motorsport victory. Don’t let the mocha-hued paint fool you, this sedate looking coupe hit 182.694 mph on the Bonneville Salt Flats, setting a class record during the 1992 Southern California Timing Association’s World Finals. And it sold online this week for[$18,323](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/1993-lincoln-mark-viii-12/?utm_source=Iterable&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=Make_Update) .


*(The current D/Production record was set in 2008 at 225.366, but I have not been able to determine when exactly the Mark VIII was de-throned.)*

It beat the 163 mph record set by the Infiniti Q45, but the truth isn’t so simple: This “Land Speed” Mark VIII isn’t purely a stock car. Modifications were likely cheap and easy for your average race mechanic, starting with the mandatory roll cage and fire suppression safety bits.


But Ford engineers also blueprinted this Modular V-8 engine, and swapped the standard 3.08 ring and pinion to a disturbingly tall 2.47 unit found in Malaise Era Fox-bodies and Ford trucks. The latter helped the Mark VIII achieve its winning speed in third gear, which is a 1-to-1 direct drive for maximum power output to the wheels. Catalytic converters were removed, as the technology to keep a vehicle with 290-ish horsepower from being restricted by pollution controls had yet to exist.


The Mark VIII’s self-lowering air suspension ensured the slippery Lincoln had a drag coefficient of 0.33 in factory guise, but Ford engineers had a trick up their sleeve: Modify the height sensors for more travel, ensuring the Land Speed Mark dropped a full two inches when desired. That much drop may not have reduced drag, but it wiped away significant frontal area from the 16-inch alloy wheels, now shod in Corvette-worthy Goodyear Eagle GS-C tires needed to safely reach speeds above 180 mph. That was the goal, and Ford Engineer Jerry Wroblewski details everything in a recollection posted on[MarkVIII.org](https://www.markviii.org/LOD2/bonneville.htm) .

While Wroblewski’s name doesn’t quite have Carroll Shelby cachet, modern day hot rodders owe this man a debt of gratitude. His work with cars like the Mark VIII helped unlock engine computers for aftermarket tuning, founding SCT performance in the process. Yes, *that[SCT](https://sctflash.com/) .*


Aside from OBD-II programmers and old school performance chip burning, Wroblewski[wrote an essay](https://www.tccoa.com/threads/ford-overdrive-transmissions-101.190929/?post_id=2070263) on this car’s transmission (4R70-W) to improve known fail points. Not only did he ensure Fords shift like a dual-clutch transmission on a modern performance car, he was beyond generous with his time. The community did not forget Wroblewski’s generosity, as his open sourcing led to the aforementioned transmission tweaks being dubbed the “J-Mod.”


Be it in person or on the burgeoning car forums of the late 1990s, Wroblewski did a fantastic job ensuring modified Mustangs beat their competition while happily idling in traffic like a stock example. His dedication to powertrain software ensured third parties provided superchargers with exceptional performance, and automatic transmissions snapped to attention well before the ubiquitous “sport” mode on modern cars. But the Land Speed Mark VIII is more than just the sum of Wroblewski and his skunkworks’ efforts in Dearborn. The deeper chin spoiler and smooth undercarriage ensured a shockingly competent machine got its just deserts, even without a prestigious badge like AMG to lift its spirits (and valuations).

While we noted a 76% increase values[from 2020–25](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/hagerty-insider/1990s-luxury-coupes-are-sweet-treats-getting-their-just-desserts/#:~:text=Lincoln-,76%25%20increase,-The%20Lincoln%20Mark) , Mark VIII asking prices are still shockingly low considering its 32-valve character is shared with premium nameplates from around the world. These cars are still very much flying under the radar relative to other ’90s cars with similar ingredients.


Which is why I wasn’t particularly surprised to see this Land Speed Hot Rod Lincoln with 183 (rounded up) MPH credentials sell for just $17,450 ($18,323 with buyer’s premium), though it did at least appreciate since its[$12,650](https://www.mecum.com/lots/326589/1993-lincoln-mark-viii-prototype/) sale price at Mecum Indy back in 2018. Lincoln just isn’t the brand we associate with high-speed accomplishments, especially when the 1993 Mark VIII’s minimalist styling took it to dangerously aggressive places. The softer styling (and Town Car like tuning) of the later 1997–98 Mark VIIIs was not only better for the brand, but for the Mark VIII’s prospects on the classic car market: A museum worthy black-on-black LSC from 1998 is one of the most expensive transactions to date,[ringing up $43,000](https://bringatrailer.com/listing/1998-lincoln-mark-viii-lsc-6/) on Bring A Trailer.


As a Mark VIII owner that recently celebrated my car’s 30th Birthday with a[massive road trip](https://www.hagerty.com/media/great-reads/i-celebrated-my-30th-birthday-on-a-lemons-rally/) , I know the pitfalls of long term ownership. I fear to tally my receipts, but they add up to a daily driven Mark VIII with over 200,000 miles that is still rock solid on an open highway. My loyalty is likely impenetrable, then, but I am not alone. There was a potential bidding war on this week’s sale of the speed record car, between two loyalists seeking to add the Land Speed Mark to their collection. In one of the corners was[Brian Herron](https://herron4sema.com/about-me-2/#:~:text=Tasca%20prototype%20Mark%20VIII) , looking for the perfect compliment to his supercharged Mark VIII prototype (One day we shall discuss this precursor to the 2003-04 Terminator Cobra, as it’s known as the “Tasca Mark VIII” because of its affiliation with none other than[Bob Tasca](https://www.hagerty.com/media/motorsports/bob-tasca-the-fastest-man-in-drag-racing-says-he-can-go-even-faster/) ).


But Herron admitted he missed his chance, and my friend *JP Driller* got it instead. He’s been in the Mark VIII game longer than me, he goes way faster in the quarter mile, and posted on BaT that adding this car to his collection is “\[b\]eyond my dreams.” Which is a nice sentiment, but I wanted to get more for our readers. I reached out, and he suggested he needs to actually take delivery before making a formal statement. But he left me with the following:


> “There’s an inspirational piece of wall art in my house that I think sums it up best: *Dreams are whispers from the soul.* ”


And that makes sense. This car is far from a household name, but all Mark VIII nerds know it on reputation alone. That’s in part to the long reach of the Internet and contributors like Jerry Wroblewski, but finding this footage recently uploaded to YouTube is another unearthed delight.


Perhaps on a long enough timeline, dreams do indeed come true. Knowing that the Land Speed Mark VIII went to the right home is also good for our souls, so *Driller’s* poster might be more prophetic than it sounds.
