# 11 Tools Every DIYer Has to Have

Fact: it takes tools to work on cars. Even if you were He-Man incarnate under a Hudson, you aren’t getting a drain plug out with your bare hands. Things just don’t work that way. So if maintaining your car yourself is something you’re interested in, you are going to need tools. A bunch of them.


But not all of them. At least not at first. The vast majority of service and maintenance on vintage cars can be done with a relatively basic set of tools. In fact, this list is likely enough to cover everything you need for basic jobs at home, and also serve as a good base for a travel toolkit for when a longer adventure beckons. No matter what may break, need to be restored or adjusted, here are 11 tools that every do-it-yourself car enthusiast should have on hand.


## Big hammer


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Let’s get this out of the way before you jump straight to the comments section. Percussive force is something that, whether we like to or not, often needs to be leveraged in order to break free stuck parts. Corrosion is indiscriminate, and time allows it to run wild underneath a car. A little rust on the taper of a stuck ball joint? Big hammer will get that free. Brake shoes stuck to the drum? Yep, big hammer will help. Starter on the fritz? Surprisingly, big hammer might be your answer there too. It shouldn’t be the first thing you grab, but big hammer will all too often be the last thing you grab.


## Multimeter


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


For every two problems solved by the big hammer, I’ve found at least one that required the delicacy of a multimeter because, try as I might, I still haven’t found a consistent way to measure voltage with a hammer. I’ve confirmed that a wire had voltage with a hammer, but sparks are something I try to avoid these days. But seriously, electrical problems will appear as cars age, and it is best to be prepared by having a[means of measuring](https://www.hagerty.com/media/maintenance-and-tech/how-to-use-a-multimeter/) volts, ohms, and amps on hand to deal with it.


## Sockets


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


There exist a variety of ways to attach the multitude of parts that compose a car. Threaded hardware is one of the most popular, and that means needing to turn a variety of hex-headed hardware. A set of six-point sockets is likely ideal, but 12-point versions can be more useful in edge case scenarios despite having a higher chance of rounding off bolts and creating headaches when working with stuck hardware. Most affordable tool kits cover most of the sockets needed for regular repair tasks, but in due time anyone who escalates their DIY habit will end up with a plethora of sockets in multiple measurement standards.


## Ratchet (bonus points for quality)


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Those sockets are likely round and smooth on the outside, meaning they are not going to help all by themselves. No, to turn sockets requires an outside lever, and while breaker bars are great, a ratchet is ideal. There are two ways to think about the purchase of something so essential to your toolkit: Either grab one off the shelf that is convenient and easily returned or warrantied, or go to the tool truck and pay up to likely never have an issue. Professional tools often bear a professional price, though. As someone who followed that second path a few years ago, I still love the feel of my Snap-On ratchets, but it took a long time for me to justify the price even to myself. A ratchet that works is plenty. One you enjoy using is luxury.


## Screwdrivers


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Nuts and bolts tend to only cover the larger parts of a car; once you’re working on the interior or smaller assemblies, the need for screwdrivers will appear. Carburetor rebuilds and interior panels often require careful dealings with Phillips-head screws or JIS if you’re more the metric type. The right screwdriver is harder to pick than the right socket, but taking the time to use the right tool will help prevent headaches later. Ask anyone who has had to drill out stripped hardware if it took less time than walking to the toolbox to grab the screwdriver with the correct-width head compared to using at least three more tools and being forced to make a repair.


## Box-end wrenches


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


For all the convenience provided by a ratchet and sockets, the form factor of the pair can be a bit tough in confined spaces like an engine compartment. That’s especially true on late-model cars. Box-end wrenches have a closed loop on one end and an open end on the other, both specifically sized to fit certain sizes of nuts and bolts. You can find these in fixed-head and ratcheting versions, and while the latter are very helpful, it’s best to start with an old-school set, as they offer more utility in most situations.


## Torque wrench


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Bolt tension is a funny thing. When we didn’t know better, it was good enough that a bolt was tight. Then we learned about how there are differing levels of tight, including too tight. While I am no stranger to just tightening hardware to “mechanic’s elbow,” a torque wrench is something I have learned to embrace, especially once I reached the point that I have kept multiple of my projects long enough to be back in and servicing my own previous work. I fight hardware less when things are properly torqued, and I also find it easier to relax while driving knowing that things are properly assembled. Even if this is only for putting your wheels back on, get a torque wrench and get familiar with using it.


## Slip-joint pliers


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Sometimes referred to as water-pump pliers, a big set of slip-joint pliers can be something that feels sent from above when there is simply nothing else in the toolbox to get the job done. These will hold the backside of something, or get leverage to pull another thing loose, or grab hold of a piece to help unseize parts that should be spinning, or hold pieces that are spinning and need to be removed—like a water pump pulley. These are sometimes the only solution, but be wary of reaching for them in the wrong situations, as slip-joint pliers do have the ability to round off hardware or crush things if used carelessly.


## Vise


A sturdy vise is mandatory for any workbench.


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Holding parts or sub-assemblies while cleaning or repairing can be awkward, especially if you need to put some force into the part. That’s where a vise comes in. Not to be confused with the cold ones that come at the end of a job, a vise is best embraced when it’s firmly mounted to a sturdy workbench. In a pinch, a vise can be a press to push bearings or bushings into or out of place, along with being perfect for holding things that need to be drilled or heated in a way that makes hand-holding them a bad idea.


## Flashlight


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


Human eyes are pretty impressive when it comes to seeing in low light or odd angles, but we all know they are not flawless and age does nothing to help. A compact, bright, and durable flashlight cuts down on attempts to learn automotive Braille by just feeling for hardware in the nooks and crannies of a car. Seeing is believing, and light helps you work better. A headlamp is another option, but that is a matter of personal choice.


## Catch pan


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


One of the first DIY jobs a lot of folks tackle is fluid swaps and flushes in their car, and that is indeed a great place to start. In order to perform good clean fluid changes, one must have something to catch and contain the fluids being drained. While some of us have managed to drain 5 quarts out of a car directly into the narrow opening of a gallon jug, life is far easier with a catch pan. Multiple types exist and most work largely the same. Be careful not to leave a catch tray full of used fluid on the floor or under a car as it’s easy to spill and create a hazard. Of course, always dispose of used fluid properly.


The more projects you do, the more tools you will likely need. Whether that is a need or a “need” becomes moot if you find you derive value from completing those projects. Have one you think should be added to this list of necessities? Tell us about it in a comment below.
