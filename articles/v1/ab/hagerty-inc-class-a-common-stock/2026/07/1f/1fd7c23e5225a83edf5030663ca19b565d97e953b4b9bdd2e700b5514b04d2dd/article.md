# Our Two Cents: The Cars We’d Carve on Mt. Rushmore

It was a fun thought exercise: If there were a car version of Mt. Rushmore, what cars would we put on there? The idea came from the inimitable mind of Stefan Lombard, who’s always got something witty or thought-provoking up his sleeve. We scratched our heads and gave some thought to what cars deserve commemoration for guiding or dramatically changing the American automotive landscape. While we mostly agreed on the early eras, our takes on later models began to diverge.


What became clear, though, is that there are plenty of great American models worth considering. Plenty of cars that didn’t make our mountains have viable cases—the[Pontiac GTO](https://www.hagerty.com/media/member-stories/from-peking-to-paris-in-a-pontiac-gto/) and[Chrysler’s minivans](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/hagerty-magazine/the-van-that-saved-chrysler-and-changed-the-way-america-hauled/) , for instance. Which cars would you carve into your automotive version of Mt. Rushmore? Let us know in the comments below.

## Looking Back, Looking Forward


Gemini


If we’re commemorating significance in the American automotive industry, the Ford Model T would be the obvious George Washington of the group. One could argue the ’32 Ford’s available flathead expanded V-8 ubiquity like Jefferson’s Louisiana Purchase, but in that spot, I’d instead put the ’55 Chevy and the introduction of its venerable[small-block](https://www.hagerty.com/media/magazine-features/chevy-small-block-the-little-engine-that-did/) . I think Sajeev’s right to include a truck, but I am going to go with the 1980 introduction of the seventh-generation Ford F-Series. The prior-gen trucks were a big step forward in terms of creature comforts and adding things like front disc brakes, but the seventh-gen was a more complete step toward the modern truck. And finally, taking us into a new era whether we like it or not (and I don’t, but I accept that it was a game-changer—Jason Cammisa does a good job sharing why[in this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IwnJzP0TlCk) ), the Tesla Model S. — *Eddy Eckart*


## Proof-of-Concept and Utility


Gemini


For me, this is really tough. I know the cars that would go on my own Mount Rushmore, but I think this is about a national monument, not a personal one. So let’s start at the oldest: 1901 Olds Curved Dash. No, it didn’t reach anywhere near the sales numbers of the Ford Model T, but it proved the idea had legs. Next up is the Willys MB. You don’t help win a World War and not get chiseled into the mountain. It’s an inarguable icon. Next, I’m split between a big-block ’67 Corvette or a Tri-Five Chevy. Both capture some of the highlights from their eras and have proved timeless. I’ll stick to the Tri-Five just for relatability. Last, I agree with Eddy that the F-150 belongs on the Mount. Which generation is something I’m not smart enough to pick, but one of them for sure. — *Kyle Smith*


## There’s Always Room for One More


Gemini


So many directions you can go… I’ll start with the 1965 Ford Mustang because it was modern-day proof of cheap fun. And I think a Jeep needs to be on there, whether it’s a WWII-era model or something newer. It’s pretty tough to ignore the Ford Model T, and I agree that you’d need something electric that points the way to the (inevitable) future, and I’d pick the Tesla Roadster because it was the first Tesla I drove (and also the last). And Since President Trump seems convinced that there is just enough rock left for a fifth likeness, I guess I need to add a fifth entry just in case: I can’t decide between a 1953 Chevrolet Corvette, and what was perhaps the first true stylish but heavy-duty pickup, the 1939 Hudson Big Boy, because I likely would have been the peculiar guy down the street who had a pickup truck as his daily driver (thus becoming, I suspect, the most popular guy in the neighborhood when somebody needed help hauling something). — *Steven Cole Smith*


## Nate Chooses Ones that Push the Envelope


Gemini


I think one of the most wonderful things about the American automotive landscape is how free companies are to push the boundaries. This nation has produced more moonshots than most, at least in the last 250 years. So let’s salute that by nominating a few cars that really pushed the envelope of what is/was possible. First, a Cord 810. Not the first front-wheel-drive car, nor the first American FWD car, but the first car that packaged the drivetrain in an absolutely stunning way. Next, a Tucker 48. Gorgeous, visionary for the time, and reminiscent of a post-war era that was brimming with optimism. Hard to talk about pushing the envelope without mentioning Tesla, so I’d nominate a Model S. Sure, the Roadster was a bigger push from an engineering standpoint, but the Model S was the first EV that truly overcame the challenge of, “yes, you can build a few, but can you scale that up?” Finally, I’d go with a Duesenberg Model SJ, a car that redefined what the term “great automobile” could actually mean. Also, just flat-out gorgeous.


Is the list a little disparate? Absolutely. But variety is the spice of life and central to what makes our country so great, so I’d argue that this group fits on multiple levels. — *Nate Petroelje*
