# Celebrating the Unexceptional with a Volvo 144E

Once a year, the grounds of the very grand Grimsthorpe Castle in Lincolnshire are taken over by a car show like no other.


The[Hagerty Festival of the Unexceptional](https://www.hagerty.co.uk/official-events/hagerty-festival-of-the-unexceptional/) offers a chance for the most mundane vehicles to have their day in the sun. The most recent event, held July 25, was no different, and the humble heroes of day-to-day driving from 1971 to 2001 got all the glory. Sports and supercars were banished to a distant car park, and the judges of the Concours de l’Ordinaire awarded prizes based on the heartwarming stories of the entrants as much (if not more) than the condition of their cars.


For enthusiasts of everyday motoring, FOTU is an opportunity to celebrate the cars that took them to school and the shops, that their parents or grandparents would drive to work. These machines bring back memories of family holidays with only I-Spy for in-car entertainment, sitting sweltering in vinyl or drab cloth seats with no air conditioning for comfort.


Many of the stars of the show would be unfamiliar to Americans, who never would have encountered a Talbot Tagora, Skoda Estelle, or Vauxhall Cavalier, yet with so few examples surviving on British roads, they get more love than any Lamborghini.

The crowd skews young, with many picking bland over brand for their first cars and investing time and money to keep them on the road well beyond their resale values.


It’s a big crowd, too, with 2500 unexceptional automobiles on display and 50 selected for entry to the concours. More than 4500 people spent the day bantering about the boring, ogling the ordinary.


In previous years, I’ve sheepishly entered the castle grounds in my Lotus Esprit and a Caterham Seven and parked well out of sight, but this year was different.

This year, I arrived in true unexceptional style behind the wheel of a 1974 Volvo 144E—a sturdy Swede that ferried so many families for decades. Although more than a million were made between 1968 and 1974, this is the only surviving 144E of just 90-odd 144s in total left in the U.K.


It’s a classic example of three-box design, and its unapologetic squareness was quite a departure from the soft, rounded lines of the Amazon that came before. There’s a real solidity to it that underlined Volvo’s commitment to safety.


Its oatmeal paintwork is immaculate, but whoever originally specified this car certainly didn’t play it safe with the interior. The vivid red velour is like something from a 1970s boudoir.

Voluptuous color aside, the cabin is a lesson in functionality. Instrumentation is basic and distinct, controls are chunky, clearly labeled, and operate with a reassuring click.


The driving position is a little twisted, with the pedals offset to the right, but having sunk into the plush driver’s seat it’s not really an issue.


It’s around 100 miles from my house to the castle, and it was clear very early I wouldn’t be getting there in a hurry. The 144’s 2-liter engine is capable of 128 horsepower, but it’s not especially enthusiastic about delivering it. The gearing is quite short, which does help keep things going along, but high revs make for more noise than movement.


The bulk of the trip was on A-roads and motorways, and I settled into an easy 65 mph cruise. Having dug through the garage to find a bootleg cassette of The Police’s 1983 *Synchronicity* Tour, I had been looking forward to a soundtrack that would take me back to my teens, but sadly the Blaupunkt tape deck wasn’t working.


On to more entertaining country roads and the 144 rolled and listed left and right through the turns. On long radius bends, the steering got progressively heavier and heavier, the car actively discouraging me from pressing on. Hats off to any parent, late for the school run, who had to hustle a 144 to get to the gates in time.


By the time I arrived at Grimsthorpe, there were thousands of cars already in place. I drove past row after row of humdrum automobiles, and the 144 got its share of the spotlight, with plenty of people taking snapshots.


As it’s owned by Volvo’s Heritage department, this 144 wasn’t eligible for competition at FOTU, so I parked between a Renault 5 and a Ferrari 400i (someone didn’t quite get the memo) and headed off to explore the ordinary.


In the courtyard of the castle were the Concours de l’Ordinaire cars—Nissans that nobody would normally care about and Fords that wouldn’t get a fleeting glimpse at any other event. But the panel of judges took great care to examine each and every one before they managed to whittle them down to three.

In third place was a 2001 Ford Focus, currently owned by Jake Seddon, having been handed down from his grandfather to father and then to him. The runner up prize went to a 1992 Toyota Previa, a family hauler preserved exactly as its owner, Mat Swinn, found it. He even went so far as framing the chocolate wrappers and other detritus he discovered under the seats. Finally, the recipient of the gleaming concours cup was a 2001 Volkswagen Polo E. It was completely unremarkable aside from the fact that it is Gordon McNeill’s first and only car, purchased 20 years ago. It now shows 176,000 miles, and McNeill used at his wedding, has lapped the Nürburgring in it, and generally just drives it day in, day out to get to work.


“My plan is to keep driving it as my daily driver indefinitely,” he says. Now that’s an exceptional commitment to an unexceptional car.
