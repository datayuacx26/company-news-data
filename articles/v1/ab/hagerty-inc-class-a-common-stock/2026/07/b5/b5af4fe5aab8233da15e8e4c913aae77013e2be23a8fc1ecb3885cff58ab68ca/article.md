# Picture Car Confidential #43: Le Showdown

I watched a lot of TV when I was a kid. And not unlike today’s parents decrying their young’uns’ addictions to TikTok and smartphones, my parents—in particular, my father—were very disapproving of the many hours my two sisters and I passed in front of the boob tube. Said judgement would be the subject of much household mirth when he turned in his freelance satirist card to become a full-time television critic. I was 11 then, and suddenly gone were days like the one when he belittled me, aged 7, for excitedly telling him at the dinner table one evening that a lady won a Triumph Spitfire on *Let’s Make a Deal* .


“Is that how you spend your time on a beautiful summer day?” the man who once hated television inquired disapprovingly. Things changed with his new job. I remember one trip home from college, him asking me with a straight face what I thought of the new “angel” on *Charlie’s Angels* .


No earthly idea, dude, I had to tell him.


Those were the days when a no-TV policy was an intellectually defensible, virtue-signaling life choice. Today, they teach graduate courses on *Baywatch* and *The Simpsons* and people will look at you like you’re crazy should you say you don’t do streaming, YouTube, or any other televisual material. *Of course you do. Who doesn’t?* I watch stuff and am no longer ashamed. The culture gives you what it gives you. Just like I don’t blame myself for interacting with AI chatbots. You may try to resist, but what choice do you really have?


My childhood penchant for TV viewing came to mind the other week when I joined the crew from *[The Autopian](http://www.theautopian.com/)* for their reader event at Lime Rock Park, the idyllic racetrack in Salisbury, Connecticut. Why? One of my favorite things when my younger self resided in TV Land was how the cast of one show might magically appear on another show, an ingenious cross-promotional technique that somehow reinforced the reality of a faraway TV world in which everybody actually knew each other. For instance, when the cast of *Green Acres* folks turned up to rub shoulders with the denizens of Hooterville on *Petticoat Junction* and *The Beverly Hillbillies* it amused me endlessly.


In fact, many automotive journalists know each other. It’s not a very big pool. But as a fan, when I got invited to join the website founders Jason Torchinsky and David Tracy, along with their publisher, Matt Hardigree, at the track with several dozen of their readers and their cars, I accepted in a heartbeat. The moment seemed only slightly less momentous than when Samantha and Darrin of *Bewitched* famously dropped in on *The Flintstones* .


But what really pushed me over the top was learning that Torchinsky, a longtime friend of the automotive friendless, was towing to Lime Rock, from North Carolina, a Citroën 2CV. And perhaps they’d take some hot—I mean lukewarm —scratch that, I mean, unbelievably tepid—laps around the autocross course while he plumbed the outer limits of the 2CV’s 29-hp performance envelope at speeds a fast bicyclist might realistically aspire to. With lurid demonstrations of world-beating body roll at every corner (and a reader-supplied grandfather clock sticking out of the full-length canvas sunroof).


Jamie Kitman


I’ve driven 2CVs, always admired them, and once came close to buying a very old one. A few years back, however, for a series set in Paris but meant to be filmed in New York, my picture car outfit, Octane Film Cars, opted instead for a Renault 4 procured through a trusted Dutch trader, Ward Haagenar. A 1973 model that if you get real close looks like it was hand-finished with the kind of brush you’d use to paint a barn, it runs great (generally), an adjunct of having only about 37,000 miles on it from new when we first got it. In the end, sadly, the show decided to film in France. So much for getting ahead of the plans of others—we were stuck with the R4. And I’m glad for it.


[Ronan Glon](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/rglon/)


Independent of my enthusiasm for the 2CV’s distinctive looks, its unique mission and driving experience, I believe these have for most American enthusiasts obscured the merit of the R4. Launched in 1959, the new Renault came years after the 2CV’s 1948 arrival, but it was clearly a response to it. The small, low-priced Citroën which remained in production for 42 years had become synonymous with La France. Renault’s belated answer, which ran from 1961 to 1994, was to build the R4, a better car. Renault sold more of them (8,134,424) than Citroën sold 2CVs, of which under 4 million shifted over a longer period of time. (Though if one includes all 2CV variants, including the trucks, Dyane, Ami, and Mehari, its numbers are remarkably close.)


Stefan Lombard


How much better was the R4? This we hoped to find out. To put its real-world usability advantage in the win column, I planned to drive one the roughly 200 miles from my home to Lime Rock and back in 90-degree heat. Setting out, the car which had not once let me down—well, hardly—chose this inopportune moment to stop running two blocks from my home. When I phoned French car maestro Fred Gilbert (mentioned previously in this series) for advice on how without tools I might explore the gas smell that I’d noted along with an utter unwillingness to run, he happened by rare chance to be passing through my town with tools in his car. He identified the culprit as a sticky plastic carburetor float. Fifty-three years had caused its pivot points to swell. A fix was affected with a file and switch blade and half an hour later I was on my way with no further incident.


Jamie Kitman


Jamie Kitman


The mighty 35 horsepower Renault coaxes from the R4’s 845-cc inline-four make it more accelerative despite its higher 1455-pound curb weight, with zero-60 taking a mere 22.7 seconds, nine seconds faster than the 2CV. Our speedometer nudged an indicated 75 mph (downhill), with water-cooling making the Renault engine quieter than the 2CV, a bonus compounded by reduced wind noise.


Its body-on-frame construction also seemed a model of rigidity next to the Citroën’s setup—easily replaced panels, attached to a steel frame unstressed but kind of floppy. The sound of the R4 doors closing and the noises its body doesn’t make when headed down the road moves the vibe that important bit upscale, as does a torsion bar suspension, which rides just as comfortably as Citroën’s coil-sprung effort, interconnected front-to-rear by steel rods to limit pitch. Both cars are front-wheel drive, though the Renault has its transmission in front of the engine and the Citroën’s behind it, their wacky shift linkages completely different sights to behold under their hoods, but both terminating in shift levers that emerge from high up the firewall and not the floor. All four of the Renault’s windows slide forward to open versus the Citroën’s unusual flip-up jobs, which flip up in front, with fixed windows to the rear. There is that full-length sunroof, in favor on days when it is neither raining, too sunny, or cold.


Jamie Kitman


Summing up: the Renault is faster, quieter, substantially more rigid with excellent ride, superior handling, and quicker steering imparting a sportier feel. Because automotive affection needn’t be grounded in hard fact, I’m not going to say the R4 is a better car, though the case could be made. And just in case it mattered, I challenged Torchinsky to a drag race: a not unexpected victory for the R4. To my surprise, the Renault even chirped its front wheels. No one was surprised. In fact, no one cared. The Citroën chirped its wheels, too.


Though the 2CV has garnered more attention historically, the R4, which won the race, was no slouch. They both deserve awards in automotive history’s Best Supporting Actor category.
