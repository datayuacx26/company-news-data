# Behind the Daunting Preparation for the 2026 Mille Miglia

Completing the Mille Miglia is an act of extreme endurance. 1000 miles on north and central Italian roads, all open to the public, it used to be a race that saw competitors hit speeds of over 190 mph. The record running was a famously improbable 10 hours, 7 minutes, and 48 seconds set by Stirling Moss and Denis Jenkinson in 1955, averaging more than 97 mph. Two years later, following a deadly crash in Guidizzolo when the[Ferrari 335S driven by Alfonso de Portago](https://www.hagerty.com/media/entertainment/stunt-director-robert-nagle-made-ferrari-feel-real-alive/) blew a tire and cartwheeled into spectators, the race was cancelled amid a massive public outcry and all motor racing on public roads was banned by the Italian government. In 1977, the race saw its second life start as the Mille Miglia Storico, a regularity race featuring cars of the exact make and model that competed in the original races. Today, it’s known simply by most as the 1000 Miglia, and has earned its place as one of the world’s most important historic motoring events.


John Mayhead


It’s still an endurance event, and in some ways more so than the original. Now roughly 1200 miles, the route is completed in five days and varies each year but retains the spirit of the original. After starting in Brescia, this year the competitors visited Padua before heading southwest across the Apennines to Montecatini Terme, through Rome, then back up to Rimini before crossing back in a figure eight to the finish back in Brescia. The route is navigated with a tulip-style logbook issued daily, without street names and with over 100 time trials along the way. Successful completion relies on extraordinary levels of concentration and communication between the navigator and driver with little more than an odometer and a trip computer to help.


One of the greatest challenges in the modern event is the selection process that often starts years before. First, the purchase of a qualifying car of the correct make, model, and year that must be inspected by the owner’s national historic automobile organization to ensure it is all original and correct, then issued with the all-important FIVA (Fédération Internationale des Véhicules Anciens) passport. Next, the owner must sign up on the 1000 Miglia Registro, send in paperwork and photographs, pay just over €900, and wait.

This is where the process becomes difficult. Questions are fired back and forth about the car: what’s been restored, is it an exact replica of the original, how can you prove this supercharger or that differential ratio was used in period? Once answered to the satisfaction of the organizers, there are two routes that ensure entry: those cars that took part in the original pre-1957 race, and the drivers who have won one of the warm-up events run by the 1000 Miglia team are both guaranteed starting places. The rest must apply and hope: there are roughly 1000 applicants every year for around 400 places, which includes those already guaranteed entry and the entries allocated to sponsors. Once that bar is cleared, then there are the costs, which can be significant. The standard, four-night package that includes hotels on the road, meals, mechanic passes and some gifts costs just under €16,000 including tax; the silver package, offering accommodation for two additional nights and uprated gifts, costs around €18,600; the gold package at around €22,000 offers six nights’ accommodation in two separate rooms. Then there’s shipping the car to Italy, the preparation of the car and the mechanical support on the road: although the organizers provide some, most opt for dedicated support. With flights, hotels before and after the event and other incidental costs, the total bill can easily reach €100,000 (about $114K as of this writing). Yet even after that investment, there is no guarantee of finishing; the physical toll on cars that are, in most cases, more than 70 years old is immense. To achieve that end, you need a team of superb, dedicated engineers and people with local knowledge combined with a sense of presence to overcome the inevitable Italian bureaucracy.


This is the point at which Andrea Rizza enters stage left. He has that presence: imagine the archetypal Roman gladiator: tall, chiseled face, dark hair, dark eyes. I met Andrea at Salon Privé, the preeminent British concours that takes place annually at Blenheim Palace near Oxford, where we’re both judges. He is head of Rizza Classic, the historic department of Carrozzeria Rizza, a family business formed in 1949 and today run by father Alberto and son Andrea, with his brothers Mirko and Daniele, that specializes in the restoration and maintenance of historic cars to an exceptional level. He’s also a pragmatist: He knows that cars need to be well prepared, regularly driven, and their drivers need time behind the wheel. If you want your car to start, and more importantly finish the Mille Miglia, Andrea is the guy to know. A few years ago, when contracted to support another company, he realized he could do it better than the rest.

At the same time, on the other side of the Atlantic, John Houghtaling and Franco Valobra were in search of a new challenge. From 1999 onwards, they’d been leading a thriving Ferrari racing scene in New Orleans that had grown into the Challenge Car Racing Championship (CCR), fielding races with up to 42 cars on the grid for twelve races per season, making them one of the biggest privateer Ferrari groups in the world.


John Mayhead


“When it started, we were just a group of guys with old Challenge Ferraris who wanted to race; we used to get the cops to shut the road, race up and down for a bit, then we’d stop and they’d let the traffic go,” Houghtaling told me. These early races, styled as the French Quarter Classic, started to gain a country-wide reputation and the social aspect grew; families were involved from the start. The word spread, and others were approached directly. John Herlihy bought a 360 Challenge, the first racing Ferrari he’d owned. “I asked him what he intended to do with it,” his wife, Julie told me. “He didn’t really have an answer.” Then Houghtaling rang, and Herlihy was in the club.


By 2016, it was a successful non-profit, having a $20M+ impact on local good causes, but Houghtaling and Valobra had their sights set on the Mille Miglia. They bought the ex-Walt Hansgen Jaguar XK120M, a car with a stack of competition history that should be a dead-certain entry to the 1000-mile Italian race, but they didn’t get a place: there were already enough XK120s on the list.

John Mayhead


“We’d spent all this money on the car and the entry,” Houghtaling told me. “We just couldn’t find a way around it.” Then, speaking to the Mille Miglia team one day, he was given a lifeline when they told him many places were reserved for the cars of sponsors. He rang Franco. “What would you say if I could get you entry to the Mille Miglia as a sponsor?” he asked. It was a simple question to answer. Their social media company[themanualshift.com](https://themanualshift.com/) was accepted as a sponsor by the Mille Miglia organizers with five entries per year included in the price. Taking one slot for themselves, they offered the others to their Ferrari-racing friends, with the suggestion that they source and prepare all five cars together to the same standard, with the attention to detail extending to period racing overalls and helmets. Ken Marlin was one of those who received the call. “John rang me and asked me whether I wanted to drive the Mille Miglia. I told him I had many questions, all under the heading of ‘yes’. My first was where do I wire the money?” John Herlihy shared the car with Ken, with Bruce Roch and his son Harrison in the third car, Chris Fountas and his friend Walter in the fourth, and two other friends of Houghtaling in the final Jaguar. Manual Shift Motorsports was founded.

Then, in 2018, the two automotive worlds collided. Houghtaling and Valobra met Andrea, and were impressed by his ability, as he was of them, finishing as the second-highest-placed U.S. team. By 2022, Rizza had taken over all the Italian administration, booking a week-long build-up event that mixed the best hotels and moonlit dinners next to the impossibly beautiful Lake Garda with top-grade competition preparation, both mechanical and bureaucratic. If there was a problem with the car or the entry paperwork, Andrea’s crew fixed it, fast.


John Mayhead


This year, Andrea invited me to Italy to be part of the week-long preparation for the race, and to watch the start. The Manual Shift crew are old hands now: I asked John Herlihy, now representing the pre-war crowd with his 1937 BMW 328 partnered by his wife Julie, how many 1000 Miglias he’d completed. “Seven… maybe eight,” he replied, and it is extraordinary that so many of the original lineup are still active: this year, the two Johns and Franco were again joined by Ken, Chris, and Bruce. Although Jaguars are still well represented in the expanded group, some of the group have moved back to their first love of Ferrari: Houghtaling drove his 121 LM Spyder Scaglietti, the car in which Piero Taruffi was leading in 1955 before the oil pump ruptured, Valobra an exquisite 1955 750 Monza and Ken Marlin in his 1952 212/225S Sport Vignale Berlinetta, one of just two built with the stiffer Tuboscocca competition chassis. This isn’t just an aesthetic choice, either: The 121LM and the 225S both competed in the race before 1957, so both are automatically eligible, and these cars all have a higher coefficient, the number allocated by the organisers for age and historic importance to the race that is used to shade the final score, than the XK120s.

But despite the experience within the group, the need for such a shakedown before the race is still obvious. Daniele Rizza, brother of Andrea, told me about the endurance upgrades to the cars—improved fuel and brake lines, uprated cooling and batteries, suspension set up for the bumpy Italian roads—and then I followed as he took Marlin’s Ferrari to a registration office to gain a necessary piece of paper that would have, undoubtedly, failed to materialize for lesser mortals. Later, I drove to an underground garage in the center of Brescia where Andrea and one of his mechanics were dismantling the rear differential of the BMW, watched with great concern by Herlihy. Soon, they identified the problem: the pinion had disintegrated through age, dropping five teeth into the sump of the rear axle. The whole lot needed to be rebuilt in 24 hours prior to scrutineering.


They made it, of course. A replacement crown and pinion was sourced from their workshop in Rome and driven up to Brescia overnight. Then, the car moved to the workshop of an old colleague, and the team set about stripping, cleaning and rebuilding the axle. The bearings were rebuilt, gaskets cut, and the new crown and pinion fitted, but even that came with its own complications as it had a slightly different gear ratio than the original, so Herlihy and Rizza’s team had to factor in how that affected speed and performance. Later, not far from Brescia at a Porsche Experience Centre, we watched as Chris Fountas calibrated his trip computer for the timed regularity (PC or *Prove Cronometrate* ) phases of the race, when cars must drive a set distance within a fraction of a second of the target time.


John Mayhead


The following day was Sunday, two days before the start of the race. In convoy, the cars left the grand Villa Cortine Palace hotel in the old town of Sirmione, the picturesque spit at the southern shore of Lake Garda, and made their way into Brescia, about 45 minutes distant. There, in a huge, modern expo center, the cars were inspected by the scrutineers and finally awarded their official race roundels showing their start number. That night, we returned to Sirmione for dinner and almost took over a restaurant, the group now having been swollen by more family and friends who had come to offer support. The following day, it was back again, this time for the official sealing parade when the cars are fitted with their traditional wire around the steering column fixed with a lead seal, the mark of a very special car. The stickers may disappear from a car over time; the lead seals very rarely do.


That night, the group moved to the Villa Biondelli in the Franciacorta wine region closer to Brescia. From here, it was a 30-minute drive to the Viale Venezia, a tree-lined road on the outskirts of the town that hosts the starting line. There, you gain the first real concept of how large the event is: not just the 430 entrant cars from pre-war Alfa Romeos and Mercedes-Benz to Porsche 356 and Fiats, but also the crowds: half of the roads in Brescia are seemingly shut, and the rest thronged with people and cars. The famous red arrow flags are ubiquitous, and a Mille Miglia association gets you everywhere: even with our lowly “MEDIA” sticker, we were given a police motorcycle escort when the traffic blocked our path.


John Mayhead


The cars line up in order of their race number, roughly by age, and that’s important: the first cars leave at 11:30 a.m., then depart in groups of three every minute thereafter. If you’re one of the later cars, you still have the same 11-hour journey to complete; it’s a long day, and the other days can be even longer.


We watched as the groups wished each other good luck, had photos taken with their cars, then finally climbed aboard. There was a sense I’ve only really felt one place before, at the forming-up area at the Goodwood Revival, where the noise of engines revving and the smell of high-octane gasoline is pervaded by a sense of nervous impatience. Then the time came: the cars were marshalled to a large digital clock, then released to the starting ramp, and set off on a journey that would last five days and 1200 miles. We watched them, first alongside in our car, then on the app that tracked them by GPS throughout the tour.


I’m delighted to say that every one of the Manual Shift group and their cars completed the race, unlike 45 other competitors who were forced to retire and three who failed to reach the start line. John and Julie Herlihy achieved a phenomenal 37th place, made all the more impressive by their coefficient score of 1.60, giving them a significant scoring disadvantage compared to the 1.80-coefficient cars that inevitably dominated the top of the leaderboard. Bruce Roch, this time partnered by his younger son Logan, secured 87th place, a massive achievement for a team in a Jaguar XK with a coefficient of 1.40, but for everyone, the experience was almost as important as the result. Notable was the strong family element of the Manual Shift group, and the younger generation were well represented: As well as Logan and Bruce, Alisia Houghtaling drove with her mother Yulia, and Gabe Hrib Senior and Junior doubled up.

Clips on social media of the Mille Miglia inevitably show the glitz of the pre-start events, the glamour of the routes winding through the most beautiful parts of Italy, and the thrills and spills of the race, but the reality is still a true endurance event. This year, the weather swapped between sweltering heat and rain on days that often lasted 15 hours for the competitors, much longer for the Rizza Classic team who, while the competitors ate or slept, were often checking over the cars, trying to address problems before they developed into something more serious. When things did go wrong, the two van loads of spare parts were essential, as was all their mechanical experience, although they proved preparation was everything: other than a differential problem suffered by Hrib’s Jaguar that needed managing until the finish, the rest of the group’s cars completed the event with no serious issues. That’s not to say they didn’t earn their places at the finish: midway through the race, Ken Marlin messaged me, giving me an idea of the reality. “I am hot, tired and sleep deprived,” he said. “My hands hurt from holding the skinny steering wheel and if not for my gloves they would be blistered. The ball of my left foot hurts from so much clutch work; my left butt cheek and left thigh are cramping from the same. I have blisters forming on my right foot from the heel/toe required… But we are having an adventure.”

It’s an addictive adventure, too. Next year, Marlin wants to try to break into the top 100 for the first time, and Houghtaling wants to double the size of the group to give more of the Manual Shift drivers the chance to take part. 2027 will be one hell of a good year to drive, too: it’s the 100th anniversary of the first race, 70 years since it was cancelled, and 50 since it was restored to its current form. Having been so close, I can totally understand why it is on so many people’s bucket lists, especially with a group of friends; this is gearhead nirvana. If I could do it, I would in a heartbeat. Blisters heal. Memories like this don’t fade.
