# Electric Cars? Nah, It’ll Never Happen!

In July 1995, a small group of motoring journalists flew to the French port city of La Rochelle, to “Drive the Future.” The trip was organized by Peugeot and Citroën, which were about to launch electrically driven versions of the Peugeot 106 and the Citroën AX on the largely uninterested world of motoring. As data editor (and notionally new technology editor), I was the most likely member of the BBC *Top Gear* magazine team to report on the experience.


Exciting stuff. The French were beating General Motors’ EV1 by a year. Citroën and Peugeot were hoping to impress us with “The Future of Motoring;” namely, turning kids’ battery-powered toy cars into the real thing. Cars with rechargeable Ni-Cad batteries with a range of just over 68 miles. (The following year’s GM EV1 initially used 24 lead-acid batteries and had a similar range to the French cars.)


Citroën


As there were just six cars for 12 journalists, we were paired up. I looked at the *Telegraph* motoring correspondent I chatted to on the plane and he nodded. Our car was the Citroën AX Electrique. The press pack info said the car had a 20kW (27-hp) motor powered by 20 SAFT nickel-cadmium monoblocs providing 12kWh. The batteries were under the rear seats and in the boot (trunk). The range was 75 to 100 km (40 to 60 miles). The performance wasn’t impressive: 8.3 seconds to 50 km/h (30 mph) with a top speed limited to 91 km/h (56 mph). But, as was pointed out by the Citroën PR guy, it had regenerative braking that recovered kinetic energy on lift-off, and that power was returned to the battery. A bit of a novelty tech in 1995.


Flickr/Perico001


We were given a map with two routes: one around the city ring road and the other driving in the city center. The idea was to demonstrate the Electrique’s ability as an urban commuter. In case of problems, we were given walkie-talkies.


My co-driver had chosen the ring road and set off foot hard down. And he left it there for the rest of his stint. I immediately took to studying the needle on the “charge remaining” dial and thus began to experience my first episode of range anxiety. We very quickly reached the car’s limited speed. Except for wind and road noise, and the Citroën 2CVs that overtook us, the silence was eerie. I sat there watching the charge needle move ever downward and fingering the send button on the walkie-talkie.


But then we missed the turn-off to the city center and had to go round the ring road again. By the time it was my turn to drive, the evil range-o-meter was showing 25%. My drive was stop-start city stuff, which was much more suited to the car. It felt like a normal automatic except for the regenerative braking, which was unexpected and rather fierce, so it was pretty much one-pedal driving all the way. No chance though of a traffic-light getaway. I’ve driven faster-accelerating fun-fair bumper cars. The ride itself was just like the combustion-engined cars, and indeed most French cars: very soft, with a good bit of body roll when cornering.


Citroën


As I parked at the garage start point with 10% range left (about 5 miles), we looked at each other, shook our heads and said, “Nah!”


I remember writing that the internal combustion engine was safe, as there had to be a miraculous leap in battery technology before the electric car became a practical reality, and that was way into the future, maybe 25 years or more—and might never happen.


But, of course, it did happen. By 2020, electric cars were everywhere. How’s that for forecasting?


Peugeot


Unlike the[GM EV1](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/gm-ev1-true-inside-story/) , which was discontinued and[nearly all leased cars crushed](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/general-motors-helps-enthusiasts-restore-a-derelict-ev1/) —see movie *Who Killed the Electric Car?* —all 325 Citroën AX electric cars continued running, and even today, 31 years since my trip to La Rochelle, you can occasionally spot the odd one or two AX Electriques running around the city. They’re considered niche classics, and cars in working condition can fetch around €6000 ($7000). Interestingly, the SAFT Ni-Cad batteries have an extraordinarily long life and are still made today.


Citroën


Peugeot


Peugeot optimistically projected sales of 100,000 for the electric 106, but sales proved elusive, and the cars had a production run of about 6000 by the time the company, er, pulled the plug.


I wonder why? The answer is easy. Gas and diesel versions of the 106 were cheaper and very popular, and range, of course, was not an issue. The Peugeot 106 Rallye was also a runaway success. As its name suggests, it was an FIA-approved competition rally car and, at just 1800 pounds, probably one of the first of the 1990s supermini hot hatches. Agile and exciting to drive, they really are highly collectible, today fetching up $20,000 for mint examples in England and Europe, though seeing one on U.S. roads would probably be like seeing, well, an electric 106…
