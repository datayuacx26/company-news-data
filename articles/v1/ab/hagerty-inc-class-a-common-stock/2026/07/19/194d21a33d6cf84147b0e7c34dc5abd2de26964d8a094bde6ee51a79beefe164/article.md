# 1968 Volvo 123GT: Safety, Fast

When Volvo introduced the 120[Amazon](https://www.hagerty.com/media/our-garage/project-sven-1-need-for-swede-volvo-amazon/) in 1959, it ushered in a new era. Not just for the Swedish carmaker, but for the whole automotive industry.


Front-seat passengers in the Amazon (and its PV544 sibling) were the first to buckle up with three-point seatbelts designed by[Nils Bohlin.](https://www.hagerty.com/media/news/volvos-three-point-seat-belt-just-turned-70/) In a move that’s estimated to have saved over one million lives since, Volvo shared its design with other automakers at no cost, allowing rivals to significantly improve their safety records.


Unlike today’s inertia reel units, Bohlin’s belt has to be adjusted manually, like an aircraft lap belt. The buckle slots into an impressively over-engineered mechanism with a big red release handle between the seats and, when not in use, can be dropped into a holder in the B-pillar.


Given the technology of the time, it’s all very well considered, but does feel a bit restrictive, with no give at all. However, considering just how close to the steering wheel the Amazon puts you, being firmly secured is, no doubt, for the best.

It’s certainly a peculiar driving position. Being high and upright is good for visibility but also puts you right on top of the pedals and the huge, thin-rimmed wheel. There’s precious little room between the bottom of the wheel and the seat base, so larger drivers might have trouble getting in at all, while everyone else will be bent of knee and elbow.


That does help with the leverage required to turn the wheel, mind you. The steering box system is heavy, yet also a bit woolly off-center and, should you attempt to tackle a bend with any gusto, it loads up alarmingly.


Such antics may seem inappropriate, but this particular Amazon is the 123GT, the sportiest 120-series variant of them all, and as such it is quite possible to arrive at a corner with reasonable speed.


[Nik Berg](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/nberg/)


Under the hood is a 1.8-liter four-cylinder shared with the[P1800](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/klockau-classics/1967-volvo-1800s-practical-sports-car-and-a-klockau-favorite/) , with twin SU HS6 carburetors and a warmer cam pushing 116 hp. In its heyday, British tuner Ruddspeed wrestled more than 130 horses and 130 mph from it, but this 1968 car is as it left the factory. At low engine speeds it’s almost truck-like, able to trundle along at idle, but at 3000 rpm it politely clears its throat and delivers an eager surge of acceleration, accompanied by a very fruity intake and exhaust note.


Meanwhile, inside, the gear selector may be as long as Gandalf’s staff, but it delivers precise shifts that aren’t too prolonged. Keep an eye on the standalone rev counter that’s perched high on the dash; if you shift up at the 6000 rpm power peak, the 123GT has little trouble keeping up with traffic. An electric overdrive on fourth, operated by a column stalk, drops the revs for more comfortable cruising.


The 123GT scrubs speed well, too, thanks to front disc brakes. It doesn’t dive too steeply, courtesy of upgrades to the double wishbone front (and trailing arm rear) suspension that don’t compromise the ride.


In fact, the 123GT floats down the road rather well, with just the steering idiosyncrasies that require an adapted driving style. Set the car up early, take the line of least resistance and it’s a delight. Try to muscle it through corners and it’ll fight back.


Volvo


It’s not too hard to imagine why the Amazon was a success on Europe’s racetracks and rally stages, winning events like the Acropolis and Swedish rallies, as well as the European Touring Car Championship. It was the earlier 122 that scored these victories, but the 123GT, which was introduced in 1967, was the closest Volvo drivers would get to a road-going racer. The plan was to build 5000 cars, although it’s estimated that only around 1500 examples were assembled, making surviving examples rare. U.S. dealers didn’t stock the 123GT, but a few made it over via Volvo’s European Delivery Program, whereby customers would fly to Sweden, collect the car from the factory, and then have it shipped home after a jaunt around the Old Continent. 123GTs were also built and sold in Canada.


[Nik Berg](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/nberg/)


From the outside, you can tell an original 123GT by its dished wheels and chrome hubs, and, more obviously, by the twin Hella front spotlights and badging. Some cars came with Recaro seats, which offered more support than the standard vinyl-covered items in this particular car. The door mirrors on our loaner car surely weren’t factory-spec, as they prevent the front quarterlight windows from opening and don’t really adjust. Outboard fender mirrors were the ordinary fitment.


With so few real 123GTs made, and fewer extant, there are a number of clones out in the world. Find an original example in #2 (Excellent) condition and it would be worth $32,200 on average, according to Hagerty’s valuation team. Lesser examples are out there for as little as $13,300. What’s more, the 123GT is trending upwards in value, with our data showing a rise of around four percent over the last year.


“The cars were typically driven in a manner consistent with their purpose and survivorship today is relatively low, making the 123GT an interesting and worthwhile collectible ride,” says our price guide.


I couldn’t agree more.
