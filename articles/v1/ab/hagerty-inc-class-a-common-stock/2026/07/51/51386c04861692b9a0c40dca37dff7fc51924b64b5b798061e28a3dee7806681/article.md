# Our Two Cents: Concours or Custom Paint Job?

Be it a product or a service, the lure of getting something for free is hard to resist. That might be even more true these days, as the steadily increasing cost of paint recently provided another shock to our systems. Back in April, Pittsburgh Paints (aka[PPG](https://www.ppgpaints.com/) ) published a press release informing everyone of a “global price increase of up to 20%.” That’s not exactly heartwarming for car enthusiasts, so I asked the team here at Hagerty Media a very appealing question: *If you could commission a concours or a custom paint job for free, which would you choose?*


Eagle-eyed readers may recall that[I already asked this question](https://www.hagerty.com/media/hagerty-community/according-to-you-concours-or-custom-paint-job/) to our readers, but it felt right to ask for the opinions of people who are immersed in car culture on a daily basis. Our answers are about the same as yours, dear readers, but it’s fun to see how our staff came to their respective conclusions.


## Candy Apple Avanti?


Detroit Public Library/Studebaker


“Oh, I have absolutely seen, and admired, some breathtaking custom jobs over the years, but not many that I wanted to own—they always seemed a little like leaving the house wearing the same clothes day after day.


As a kid, I was absolutely enamored with candy apple paint jobs, and managed to spray a few models successfully, starting out with the base coats of gold or silver (or both!) before applying the color coats—certainly candy apple red was traditionally appealing. I managed to paint a first-gen Avanti model using that candy apple formula in emerald green, and it came out looking pretty kick-ass.


I’ve painted some big cars, including a couple that required demanding procedures, such as hand-rubbed lacquer. But I never had the guts, nor the patience, to shoot one in candy apple. So if you could provide to me an emerald green formula, and throw in an Avanti, say a ’63 model with the R3 powertrain, I’d be most grateful.” — *Steven Cole Smith*


## I Reject Your Premise!


Devaney/Getty Images


“Can I answer neither? I don’t want concours, and I don’t want custom. I want factory.


In some ways is a lot harder to replicate, since it means you have to paint it the way the factory did, as a bare shell, often with some panels separated from the main body. A ’48 Chevy is thus a $20,000 car that costs $30,000 to paint right, since those cars still had quite a bit of handwork in their bodies. The painting technique should be appropriate to the era of the car. For example, a Brass Era car should be painted with brushes, but in the old style such that the paint is just reduced enough that it can be brushed but without showing the brush marks. Talk about a lost art!


“Customs are one person’s vision and while they are interesting to look at, I wouldn’t want to own one. Concours cars (unless we’re talking Pebble Beach) tend to be way too over-restored and I am turned off by that as well. I want a time machine.” — *Aaron Robinson*


“I admire a well-done custom and a well-done paint job is just as important as the choice of engine or suspension. But I’m a bland person with bland tastes. No, seriously. I’d be perfectly happy with a well done, stock paint job on my car.” — *Greg Ingold*


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


“Painting a car like it was when new doesn’t mean the paint job has to be boring. As my example, I’ll reach all the way back to the Brass Era, like Aaron. In the Brass Age, cars were novelties for the rich, and that meant that customs builds were essentially the norm. (‘Factory’ is probably up for debate, too, since for many years coachbuilders were crafting the bodies for the cars sent from the ‘factory.’)


“On a recent staff outing to the[Gilmore Museum](https://gilmorecarmuseum.org/) , I beelined to the Museum of the Horseless Carriage. The paint jobs on some of these cars are astounding—I mean, they were adding pinstripes to each of the spokes of the (wooden) wheels. The color combinations from this era are unusual and fun: Imagine a car today wearing ochre pinstripes on a cream body with a white cloth top, or painted entirely, down to the undercarriage, in a mint-chocolate-chip green, with pastel yellow accents. Even the later, upper-middle-class Studebaker Commander 8 from 1930 (shown above) wears no less than three colors. I would be thrilled to drive around a car that showed off this type of painstaking and beautiful handiwork.” — *Grace Jarvis*


## Red Won’t Hurt My Head


[Sajeev Mehta](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/sajeev-mehta/)


“I want an endlessly deep, and glossy red paint job for my Cougar. This cat is anything but factory fresh, and a Lincoln-Mercury dealership’s body shop ensured this reality in the early 1990s when they added overspray to the fuel tank and surrounding areas. Those dealer-caused flaws and 210,000 miles traveling around Houston have not been kind to the paint, but the rest of the car is a peach.


Gimme perfection, and thank you very much for paying the tab. I sure hope the concept of a free lunch has no unintended consequences!” — *Sajeev Mehta*


## A Boring Custom?


[Kyle Smith](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/kyle-smith/)


“While I would like to do a concours-level restoration one day, that day is way off in the future, and in the interim I guess a custom paint job would be quite nice.


I’d elect to slather a fresh coat of color on my 1965 Corvair, and while it might be custom, I would prefer something on the boring end of custom. My coupe left the factory in Ermine White, and I like the color, so a fresh coat of boring white would be great, but maybe doing the taillight panel in something other than Argent Silver would be enough to put me in custom territory? Not sure, but that would be all I need. Keep the coats of clear to a minimum; I just need the car to be white. It’s as simple as that.” — *Kyle Smith*
