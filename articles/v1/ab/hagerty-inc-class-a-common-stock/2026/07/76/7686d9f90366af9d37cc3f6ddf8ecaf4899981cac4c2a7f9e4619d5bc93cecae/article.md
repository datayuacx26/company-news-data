# 1965 Imperial Crown: Rare and Lovely

Imperial. A car apart. And once, a more than worthy competitor to Cadillac and Lincoln. Imperial, as a separate luxury car marque, only lasted from 1955 to 1975, with brief returns in 1981–83 and 1990–93. But the Fifties and Sixties[Imperials](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/imperial-dreams-why-chryslers-forgotten-luxury-brand-couldnt-make-it-with-the-sinatra-set/) , while rarely seen, were marvelous luxury cars. Today’s example is a case in point.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Starting in 1957 and continuing through 1961, Imperials were very swoopy, with a huge wraparound windshield and giant fins. But the vivaciousness of these[Virgil Exner](https://www.hagerty.com/media/design/vellum-venom-the-virgil-exner-jr-interview-part-1/) -era cars began to tame after he departed from the Chrysler Corporation in 1961.


1962 Imperial at the 8/26/17 Aledo, IL show.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


The 1962 Imperial, while essentially the same basic car as the ’61, traded its big rear fins for squared-off rear quarters with “jet exhaust” taillights mounted in chrome pods atop the rear deck.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


With Elwood Engel’s arrival, the cars were even more tamed from their ’50s vivaciousness. And since Engel came from Ford Motor Company and had a hand in the ’61 Continental’s final form, Imperials took on more than a little Connie slab-sidedness.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Not that that was a bad thing. While there was a definite resemblance, especially with the squared-off nose and tail and the chrome-bladed fenders, Imperial still retained its own identity—and still some of its late ’50s/early ’60s flair.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Even better, there were still zero compromises in size and room. All ’65 Imperials rode an appropriately regal wheelbase—129 inches—and an overall length of 227.8 inches. To put that in perspective, a new 2026[Cadillac Escalade ESV](https://www.hagerty.com/media/new-car-reviews/first-look-review-2023-cadillac-escalade-v-esv/) is 226.9 inches. This two-row convertible is almost an inch longer than a three-row SUV with a massive trunk. Not bad.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


One did not have to worry about power, either. All Imperials had a V-8, and it was Chrysler Corporation’s biggest: 413 CID, with a 4.18-inch bore and a 3.75-inch stroke. These brutes made 340 horsepower and an even more impressive 470 lb-ft of torque.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


And that was the *only* engine. If you were worried about fuel economy in 1965, perhaps you weren’t Imperial material. All the ’65 Imperials had Chrysler’s excellent TorqueFlite three-speed automatic too—naturally.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


As the elegant 1965 Imperial brochure enlightened prospects, “Imperial’s roadability is no less surprising. This is largely due to a suspension system unique among American luxury cars. Torsion-bar front suspension, synchronized with widely-spaced rear-leaf springs, provides exceptional stability at highway speeds.”


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Interiors received even more prose. “In the quiet expression of good taste, Imperial has no equal. Inlays of rare 100-year-old walnut; tempered-glass protected headlamps; an unmistakable elegance of line, from a distinctive new grille to a striking eagle medallion—these are a few of the reasons the 1965 Imperial is a rather singular automobile.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


“Tahitian coconut and whale oils make the leathers unusually supple. Argentine capracho wood, bark from the South African wattle tree, the leaf of the Sicilian sumac—these are a few of the ingredients used to give Imperial leathers a softness, strength and patina finer than that of the most costly imported cars.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


“But then, Imperial is an automobile of absolutes.”


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


And there would be no mistaking an Imperial in traffic if one were driving any of the nifty European sports cars popular at the time. It would feel as if you were in a speedboat being overtaken by the SS United States.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


Today’s example was spotted by your author at the[2025 Geneseo, IL “Trains, Planes and Automobiles”](https://geneseo.org/trains-planes-and-automobiles/) show, held in downtown Geneseo, and mere blocks from Klockau’s home away from home, The Cellar supper club.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


It is an excellent show—probably one of the top five shows in the area, and I always make sure to attend. The 2025 event did not disappoint either. In addition to this glorious Imperial—one of 633 produced for the year—there was also a gorgeous ruby red ’63 Bonneville two-door hardtop and a metallic blue ’75 Chevy Laguna coupe that I also need to write up soon.


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)


But except for my friend Dave Mitchell’s 1934 Cadillac V16 (don’t worry, readers, I’m already working on a column on that one), this was my favorite. I especially loved the Formal Black paint, contrasted with the lovely white leather interior. So pretty!


[Thomas Klockau](https://www.hagerty.com/media/author/tklockau/)
