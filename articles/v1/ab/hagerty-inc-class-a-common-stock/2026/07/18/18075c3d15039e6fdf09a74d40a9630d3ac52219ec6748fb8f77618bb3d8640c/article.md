# 2026 Nichols N1A: When Can-Am Dreams Come True

There’s just a moment where I imagine myself as Bruce McLaren or Chris Amon, pulling out of the pit lane in a Can-Am McLaren. Then, the instant that I accelerate towards turn 1, it’s gone, every synapse focused on getting this car around the track without incident.


I certainly do not possess the skills of those legendary Antipodean drivers, but neither did they have access to the mind-blowing performance of this machine.


It’s called the Nichols N1A and is a tribute to McLaren’s first sports racing car, the[M1A](https://www.hagerty.com/media/automotive-history/mclarens-very-first-race-car-was-also-a-film-star/) , sharing the same essential elements of a big-bore American V-8 in a lightweight open-cockpit chassis.

[Steve Nichols](https://www.nichols-cars.com/racingheritage) , who gives his name to the car, is a former chief designer at McLaren, responsible for the most successful F1 car of all time. His MP4/4 won 15 out of 16 races in 1988, driven by Ayrton Senna and Alain Prost, while his partner John Minett had spells at the Williams and Super Aguri F1 teams.


The duo had been looking for a project to work on together for some time—even coming close to buying and reincarnating TVR—before settling on a tribute to the company with which Nichols had such success.


“I’d always liked the flowing shapes from the ‘60s, and that showed to me that you could take that sort of design theme and update it and come up with a modern-looking car,” says Minett. “I love the Can-Am era and I like the McLaren M1A particularly.”


Together they built a prototype, raised funding, and now the first customer cars are almost ready for delivery, priced from around half a million pounds ($675,000). There will be no more than 100 in the production run, while there are already plans to build other derivatives on the platform. One of the earliest examples is headed to a U.S. customer, and moves are being made to fully federalize the N1A, likely with a GM LT1 engine.


Nichols


Unlike the tubular steel chassis that underpinned the M1A, the N1A uses a bonded aluminum and carbon fiber tub with adjustable independent suspension at each corner, and where the most potent McLaren made do with around 550 hp, the Nichols delivers 700 hp and weighs in at just 900 kg (1984 lbs) wet.


In this car, the GM V-8 is bored and stroked to seven liters, dry-sumped, and fitted with new internals and individual throttle bodies. There are two engine maps, the first offering 450 hp and the second, accessed by a button marked “11” in a nod to *Spinal Tap* , unleashes the full 700 horses.


## Specs: 2026 Nichols N1A


- **Price** : From $675,000
- **Powertrain:** 7.0-liter V-8, six-speed manual transmission
- **Output:** 700 hp/600 lb-ft of torque
- **Layout:** Mid-engine, rear-drive rocketship
- **0–62 mph:** 3.5 seconds
- **Competitors:** McLaren M1A


On the road (yes, the N1A is fully street legal), the former is plenty, especially given the all-round sensory assault that driving entails.


Actually, the thrills begin even before I get behind the wheel. The N1A looks gorgeous. On this pre-production model, the body is fiberglass so the panel gaps are almost old Lotus-wide, but using carbon for customer cars will see a consistent 4 mm. It’s larger in every dimension than the M1A, but still tiny by modern car standards.


Nichols


It’s also pleasingly free of obvious aerodynamic accoutrements. Just a classic duck tail spoiler, a low front splitter and rear diffuser are there to be seen, with most of the aero work done by the flat floor.


The only jewelry to note is chrome finishes on the roll bar, mirrors and, most obviously, the eight intake trumpets that emerge through a cutout in the rear clamshell.


The interior is also minimalist, with exposed aluminum and carbon, and just a smattering of leather. Two vertical columns house the switchgear. In the center are buttons for lighting, nose lift and, most prominent, that tempting number 11. To the right of the steering wheel are the starter and adjustments for ABS and traction control. Next to them is a polished gated gear selector linked to the Graziano-based transaxle.

There’s a wide sill to clamber over, which provides a handy storage bin, and it’s best to stand on the carbon fiber bucket seat before lowering oneself in. The steering wheel is also removable for easier access.


Customers can choose from these standard seats, interchangeable custom-molded inserts, or opt for full-race-spec versions to deliver the ideal driving position. A movable pedal box can also be specified.


I find it easy enough to get comfortable and, at first, I’m reminded of cars like the Lotus 2-Eleven and RenaultSport Spider, although the driving position is lower here. Then I press the starter and, after a prolonged whirring, seven liters of American muscle brings me abruptly back to the present.


The unassisted steering is initially heavy but lightens up just as soon as the car is rolling, and the clutch is refreshingly light, while those flowing fenders really help to position the car accurately on the road.


Minett admits there’s some mapping work still to be done, as occasionally the engine stutters at low speeds and a quick clutch dip is needed to smooth things out, but otherwise it’s all quite civilized.


Up to around 50 mph you can even hear your passenger talking (or squealing) as you’re brushing shoulders anyway. For long distances or higher-speed drives, a full-face helmet is a must.

Fresh from being run up the Goodwood Hill Climb, the suspension is set a tad stiff for English country roads, but it’s never crashy, while the steering has a really nice weight and feel. The click-clack gearshift is just glorious and you’d never believe that cables are actually doing the work.


Obviously, it’s remarkably rapid with so much power and so little weight, but it’s not a combination that ever feels intimidating.


Until I press that big button marked 11.


Popping into the Goodwood Paddock to shoot a spot of video for Hagerty’s social channels, Minett and I are approached by a very nice chap called Ben. He’s hired the circuit to drive a couple of Dallaras and asks if we’d like to take the Nichols for a few laps.


So that’s how I find myself emerging from the pit lane, barreling toward the right-hander at Madgwick. I aim for the late apex, hit the power in third and gun it up through Fordwater toward St Mary’s. The acceleration is absolutely brutal, and so is the noise emanating from immediately behind my head. I grab fourth briefly, not daring to look at the speedometer, before slowing for the right-left complex. This car has perfectly capable steel brakes, but carbon ceramic discs will also be offered.


Lavant corner demands second gear, the N1A gripping hard, and then again simply rocketing down the straight through third and fourth towards Woodcote corner. I’m early on the brakes and cautious, not running the exit kerb, and even more careful entering the final chicane.


One more lap to live out my Can-Am dreams and I’m back in the pit lane. Sweating, buzzing. Breathing again.


No doubt there are faster road and track cars (such as those Dallaras), but it’s hard to imagine one that is so totally enthralling, so analog and visceral.
