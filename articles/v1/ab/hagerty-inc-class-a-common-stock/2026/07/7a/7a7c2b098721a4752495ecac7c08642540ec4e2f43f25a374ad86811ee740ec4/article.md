# Updating My Old Corvette’s Hubs with Slightly Less Old Tech

The wheels on the Corvette go ’round and ’round—at least, in an ideal world. But the wheel and tire are only a small part of the functional-automobile equation, and I’m not talking about engines or transmissions; I’m talking about wheel hubs. These are so critical that most of us forget about them (until that grinding noise we hoped was brakes turns out to be failing bearings). As I dive into the rear end of[my 1985 Corvette LS-swap project car](https://www.hagerty.com/media/tags/project-corvette/) , I’ve decided to get ahead of such issues, and in the process I found that a simple wheel-bearing upgrade is anything but.


In order to get the wheels on the bus to actually go ’round, and to do smoothly, you need a bearing of some kind. Its job is to allow the suspension to support the car and to allow the hub to turn. Wheel bearings have been a problem since day one of the automobile, but thanks to advances in technology, we are no longer dealing with brass bushings on a plain shaft. My Corvette’s hubs have comparatively modern ball bearings, and even those have been surpassed by better designs in the 40 years since the car was new.

I have been careful as I modernize this Corvette, because I’ve driven many cars that felt as though they were pieced together by someone who could be convinced by three lines of good advertising copy. I have fallen prey to the same thing; the prime example is the steamroller wheels on[my 1965 Corvair](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/mechanical-sympathy/my-corvair-needed-love-and-now-its-stranded/) . Those wheels are so heavy that the car’s acceleration suffered, but I honestly don’t care because I love how the car looks, and it drives good enough.


Unlike the Corvair, this Corvette is go-before-show, and thus I am investing a small amount of effort to find and use better parts as I replace and restore the various bits that are either tired after the first 120,000 miles of use on this chassis or are not suited to the miles this now LS-powered car will see in the future. Removing the differential from the car introduced a litany of opportunities “while I’m in there.” Wary of that trap, I’m treading carefully, because I want to ensure this car doesn’t become some overpowered, unreliable missile that can’t be trusted.

That’s all to say things like U-joints are non-negotiable. After nearly two years with this car, wheel bearings are no longer an item worth skimping on. Previously, I beat the stock lug studs out of the hubs and installed longer versions to allow my fifth-generation Corvette wheels to fit with slim wheel spacers. Those ARP studs are still good since they are straight, undamaged, and the knurling remains intact. Thus, I quickly pulled them into the new hubs for this Corvette.


Like many parts I’ve installed on this car, these wheel bearings are not intended for this application. Like the iron-block V-8 engine now under the Corvette’s hood, the bearings are another example of truck parts I’m happily installing on my sports car. Specifically, they are SKF wheel bearings for a 1999[Chevrolet S10](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/final-parking-space/final-parking-space-1982-chevrolet-s-10/) . These bearings have the same hub flange and bolt circle diameter, and the same overall packaging with the exception of the antilock braking sensor. That sensor is only slightly in the way, according to a few forum posts, and while there is a way to accommodate the sensor and associated wiring, I’m opting to simply unbolt it, remove it, and plug the hole left in the hub. (Yes, this likely negates any chance of claiming the warranty, but I’m also changing out the studs, so that was unlikely anyway.)


I will make a small plug for the ABS sensor hole and keep the sensor on the shelf for some fun project one day.

These particular truck hubs are an upgrade for two reasons: Durability and price. While the outer form factor is largely the same compared to the factory-fit hub bolted to this chassis in Bowling Green, late 1984, the roller bearings inside are quite different. The S10 pickup is what SKF calls its X-Tracker design, and it’s only new compared to the Corvette. This design won awards in 2006, which is two decades ago, but it’s still newer than the 40-year-old car it’s going in. (Sometimes “updating” isn’t as sexy as it sounds.) The basics are that the inner and outer roller bearings that support the wheel flange are different sizes. One is larger and has extra ball bearings to help spread out the load a little bit more. This creates a hub that withstands side load better than the stock C4 hubs, which use identically sized bearings inner and outer and are known to wear out quickly during track or autocross usage.


Even if all that doesn’t convince you, the X-Tracker hubs came in nearly $30 cheaper, at just $92 *each,* compared to the “correct” SKF part. This Corvette is not a budget build, and I’m willing to splash some cash where it makes sense. At the same time, I’m not buying a bunch of fancy stuff simply to buy fancy stuff. Which is why these X-Trackers will go on the rear of this car and the front will get standard wheel bearings. Updating to X-Tracker bearings on the front of a C4 doesn’t appear to be as cheap and easy as upgrading the rears, but at some point, the advantages may be worth the labor.

Currently though, I’m simply trying to get the car back on the road and sort out the final bits of the drivetrain swap. The differential being on the bench is a huge step forward, and I’ll be even more excited once the 4.10 gears are bolted into and set up in the case. That will hopefully happen in the next couple weeks. After[two seasons driving](https://www.hagerty.com/media/opinion/mechanical-sympathy/the-freedom-of-owning-a-crusty-car/) this pretty tired Corvette chassis, I’m convinced that with fresh suspension bushings, a good set of shocks, and a steering refresh, this LS-swapped car will be more capable than me and very fun to drive. I’ve got a lot of work to do before I can test that theory, though, and that is part of the fun.
