# Original Owner: ’71 Malibu Went from Family Car to 12-Second Hot Rod in 40 Years

Dan Hawkins was three years old in 1989 but said he remembers his Uncle Greg coming around to his house in a silver 1971 Chevelle Malibu 350 convertible. Both Greg and Bill, the younger of his mom’s brothers, got him revved up for horsepower from an early age. When the Chevelle eventually became Dan’s 23 years later, after his two uncles had it, the drop-top Chevy was turning 12-second quarter-miles and taking his family out for ice cream.


Dan’s mother, Shari Winstead, was seven years old and with Dan’s grandparents when they bought the convertible new from Lowe Chevrolet in Marlboro, Maryland, in June 1971. His mom recounted walking into the dealership and Dan’s grandmother, Dottie, pointing to the Nevada Silver Malibu convertible across the showroom and calling out, “That’s the one!” Indeed, it was. Just a year later, the young family took the Malibu on a 2800-mile cross-country journey to a new home in the Seattle suburb of Bothell, Washington. Dan’s research decades later confirmed how special the car was.


Dan’s grandfather with the Chevelle he’d bought new.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


Industrywide, convertible sales had gone into a steep decline in the early 1970s. Just 5089, or 1.3%, of the 387,000 Chevelles made in 1971 were droptops. Despite those figures, that Maryland Chevy dealer had the confidence to order a well-optioned model for the showroom. The ’71 Malibu convertible, which came standard with the 307-cubic-inch two-barrel V-8 and a power convertible top, started at $3362, including a $107 destination fee.


Dan’s grandparents bought the Chevelle in Maryland in June 1971.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


This one was well-optioned with the 350-cube four-barrel V-8, Turbo-Hydramatic transmission, air conditioning, F41 Sport Suspension, Positraction rear axle (with 2.73:1 ratio), tilt steering wheel, power steering, AM/FM radio, Rally Wheels, and a few other items for a $4589.25 total. Though not shown on the sticker, Dan remembered the car having power-assisted drum brakes.


The reproduction window sticker shows a well-optioned convertible.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


## Pacific Northwest-Bound


Not long after landing in the Pacific Northwest, the Malibu went into the garage and remained there for the next 15 years. At that point, Dan’s Uncle Bill, then 16, convinced his parents to let him revive the Chevelle to drive in high school. As is often the case, the high-schooler was not gentle with it.


“It was known to have been airborne on more than one occasion,” Dan said. Bill never officially owned the Chevelle, though, and Dan’s grandparents sold it to his Uncle Greg in 1989 for $3000.


“Greg began all the overdue maintenance and repairs left over from the heavy-footed younger brother,” Dan said. “He’d take me to a local demolition derby or the fair, and on other rides in the Chevelle.”


From the get-go, the cool Chevy instigated young Dan’s interest in cars. He began to ask questions, such as, “What does the ‘350’ mean?” He knew that and much more by 13 years old, just when his Uncle Greg left Washington in the Chevelle to take a job in South Carolina. His stay there was brief, and he moved to Florida, where, within a year, he got into an accident that damaged the Chevelle’s front end. The silver convertible then spent about 12 years in his garage and being taken to different shops for repairs and upgrades.


“My uncle did some work himself, but he suffered from chronic back pain, so he often had to pay people to work on it,” Dan said. He saw the car on a visit to his uncle not long after the collision, and, educated by car magazines, the teen offered some suggestions.


“I told him that with the front end taken apart, it was a good time to do an engine swap and maybe put in a 700R4 transmission to make it better for the highway.”


The Chevelle in South Carolina in 1999, before a subsequent move to Florida.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


Greg liked his car-savvy nephew’s ideas. The original 350 small-block and automatic were pulled, and a body shop in Apopka, Florida, replaced the banged-up front sheetmetal, including both fenders, the hood, and core support. Greg got lucky with a swap motor. He’d befriended a Chevy-specialist performance shop owner who’d built up a 402 big-block from a ’71 Chevelle or Camaro. A 0.030-in. overbore brought it to 408 cubic inches. The original customer had bailed on the project, so the shop owner sold it to Greg for the unpaid balance. The prepped motor went in, along with a rebuilt GM 700R4 automatic overdrive transmission.


“He got it painted but couldn’t afford to get it across the finish line,” Dan said. “I occasionally visited him, and we’d always make time to see the Chevelle.”


Teenage Dan visiting his Uncle Greg in Florida while the Chevelle was a work-in-progress.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


From his teenage years, Dan had been asking his uncle about selling the Chevelle to him when he could afford it. Greg told his eager nephew he’d always planned to keep the car but eventually mentioned two conditions under which he would sell it to him.


“He told me I had to achieve my dream of becoming a full-time firefighter, and I had to own a house with a garage to keep the Chevelle out of the weather. I think he was trying to figure out a way to make sure I was mature enough to be responsible with it.”


Dan admits that, as a young driver, he did not meet the “responsible” criteria. Following a couple of Chevy pickups, he got a[Fox-body 5.0-liter Mustang](https://www.hagerty.com/media/buying-and-selling/1979-93-ford-mustang-fox-body-buyers-guide/) . One reckless-driving ticket later, he sold the ’Stang and went back to pickups.


By 2008, Dan was a full-time firefighter for Renton, Washington, a suburb just south of Seattle. He married his middle-school sweetheart, Shawna, in 2012, and the couple lived in a house they’d bought the year before in Mountlake Terrace, a northern suburb.


Dan had his dream job and his garage, and he still had his heart set on the Chevelle. Christmas season that year brought a special present. On a phone call, Dan’s uncle offered to sell him the car.


Dan still has the original broadcast sheet for the Chevelle.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


“I knew the car would be worth more than the $3000 he paid in 1989, and he knew there was a family deal to honor, since he’d gotten that,” Dan said. “When he got the Chevelle, it was running and driving. I’d be getting it in worse shape. It would take a lot of work to get it back on the road.”


They agreed on $6000. Greg told the owner of the Chevy shop that a shipper would be coming to pick up the car. Learning the sale price, the man offered Greg $16,000 in cash on the spot.


“I didn’t fault my uncle for it. I knew money was tight for him,” Dan said. “I didn’t want the car to come between us. But he said he’d think about it for a few days.”


After those few days passed, Dan got the call. The Chevelle was his for six grand.


The Chevelle spent over 25 years off the road, so total miles stayed low.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


## Factory Basics: 1971 Chevelle Malibu


The mid-size Chevelle was a hit for Chevy right out of the gate in 1964. Body-on-frame construction and coil-spring rear suspension gave big-car ride comfort, and the Malibu was the popular upgrade trim line. The 1968 redesign brought curvier lines, and the coupe continued to dominate sales.


Chevy sold very few six-cylinder Chevelle convertibles and made it V-8-only for 1971, with the base version having the aforementioned 307-cubic-inch engine. Ordering either the optional two-barrel or four-barrel 350 added “Malibu 350” badges to the front fenders, or a “Malibu 400” would adorn the sides if buyers stepped up to the LS3 400, which was really a 402. (And that, Chevy fans know, was the[former 396](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z5U5Kxrv6SY) with a slight bore job from 1970.) The 1971 Z15 SS Equipment option allowed either of the 350s for lower insurance premiums, with the 402 and LS5 454 available as step-ups.


Chevrolet


GM dictated reduced compression ratios for all 1971 models and began publishing both the familiar SAE gross horsepower and the as-installed net ratings. The 350 two-barrel offered 245 hp gross / 165 hp net, with the L48 four-barrel rated at 270/175 in the Chevelle. The 402 had 300/260 ratings, and the 454 produced 365/285.


Appealing design, known reliability, and the wide array of models and option choices made the Chevelle America’s best-selling mid-sizer for 1971. The 387,000 total included 36,000 El Caminos, which Chevy listed as a “two-door sedan pickup” body style rather than a separate model. Ford’s Torino took second place in the segment with 326,500, including 25,000 Ranchero pickups.


Chevrolet


## From Jigsaw Puzzle to “Day 2” Hotrod


Having the Chevelle shipped to Washington added a big chunk over Dan’s $6000 investment. The 408 big-block and 700R4 trans were installed, but not much else.


“The car was like a roller with a powertrain, but there was no driveshaft, the parking brake didn’t work, and the engine had none of the accessories or carb installed,” he said. “It had to be winched on and off the trailer, and I pushed it inside my garage with a couple of my firefighter buddies. The car was packed almost to the headliner with parts my uncle had removed and new ones he’d bought. It was like being handed a puzzle, but there’s no picture as a guide, and you don’t even know if you have all the pieces.”


Dan dug through and sorted everything, cross-referencing shop manuals with photos he found online. He wanted to keep the Chevy’s appearance as his grandparents had bought it, while acknowledging the car’s hotrod heart and spirit. The Chevelle had 67,000 miles showing when he took delivery and by then had spent more than 25 of its 40 years off the road.


Dan had additional work done to the big-block his uncle had installed years before.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


Replacing the “Malibu 350” badges with original “Malibu 400” badges he’d found and restored was the smallest part of the reassembly that took the better part of a year.


Dan wasn’t trying to pass off the car as an original Malibu 400 convertible, though. The real one was mighty rare, with some estimates suggesting just three dozen cars out of the 17,500 ’71 LS3-optioned Chevelles. Rather, he was going for an in-period “Day 2” hotrod theme.


Edelbrock’s RPM aluminum heads, EnduraShine air gap intake, and 750-cfm carb already topped the big-block. Dan gave the 12-bolt Posi 3.73:1 gears, installed Mickey Thompson ET rear street tires, and upgraded to front disc brakes. In the cabin, the original bench seat remained, and a floor shifter replaced the column shifter. At age 27, Dan was finally driving his teen-dream car. A few years later, a hit-and-run while the car was parked sent it back in for some bodywork. When the transmission blew shortly after that repair, Dan learned it had been a stock rebuild and not nearly up to the 402’s output. He had it rebuilt to handle 550 horsepower, and though he knew his Chevelle had nowhere near that, getting it dyno-tuned revealed even less than he thought.


The Chevelle kept its bench seat but gained a floor shifter and other hotrod touches.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


After the intake gasket blew during a dyno run, the tuner suggested bore-scoping the engine. Numbers on the Speed-Pro forged aluminum pistons revealed that the engine builder had chosen the incorrect part number by one digit. The error meant 8.65:1 compression rather than the 10.5:1 Dan believed it had.


Rather than change the pistons, the tuner suggested ordering a custom-grind camshaft from Bullet Racing Cams to maximize the dynamic compression ratio. That swap, plus a Holley XP 950-cfm carb, put the engine into peak health, the dyno revealing 333 hp and 475 lb-ft. of torque.


A new cam and bigger carburetor unlocked big power, even with low compression.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


“The real test of horsepower is on the track,” Dan said, proving that maxim by running five passes at Pacific Raceways in Kent, Washington, all from 12.0 to 12.5 seconds.


Using the Chevelle’s 3920-lb. weight with him in it, 3.73:1 axle ratio, and factoring in the density altitude, he calculated 425 flywheel horsepower, which lined up with the dyno’s wheel hp figures.


## Making New Family Memories for 14 Years and Counting


Dan drives his family treasure Chevelle from May through September. He and Shawna will take their sons out for ice cream and to their baseball practices and games, and Dan enjoys turning heads and entertaining his boys by opening the electric exhaust cutouts he installed.


“The car does not go out in the rain, ever,” he said. “I got caught in the rain once, and that was about the most scared I’ve ever been driving any vehicle. It would need a good set of all-weather tires just to barely contain it. I also want to prevent rust.”


Dan, his wife Shawna, and their young boys out for a cruise in the hotrod Chevelle.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


About five years ago, with COVID shutting down local shows, Dan and some friends organized a car cruise around their large residential neighborhood, Gold Creek in Snohomish. They knew there were other muscle cars in the development, and the cruise brought out about 50 cars thanks to Dan’s friends posting on social media.


“Everybody came out to their front yards and waved, and there were lots of kids,” he said. “We’ve done it a few times and gotten about the same number of cars.”


Dan and friends organized a local car cruise that raised money for a first-responder financial-support group.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


The drivers were cruising for a cause, too, raising money for Lionhardt, a local organization that raises money to help support families of ill and fallen first responders. It was started in honor of and named for a Renton firefighter Dan had been hired with, Donovan “Dono” Eckhardt, who died from cancer at age 37.


The Chevelle ferried Dan’s brother-in-law and his wife in their wedding, and his most cherished moment was proudly showing the car to his grandmother when it was finished.


“She had not seen the Chevelle since my uncle left with it in 1999. I took her for a few rides in it before she passed away in 2024. Those were special family moments.”


The Chevelle ferried Dan’s brother-in-law and his wife in their wedding.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


Dan Hawkins parking the Chevelle at his grandmother’s house when she saw it for the first time in about 15 years.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


Dan with his firstborn son working on the Chevelle in 2016.


Courtesy Dan Hawkins


Both of Dan’s boys love being around the Chevelle and helping him work on it, and he’s started buying them their own tools. He’s confident the car will remain in the family.


“I love driving it, and there’s of course the nostalgia and heirloom factors that make it a forever car for me.”
