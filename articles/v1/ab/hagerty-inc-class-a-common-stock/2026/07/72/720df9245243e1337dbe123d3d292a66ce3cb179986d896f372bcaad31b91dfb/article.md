# VW’s Golf Rallye G60 Truly Was the Golf R of the ’80s and ’90s

Nowadays, the facelifted version of Volkswagen’s eighth-generation Golf R is widely acclaimed as a potent, grippy hot hatchback. With 328 horsepower, 295 lb-ft of torque, all-wheel drive, and a sub-five-second run to 60 mph, who would disagree? But long before today’s model established its supremacy, VW was applying the same formula, utilizing both off-the-shelf and exclusive equipment in the Mk II Golf Rallye G60, produced from 1989 to 1991. The Rallye was not just a response to other brands’ extra-hot hatches that began stealing bites of VW’s enthusiast-class lunch. It was also its[very own homologation special](https://historicdb.fia.com/car/volkswagen-golf-rallye-g-60-1764-0) .

I recently had the chance to take a friend’s imported Golf Rallye G60 for a spin and was impressed by how well it drove, even by today’s standards. Coincidentally, this was shortly after I had a chance to get my hands on a 2026 Golf R. I couldn’t help but chart some clear connections between them.


## The Makings of a Classic


Golf II GTI Group A ‘World Champion‘


Volkswagen AG


FIA Group A rally racing was far less over-the-top than the infamous Group B; the former featured more production-based bodywork and lower power outputs. Volkswagen competed in Group A in the mid-to-late ’80s, with driver Kenneth Eriksson earning the title in 1986 behind the wheel of a front-wheel-drive[Golf GTI](https://www.hagerty.com/media/car-profiles/compressed-timeline-test-drive-three-hours-in-three-generations-of-the-vw-golf-gti/) . (The FIA only awarded a Group A-specific World Drivers’ Championship for the 1986 season because it was the only season that included both Group A and Group B. When the Group B regulations ended after the ’86 season, and Group A cars became the top-level machines, there was a single World Drivers’ Championship once again.) To be competitive in the ensuing years, Volkswagen added an all-wheel drive system to the car and juiced the power output. To satisfy the FIA’s homologation requirements, Volkswagen had to produce 5000 examples of the race car to be sold publicly. Thus, the Golf Rallye was born. (It’s also known as the Rallye Golf, or just Rallye. The names are all seemingly interchangeable.)


The street-going Rallye’s special sauce began under the hood. Instead of using a free-breathing eight- or 16-valve four-cylinder, the Rallye boasted a supercharged version of the eight-valve engine with a small G-Lader blower. Power output was respectable for its day: 158 hp and 166 lb-ft of torque to haul around roughly 2600 pounds. This was around 100 ponies less than the Group A race car, but the street car still hit the 60-mph mark in just over eight seconds. To put the added grunt to the pavement, the Rallye used VW’s Synchro all-wheel-drive system paired with a five-speed manual transmission.

Relative to a[standard Golf or a GTI](https://www.hagerty.com/media/buying-and-selling/1985-92-vw-golf-gti-buyers-guide/) of the era, the Rallye’s wider bodywork helped set it apart visually, with signature bumpers, an HID lighting package, and a limited color palette. Those exterior tweaks, plus Recaro seats and interior accents, placed the Rallye well above anything else in VW’s lineup at the time. All Rallyes were assembled in the brand’s[Studebaker factory](https://pvwmag.com/vw-to-close-the-factory-that-made-the-mk2-golf-rallye-did-you-even-know-that-factory-wasnt-in-germany) in Brussels, Belgium, but the car was never sold in the USA. However, because it’s clear of the 25-year import rule, a handful have made their way stateside.

## An Imported, OEM+ Work-in-Progress


Dylan Richard is a lifelong VW-head with a particular affinity for Mk II Golfs and Jettas. I first met him over 20 years ago at a Chicago Volkswagen Organization meet, and he instantly became a buddy. My steed at the time was a Red Pearl Mica Jetta Carat with the sturdy but basic 1.8-liter, 8-valve four-cylinder. Along with Richard’s good friend Kieran Delaney, he was a helpful guide for learning all about the potent and fun Mk II chassis.


Fast forward two decades, and I’m standing on Richard’s back driveway north of Chicago, poring over his imported black Golf Rallye G60. Richard was out of town, so Delaney happily lent a hand as I got acquainted with the car.


Peter Nelson


Richard’s example isn’t completely stock. Rather, it’s an[OEM+](https://www.hagerty.com/media/advice/piston-slap/fan-of-oem-plus-but-didnt-know/) work-in-progress. It sports three-piece Momo R3 wheels (16×7.5 inches in front, 16×8.5 inches in rear) wrapped in lightly stretched Toyo rubber and adorned with body-color-matched centers. It rides lower than usual thanks to a set of KW V1 coilovers. Some other minor bits have been refreshed and changed, and a big exhaust tip sticks out from underneath the rear bumper. **** The interior features a set of fitted Recaro LS-C seats upholstered in a custom fabric that’s carried over onto other interior bits and bobs, such as the center of the Italvolanti steering wheel. A previous owner fitted a moody immobilizer and a gaudy CD player—both, along with some general service and a sagging headliner, are on Richard’s to-do list. Hopefully, the immobilizer is first in line.


## Going for a Spin


When we fired up the little beast, it was immediately apparent that it wasn’t completely stock under the hood. The idle was lumpier than average, a difference which Richard says is due to a stage-three engine build that involves a more aggressive camshaft, a custom tune for the Bosch Digifant ECU, and more. The supercharger was also refreshed and upgraded by[Bahn Brenner Motorsport](https://www.bahnbrenner.com/store/c28/Corrado.html#/) . This Rallye hasn’t seen a dyno in a while, but there’s no doubt its output is higher than stock.


Peter Nelson


Once behind the wheel, the first thing I noticed was how well it rode, even with an underinflated passenger-side front tire. (We filled it up shortly after snapping these photos.) The KWs did their job well, especially over Chicagoland’s notoriously crappy roads. The chassis felt taut overall, and there was barely a squeak inside. There’s no doubt this Rallye’s owners kept up with chassis maintenance, but I was impressed by how well-screwed-together it felt. The same can’t be said for the shifter, but its linkage is on the list of to-be-refreshed items.


Taking off from stoplights kept a smile firmly plastered across my face. Between the five-speed’s shorter gearing and the engine’s sturdy low-end torque, it always felt rarin’ to go, with wonderful notes of aggression resonating from under the hood and out the tailpipe. There was no hint of tire slip, either, no matter the throttle input, thanks to the Synchro system, just a hearty lunge.

Revving out the Rallye with the throttle wide-open was truly something to behold. Thanks to the supercharger, torque piled on throughout the tach needle’s sweep, crescendoing once I hit the midrange. Risking being made a fool of once it’s properly dyno’d, I’d guess that this boosted 8-valve makes at least 200 hp and 200 lb-ft of torque.


The only slight aural downside of the G60’s G-Lader supercharger is that, for the most part, it’s pretty silent. You don’t get that clear indication that something else is there to help shove you along. G60 owners (it was also one of the[VW Corrado](https://www.hagerty.com/media/market-trends/your-handy-1990-95-volkswagen-corrado-buyers-guide/) ’s factory powertrains) often make small modifications to up the volume in this department, and if it were my car, I would, too.

Sadly, this corner of Illinois is devoid of any twisty roads, but the way Richard’s Rallye took to the tarmac had me imagining how fun it would be on winding asphalt. It turned in with little effort, and steering response was direct, which is something VW still nails in its current enthusiast fare. The KW dampers certainly influenced its handling characteristics, and I bet overall this thing would feel more modern than one might assume under higher lateral *g* s.


Then, there was the fact that I was riding in one capacious little two-door hatchback. I’m 6’3” and had plenty of headroom, yet this Mk II would look tiny next to any more modern GTI or Golf R. Along with its old-school, enthusiast-focused looks, this extra-hot hatch’s legitimately small silhouette helped elicit plenty of thumbs-up from passers-by.

## A Clear Family Lineage


The Mk II Golf Rallye G60 ripped so that the eighth-gen Golf R, known to fans as the Mk 8.5, could rip harder. The latter is packed with modern tech to help it do so, but the basic formula is still there, down to forced induction and AWD. The supercharged Rallye had factory sport suspension, while the turbocharged R has adaptive suspension with a range of tuneability. Had this tech been widely available back then, the Rallye would’ve had it, too. It would’ve been a hell of an accompaniment to its AWD system. The modern Golf R’s powerband is more linear, whereas the Rallye’s is more progressive. Both are tremendously fun, but the R feels powerful all over, no matter the rpm or throttle input. We’ve come a very long way since the multi-port electronic fuel injection systems of the late ‘80s.

They both make solid power for their respective eras, too. Nobody scoffed at 160 horsepower and similar torque in such a small package back in the day, and nobody would call the 328-hp R slow in 2026.


These two definitely stand out in the looks department for their respective eras, too. It’s immediately clear that an R sits above the GTI—its rear spoiler, massive intakes moulded into its front bumper, and signature wheels ensure that. The Rallye’swidened fenders, signature headlights, and aggressive stance do the same for a bygone era.

It was tremendous fun getting to drive the 2026 Golf R and a classic Golf Rallye G60 within a month of each other. Even if VW hasn’t been as keen as it used to be regarding its racing activities (the automaker’s in-house motorsport division ended in 2020), there’s a clear lineage between these two cars, indicating that forced-induction, extra-hot-hatch thrills have been a part of the brand’s ethos for over 35 years.
