# How to Send Money from Nigeria to Japan

The naira closed at N1,607 to the dollar this week. If you have a payment heading to Japan, you felt that.


Whether you are supporting a family member in Tokyo, paying tuition at a Japanese university, covering a supplier invoice for electronics or machinery, or just trying to send your child spending money for the semester, the question of how to move naira to Japan without losing a painful amount in the process is one more Nigerians are asking right now. I have seen the question come up in WhatsApp groups, on Twitter, in direct messages. And honestly, I did not find a clear answer when I went looking online. What I found were comparison tool pages and landing pages from the big global transfer apps, but nothing that actually walks you through what the process looks like from Nigeria.


So this is my attempt to do that.


## Why Nigeria-Japan Transfers Are More Common Than You Think


Japan does not always come up in conversations about Nigerian diaspora destinations, and the numbers on the ground are smaller than, say, the UK or Canada. But the Nigeria-Japan corridor is real and growing.


Nigerian students are choosing Japanese universities in growing numbers, drawn by engineering programs, robotics research, and scholarship opportunities from the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA). Some are there on government-sponsored technical programs. Others went initially for the culture and stayed for the career. There is also a steady stream of Nigerians in Japan's automotive and manufacturing supply chains, particularly around Toyota, Honda, and Denso operations. And then there is the trade side: Nigerian importers buying Japanese electronics, used vehicles, and industrial equipment are sending payments into Japan every week.


Nigeria sent over $20 billion in remittances in 2024, and the destinations keep widening. The UK, US, and Canada dominate the headlines. Japan does not. That gap between where the money is actually going and where the content exists is what I noticed when I started digging into this.

The naira hit N1,607 at the official market on July 19, 2026, down from N1,576 just three days earlier. That kind of movement, N31 in less than a week, changes the math on your transfer. If an operator gets suspended mid-transfer, your money sits in limbo. That is not a theoretical risk right now.


## How Banks Handle Nigeria-Japan Transfers (And Why Most People Regret Using Them)


My first instinct when I needed to make an international transfer years ago was the same as most people's: walk into the bank. It feels safe. The building is right there. You know the teller.


The problem with using a Nigerian bank for a Japan transfer is not that it does not work. It does work, eventually. The problem is the combination of costs and timelines that nobody tells you upfront.


Nigerian commercial banks process international wire transfers through correspondent banking networks. A wire from Lagos to Tokyo might route through a US correspondent bank, then to a Japanese correspondent, before reaching the beneficiary's account at something like MUFG, Sumitomo Mitsui, or Mizuho. Each hop adds a fee. The sender pays an initial wire charge. The correspondent banks in the middle deduct their own fees. And the recipient sometimes gets hit with a receiving fee on the Japan side. By the time the money arrives, you may have lost three to five percent in fees alone, on top of an exchange rate that was not competitive in the first place.


The timeline is also frustrating. Most international wire transfers through Nigerian banks take three to five business days. Some take longer if there are compliance checks. Japan's banking system is highly organized on the receiving end, but the bottleneck is usually on the Nigeria side.


For small to medium transfers, especially under $5,000, bank wires are rarely the right answer from Nigeria.

## What to Have Ready Before You Send


Anyone who has tried to make an international transfer from Nigeria and hit a verification wall knows how annoying it is to start the process and then pause because you are missing a document. Let me save you that.


For a personal transfer, most platforms will ask for: your BVN or NIN, a valid government-issued ID (national ID, international passport, or driver's license), and sometimes a selfie or video verification. For larger amounts, you may need proof of the reason for the transfer, whether that is a school acceptance letter, an invoice, or a rental agreement.


For a business or supplier payment, you will likely need: your CAC registration documents, the beneficiary's full bank details (including SWIFT code, branch, and account number), and an invoice showing the reason for the transfer. Japanese bank accounts follow a specific format, and the SWIFT code for major Japanese banks is well-documented. For MUFG, it is BOTKJPJT. For Sumitomo Mitsui, SMBCJPJT. For Mizuho, MHCBJPJT. Getting those details right upfront prevents delays.


## On Exchange Rates: What You Should Actually Be Comparing


Most people look at the fee and ignore the rate. That is a mistake.


An app might advertise "zero fees" while applying an exchange rate that is two or three percent worse than the mid-market rate. Another app charges a visible 1.5% fee but gives you a rate that is 0.5% off mid-market. The second option is cheaper, but most people would not realize it from the fee label alone.


My habit is to cross-check the rate any platform offers me against the current mid-market rate on xe.com or Google's currency converter. The gap between what the platform offers and the mid-market rate is the true cost, in addition to any stated fees. For a significant transfer, a one percent difference on the exchange rate can mean more in absolute terms than any flat fee.


For Nigeria-to-Japan specifically, you are converting NGN to JPY, and that cross-rate is derived from NGN/USD and USD/JPY. Platforms that offer a direct NGN-to-JPY conversion may give you a blended rate that looks competitive but hides margin in the cross. Knowing what the underlying USD/JPY rate is at the time of your transfer helps you reality-check whatever the platform tells you.


## For Nigerians Supporting Students in Japan


If you are regularly sending money to a child or dependent in Japan for living expenses, there are a few things worth setting up properly rather than scrambling each time.


Japanese universities often have student accounts at major banks or Japan Post Bank (Yucho). Getting that account information early, before the semester starts, means you are not chasing it under pressure when tuition is due or rent is coming up.


Japan Post Bank (Yucho) is popular for students because of its ATM network. The SWIFT code is JPBKJPJT. Some transfer apps can send directly to Yucho accounts, though not all. Confirm before committing to a platform.


Setting up a transfer schedule rather than sending ad hoc can also help you time the naira side better. If you know you need to send every 30 days, monitoring the NGN/USD rate over a two-week window and sending when the rate is favorable is a simple discipline that adds up over an academic year.
