# How to Send Money from Nigeria to Switzerland

When a friend told me she was moving to Basel to work at one of the pharmaceutical companies there, her first question wasn't about housing or the weather in winter. It was: "How do I get money from home?"


She'd heard stories. Someone waiting three weeks for a SWIFT transfer that arrived short. A cousin who paid fees twice over and still couldn't explain where the money went. She just wanted to know if sending naira to Switzerland was going to be a headache every month.


I told her it doesn't have to be. But she needed to know what she was doing before sending the first one.


The Nigerian community in Switzerland is smaller than in the UK or the US, but it's growing in ways that matter. There are Nigerians at ETH Zurich and the University of Zurich on research fellowships. There are professionals in Geneva working with the UN, WHO, and the dozens of international bodies headquartered there. There are engineers and scientists at Novartis and Roche in Basel. And every month, families in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, and everywhere else are trying to send financial support across that corridor. The demand is real. The confusion about how to do it well is also very real.


## Why Switzerland Is a Trickier Corridor Than You'd Expect


Switzerland uses the Swiss franc, not the euro. Most people know this in theory, but the implications don't land until you're comparing transfer services and wondering why the rates look different from what you'd get sending to Germany or France.


The core issue is that most digital remittance services have optimized their infrastructure around EUR transfers. They hold euros in European bank accounts, settle in the eurozone, and pass those efficiency gains to senders. Switzerland sits outside that system. The CHF has its own settlement network, its own banking compliance stack, and some of the faster pathways that work beautifully for EUR transfers introduce friction when CHF is the destination currency.


That friction lands in two places in practice: transfers take longer, and the exchange rate applied at settlement is worse than the headline rate you saw when comparing. You might find a service that moves money to a German bank in six hours but takes two days to a Swiss one. Or you find a rate comparison tool quoting you N1,560 to the franc when the rate applied at the actual point of transfer is N1,620. The spread is where money disappears, not the headline fee.


As of mid-July 2026, the naira has been holding relatively stable against major European currencies, settling around N1,557 per euro on the official market after significant recovery from the lows of early 2025. The CBN's decision to hold its Monetary Policy Rate at 26.5% has kept foreign portfolio inflows steady and narrowed the gap between official and parallel rates to levels we haven't seen in years. The Swiss franc tracks the euro closely, which means the NGN/CHF exchange environment right now is more predictable than it has been for a long time. That matters for anyone setting up regular transfers.


## The Three Main Options Worth Considering


Let me walk through the realistic paths here, because people either overcomplicate this or pick the first option they see without comparing properly.


**Bank-to-bank SWIFT transfers** are the instinct for most people, especially for large amounts. You go to your Nigerian bank, fill out the required documentation, provide the beneficiary details, and send the wire. It works. The problem is cost. Nigerian banks charge sending fees. Swiss banks charge receiving fees. And in between, a correspondent bank often takes a cut that nobody discloses upfront. A transfer of N500,000 can lose N25,000 to N40,000 across the chain depending on which banks are involved and which routing path the payment takes.


For a one-time transfer, say for tuition fees, a rental deposit, or a large family expense, the wire might still be worth it. The bank record, the familiarity of the process, and the lack of transaction limits have value. For monthly recurring support, that cost structure will hurt over time.


**International digital transfer services** like Wise, Remitly, and WorldRemit are the middle ground. They're faster than banks, generally cheaper, and usable from a mobile phone. My habit is to check two or three platforms side by side before committing to any transfer, because the corridor pricing varies more than people expect. That comparison takes less than five minutes and frequently saves real money.

What services built for African senders tend to do better is match the reality of sending from Nigeria: naira as the source currency, mobile-first flows that account for Nigerian bank verification steps, and customer support that understands the specific issues that come up with this corridor. That becomes relevant the first time a transfer is flagged and you need someone who actually speaks that language.


## What You Need to Have Ready on Both Sides


I've watched this catch people by surprise: Swiss banks take compliance seriously, and a transfer that arrives with incomplete beneficiary information or an incorrect IBAN gets held or returned. I've seen this delay transfers by five to seven business days on a corridor where speed matters.


Before you initiate any transfer, confirm three things with your recipient: the full IBAN, which is 21 characters for Swiss accounts and starts with CH; the account name exactly as it appears on the bank record; and the bank's BIC/SWIFT code. For larger amounts, ask whether the recipient's bank has incoming transfer notification requirements. Some Swiss cantonal banks flag large international inflows and request documentation of source of funds.


If the transfer is going toward something where a paper trail matters later, tuition, rent, medical expenses, keep a screenshot of the confirmation, the rate that was applied, and the transaction reference. Switzerland's banking system is precise, and that precision protects you as much as it creates friction.


## Reading the Exchange Rate Before You Send


NGN/CHF is not a widely tracked pair, so most people end up looking at NGN/EUR as the proxy, which is reasonable given how closely the Swiss franc and euro track each other. As of mid-July 2026, that rate sits around N1,557 per euro on the official market, a significant recovery from the N1,774/€ levels seen in January. The naira has strengthened against the pound too, from lows near N1,950/£ to around N1,841/£ this month.


The FX stability story in 2026 is real. Weekly FX turnover in Nigeria's official market hit $2.39 billion in the week ending July 17, a 46% jump week-on-week driven almost entirely by spot transactions. That kind of liquidity is what you want to see when you're planning transfers. It means the rate you get today is more likely to reflect the actual market than it would have been in 2024 when thin liquidity exaggerated rate swings.


That said, stability is not permanence. If you're setting up a monthly transfer arrangement, build in a small buffer, around 5% above the amount you need to land, to absorb normal rate movement. In a stable period, that buffer sits unused. In a volatile month, you'll appreciate having it.


## A Realistic Guide to Comparing Fees


The headline fee is not the full cost. Here's how to actually compare services for this corridor.


The exchange rate margin is the biggest variable. A service advertising zero transfer fees but applying a 2.5% margin above the mid-market rate will cost you more than a service charging N2,500 flat at the mid-market rate, once your transfer gets above roughly N250,000. For smaller amounts under N200,000, flat fees can add up faster. For larger amounts, percentage-margin services often take more. Running the numbers for your specific amount takes a minute and is usually worth doing.


Delivery time has real value too. For urgent payments, paying a premium for same-day or next-business-day delivery is a legitimate calculation, not a luxury. Don't optimize purely for cost and then find yourself explaining to your recipient why money they needed on Tuesday arrived on Friday.


Transfer limits are also worth understanding clearly before you set up an account with any service. Some platforms cap individual transactions at amounts that require you to split a larger transfer across multiple transactions, each carrying its own fee structure. If you're sending N1.5 million or more, check per-transaction and per-month limits before you start the account verification process.


## What the Recipient in Switzerland Needs to Know


Swiss banks don't always send real-time push notifications for incoming international transfers. Your recipient may need to log into their account and check, rather than wait for an alert. This is not a problem, just a characteristic of how Swiss banking tends to work.


For new arrivals in Switzerland who don't yet have a bank account, PostFinance is often the most accessible option because it serves residents regardless of their banking history in other countries. Neon and Yuh, two Swiss fintech apps, also have faster onboarding for people with a valid Swiss residence permit.


For time-sensitive situations where the recipient needs cash and the bank account isn't set up yet, some services offer alternative delivery options. This is less common for Switzerland than for markets in West or East Africa, but it's worth checking with whichever platform you're using.


## Getting This Right the First Time


I'll be honest: the Nigeria-to-Switzerland corridor has a reputation for being complicated, and that reputation isn't entirely wrong. Swiss banking compliance requirements, the CHF sitting outside the eurozone, and the relatively thin digital infrastructure for this specific pair combine to create friction that doesn't exist for more trafficked corridors like Nigeria to the UK or Kenya to Germany.


Most of that friction is front-loaded. Getting your recipient's details exactly right, choosing a service that handles CHF competently, and understanding the current rate environment takes real effort the first time. Once you have done it once and the money has arrived cleanly, the second and third transfers are routine.


The naira is more stable now than it has been in years. The tools available to Nigerian senders have improved significantly. What remains is for more people to do the comparison properly rather than defaulting to a bank wire that charges them three times over and still takes a week.


Whether you're sending monthly support to someone studying in Zurich, covering an emergency expense for a family member in Geneva, or handling business payments into Switzerland, the right setup saves real money every month. That's worth taking the time to figure out properly.
