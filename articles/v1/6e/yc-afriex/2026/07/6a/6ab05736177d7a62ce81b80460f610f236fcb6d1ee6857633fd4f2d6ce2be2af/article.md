# How to Send Money from Kenya to France: A Practical 2026 Guide

The first time someone told me about their experience trying to send money from Nairobi to Paris, the detail that stayed with me was not the fee. It was the rate. Four days, two bank visits, and a conversion rate that was nowhere near what Google showed when they typed "KES to EUR" that morning. The money arrived. A chunk of it didn't.


That story is not unusual. It is essentially the standard experience for this corridor, which is why I want to be specific about what you're actually dealing with before you pick a service and send.


The Kenya-France remittance corridor is larger than most people realise. There are tens of thousands of Kenyans in France, many who came to study at grandes écoles and engineering schools in Paris, Lyon, and Toulouse and stayed for careers. Kenyan businesses are importing French machinery, wine, cosmetics, and specialty goods. Parents in Nairobi are paying tuition fees and monthly support for children working through some of Europe's most demanding degree programmes. All of them are doing the same thing: trying to move Kenyan shillings to euros in a way that doesn't lose money to friction they didn't know was there.


This guide is my attempt to close that gap.


## The hidden cost most senders miss


The real problem gets buried in most transfer guides, so I'll put it up front. The transfer fee is the visible cost. The exchange rate spread is the real cost, and it is almost always larger.


When a bank or a remittance service converts your Kenyan shillings to euros, they apply an exchange rate that is typically worse than the mid-market rate you see on Google or XE. Sometimes by 2%. Sometimes by 5% or more. On a 150,000 KES transfer, roughly EUR 1,050 at mid-market, a 4% spread costs you about EUR 42 before you have paid any stated fee. Most people don't see this until they compare what they sent to what actually arrived.


My habit when evaluating any remittance service is to run a quick check: find the current KES/EUR mid-market rate on Google or XE, then compare it to the actual rate the service quotes you. The gap between those two numbers is your real cost, not the fee displayed in bold at checkout.


Kenya's shilling has faced depreciation pressure over the past two years. The Central Bank of Kenya has managed it carefully, but the exchange rate environment means families sending support from Kenya to France feel the squeeze more acutely when the shilling weakens. You can't always time a transfer, but understanding that the rate you see today is not guaranteed tomorrow is the first piece of useful context for anyone using this corridor regularly.


## Who actually sends money on this corridor


The best method genuinely depends on why you're sending, so I want to be specific about the different use cases.


The largest group is family support. Kenyan students in French universities receive monthly or term-by-term transfers from home. These are typically recurring, often between 50,000 and 300,000 KES, and cost matters more than speed when you're sending every month for three or four years. Shaving even 2% off the effective rate on a 200,000 KES transfer saves 4,000 KES per send.


The second group is business payments. Kenyan importers paying French suppliers need to send euros reliably, on a specific payment schedule, and sometimes above amounts that trigger additional documentation requirements. For these senders, certainty and speed matter as much as the rate. A supplier invoice due on the 15th cannot wait four days for a bank wire to clear.


The third group is Kenyan professionals already in France, sending back to Nairobi to support family. This is the reverse direction from what most of this guide covers, but most of the platforms below work both ways.


## What most people get wrong


The most common mistake is defaulting to a bank wire. KCB, Equity Bank, Co-operative Bank, and the other Kenyan commercial banks all process international SWIFT transfers. The process feels familiar and safe. I understand why people default to it.


The problem is the combination: SWIFT fees on the Kenyan side typically run between KES 3,500 and KES 5,000 per transfer. The bank's FX spread is usually 4-6% worse than mid-market. And the transfer takes 3-5 business days. You are paying more, waiting longer, and getting a worse rate, all at once.


The second mistake is over-relying on M-PESA for international transfers. M-PESA Global Pay has been expanding its corridors, and Safaricom has invested significantly in its international payment infrastructure. But for Kenya-to-France specifically, the options through M-PESA are more limited than they are for the UK or US corridors. It is worth checking the current M-PESA Global Pay country list and rates, but you should not assume it is your cheapest path for EUR transfers just because it is the default for everything else.


The third mistake is finding one service and never switching. The remittance industry has been pricing down aggressively over the last two years. Competition between platforms has pushed rates and fees lower, especially on African corridors that used to be ignored. The service that was cheapest when you first discovered it in 2023 may not be the cheapest now. Spending five minutes comparing before each large transfer is not paranoia. It is good financial hygiene.


## What the options actually look like in practice


This guide covers four categories of options. Run the comparison yourself before you commit.


Dedicated cross-border transfer apps are the workhorses for this corridor. Services like Wise operate on mid-market rates with transparent fee disclosures, meaning what you see at checkout is what your recipient gets in euros. For a 100,000 KES transfer, you can see the full cost before you confirm. The main friction for first-time senders is identity verification, which can take time on the first transfer but is a one-time process.


African-focused platforms have gotten significantly better on European corridors. Afriex covers Kenya and serves European destinations including France. I'd encourage you to compare it against other options since rates change, but African-built platforms tend to understand the KES corridor more specifically than global products that treat Africa as an afterthought. We built Afriex to solve exactly this kind of friction, though the honest advice is to run your own comparison and find what gives you the best rate for your specific amounts and timing.


Your bank is still worth considering if you are sending a very large amount, above EUR 5,000, and have an existing relationship that comes with negotiated FX rates. Some business banking clients get better rates through their relationship manager than through retail channels. If you are making regular trade payments from Kenya to France, this conversation with your bank's trade finance desk is worth having. Just go in with the mid-market rate open on your phone.


For senders whose recipient does not yet have a French bank account, Western Union and MoneyGram operate physical locations in Nairobi and can deliver to France. The rates are usually worse than the digital options. But if the recipient needs cash or the ability to pick up without a bank account, this remains a practical option.


## What to have ready before you send


One thing that catches people off guard, especially on first transfers, is documentation. For transfers above the equivalent of roughly USD 1,000, most services will ask for the purpose of the transfer. Having a clear answer ready, whether that is tuition payment, family support, or supplier invoice, along with any supporting documentation, prevents holds at the worst possible moment.


If you are a business paying a French supplier, get the full banking details in advance: IBAN, BIC/SWIFT code, the bank's full name, and the supplier's exact legal entity name. A single digit error in an IBAN causes the transfer to fail or be returned, and recovery takes time you do not have when a payment is due.


One more practical note: some services will verify your KRA PIN or national ID before releasing larger transfers. If you are sending above 500,000 KES, check whether your chosen platform will ask for this before you initiate. A verification delay in the middle of a time-sensitive payment is avoidable with a few minutes of preparation.


## On timing and recurring transfers


For monthly support transfers, I would not overthink timing. The rate will fluctuate, and trying to catch the optimal window on a recurring payment creates more stress than it saves money. Pick a service with consistent rates and transparent fees, set up the transfer on a fixed day each month, and move on.


For business payments where the amount is large and the rate environment feels unfavorable, some platforms offer rate locks or forward contracts that let you lock in today's rate for a transfer you plan to make in 30 to 60 days. If you know you need to send EUR 3,000 next month and the KES is currently at a better rate than usual, this option is worth asking about.


The Kenya-France corridor is not going to become perfectly easy overnight. The infrastructure is improving, the rates are coming down, and the tools are getting better. But the gap between what you can pay if you know your options and what you will pay if you default to the bank wire is still large enough to matter, especially if you are sending regularly.


Run one comparison before your next transfer. Three services, the same amount, side by side. The numbers will tell you more than I can.
