# How to Send Money from Ethiopia to China

I've talked to enough Ethiopian importers to know what the payment process usually looks like. You have a supplier in Guangzhou or Yiwu. You have a commercial invoice and agreed delivery terms. And then the real work starts: figuring out how to actually get the money from Addis Ababa to a Chinese bank account without it disappearing into a foreign exchange queue for two months.


China is Ethiopia's largest trading partner. Ethiopia brings in machinery, electronics, building materials, and consumer goods from Chinese manufacturers at a scale that puts this corridor among the most active on the continent. Bilateral trade has exceeded three billion dollars annually, and the relationship has deepened steadily through infrastructure investment and manufacturing partnerships under the Belt and Road Initiative.


What the trade data doesn't capture is how hard the payment leg of that relationship can be. The route from birr to yuan has real structural challenges, and knowing what they are before you try to move money will save you a lot of frustration.


## Why the forex situation matters to you specifically


The National Bank of Ethiopia (NBE) maintains foreign exchange controls that shape every outbound transfer from the country. The Ethiopian birr is not freely convertible. When a business wants to pay a Chinese supplier in US dollars or yuan, it needs to go through its commercial bank and apply for a foreign exchange allocation. Priority goes to essential imports: petroleum, pharmaceuticals, certain food products. Everything else joins a queue.


For importers in fashion, electronics, building materials, or consumer goods, this creates a practical timing problem. Your supplier quoted a price. You agreed on delivery. But by the time the FX allocation clears, the birr has moved, your cost calculations are off, and your supplier has sent three unanswered messages on WeChat wondering what happened to the wire.


The wait can stretch from weeks to months depending on your sector, your bank relationship, and the overall pressure on Ethiopia's foreign reserves at that moment. Nigeria has had its own version of this problem. The difference with Ethiopia is that the controls sit closer to the top of the transaction, so there are fewer informal channels and more formal ones that you actually need to understand.


## The mistake most Ethiopian importers make


Most businesses I've watched approach this corridor treat it as a banking problem. They go to Commercial Bank of Ethiopia, Dashen Bank, or Awash Bank, submit the required paperwork, and wait. When things slow down, they escalate. When that doesn't help, they call someone who promises to move things faster.


The issue isn't which bank you use. Your bank isn't slow because it's inefficient. It's operating under the same foreign exchange constraints as every other commercial bank in Ethiopia. Switching institutions rarely solves the core problem.


What changes your outcome is understanding which payment channels work for which transaction types, structuring your imports to match the channels that are actually available to you, and knowing the documentation requirements before a compliance flag shows up and stalls a transfer that was already in motion.


## Four channels worth knowing


Bank wire transfers via SWIFT are still the backbone of commercial payments out of Ethiopia. CBE, Dashen, Awash, and Wegagen Bank all offer outbound international wire services. The process requires documentation: commercial invoice, purchase order, and import permit where applicable. NBE priority FX allocations go to certain sectors. If your business imports fall into a priority category, bank wires can move faster than you might expect. If they don't, the queue is real and planning around it is more useful than fighting it.


Licensed money transfer operators are a second channel. Ethiopia authorizes a set of International Money Transfer Operators to operate in the country, including Western Union and MoneyGram. These work well for personal transfers and smaller amounts. Business payment limits are tighter, and the same documentation requirements apply.


Digital transfer platforms are where I'd point most small and mid-sized Ethiopian importers right now. Mobile-first apps that connect into international payment rails are increasingly available in the Ethiopian market, and the practical advantage they offer is real: they aggregate foreign exchange capacity across multiple sources rather than drawing from a single bank's allocation under NBE constraints. Rates, limits, and processing times vary, and the only honest way to compare is to look at end-to-end cost on a specific amount, not just the headline fee.


For larger importers moving high volumes per shipment, Letters of Credit are worth understanding. Ethiopian commercial banks offer LC facilities, and Chinese suppliers who've worked with African buyers understand how to receive them. The setup overhead isn't worth it for small or one-off transactions, but for businesses running consistent high-volume import cycles, LCs can reduce supplier friction significantly and sometimes unlock better payment terms from the Chinese side.


## What to have ready before you initiate a transfer


Chinese banks are specific about incoming wire details. A small error on beneficiary information causes a return transfer, which means delays, additional fees, and an unhappy supplier. Before you initiate anything, get the following from your supplier in writing:


The full legal name of the bank, not a nickname or abbreviation. The branch name and address. The account number as it appears on the account. The SWIFT/BIC code for the bank. The beneficiary name exactly as it appears on the bank account. For amounts above a certain threshold, your bank or platform may also require the purpose of payment. Stating "payment for goods under contract reference \[number\] per attached commercial invoice" is a clean statement that moves through compliance review without triggering unnecessary flags.


Keep your commercial invoice and purchase order on hand during the transfer process. Some platforms request these upfront as part of onboarding or transaction initiation. Others only ask when a compliance review is triggered on the back end. Either way, having them ready means you're not hunting for documents when the clock is running.


One additional thing: verify that your Chinese supplier's bank can receive international wires directly. Some smaller regional Chinese banks route incoming foreign wires through correspondent banks, which can add a day or two to settlement. Asking your supplier which bank they use, and whether they've received international transfers to that account before, is a five-minute conversation that can save you a week of confusion.


## Working out what you're actually paying


Ethiopia-to-China transfers typically involve two currency conversions: birr to US dollars, then US dollars to Chinese yuan. Each step carries a margin, and not all platforms show you both margins transparently when they present their rates.


My approach when evaluating any transfer option is to calculate end-to-end. What does 500,000 ETB become, in yuan, by the time it hits the recipient account? That final number, compared across platforms and against the interbank rate for both pairs, tells you the real cost of the transfer. The headline "zero fees" or "low fees" claim on its own tells you very little.


The NBE publishes its daily reference exchange rate for the birr. The People's Bank of China publishes daily rates for the yuan. Use both as reference points when you compare platform quotes. You may not get the interbank rate on either conversion, but you should know how far off it you are.


## A shift happening across the region


Kenya finalized its Virtual Asset Service Provider regulations in July 2026, completing a licensing framework for all digital financial platforms operating in the country. I've been watching developments like this across East Africa, not because stablecoin regulation is directly relevant to most Ethiopian importers paying Chinese suppliers today, but because these regulatory frameworks shape which platforms can operate and how reliably they can serve you over time.


Ethiopia's NBE has moved more conservatively than its East African peers on digital payment licensing. But the direction of travel across the region is toward more structured and formalized digital transfer ecosystems, not less. Platforms that have been navigating regulatory complexity in multiple African markets are building the compliance infrastructure that will make them more reliable over a three to five year horizon.


For Ethiopian businesses planning their payment infrastructure with any foresight, understanding the digital transfer landscape now is better than being forced to learn it quickly when a bank wire is stuck and you're looking for alternatives under pressure.


## If you're sending as an individual


If your situation is a personal transfer rather than a business payment, the practical dynamics are different. You're not dealing with FX allocation queues for import licenses. The channels available to you are licensed MTOs and digital transfer platforms, and both work reasonably well at personal transfer volumes.


What matters most: verify the platform you use is authorized to operate in Ethiopia. Check the rate you're being quoted against the NBE's published rate for the birr. Confirm the transfer method your recipient prefers. Chinese bank accounts can receive international wires with the standard beneficiary details. If your recipient wants funds into Alipay or WeChat Pay instead, check with them about whether their setup can accept international transfers, because some configurations of those accounts don't support inbound international transfers without specific settings enabled.
