# What Are Corporate Bonds? A Complete Guide

Corporate bonds represent one of the largest and most actively traded segments of the global capital markets. The U.S. corporate bond market had approximately $11.7 trillion outstanding as of Q1 2026, with year-to-date issuance through June 2026 reaching $1.52 trillion, up 28.1% year over year (SIFMA, US Corporate Bonds Statistics, sifma.org, as of Q1 2026). For investors looking to generate income, manage portfolio risk, or diversify beyond equities, understanding how corporate bonds work is a foundational step.


This guide covers the mechanics, types, risks, and market structure of corporate bonds, grounded in current market data and primary regulatory sources.


**What Is a Corporate Bond?**


A corporate bond is a debt security issued by a company to raise capital. When you purchase one, you are lending money to that company. In exchange, the issuer makes a legal commitment to pay periodic interest, called a coupon, and to return the original principal at a specified maturity date.


As the SEC's investor education materials describe, buying a corporate bond means the company "promises to pay a specified rate of interest during the life of the bond and to repay the principal when it matures" (SEC, Saving and Investing, sec.gov/securities-topics/saving-investing-teachers). Corporate bondholders are creditors, not owners. In a bankruptcy or liquidation, bondholders generally rank ahead of common stockholders in the claim on company assets, though there is no guarantee of full recovery.


This distinction from equity matters: bond investors forgo the upside of ownership in exchange for a more predictable, contractually defined income stream.


**How Corporate Bonds Work: Core Mechanics**


Several key terms define how a corporate bond functions:


- **Face value (par value):** The principal amount the issuer promises to repay at maturity, commonly $1,000 per bond in U.S. markets.


- **Coupon rate:** The stated annual interest rate, applied to face value to determine periodic payments. Most U.S. corporate bonds pay coupons semiannually (PIMCO, Understanding Corporate Bonds, pimco.com/us/en/resources/education/understanding-corporate-bonds).


- **Maturity date:** When the company must repay the principal. Corporate bond maturities typically range from 1 to 30 years, often categorized as short-term (up to 5 years), medium-term (5 to 12 years), and long-term (over 12 years) (Corporate Finance Institute, corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/fixed-income/corporate-bonds).


- **Yield:** The annual return expressed as a percentage of the bond's current market price, which fluctuates, rather than its face value. Yield and price move in opposite directions.


A simplified example: An investor purchases $10,000 of a 5-year corporate bond with a 7% annual coupon and semiannual payments. That investor receives $700 per year in interest until maturity, then the $10,000 principal, assuming no default.


After issuance, bonds trade in the secondary market, where prices fluctuate based on interest rates, the issuer's credit quality, and broader market conditions. Investors who sell before maturity may realize a gain or a loss relative to par.


**Types of Corporate Bonds**


Corporate bonds come in several structural forms. Understanding the differences may help investors evaluate which characteristics align with their individual circumstances.


**By interest structure:**


- **Fixed-rate bonds:** Pay a constant coupon for the bond's life. Cash flows are predictable, but prices are more sensitive to interest rate movements.


- **Floating-rate bonds:** Coupons reset periodically based on a benchmark rate. These may reduce interest rate risk because payments adjust as market rates move.


- **Zero-coupon bonds:** Issued at a deep discount with no periodic interest payments. The investor receives the full face value at maturity; the price difference represents the return.


**By convertibility and embedded options:**


- **Convertible bonds:** Standard coupon-paying bonds that give the holder the option to convert into a set number of shares of the issuer's common stock. They typically offer lower coupons in exchange for potential equity upside (PIMCO, pimco.com/us/en/resources/education/understanding-corporate-bonds).


- **Callable bonds:** Give the issuer the right to redeem the bond before maturity, usually after a specified call date. Issuers generally pay a higher coupon to compensate for this feature, which introduces reinvestment risk for bondholders if the bond is called when rates have fallen.


- **Putable bonds:** Give investors the right to sell the bond back to the issuer at a predetermined price before maturity, providing downside protection if rates rise or the issuer's credit deteriorates, typically in exchange for a lower yield.


**By security and collateral:**


- **Secured bonds:** Backed by specific company assets pledged as collateral. If the issuer defaults, bondholders have a claim on those pledged assets.


- **Unsecured bonds (debentures):** Backed only by the issuer's general creditworthiness. In a default, unsecured bondholders rank behind secured creditors.


**Credit Ratings: The Risk Spectrum**


Credit ratings are the primary tool used to gauge an issuer's default risk. The two main categories are:


- **Investment-grade bonds:** Rated BBB-/Baa3 or higher by major rating agencies. Generally associated with lower default probability and lower yields.


- **High-yield bonds (speculative-grade or "junk"):** Rated below BBB-/Baa3. These carry materially higher default risk and typically offer higher coupons as compensation.


Historical data suggest investment-grade bonds have experienced default rates below 1% per year on average, while high-yield bonds have averaged roughly 4 to 5% annually, though rates vary significantly with economic cycles (PIMCO, pimco.com/us/en/resources/education/understanding-corporate-bonds).


Where does the market stand in mid-2026? According to the Federal Reserve's May 2026 Financial Stability Report, triple-B corporate bond yields were approximately 5% and high-yield bond yields approximately 7% as of April 2026. Investment-grade spreads ran close to 100 basis points over comparable Treasuries, while high-yield spreads were approximately 300 basis points, both described by the Fed as near historically tight levels (Federal Reserve, Financial Stability Report, May 2026, federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-may-financial-stability-report-asset-valuations.htm).


More recent FRED data for the ICE BofA US High Yield Index show an option-adjusted spread of 271 basis points as of July 16, 2026 (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, FRED, BAMLH0A0HYM2, fred.stlouisfed.org/series/BAMLH0A0HYM2). Tight spreads reflect the market currently pricing in relatively low credit risk, though issuer fundamentals and individual circumstances vary considerably.


**Key Risks to Understand**


Corporate bonds carry several distinct risk categories that investors may want to evaluate before making any decisions:


- **Credit (default) risk:** The issuer fails to make interest or principal payments. This is the most fundamental risk specific to corporate bonds versus government securities.


- **Interest rate risk:** Bond prices fall when market interest rates rise. Longer-maturity bonds generally experience greater price swings. Investors who hold to maturity receive par value (assuming no default), but those who sell early may face losses if rates have risen.


- **Inflation risk:** Fixed coupon payments lose purchasing power when inflation rises. Longer-term bonds carry more exposure to this dynamic.


- **Liquidity risk:** Some corporate bonds trade infrequently, with wide bid-ask spreads, making it difficult to sell quickly at a fair price.


- **Call risk:** If a callable bond is redeemed early, investors lose future coupon income and may need to reinvest at lower prevailing rates.


- **Event risk:** Unexpected corporate developments (restructurings, regulatory changes, or major economic disruptions) can affect credit quality and pricing.


**The Corporate Bond Market: Primary and Secondary**


Corporate bonds are bought and sold through two distinct market structures.


**Primary market:** New bonds are created and sold for the first time. Proceeds flow directly to the issuing company for capital-raising purposes. Institutional investors typically receive the majority of allocations, though retail investors may access certain investment-grade offerings through brokerage platforms during designated retail order periods.


**Secondary market:** Existing bonds trade between investors, predominantly over the counter (OTC) through dealers. The issuer receives no proceeds from these trades. Prices are quoted as a percentage of par value: a bond quoted at 98.50 costs $985 per $1,000 face value.


FINRA TRACE (Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine) is the regulatory mechanism that requires dealers to report OTC corporate bond transactions, providing post-trade price transparency to investors. Investors may use publicly available TRACE data to benchmark dealer quotes before executing a trade (FINRA, finra.org).


Retail investors typically access corporate bonds through a FINRA-member brokerage account, using the firm's new-issue offerings and fixed-income trading tools. Bond screeners allow investors to filter by maturity, yield, credit rating, coupon type, and call features.


**Corporate Bonds vs. Other Fixed-Income Instruments**


Understanding how corporate bonds compare to alternatives may help frame where they fit within a broader fixed-income context:


- **Versus U.S. Treasuries:** Corporate bonds typically offer higher yields to compensate for credit risk that government bonds do not carry. As of April 2026, investment-grade corporate yields ran roughly 100 basis points above comparable Treasuries (Federal Reserve, Financial Stability Report, May 2026, federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-may-financial-stability-report-asset-valuations.htm).


- **Versus equities:** Bondholders are creditors, not owners. Returns are generally more predictable via coupons, and bondholders rank ahead of stockholders in bankruptcy, though they forgo equity upside.


- **Versus municipal bonds:** Corporate bonds are generally subject to federal income tax, while municipal bond interest is often exempt. Individual tax circumstances vary and may affect the relative after-tax yield comparison.


**How Companies Use Bond Proceeds**


Companies issue bonds to fund a range of activities: expansion and capital investment, acquisitions, refinancing of existing debt at potentially lower rates, and general working capital needs. The scale of 2026 issuance reflects continued corporate demand for debt financing. Year-to-date issuance through June 2026 reached $1.52 trillion, with investment-grade technology issuers alone approaching $100 billion in Q1 2026 bond issuance (Federal Reserve, Financial Stability Report, May 2026, federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-may-financial-stability-report-asset-valuations.htm; SIFMA, sifma.org/research/statistics/us-corporate-bonds-statistics).


Understanding why a company is issuing debt, and whether that purpose is consistent with a sound credit profile, is one dimension that credit analysis addresses.

**Considerations for Investors**


Corporate bonds may serve different roles depending on an investor's broader financial picture. Some investors focus on the income stream from coupons. Others use corporate bonds to diversify a portfolio concentrated in equities. Still others evaluate bonds as a way to match a specific future cash need with a maturity date.


Individual circumstances vary considerably. Factors such as time horizon, tax situation, income needs, and risk tolerance all bear on how any fixed-income instrument may fit within a broader strategy. Allocation decisions of this nature are ones many investors find useful to discuss with a qualified financial advisor.
