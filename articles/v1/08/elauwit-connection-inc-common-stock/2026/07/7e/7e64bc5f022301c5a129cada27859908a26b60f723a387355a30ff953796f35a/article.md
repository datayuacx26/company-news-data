## What Is a Managed WiFi Contract for Multifamily Properties?


A managed WiFi contract is an agreement between a property owner and a connectivity provider covering the design, installation, operation and ongoing support of a property-wide wireless network. Unlike traditional bulk internet deals where you simply resell ISP service, managed WiFi contracts create an infrastructure partnership with defined responsibilities on both sides.


The contract scope typically includes network design and engineering, hardware procurement and installation, 24/7 monitoring and maintenance, firmware updates and security patches, and direct resident support. Your provider handles the technical complexity while you focus on property operations.


These agreements generally run five to 10 years. That timeline reflects the significant infrastructure investment involved—fiber backbone installation, access point deployment throughout units and amenity areas, and integration with property management systems. The length also means contract terms matter enormously. A bad deal locks you in, while a good one creates genuine value for years.

## How Should You Evaluate Service Level Agreements for Managed WiFi?


Service level agreements define the provider's performance commitments in measurable terms. A strong SLA transforms vague promises into enforceable obligations. A weak one gives you nothing to hold onto when service falls short.


### Uptime Guarantees and What They Actually Mean


The standard industry benchmark is 99.9% uptime, sometimes called "three nines." This sounds impressive until you calculate what it allows: roughly 8.7 hours of downtime per year. For a property with hundreds of residents working from home, streaming entertainment and relying on connected devices, 8.7 hours of outage is significant.


When evaluating uptime guarantees, look beyond the percentage. Ask how uptime is measured—does the provider use their own monitoring tools or independent verification? What counts as an outage? Some contracts define downtime narrowly, excluding "scheduled maintenance" windows that could add hours of annual interruption.


Also clarify what the guarantee applies to. Property-wide outages are one thing. But what about partial outages affecting specific buildings or floors? The best SLAs address both scenarios with specific commitments.


### Credit Structures That Create Real Accountability


Uptime guarantees mean nothing without consequences for missing them. SLA credits are the mechanism that creates provider accountability. Review the credit structure carefully.


Meaningful credits typically start at 10% of monthly service fees for each violation, escalating with repeated misses. Some providers cap total credits at one month's fees per year—which may be reasonable or may limit your recourse if service consistently underperforms.


Automatic credits demonstrate provider confidence. If they require you to document outages, submit claims and wait for review, you'll rarely see compensation even when it's warranted. Automatic crediting based on their own monitoring data shows they stand behind their commitments.


### Speed Guarantees and Peak Performance Standards


Advertised speeds create expectations. Contract guarantees determine whether those expectations are met. Push for specific minimum speed commitments—typically 80% of advertised bandwidth—measured at the unit level during peak usage hours.


Peak hours matter because network performance degrades when everyone comes home from work, starts streaming and loads up their connected devices. A provider delivering 900 Mbps at 2 a.m. but 200 Mbps at 8 p.m. isn't meeting resident expectations, regardless of what the marketing says.


Testing methodology should be defined in the contract. Independent speed tests from resident devices provide more meaningful data than measurements at the network switch. Specify how and when testing occurs, and what happens when results fall short.

Support quality separates tolerable connectivity from genuinely good resident experience. Your managed WiFi provider's support model determines whether network issues become property management headaches or get resolved before your leasing team ever hears about them.

Response time commitments should be explicit in the contract. For critical issues affecting the entire property, four-hour response is a reasonable expectation. For individual unit problems, next-business-day may be acceptable depending on your resident population and expectations.

## How Do Ownership and Infrastructure Terms Affect Your Flexibility?


Network ownership is one of the most consequential—and least discussed—elements of managed WiFi contracts. Who owns the infrastructure determines your options at contract end, your ability to switch providers and your control over the network serving your residents.


### Provider-Owned vs. Property-Owned Infrastructure


In provider-owned models, the managed WiFi company owns all network equipment—fiber, switches, access points and in-unit hardware. You pay a monthly service fee that covers both connectivity and equipment. When the contract ends, they can remove their equipment, leaving you to start fresh with a new provider.


Property-owned models give you the infrastructure. You may pay higher upfront costs for equipment, but you own assets that stay with the building regardless of which provider operates the network. This approach offers more flexibility but requires capital investment.


Hybrid models exist as well. Some providers offer[Network-as-a-Service](https://www.elauwit.com/services) options with zero upfront capital, while others support owner-funded builds. ### Transition Rights and Provider Changes


Contracts end. Providers get acquired. Circumstances change. Your contract should address what happens when you need to transition to a new provider.


Key questions:


-


What is the process for terminating service?


-


What notice is required?


-


Does the provider assist with transition, or do they simply disconnect?


-


For provider-owned infrastructure, can you purchase equipment at fair market value rather than starting from scratch?


Exit provisions deserve as much attention as initial pricing. A contract with favorable monthly rates but punitive early termination fees and no transition assistance locks you in even when service falls short. Negotiate buyout options at specific intervals—years three and five are common checkpoints—with declining penalties over time.


### Infrastructure Documentation and Handoff


Whether you own the network or not, you need documentation. As-built drawings, equipment inventories, configuration backups and maintenance records should be contractually required deliverables.


This documentation protects you during provider transitions, supports your property team's understanding of the network and provides critical information for future upgrades or expansions. A provider unwilling to commit to thorough documentation raises questions about their long-term partnership orientation.


## What Commercial Terms Shape the True Cost of Managed WiFi?


Per-unit pricing is the number everyone focuses on, but total cost depends on a range of commercial terms that may not appear prominently in the initial proposal. Understanding these terms helps you compare providers accurately and avoid unexpected expenses.


### Base Service Fees and What They Include


Monthly per-unit fees typically range from $25 to $50 for managed WiFi service, depending on speed tiers, provider and portfolio size. But "per-unit fee" can mean different things across providers.


Clarify what's included. Does the fee cover equipment, or is that separate? What about installation? Support? Premium SLA tiers? Some providers bundle everything into a single per-unit rate. Others price components separately, making apples-to-apples comparison difficult.


Also confirm how fees are calculated. Do you pay for occupied units only, or all units? What about model units, vacant units or amenity spaces? These details affect your actual monthly expense.


### Installation and Infrastructure Costs


Upfront installation costs vary dramatically based on building condition, existing wiring and scope of work. New construction deployments integrated during the build process differ significantly from retrofits requiring extensive cabling work in occupied communities.


Get fixed-price quotes for installation rather than estimates. Unexpected costs during deployment create budget problems and strained relationships. Reputable providers conduct thorough site assessments before quoting and stand behind their numbers.


Network assessment fees, permit costs and riser construction charges may appear as separate line items. For portfolio deals, these should typically be waived or heavily discounted. Push back on fees that feel like padding.


### Revenue Sharing and NOI Contribution


Managed WiFi can contribute to NOI through revenue sharing arrangements, bulk billing models or amenity fee structures. The contract should clearly define the economic model.


In revenue-sharing arrangements, you receive a portion of what residents pay for upgraded service tiers. The split varies—50/50 is common, though larger portfolios may negotiate more favorable terms. The contract should specify how revenue is calculated, when payments are made and what reporting you receive.


Bulk billing models allow you to include internet in rent or charge it as a mandatory amenity fee. The spread between what you pay the provider and what you charge residents becomes direct NOI contribution. ### Price Escalation and Long-Term Cost Predictability


A five-year contract with 5% annual escalation costs 27% more in year five than year one. Understand how prices change over time and negotiate caps on increases.


Annual escalation tied to CPI (Consumer Price Index) provides some predictability while acknowledging inflation. Fixed percentage caps—2-3% annually—give you more budget certainty. Some providers offer locked pricing for the full contract term in exchange for longer commitments.


For portfolio acquisitions, negotiate price protection for properties you add during the contract term. Locking in current per-unit rates for future additions—typically with 12-24 month windows—protects you from price increases as your portfolio grows.

## How Should You Structure the Contract Negotiation Process?


Negotiating managed WiFi contracts requires preparation, leverage and patience. Providers expect negotiation—their initial proposals leave room for it. Rushing through this phase leaves value on the table.


### Building Leverage Through Competition


Request proposals from at least three qualified providers. Even if you have a strong preference, competitive tension improves your outcome. Providers who know they're competing sharpen their pricing and terms.


Share your evaluation criteria transparently. When providers understand what you prioritize—support quality over lowest price, for example—they can tailor proposals accordingly. This saves time and surfaces real differences.


### Prioritizing What Matters Most


Not everything is equally negotiable. Providers have more flexibility on some terms than others. Identify your priorities and focus negotiating energy there.


Per-unit pricing often has less room for movement than people expect—margins in managed WiFi aren't enormous. But installation costs, escalation caps, SLA credits and exit provisions often have significant flexibility. Trade-offs between categories can create wins for both parties.


### Getting Terms in Writing


Verbal commitments made during sales conversations don't bind anyone. Every important term should appear in the contract itself—the main service agreement, SLA exhibit and any referenced schedules.


Read the entire document, including standard terms and conditions. Providers sometimes bury unfavorable provisions in boilerplate language. Have legal counsel review the agreement before signing, particularly for larger portfolio deals.


## What Red Flags Should Disqualify a Managed WiFi Provider?


Some warning signs should end your evaluation immediately. Recognizing red flags early saves time and prevents costly mistakes.


### Vague SLAs Without Specific Metrics


If a provider can't define uptime guarantees, speed minimums and support response times in specific, measurable terms, their commitments mean nothing. Vagueness in the SLA predicts vagueness in actual service delivery.


### Resistance to Third-Party Testing


Providers confident in their service quality welcome independent verification. Those who resist third-party speed testing or insist their own measurements are the only valid standard have something to hide.


### Exclusive Marketing Rights


Contracts that require you to exclusively promote the provider's brand to residents, limit your communications or prevent you from discussing service issues with prospective providers create unhealthy relationships. Reasonable marketing cooperation is fine; restrictive exclusivity is not.


### Financial Instability or Acquisition Risk


Providers with recent layoffs, executive departures or rumors of sale create contract continuity risk. While acquisition isn't always negative, entering a long-term agreement with a company likely to change ownership introduces uncertainty.
