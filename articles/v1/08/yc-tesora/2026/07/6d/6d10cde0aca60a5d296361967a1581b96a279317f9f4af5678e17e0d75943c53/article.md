# Some 4th of July doomerism: Is AGI an endgame for actuaries?

# Some 4th of July doomerism: Is AGI an endgame for actuaries?


### Predictions by an investor who came to insurance sure AGI would delete the actuary, but left thinking actuaries are irreplaceable surgeons in the insurance org.


[AI for Actuaries @Tesora.ai](https://substack.com/@aiforactuariestesoraai)


and[Vivek](https://substack.com/@tesora3)


Jul 02, 2026


I am not an actuary. I am on the other side of the curve, an AI enthusiast who assumes almost every white-collar job is a wrapper around a model that does not quite exist yet but soon will. When I went looking for the industries that fall first, insurance


*initially* sat at the top of my list. On paper, it seems like a very automatable business in the economy. A claim about risk, backed by data, sold to someone willing to stand behind it. That is the whole thing. Point a good enough model at the data and the humans start to look like latency.


The more I looked, the more the setup confirmed the thesis. Insurance MGAs are people focused on specialist distribution – think Jiffy Lubes, car rentals, ATVs, funerals, anything you can hang a loss distribution on. The old end-to-end carrier long ago shattered into specialists: retail broker, wholesale broker, MGA, and paper that has drifted toward commodity, with reinsurers trading perils the way an FX desk trades currency. Every one of those layers, when you squint, is the same actuarial function wearing a different suit. Pricing is modeling. Reserving is modeling. Setting an appetite is modeling with a dial on it. Four copies of one job, each charging a toll, and the cost of that job is sliding toward zero. This was going to be easy.


And the models did get good. Not demo-good. Good enough that the people inside stopped re-checking them on routine work, the way you stop re-checking a junior in year two. That is the threshold that matters, because it is the moment a company wires the model straight into its own data and lets it act. The exposure already lives in a warehouse. Soon the warehouse hands the whole thing to an insurance endpoint that reads it, runs the claims logic, and returns a bound price. The insurance company stops being a place you call and becomes an MCP you talk through. The broker-to-MGA-to-paper relay collapses into one endpoint. I had the blog post half-written.


Then I met the actuary, and my thesis walked into a wall.


The machine runs at full speed right up to one desk, and then it stops. Somebody has to explain the number to a regulator who did not enjoy their morning. Somebody has to still be there, in person, when the book develops badly and a plaintiff’s attorney wants to know who set the rate and what they were thinking. This is a profession that gets sued for being wrong slowly, and slow wrongness is exactly what a fluent machine is best at hiding. I had been treating the actuary’s signature as a ceremony. It is not a ceremony. It is more like a surgeon’s hand. Let me explain.


> The entire value of the signature is that a human with a career to lose looked at the thing and said yes.


That was the analogy that reorganized my thinking. We have had AI reading scans better than radiologists for years, and the famous prediction that it would end the specialty simply did not happen, because someone still has to be accountable in the room when a life is on the table. The actuary is the surgeon of the balance sheet. The model can imagine the risk all day. It cannot be the one who is liable when the incision goes wrong, and you do not actually want it to be, because the entire value of the signature is that a human with a career to lose looked at the thing and said yes.


With that, here is my predicted timeline of where we are going, as someone who once badly wanted the aggressive version to be true. In 2026, “Copilots” everywhere and trust nowhere. By 2027 someone runs a small, boring book where the model writes the rate and the actuary merely signs it, and that book runs at an expense ratio the rest of the company cannot match, the kind of number a CFO cannot un-see. By 2028 an AI-native MGA exposes its underwriting as an endpoint. By 2029 the bill comes due for whoever let an over-eager machine rubber-stamp its way through a book, because slow wrongness only starts to show up after two years. The winners are not the firms that removed the actuary. They are the firms that aimed all that automation at everything except the signature, and kept a surgeon on call.


I stopped believing accountability is a task you can hand to something with nothing to lose. A model cannot be sued, cannot be sanctioned, and cannot lose a license it never held, and those are the exact levers that make a human signature mean anything. So the actuary does not disappear. The actuary gets a smaller team, a wider blast radius on every decision, and a machine that has finally worked out why there was a human standing there in the first place. Maybe one day the judgment itself loads into the model like a skill and the room gets smaller still. Until then, the winning move is not to replace the surgeon. It is to hand them a better prepared operating room, and let them continue handling the incisions.
