# Can AI ever be useful for actuaries? My first ‘Wow Factor”

# Can AI ever be useful for actuaries? My first ‘Wow Factor”


### An actuary’s frustration with software that never seemed to work.


[AI for Actuaries @Tesora.ai](https://substack.com/@aiforactuariestesoraai)


May 14, 2026


I can love technology, but I quickly lose patience when it falls short. Even in the old days, I would rather build my own application than deal with software that had numerous stray clicks and workarounds.


This shortness may seem like a hindrance, but to be honest, the unwillingness to deal with frustration has served me fairly well. It forced me to better understand complex models and kick the tires more often. I build better UIs (on-screen interfaces) and ask what the user actually wants. Perhaps I gleaned the trait from one of my early heroes, Hadley Wickham (renowned creator of the Tidyverse and prolific R statistician), who quipped in an interview, “One of the attributes that has made me successful is that I am exquisitely sensitive to frustration.”


Thanks for reading AI for Actuaries! Subscribe for free to receive new posts and support our work.


Bringing these feelings into 2026, my fascination with AI has been mired with endless frustrations. Hallucinations, sycophancy, math dyslexia. Solutions that seem right (and it insists they are!) but no; never right. Just watch numerous


[videos](https://www.instagram.com/p/DXAj4OOjiD7/) on social media exposing its shortcomings. My drive to see it work gave birth to an endless cycle.


My solution, for a time, has been to just use AI at what it was good at, coding. This worked for some time, but it brought me back to the numerous tasks where I was still wondering why AI wasn’t doing this yet. The limits of AI for me stopped at very narrow tasks or a pretty competent coder, the one thing I thought AI did really well.


And that’s when I saw a little demo from two guys who would become my cofounders at Tesora AI. They gave it an actuarial problem — the kind where assumptions and source data are more important than methods. Its behavior stopped me cold. It didn’t just answer. It paused. It asked the user essential requisite questions instead of guessing. It referenced the precise CAS syllabi that I would have referenced. It listed its assumptions. It questioned one of its own intermediate steps and redid it. By the time it gave a final answer, it had left a full audit trail — sources, methods, caveats — the way a careful junior actuary would present it if they knew their work was going to be reviewed. I had never seen AI behave like this, with the self-awareness to defer to the real actuary where needed.


Without guardrails, these systems can be presumptuous and opinionated. But we all are that way. What makes this system different is that it works hard to avoid things like hallucinations and nonworking solutions. It can actually do actuarial tasks, but it also knows when it needs sign off from someone with authority and expertise.
