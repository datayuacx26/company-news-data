# Actuaries’ Logical Aversion to AI

# Actuaries’ Logical Aversion to AI


### Fear of agentic AI is well-founded, but can be mitigated.


[AI for Actuaries @Tesora.ai](https://substack.com/@aiforactuariestesoraai)


May 22, 2026


I have been an actuary long enough to develop a healthy skepticism toward shiny new tools. In consulting, where your work product can sit in a carrier’s rate filing or support a multimillion-dollar reinsurance treaty, good enough is never good enough. One misplaced assumption or undocumented step and you are explaining yourself to a regulator, an auditor, or worse, opposing counsel years later.


That instinct is why many actuaries I respect still eye AI with suspicion. In my previous piece, I shared my first


[Wow Factor](https://tesoraai.substack.com/p/can-ai-ever-be-useful-for-actuaries) moment with a system that actually paused, asked the right questions, and left a proper audit trail. That experience opened my eyes. Yet here I am, still wrestling with the same old doubts like an actuary who just found one more undocumented macro. Some habits die hard, especially when your name is on the work and the stakes are this high.


The pushback is not Luddite resistance. It is professional prudence rooted in decades of hard lessons and even harder Excel spreadsheets that somehow survive every system migration. It is not enough that I can reproduce the work myself. What matters is fulfilling the actuarial standards laid out in the ASOPs — that the work can pass a rigorous audit, that other actuaries can pick it up and fully understand it, and that if I get hit by a bus tomorrow, I don’t create the exact key person risk that ERM is always trying to avoid. Messy models carry institutional knowledge that lives in the heads of people who may have left the firm years ago, probably right after they finally documented something. Clients demand deliverables they can hand to the next actuary with confidence, not a vague promise that the AI seemed pretty sure about it this time.


This brings us to the central tension I keep turning over in my head.


**How do we balance our deep aversion to unreliable tools with the genuine potential in front of us?** My gut still says no when I ask whether agents can truly be better auditors than experienced actuaries. My head, however, knows it is likely only a matter of time. An agent could be trained on the latest ASOPs, on thousands of historical filings, and on best practices for traceability. It could maintain perfect memory of source documents, assumptions, and methodological choices. It could flag inconsistencies faster than any single human, and without the afternoon sugar crash or the sudden urge to check email.


That internal conflict, gut versus head, perfectly sums up the actuarial aversion. We will always want to see it a dozen times, just as we first collect extensive data before modeling any risk. Call it professional paranoia. I call it due diligence that has saved my skin more times than I care to admit. This caution is not a flaw. It is the feature that makes our profession valuable, even if it makes adopting new technology feel like pulling teeth while wearing gloves and a hard hat.


The practical concerns are very real. Final deliverables must remain fully auditable Excel work products. Not because Excel is sacred, though it might as well have its own shrine in the actuarial hall of fame complete with incense and quarterly offerings. Its transparency and manual editability will endure long after fancier interfaces come and go. Any solution that treats the spreadsheet as an afterthought risks becoming shelfware right next to that one predictive model from 2018 that nobody trusts anymore.


Institutional memory offers a clear opportunity. When a key modeler leaves, firms spend weeks doing forensic archaeology on old files. An agentic system with strong context retention could become a net positive here, turning scattered knowledge into human-readable, ASOP-aligned artifacts instead of trapping it in a black box.


I believe the future is an agentic harness. A coordinated set of specialized agents would handle data ingestion, normalization of factors like All Other Perils deductibles, statistical curve fitting with proper documentation, audit trails, and human review checkpoints. This builds ASOP principles directly into the workflow instead of tacking them on at the end like a compliance checkbox.


**Actuaries’ aversion is not illogical. It is the natural reaction of a profession whose value rests on careful judgment.** The winning systems will respect that instinct. They will make the audit trail effortless, the Excel output pristine, and the handoff to human review seamless.


Until then, the healthy skepticism remains responsible. Any tool that wants our trust had better earn it the hard way: one reproducible, auditable, client-ready deliverable at a time. I remain cautiously optimistic, probably after kicking the tires a few dozen more times. And maybe a few dozen after that.
